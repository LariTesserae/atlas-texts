In this secret bowl of perpetual moisture lives something that partakes of both the cirque's ancient patience and its teeming vitality. 

She might be mistaken for a heap of moss-covered stones when still, which is often. Her form suggests something between human and pika, between boulder and beast—compact, rounded, with limbs that seem to telescope when needed but otherwise fold into her torso like a closing fist. Her skin has the quality of wet granite, dark and mineral-streaked, with patches of actual lichen growing in the creases of her joints and along the ridge of her spine. These are not parasites but partners; she cultivates them deliberately, encourages their slow spread with applications of crushed stone and bog water.

Her eyes, when open, are the most startling feature—compound like an insect's but each facet contains what appears to be a pool of that same bog water, complete with fairy shrimp swimming in endless loops. To look into them is to see the cirque from a thousand angles simultaneously, to understand how the midges' columns are actually letters in a language written on air, how the pikas' calls map the underground waterways, how each cushion plant holds a small portion of the mountain's memory.

She moves with deliberate slowness most of the time, her broad feet spreading like the cushion plants to distribute her weight across the sphagnum. But when hunting—for she does hunt, subsisting on a diet of insects, fairy shrimp, and the occasional unwary pika—she can move with shocking speed, her body unfolding in ways that seem to violate the basic principles of skeletal structure. One moment a stone among stones, the next flowing like water across the bog surface, her mouth opening impossibly wide to engulf clouds of midges or snatch a spider from its web.

Her dwelling is not the obvious stone semicircle—that she leaves for the occasional human visitor, understanding its purpose as decoy and distraction. Instead, she inhabits a network of spaces beneath the bog itself, chambers hollowed out where the water runs between bedrock and moss. Here she has accumulated treasures: stones with particularly pleasing patterns of mineral staining, the shed skins of every salamander in the cirque arranged by size and season, balls of spider silk wound as carefully as yarn, the skull of a mountain goat that wandered too high and died in a late spring storm.

She knows every fairy shrimp in every pool by the pattern of its swimming. She maintains careful relationships with the pikas, occasionally leaving specific sedges at the entrances to their burrows—messages they understand and sometimes answer. The migratory birds know her as a fixture, no more threatening than the stones, and sometimes a water pipit will perch directly on her head, mistaking her stillness for geology.

Her age is impossible to determine. She speaks of the glacier that carved this cirque as one might speak of a departed parent—with familiarity and a kind of fond grief. She claims to remember when the first cushion plants took hold, describes watching them spread grain by grain across bare stone. But her memory is not linear; she experiences time more like the bog experiences water—all at once, in every direction, past and present seeping into each other.

In the brief summer flowering, she becomes almost social. She emerges more often, moving among the blooms with what can only be called delight, tasting each species, rubbing pollen on her lichen patches, sometimes humming in harmonies that make the fairy shrimp dance in synchronized spirals. She has been known to leave small arrangements of flowers on the rim of the cirque—signals perhaps, or offerings, or simply art for its own sake.

She is both guardian and inhabitant, neither fully of the human world nor entirely separate from it. The knapped stone in the shelter is her work, abandoned not from lack of skill but from a sudden understanding that the edge was unnecessary—that everything she might need to cut was better torn or dissolved or simply waited out. She knows the names of things in languages that predate human speech, in the tongue of moving water and shifting stone, in the pheromone alphabets of insects and the ultrasonic gossip of pikas.

When winter comes, she retreats deeper, her metabolism slowing until she nearly matches the mountain's own pace of breathing. She dreams then, and in her dreams the cirque dreams too—of ice and pressure, of the slow birth of soil, of the first brave lichen making landfall on virgin stone, of all the small lives that have flickered through this cupped palm of mountain, each adding their note to a song that has been building since the world was young.

She is the cirque's memory made flesh, its witness and its child, as permanent as the granite and as ephemeral as the mist, a living testament to the strange abundances that flourish where water, stone, and time converge in just the right proportions.
