The Resonants move through the mist like questions given form. Their bodies are translucent, composed of something between flesh and mineral—a living glass that refracts the plateau's strange light into impossible spectra. They stand taller than humans but narrower, their limbs articulated in too many places, bending with the fluid grace of water finding its path down stone.

They have no faces as such. Where a face might be, there is instead a complex arrangement of crystalline plates that shift and overlap, catching sound and light, focusing both into patterns that serve as their primary sense. These plates ring softly as they move, harmonizing with the water-song of the plateau. Each Resonant's facial arrangement is unique—some have plates that spiral inward like frozen whirlpools, others wear them in cascading scales, still others in geometric patterns that seem to follow mathematical rules too complex for simple observation.

Their skin weeps constantly, not with water but with something that resembles it—a substance that emerges from pores too small to see and sheets down their bodies in thin films. Where this moisture touches the volcanic stone, it leaves traces of color, those nameless hues between violet and green. Over time, the paths they walk most frequently become marked with these stains, creating a map of their movements across the plateau that glows faintly in certain lights.

They build nothing permanent, but they tend the stone circles with religious devotion. Groups of them will gather at a circle for days, adjusting the position of each rock by fractions of degrees, listening to how the changes alter the acoustic properties within. They seem to be tuning the circles like instruments, though to what purpose remains unclear. Sometimes they stand inside the circles for hours, their plate-faces aimed upward, their bodies perfectly still except for the constant flow of their strange perspiration.

The Resonants communicate through harmonic convergence. Two or more will position themselves at specific distances from each other, angling their facial plates until the water-sound of the plateau focuses through them in precise ways. The resulting tones are hauntingly beautiful—not words but pure meaning transmitted through frequency and interference patterns. A conversation between Resonants sounds like a composition being improvised in real-time, each participant adding layers that complement and complicate what came before.

They feed on light itself, their translucent bodies acting as living greenhouses. The succulent star-colonies that dot the plateau are their gardens—they cultivate these plants not to eat but to create specific qualities of reflected and refracted light. Different arrangements of plants produce different nutritional wavelengths. A Resonant feeding stands perfectly still among their garden, their body catching and concentrating the filtered light until they glow from within like biological stained glass.

Their relationship with time seems different from other creatures. They move with extreme deliberation, each gesture carefully considered, but can also accelerate to blindingly fast motion when necessary. It's as if they exist partially outside normal temporal flow, dipping in and out of standard time as needed. Sometimes a Resonant will be observed in one location, only to appear impossibly far away moments later, with no sense of having traveled the intervening distance.

They show curiosity about the volcanic glass, often spending days examining a single piece of obsidian, turning it slowly in their articulated hands, holding it up to catch different angles of light. They seem to be reading something in the stone—perhaps its thermal history, perhaps something else entirely. Occasionally, they will carry pieces of glass to specific stone circles and place them precisely within, altering the acoustic properties in subtle ways.

The Resonants rarely gather in groups larger than seven. When they do, it's always in prime numbers—groups of eleven, thirteen, seventeen. These larger gatherings occur at specific times that seem tied to conditions of light and moisture rather than any regular calendar. During these gatherings, they create complex harmonics that can be heard across the entire plateau, songs that make the mist itself seem to dance and swirl in patterns too intricate for the wind alone to explain.

Young Resonants are nearly invisible, their bodies so transparent they can only be seen by the distortion they create in the mist. As they age, they become more opaque, gathering color from years of light-feeding until the eldest are like walking prisms, their bodies containing entire spectra of those unnameable hues. The very old ones move hardly at all, standing for weeks in the same spot, their harmonics so deep they're felt rather than heard.

They leave no dead. When a Resonant reaches the end of their existence, they simply begin to sing—a solo composition that grows quieter and more complex over days until it passes beyond the range of perception. Their body grows increasingly transparent during this process until finally there is only a slight distortion in the air where they stood, and then nothing at all. Sometimes, in those spots, new arrangements of star-colonies begin to grow in patterns that suggest intention.

The Resonants seem to be waiting for something, or perhaps listening for it. Their entire civilization, if it can be called that, appears designed to detect and amplify some signal not yet arrived. They tune their stone circles, they position themselves across the plateau in complex geometric arrangements, they sing their harmonics into the mist—all with the patience of beings who measure time in geological epochs.

Whether they are native to this young volcanic plain or arrived from elsewhere is impossible to say. They seem both perfectly adapted to this environment and subtly alien to it, as if they shaped themselves to match the plateau's strange properties or perhaps shaped the plateau to match their needs. They disturb nothing unnecessarily, leave few traces beyond their color-stains and their songs, and observe everything with the focused attention of scientists or mystics or both.

Sometimes, when the light strikes just right and the mist parts in certain ways, it's possible to see patterns in how the Resonants position themselves across the plateau—vast geometric forms that speak of purpose beyond simple survival. They are creating something, or preparing for something, with the same patient certainty with which water shapes stone. And in their translucent bodies and their impossible songs, in their careful tending of acoustic spaces and their diet of filtered light, they embody the plateau itself—that sense of newness and becoming, of forms still deciding what they will ultimately be.
