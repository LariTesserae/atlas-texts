The Masons of the Moment dwell here, though "dwell" implies a permanence they neither possess nor desire. They are beings of transition, their forms as provisional as the space they inhabit—sometimes bipedal, sometimes something more distributed, always in the midst of becoming otherwise.

Their bodies are assemblages of the same warm basalt that comprises the cisterns, but articulated, mobile, held together by a kind of mineral intention. Each Mason appears unfinished: an arm might terminate in raw stone that slowly differentiates into fingers over the course of days, while another limb smooths and simplifies, returning to the crude suggestion of appendage. Their surfaces weep the same condensation as the walls, beading with moisture that runs in rivulets down their forms, leaving temporary trails that evaporate and reform elsewhere.

They do not speak—their mouths, when they have mouths, are often just depressions that deepen and shallow with the rhythm of the dripping water. Communication occurs through placement and gesture, through the careful positioning of the tools they eternally arrange and rearrange. A Mason might spend hours placing a single obsidian hammer just so, adjusting its angle by imperceptible degrees, before another arrives to move it three handspans left. There is no conflict in this; each placement is simply another word in an ongoing conversation conducted in the grammar of objects and space.

The Masons' eyes, when present, are pools of collected water held in shallow depressions in their heads, reflecting nothing but seeing everything through the principle of accumulation. They observe by letting the space settle into them, understanding through sedimentary patience. Sometimes these pools overflow, and the tears that run down their faces carve new channels in their stone flesh, channels that may become permanent features or heal closed by the next viewing.

Their work is the Work—the endless construction and deconstruction of those partial walls, the gathering and scattering of tools, the maintenance of the pools. But maintenance suggests preservation, and what the Masons do is more like cultivation. They encourage certain stones to emerge faster, placing their warm hands (when they have hands) on the floor and waiting with geological patience for the response from below. They guide the condensation with subtle breathwork, exhaling mineral-scented air that influences where the next drops will form and fall.

Each Mason tends to particular Apertures, moving through them with a fluidity that belies their stone composition. Their bodies compress and expand to navigate these passages, sometimes leaving behind fragments of themselves—a finger, a small pile of gravel that was once a joint—that other Masons incorporate into the Works. These fragments retain a kind of purpose, continuing to participate in the grand arrangement even separated from their original whole.

Time touches them differently than it touches their space. A Mason may freeze mid-gesture for hours or days, becoming indistinguishable from the architecture, before suddenly resuming movement as if no time had passed. Others move in accelerated bursts, accomplishing weeks of arrangement in minutes before slowing again to match the rhythm of the dripping water. They seem to exist in multiple temporal streams simultaneously, choosing which current to ride based on the needs of their Work.

New Masons emerge from the stone itself, not born but recognized. A pile of rocks that has accumulated in just the right configuration suddenly stands, shakes off the excess material, and begins its first arrangement. Old Masons don't die—they simplify, their forms becoming more and more basic until they are just standing stones, still participating but no longer mobile, their awareness spread through the mineral matrix of the cisterns themselves.

They know no hierarchy, no leadership, only the democracy of placement and the consensus of accumulation. Each decision emerges from the sum of all previous decisions, creating patterns that build meaning through repetition and variation. The Works they create are not for inhabitation or use but for the sake of the making itself—practice exercises for some future moment when the universe might need structures built by those who understand the patience of stone and the persistence of water.

In the rare moments when multiple Masons gather at the same pool, they engage in a ritual of watching water fall. They position themselves to observe the same drops from different angles, creating a compound understanding of each impact. These gatherings can last days, ending only when one Mason places a tool in the pool, changing the pattern of ripples and beginning a new phase of Work.

The Masons of the Moment are process incarnate, beings who exist to enact the slow transformation of potential into form, forever building toward a purpose that remains as fluid as the condensation on their stone skin, as solid as their patient, perpetual labor.
