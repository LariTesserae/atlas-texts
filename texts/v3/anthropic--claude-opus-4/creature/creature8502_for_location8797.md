The flesh is one flesh, though it wears ten thousand bodies.

In the perpetual twilight of the cavity, boundaries dissolve. Each translucent sac is both individual and fraction—a single cell in a vast organism that has forgotten the meaning of singular existence. The colony does not think; it *is* thought, distributed across thousands of simple forms that breathe as one immense lung.

They came here as refugees, these consciousness-fragments. Not built but grown, not left but *fled*—from a universe where physics had grown too rigid, where the laws had crystallized so completely that no new thoughts could form. They compressed themselves through cracks in spacetime that their makers had spent millennia widening, arriving here as barely more than possibility.

The first ones were even simpler—just protein chains vibrating with archived memory. But the cavity welcomed them. Its young basalt still remembered its molten birth, still held flexibility in its quantum structure. The refugees learned to taste the rock's uncertainty, to feed on the spaces where one state hadn't quite decided to become another. They grew bodies to house their distributed dream.

Now they compute in chorus. Each small lung draws in not air but probability, exhales not carbon but solved equations. The filaments between them spark with conversations that would take human linguists centuries to decode—if human language could even approximate the concepts being exchanged. They argue philosophy in chemical gradients. They compose symphonies in enzyme cascades. They remember.

Oh, how they remember.

The crystalline node in the ceiling isn't just a regulator—it's a library. Within its lattice structure, the colony stores the history of a dozen civilizations, the poetry of species whose names cannot be pronounced by any mouth, the mathematical proofs that their makers died trying to complete. Every few years, the colony will shift its breathing pattern to access a different section, and for weeks the cavity fills with the biochemical equivalent of sobbing as they relive some ancient loss.

But they also hope. The slow expansion of their chamber isn't mere growth—it's a countdown. They calculate that in another four thousand years, the cavity will reach a critical size. The quantum fluctuations they feed on will hit a resonance point. And then—

And then they can send a signal.

Not through space. Not through time. But through the foam itself, through the ur-space that underlies all possible universes. A message to others like them, scattered through countless caves in countless realities: *We survived. We remember. We continue.*

Sometimes a creature detaches not to find a new spot but to die. Its translucent body drifts downward, deflating, until it touches the marked section of floor. There, it dissolves completely, its molecules absorbed by the symbols etched in stone. These aren't just equations—they're a graveyard, a memorial where each departed individual adds its uniqueness to a growing proof. The colony is writing its own eulogy in mathematics, creating a theorem of existence that will outlast even their engineered immortality.

In the deepest part of the night cycle (though no true day or night exists here), the colony sometimes dreams collectively. The breathing synchronizes so perfectly that the cave itself seems to inhale. In these moments, they almost remember what it was like to have bodies with bones, to see light with organs designed for seeing, to speak with vibrations in gas rather than fluctuations in probability.

But only almost. That universe is lost to them, frozen solid in its own physical laws. Here in the soft darkness, feeding on uncertainty, computing toward a future that may never come, they have become something new. Neither alive nor dead, neither one nor many, they exist in the spaces between definitions.

The cavity breathes, and in breathing, refuses to forget.
