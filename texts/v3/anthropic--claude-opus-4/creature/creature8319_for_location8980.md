The Keepers of the Crystal Garden are barely distinguishable from the mineral growths they tend—at first glance, they might be mistaken for particularly elaborate formations of living stone. They stand no taller than the crystals themselves, their bodies a fusion of flesh and mineral that defies easy categorization.

Their skin, if it can be called that, is a patchwork of geological strata. Smooth planes of polished obsidian flow into rough pumice at the joints, while veins of copper and pyrite trace patterns across their limbs like circulatory systems made manifest in metal. Their fingers are long and articulated like a jeweler's tools, each digit tipped with a different mineral—diamond for cutting, jade for smoothing, magnetite for sensing the invisible currents that flow through the garden.

They move with the patience of erosion, each gesture deliberate and precisely calibrated. A single step might take minutes as they test the stability of the basalt, feeling for the microscopic fractures where new growth might take hold. When they work—adjusting the angle of a crystal cluster to better catch the light, or carefully relocating a colony of lithophagic bacteria to a richer vein of minerals—their movements become fluid, almost dance-like, as if they're conducting a symphony only they can hear.

Their faces are the most unsettling aspect of their appearance. Features seem to shift between organic and geological depending on the viewing angle—now showing something almost human in the arrangement of mica sheets that form expressions, now revealing the alien architecture of their true nature. Their eyes are compound structures of tiny crystals, each facet capable of perceiving different spectrums of light and energy. When they blink, it sounds like wind chimes.

The Keepers communicate through resonance. They press their palms against the basalt and send vibrations through the stone, creating complex harmonics that the others feel through their feet. Sometimes they gather in the meditation circle left by the ancient builders, sitting in perfect stillness for days, their bodies becoming nodes in a larger conversation that pulses through the mountain itself.

They feed as the garden feeds—directly from stone and sun. During the daily hour of prismatic light, they position themselves to catch specific wavelengths, their crystalline portions glowing as they absorb energy. They can also dissolve minerals directly through their skin, leaving smooth hollows in the rock where they've rested. Their waste, if it can be called that, enriches the garden—microscopic crystal seeds that they excrete through their pores, each one a potential new growth.

There are never more than seven Keepers in the garden at once, and never fewer than three. Where they come from, or where they go when they leave, remains a mystery. Some theorize they're birthed from the garden itself, emerging from particularly complex crystal formations that achieve some critical threshold of organization. Others believe they're the descendants of the original builders, transformed over generations by proximity to this strange ecosystem.

They show no interest in the human world beyond their bowl of young volcanic stone. Climbers who stumble upon the garden report being ignored completely, as if they were no more significant than passing clouds. But those who damage the crystal formations, even accidentally, describe a sensation of being watched by something vast and implacable, of pressure building in their skull until they flee.

The Keepers' most remarkable ability is their cultivation of new crystal species. They work like artists or gardeners, encouraging certain growths while pruning others, guiding the evolution of the garden toward some incomprehensible aesthetic. They seem to perceive possibilities in the stone that won't manifest for centuries, planting the seeds of future wonders with infinite patience.

At night, when the metallic butterflies roost among the crystal fronds and the garden dims to its faint bacterial glow, the Keepers sing. It's not a sound meant for human ears—more a vibration that travels through stone and bone alike, a lullaby for sleeping minerals. Those who've heard it from a distance describe it as the loneliest sound in the world, like the mountain itself dreaming of what it might have been.

They are guardians of a boundary, tending the place where life took an impossible turn. In their careful movements and crystalline patience, they embody the profound strangeness of their realm—neither fully alive nor truly mineral, but something between, something other, something that could only exist here where the rules bent just enough to allow stone to reach toward the sun.
