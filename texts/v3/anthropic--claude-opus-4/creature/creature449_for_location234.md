In the steeping shadows beneath the great tree, where the turbine hall's metal ribs cage a green heart, lives the Tender.

No one remembers if she was always here, or if she arrived with the first colonizing vines. Her skin has taken on the texture of bark—not rough, but fissured in patterns that catch and channel moisture. Moss grows in the hollows of her collarbones. Her hair, once perhaps brown or black, has become indistinguishable from the aerial roots that cascade around her shoulders. When she moves through the turbine hall, checking the ancient machinery, it's impossible to tell where her fingers end and the tendrils begin that she uses to test flow rates and nutrient levels.

She doesn't speak much anymore. Her voice, when it comes, sounds like water moving through pipes—a liquid burble that the district's other inhabitants have learned to interpret. They bring her offerings: burnt-out grow-lights to be rewired with bioluminescent fungi, broken pumps that she'll coax back to life with a combination of mechanical knowledge and vegetal intuition. In exchange, she ensures their sector of the root-mass receives what it needs—more nitrogen here, better drainage there.

The Tender sleeps standing up, if she sleeps at all. More often, she's found in a state of active rest, her bare feet rooted in the shallow water, drawing up information through her soles. Her eyes, when open, reflect green even in darkness. When closed, you can see things moving beneath the lids—not dreams, but data flows, the constant monitoring of ten thousand biological processes.

In the flooded streets, the Boatwright poles between buildings on a craft that was once aluminum but is now more living wood than metal. Barnacles of his own cultivation crust its hull—not the saltwater kind, but something adapted to the district's particular brew of tannins and nutrients. These filter feeders process the water as he moves, leaving cleaner channels in his wake. His legs below the knee are perpetually stained amber from decades of wading. His hands are maps of scars from training young branches, from coaxing growth into useful shapes.

He knows every navigable passage in the district, every shortcut through drowned lobbies, every place where the water runs too shallow or too swift. The children who've never known dry streets follow his boat like pilot fish, diving for the coins visitors sometimes throw, though coins have little use here. The real currency is seeds—new varieties to try, proven producers to propagate. The Boatwright's pockets are always full of them, sorted by weight and water requirement.

Up in the canopy level, connected walkway to walkway, Old Yee tends the accidental orchards. They're small enough to scramble through spaces others can't reach, their body made lean and stringy by a lifetime of climbing. Their expertise isn't in growing—anything will grow here—but in pruning, in directing energy where it needs to go. They carry a pair of shears so worn they've achieved the sharpness of surgical tools, and a coil of living rope that responds to whistled commands.

Yee remembers the district before, though they rarely speak of it. Sometimes they'll pause in their work, hand on a branch that grows through what was once an office window, and their face will take on an expression of temporal vertigo. They were here for the transformation—watched it happen one building at a time, made the choice to stay and adapt rather than flee. Now they read the fruits like calendar pages, knowing which varieties mark which decade of growth, which trees are old enough to remember pavement.

In the deeper levels, where the foundation spaces have become aquifers, the Pump Keeper makes her rounds. She's young, relatively—born here, raised on walkways, educated by correspondence courses that arrive on the supply boats. Her understanding of hydrology is intuitive, learned through skin and sense before she ever saw it written in books. She speaks the language of pressure differentials fluently, can diagnose a failing impeller by the change in vibration through her feet.

The Pump Keeper wears a wetsuit patched with pond liner and inner tube rubber. Tools hang from her belt in waterproof pouches—not just wrenches and gaskets, but pH strips, sample vials, the instruments of a chemist as much as a mechanic. She's breeding a new kind of freshwater sponge in the settling tanks, something that can process the industrial legacy still leaching from old foundations. Her laboratory is a half-submerged basement where she's rigged grow-lights over specimen jars, watching filtration rates, making notes in waterproof ink.

On the building-tops-turned-gardens, the Soil Builders work in loose collective. They process the district's dead into new earth—not just plant matter but the constant shedding of the living architecture, the molt of insects, the filtered sediments from the water systems. They tend vast composting operations in old elevator shafts, manage worm farms in filing cabinets, orchestrate the slow digestion of an entire industrial age.

One of them, Marcus, specializes in the inoculation of new substrates. His fingernails are permanently black with spores. He can tell by smell alone whether a batch needs more fungi, more bacteria, a different balance of decomposers. He's patient in the way of all who work with rot, understanding that transformation can't be rushed. His partner, River, manages the distribution networks—an elaborate system of buckets and pulleys that moves finished soil to where it's needed, gravity-fed from the heights to the growing beds below.

In the market spaces—clearings where several walkways converge—the Traders set up each morning. They deal in surpluses: fruit too abundant to process, cuttings from vigorous growth, the occasional salvaged machine part cleaned and restored. The unofficial mayor is a woman who runs the tool library, her inventory hung from living racks that grow a little each year, requiring constant reorganization. She knows who has what skills, who needs what resources, who can be trusted with the delicate task of training new growth into infrastructure.

Children born here move differently than their visiting cousins. They swim before they walk, climb before they run. Their games involve riding the slow currents, building dams to redirect flows, racing through the canopy roads. They learn young which plants sting, which support weight, which fruits ripen in sequence through the seasons. Their education is practical—how to read water levels, how to graft branches, how to maintain the balance between growth and structure.

Some leave when they're old enough, drawn by the dry city's promises. But many return, finding themselves unsuited to solid ground, to air that doesn't taste alive, to buildings that don't breathe. They bring back new knowledge—engineering techniques, biological innovations, ways to better marry the mechanical and the living. The district evolves through these exchanges, its metabolism quickening with each generation.

In the depths of night, when the grow-lights dim and the mist rises thick, you might glimpse the Shepherds. They tend to creatures that aren't quite animals—mobile colonies of fungi, perhaps, or plants that have learned to hunt. These require special care, special feeding, careful boundaries to keep them from either withering or overwhelming. The Shepherds work in silence, communicating through chemical signals, their own bodies partially colonized by their charges. They ensure the district's immune system functions, that the balance tips neither toward sterility nor chaos.

The oldest inhabitants sometimes gather in the turbine hall, around the Tender, for what might be called ceremony or might simply be communion. They press their palms to the great tree's bark, feeling the pulse of nutrients, the circulation of an entire ecosystem. In these moments, the distinction between individual and collective blurs. They are the district as much as it is them—processing, growing, persisting.

The district dreams, and those who live there long enough learn to dream with it. In sleep, they feel the slow extension of roots, the patient accumulation of soil, the seasonal cycles of decay and renewal. They wake with dirt under their fingernails even when they haven't gardened, with the taste of green in their mouths, with an understanding that they are not separate from this place but part of its metabolism.

This is how they live: wet and rooted, patient and productive, neither fully human nor fully other, but something that could only exist here, in this breathing place where the city learned to rot correctly. They are its fruit, its tenders, its consciousness made manifest in flesh that gradually, gracefully, learns to be soil.
