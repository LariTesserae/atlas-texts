In the Scalding Shallows, the original inhabitants have undergone a transformation so complete that distinguishing them from their environment requires careful observation. They were once something like us—bilateral, boned, breathing air—but the vertical ocean has rewritten them across generations.

They call themselves the Dissolved, though "call" isn't quite right. They communicate through controlled clustering, their bodies having separated into collaborative clouds of specialized cells. Each individual—if individuality still applies—exists as perhaps ten thousand autonomous units, ranging from microscopic filters to fist-sized neural nodes. They maintain cohesion through chemical signals and weak electrical fields, able to disperse across cubic kilometers or condense into something almost solid.

A fully gathered Dissolved might occupy the volume of a small room, their component parts swirling in slow helices. The largest parts are the memory-keepers: translucent organs like floating brains, webbed with bioluminescent nerves that pulse in conversation. Around these orbit the workers—muscular cells that can link together to form temporary limbs, sensory clusters that taste the water's mineral content and track the infinitesimal progress of the rising ocean. The smallest components are the dreamers, nearly microscopic motes that do nothing but process experience into something like art.

They live between states of being. Sometimes you'll encounter one spread thin through a kelp grove, each cell no more than a curious sparkle in the water. Hours later, the same individual might contract into something almost humanoid—a cloudy approximation of their ancestral form, complete with the suggestion of limbs and a head-like concentration of neural matter. They adopt these shapes to work their old tools, to maintain the pressure-pods they no longer truly need but keep as museums, as reminders.

The Dissolved remember everything. Their memory-keepers are biological libraries, encoding experience in protein chains that can last centuries in the blood-warm water. They remember when their ancestors first found the Scalding Shallows, already vertical, already impossible. They remember the great decision: to build climbing machines or to change themselves. They remember the first volunteers who entered the water and didn't come back for forty years, returning as clouds that spoke in chemical whispers of what they'd become.

They tend the kelp forests with infinite patience, guiding growth by selectively filtering nutrients, encouraging the crystalline colonies to form new patterns. Some Dissolved spend decades crafting a single grove, arranging the geometric structures to refract light in ways that tell stories. When the light is right, you can swim through their kelp gardens and watch history play out in prismatic projections—the arrival, the transformation, the first time one of them successfully dispersed and regathered.

Their relationship with the giant medusae is symbiotic and strange. The Dissolved sometimes inhabit these creatures, spreading through their nervous systems like benign infections. From inside, they experience jellyfish-time, jellyfish-thought—vast, slow, utterly alien. Some never emerge from these partnerships, choosing to live out their centuries as hybrid beings, part Dissolved consciousness, part medusa dream.

They've learned to read the swarm-colonies—those vast clouds of microscopic organisms that might be their distant cousins, might be failed Dissolved who dispersed too far and forgot how to regather. The patterns these swarms make are messages, or perhaps prayers, or perhaps simply the universe thinking out loud. The Dissolved swim through these formations, letting the tiny bodies pass between their own components, tasting their chemical signatures for meaning.

Sex, for the Dissolved, has become something unrecognizable. Two or more individuals might merge their clouds, components intermingling until identity blurs. They exchange memory-keepers, trading experiences directly. Sometimes these minglings produce new configurations—a third individual assembled from donated parts, or hybrid components that didn't exist before. Their children, if that word applies, are born already dispersed, already knowing how to gather and scatter.

They worship, or at least deeply contemplate, the thermal engine that powers their ocean. Deep in the vertical water, beyond where light penetrates, they sense something vast and mechanical—not a machine, exactly, but a process of such complexity it might as well be divine. Some Dissolved spend their lives diving deeper, their components adapted for pressure, trying to understand what keeps their impossible sea warm and standing.

Death, when it comes, is gradual. Components fail one by one—memory-keepers growing cloudy, workers losing their ability to link, dreamers falling silent. The dying Dissolved often distribute their functioning parts among the community before the last neural nodes flicker out. In this way, something of every individual persists, scattered through the population like inherited memories.

They fear the summit. As the ocean rises its meter per century, they must rise with it, approaching the threshold where water meets air. Some of their kind have already made that transition, learning to exist partially in vapor, partially in liquid. These pioneers report back in chemical fragments about the thin-air world above, where thought moves faster but bodies struggle to maintain cohesion.

The Dissolved build things, but not as their solid ancestors did. They create temporary structures from their own bodies—living walls to channel currents, cellular lenses to focus the dim light, collaborative networks that function as vast, distributed computers. When the work is done, they simply disperse, leaving no trace but the memory encoded in their protein chains.

They've developed art forms impossible for solid beings. Symphony-swimming, where hundreds of Dissolved move in coordinated patterns that take days to complete. Taste-sculptures, chemical gradients crafted in the water that tell stories to those who swim through them. Light-weaving, using their own bioluminescence to create three-dimensional paintings that exist for moments before dissolving.

Some Dissolved have begun experimenting with re-solidification. They compress their components tighter and tighter, trying to recreate the density of bone and muscle. These experiments usually fail—the cells have forgotten how to be singular, how to commit to one form. But occasionally, something emerges from the compression: a hand that holds its shape for hours, an eye that focuses light like their ancestors' eyes once did.

They maintain the pressure-pods not from nostalgia but from a kind of species-wide anxiety. What if they need to become solid again? What if the ocean falls? They teach their young—clouds of newly assembled consciousness—how to use the ancient tools, how to read the etched maps, how to think in terms of inside and outside. Just in case.

The ribbon-fish schools part for them, recognizing something kindred in their dispersed forms. The medusae accept them as partners. Even the sharp-edged kelp seems to grow differently where the Dissolved tend it, forming chambers and passages as if anticipating their needs. They've become part of the ecosystem so thoroughly that distinguishing between Dissolved and dissolved is sometimes impossible.

At the boundaries of their vertical world, where the water thins and begins to fray into mist, the bravest Dissolved practice existing in multiple states. Part of them remains in the liquid, part ventures into vapor, part clings to the crystalline kelp as something almost solid. They're preparing, though for what they cannot say. The ocean rises, has always risen, will always rise. And they rise with it, becoming ever more dispersed, ever more united, ever more strange.

In their protein-chain memories, they keep the image of their original forms—small, compact beings with clever hands and wondering eyes. They honor these ancestors by maintaining their tools, their structures, their written knowledge. But they also pity them, these creatures trapped in single bodies, unable to taste the universe through ten thousand tongues, unable to think thoughts too large for any single brain to hold.

Sometimes, rarely, a Dissolved will gather all their components into the tightest possible configuration and slip into one of the old pressure-pods. They'll seal themselves inside and try to remember what it was like to be singular, to have boundaries, to experience the world from only one perspective. They never last long in this state. The walls feel like prison, the single viewpoint like blindness. They burst from the pods in clouds of relief, scattering themselves through the warm water, returning to their multitude.

This is what lives in the Scalding Shallows: a people who have traded the loneliness of individuality for the vast communion of dispersal. They swim through their vertical ocean as thoughts swim through a mind—connected, separate, eternal, momentary. They are no longer human, if they ever were. They are something new, something the impossible ocean has made possible.

And in their dispersed consciousness, in the slow pulse of their shared thoughts, they dream of the day when the rising water reaches the top of the sky, when they must learn to live in space itself, dissolved in vacuum, held together by nothing but will and memory and the ancient promise to remember what they were.
