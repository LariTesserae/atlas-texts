**The Dwellers of Morne**

They call themselves nothing, for names are a luxury of fixed dimension. The beings that inhabit the Hollow Terraces have stretched themselves thin across the broken mathematics of their home, becoming as much process as creature.

Each dweller begins at dawn as a condensation of possibility near the cisterns. They gather first as suggestions—a lengthening shadow where no body casts it, a reflection in the collected dew that precedes its source. Slowly, they accrete into form: something like a mantis crossed with a theorem, all angles and absence. Their limbs multiply or reduce based on the day's needs. For channel-cleaning, they might manifest twelve delicate appendages tipped with copper brushes. For cistern-tending, they become almost spherical, their bodies a living funnel guiding moisture into stone.

They were human once, or something near it. The oldest among them—distinguished by their transparency and the way light bends around rather than through them—sometimes coalesce into shapes that suggest their ancestry. A torso here, a recognizable hand there, the ghost of a face that almost resolves into expression before dissolving back into angles. But evolution here follows the architecture's logic, not biology's.

The dwellers discovered, over generations that stretched like taffy in the twisted timeflow, that survival meant becoming native to impossibility. Their bones thinned and multiplied into intricate lattices that could compress or expand as space demanded. Their organs learned to function distributed across probability—a heart that beats in three chambers simultaneously, lungs that breathe in one terrace while the body works in another. They digest time itself, extracting sustenance from the temporal inconsistencies that would drive unchanged beings mad.

Communication between them occurs through the arrangement of droplets. They leave messages in the pattern of morning dew, complex ideograms that shift meaning as they evaporate. A conversation might take weeks, with each participant adding their contribution as condensation permits. The young learn by tracing these water-words with their multiplicitous fingers, absorbing not just language but the art of existing in fragments.

They reproduce—if that word applies—through division of probability. When a dweller has accumulated enough presence, enough certainty of existence, they can split that certainty. The process occurs near the deepest cisterns where reality pools thickest. The parent being stretches across multiple terraces simultaneously until the tension spawns a new uncertainty—a child-possibility that must spend its first years learning to maintain coherent form.

Their tools evolve with them. The copper implements pass between generations not as objects but as techniques. A dweller doesn't hold a channel-cleaning brush so much as become it temporarily, their appendages taking on the molecular memory of bronze, the patience of patina. They maintain the waterways through a kind of devoted meditation, each stroke a prayer to consistency in a place that abhors it.

The dwellers map their city not in space but in probability. They know that the third terrace sometimes contains the fifth, that certain doorways lead to yesterday, that the cistern on the ninth level occasionally exists on all levels simultaneously. Their elongated forms navigate these contradictions by embracing them—stretching a limb through Tuesday to retrieve a tool left in Saturday, breathing air from a chamber that won't exist until next season.

They fear only one thing: resolution. To become too real, too fixed in form, is to die in Morne. The mountain's impossible geometry sustains only those willing to exist in question marks. So they cultivate uncertainty, maintaining just enough coherence to work, to communicate, to continue their eternal maintenance of the dew-channels that may themselves be metaphors for something larger—the circulation of possibility through a city that refuses to collapse into merely being.

At night, when the phosphorescence rises, they gather in loose networks across the terraces. Not together—proximity is negotiable here—but connected. They share the day's impossibilities through resonance, their bodies humming at frequencies that make the stone remember its malleability. Together they perform the calculations necessary to maintain their existence: how much reality to express, how much to withhold, how to remain both themselves and not themselves in a place that demands paradox as the price of residence.

They are gardeners tending a garden of broken space, their own bodies part of what they cultivate. Each dawn brings new negotiations with dimension, new accommodations with physics. They persist because persistence here requires constant change, constant adaptation to architecture that dreams itself into new configurations.

The dwellers of Morne are what happens when humanity—or something like it—accepts an invitation to become indigenous to impossibility. They are both less and more than their ancestors, having traded fixed form for fluid existence, certainty for the freedom to inhabit contradiction. They continue their work because the work continues them, each droplet collected, each channel cleaned, each impossible angle navigated adding to their collective evolution into something that can call this broken paradise home.
