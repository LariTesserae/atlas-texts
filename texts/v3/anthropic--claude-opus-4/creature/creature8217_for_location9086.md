In the breathing chamber lives the Tender.

She was human once—or at least, her grandmother's grandmother was. Now she exists in a state of partial translation, her flesh having made a bargain with the wood that neither fully comprehends. Her skin has taken on the texture of smooth bark where it meets the moisture most often: along her forearms, across her shoulders, down the ridge of her spine. These patches don't pain her. They flex and breathe like her original skin, but with a different rhythm—the chamber's rhythm, not her own.

She moves through the root bridges on all fours, her limbs having lengthened and thinned over generations to match the gaps in the lattice. Her fingers and toes can grip wood with a certainty that comes from being partly wood themselves. When she pauses to rest, thin rootlets emerge from her bark-skin and dig into whatever surface she touches, drawing moisture and minerals. She must consciously withdraw them to move again—a process like pulling free from a pleasant dream.

Her hair grows in long, moss-like strands that serve a purpose beyond ornament. Each strand is sensitive to the chamber's humidity, to the chemical signals the wood releases, to the minute changes in pressure that herald a shift in the weave. Through her hair, she reads the chamber's health like a lover's letter written in moisture and spore. When she sleeps—if sleep is the right word for her periods of rooted stillness—her hair spreads across the nearby surfaces, forming a sensory network that monitors whole sections of the chamber at once.

She doesn't speak in words anymore. The human vocal cords are still there, but they've adapted to produce sounds that harmonize with the chamber's water-flow. She sings to the wood in droplets and streams, a liquid language that adjusts the growth patterns of the weave. When new shoots threaten the ancient design, she trills them into compliance. When dead patches appear, she mourns them back to life with subsonic pulses that vibrate through the root network.

Her eyes have developed a nictitating membrane—not for underwater vision, but for seeing through the constant mist without blinking. Behind these membranes, her irises have taken on the green-gold of lit spores, and her pupils dilate and contract independently, allowing her to focus on multiple depths in the chamber's impossible geometry simultaneously.

The Tender feeds on the chamber itself, but gently. She grazes the newest moss growth with flat teeth that regenerate continuously like a rodent's. She sips from the pools where the water has collected enough nutrients from its journey through the wood. Most remarkably, she can photosynthesize to a degree—her bark-skin converts the filtered light into simple sugars that supplement her diet. During the brightest hours, she spreads herself flat against the weave's inner surface, maximizing her exposure, entering a state of blissful productive stupor.

Her memories exist in three layers. The first is personal—flashes of learning the root-paths as a child (though she was never truly a child as humans understand it), the taste of her first successful harmony with the wood's growth, the day she realized she could no longer leave the chamber even if she wished to. 

The second layer is inherited—passed down through moisture and touch from the Tender before her, and the one before that, stretching back to the first human woman who entered the chamber and decided to stay. These memories are fragmentary but crucial: the location of the chamber's heart where the oldest braids converge, the frequency needed to encourage fruiting in the wood, the proper way to introduce new water sources without disrupting the whole.

The third layer isn't memory at all, but the chamber's own experience encoded in her changing flesh. She knows things she never learned: how deep the root bridges extend below the visible floor, what lies in the bright haze beyond the outermost weave, why the geometry folds the way it does. This knowledge comes at a price—the more she understands the chamber, the less she remembers what it feels like to walk upright under open sky.

Her relationship with the shadow-salamanders is symbiotic and ancient. They clean her bark-skin of parasites that would otherwise take hold, and in return, she ensures their breeding pools remain at the perfect depth and temperature. When she extends her hand, they flow up her arm like living wood, their not-quite-flesh merging temporarily with hers. Through these moments of union, she can perceive the chamber from their perspective—a fractal network of moisture highways and chemical gradients that her human-derived senses could never fully parse.

She maintains the chamber not through conscious decision but through being. Her presence is as necessary as water to the weave's continued function. The chamber has evolved to need a Tender just as she has evolved to need the chamber. They are not separate entities but two aspects of a single system, a breathing equation that balances growth against decay, moisture against structure, the wild against the made.

Sometimes, in the moments between root-sleep and wake, she remembers having a name. But names are for things that can be separated from their context, and she cannot be separated from hers. She is the Tender, and the chamber tends her in return, in an endless recursive loop of care that has worn its own groove in the world.

When she dies—for even transformed as she is, she will die—her body won't be removed. It will sink into the root bridges, her flesh feeding the wood that fed her. The chamber will incorporate her thoroughly, down to the last translated cell. And from somewhere in the surrounding forest, another will be drawn by dreams of water and green light, following the ancient pull to enter through the crack in what appears to be stone.
