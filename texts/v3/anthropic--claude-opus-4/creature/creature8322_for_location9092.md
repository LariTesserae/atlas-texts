Within the Root Cathedral dwell the Tenders—though to call them dwellers implies a separation between inhabitant and habitat that no longer exists.

They were human once, perhaps a thousand generations ago. Now they are something else, their forms so perfectly adapted to this living architecture that they seem like mobile extensions of the root-walls themselves. Their skin has taken on the texture and hue of bark, not as camouflage but as convergent evolution—the same forces that shaped the beetle-shells to match the walls have worked their slow sculpture on the Tenders' flesh.

They move through the galleries with liquid grace, their limbs elongated and multiply-jointed to navigate spaces that are neither quite vertical nor quite horizontal. Their fingers have grown into delicate branching structures, each tip sensitive enough to read the health of a root system by touch, to taste the chemical messages that flow through fungal networks, to coax new growth patterns from the living walls with gentle pressure applied over months or years.

The Tenders do not speak—their larynxes have adapted to produce subsonic vibrations that travel through the root networks, communicating in frequencies felt rather than heard. When several gather, the Cathedral itself becomes their medium of conversation, the walls thrumming with meanings too slow and deep for any visitor to comprehend.

Their eyes have evolved into compound structures, each facet attuned to a different wavelength of bioluminescence. They see the Cathedral as a symphony of living light—the healthy glow of thriving moss colonies, the distress signals of infected root systems, the subtle phosphorescent trails left by the crawling carpets of fauna. In the deepest galleries, where even the dim bioluminescence fails, they navigate by touch and by the magnetic fields generated by the root-walls' slow electrical currents.

They feed as the Cathedral feeds—through symbiosis. Specialized pores in their skin host colonies of the same photosynthetic worms that coat the walls, drawing sustenance from the ambient light. Their digestive systems have become gardens, cultivation chambers for the bacteria and fungi that break down the Cathedral's offerings—drops of sweet sap, shed spores, the protein-rich bodies of the tiny creatures that die in their billions every day and rain down like barely visible snow.

The Tenders reproduce rarely and strangely. When one nears the end of their centuries-long life, they root themselves to a wall in one of the deepest chambers. Over months, their flesh merges with the living architecture, their bones becoming indistinguishable from wood, their blood vessels joining the Cathedral's circulatory systems. But before the transformation completes, they fruit—producing dozens of small, seed-like pods that split open to release what are not quite infants but not quite spores. These proto-Tenders spend their first years as crawling things, barely distinguishable from the Cathedral's other fauna, slowly accumulating mass and complexity until they achieve something like human form again.

They are the Cathedral's immune system and its gardeners, its consciousness and its dreams. They prune where growth threatens the eternal equilibrium, coax new pathways where ancient channels clog, and remember—in ways that bypass language—the million small rituals that keep this vast organism-place in perfect stasis.

But perhaps most strangely, they create. Using tools grown from their own bodies—secreted shells that harden into blades, hair-like fibers that can be woven into nets finer than silk—they craft additions to the Cathedral that blur the line between art and organism. A new chamber might take three Tenders fifty years to grow, shaped by careful cultivation of existing root systems, decorated with patterns of bioluminescent organisms arranged like living mosaics. They build slow sculptures from fungal colonies, coax water into new channels that create subtle harmonies with the eternal dripping, breed new species of the crawling fauna to fill ecological niches they've designed.

In the very deepest levels, where the root-walls grow so thick that the spaces between them narrow to mere crawlways, ancient Tenders tend to the most sacred cultivation chambers. Here they preserve the original genetic templates—seeds and spores from the world above, kept in perfect stasis, waiting for some future need that may never come. These deepest Tenders have evolved even further from human form, becoming almost sessile, their bodies spreading into branching networks that merge with the Cathedral's architecture until it's impossible to say where Tender ends and building begins.

They dream collectively through the fungal networks, sharing visions that span centuries, planning changes to the Cathedral that won't be complete for a thousand years. In their dreams, the distinction between self and environment dissolves entirely. They are the Cathedral thinking about itself, evolution become conscious, the made environment maintaining its own making through agencies that were once human but are now something unutterably more complex.

When they die—if death is even the right word for their slow merger with the walls—they leave no corpses. They simply become more Cathedral, their consciousness perhaps persisting in some form within the root-networks, their memories encoded in growth patterns that future Tenders will read and understand through touch. The Cathedral remembers every Tender who has ever lived, just as it remembers the stone tools embedded in its walls, incorporating all into its ever-growing, never-changing whole.
