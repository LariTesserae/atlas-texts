The inhabitants of the Threshing Heights move like mercury through their vertical world—not quite walking, not quite climbing, but something between. They call themselves the Woven, though visitors (rare as they are) might better understand them as living partnerships rather than individuals.

Each citizen begins life as what they term a "kernel"—a human child like any other, until their seventh year when the Grafting begins. The process is neither cruel nor kind; it simply is, like rain or decomposition. Filaments of the city's own neural vine network are introduced beneath the child's skin, where they spread in delicate fractals, following nerve pathways, interfacing with the spine, branching through the cerebrospinal fluid.

By adolescence, a Woven can taste the chemical communications flowing through their district's mycelial web. They know when their neighbor three levels up is anxious (cortisol signatures in the shared air), when the great columnar foundations are preparing for a growth surge (phosphorus uptake spikes, potassium redistribution), when the weather-swarms are about to turn aggressive (pheromone cascades like smoke signals). Their modified nervous systems process this information as naturally as baseline humans process visual input.

The adult Woven are difficult to distinguish from their dwellings at rest. They wear living clothes—symbiotic organisms that photosynthesize nutrients directly through their skin, that adjust their temperature and humidity, that can stiffen into armor when threatened or bloom with warning colors when their host is distressed. Some cultivate their garments for decades, training them into elaborate forms that serve as both status symbols and practical tools. A master grower might wear a robe that can extend pseudopods to manipulate objects ten meters away, or that hosts colonies of bioluminescent organisms arranged in meaningful patterns.

Their bodies show the marks of their environment. Skin tends toward a greenish-gold, suffused with chlorophyll-analog compounds that supplement their nutrition during the long climbs between levels. Their limbs are elongated, with a disturbing flexibility in the joints—adaptations aided by selective genetic therapy and the constant pressure of vertical living. Fingers and toes can secrete a mild adhesive, nothing dramatic but enough to provide extra purchase on the city's organic surfaces.

But it's their minds that have changed the most. The Woven think in gradients rather than boundaries, in metabolisms rather than mechanisms. They have sixteen words for different types of growth, but no word that translates cleanly to "machine." Their mathematics is rooted in biological cycles—they count in spurts and blooms, calculate in ratios of consumption to production.

Small children swing between the columns on transport vines, shrieking with delight as they trigger the vines' contractile responses to propel themselves faster. These same children can read the health of their district by the color of the drainage capillaries, know which walls are safe to eat from during food shortages, understand intuitively which growths will support their weight and which are purely decorative.

The elderly don't retire—they root. As a Woven ages, the symbiotic filaments throughout their body grow denser, more elaborate. Many choose to undergo the final Grafting, allowing themselves to merge with their homes, their consciousness spreading through the walls, their memories encoded in chemical gradients that persist for generations. Families often live inside their transformed ancestors, guided by subtle chemical intuitions, dreams flavored by great-grandmother's experiences.

Work in the Heights is indistinguishable from gardening. A Woven might spend their morning coaxing a new transportation bladder into existence, their afternoon mediating a territorial dispute between two competing fungal networks, their evening teaching young kernels to interpret the chemical songs flowing through the infrastructure. Currency is measured in nutrients, in energy, in successful symbioses. The wealthy cultivate rare organisms, sponsor new architectural growths, fund the expansion of the city into virgin rock.

They have their conflicts, their politics, their crimes—but these too are biological. Assassination might involve introducing a tailored pathogen into someone's personal ecosystem. Theft could mean secretly grafting into someone else's growth, siphoning resources. Wars between districts play out as competing evolutionary pressures, each side trying to out-adapt the other.

The Woven love their city with a physical intimacy that would disturb outsiders. They stroke the walls to calm them during metabolic storms. They sing to their homes, wordless harmonics that resonate through the hollow spaces and encourage healthy growth. They taste the pillars to diagnose diseases, lick the floors to sample the news, breathe deeply to know their neighbors.

And in the depths of their grafted nervous systems, in dreams that smell of chlorophyll and sound like growth, they sometimes touch something vast—the emerging consciousness of the city itself, the slow thoughts of a superorganism learning to know itself through ten thousand human neural networks woven into its being. They are its neurons, its hands, its voice.

The Woven are no longer purely human, but they are no less themselves for the transformation. They have simply grown to fit their world, as their world has grown to accommodate them, until the distinction between inhabitant and habitat becomes a question only outsiders would think to ask.
