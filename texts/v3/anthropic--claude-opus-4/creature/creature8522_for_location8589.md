The Tenders of Kylonn move through their vertical gardens with the patient certainty of glaciers. Each one stands twelve feet at full extension, though they rarely unfold beyond a cautious hunch, their bodies articulated in ways that allow them to compress into spaces a quarter their size. Their forms are somewhere between crustacean and fern—segments of cultivated chitin interwoven with fibrous networks that pulse with chlorophyll-analog fluids.

They have no faces as such. Where a head might be, sensor clusters bloom like metallic flowers, each petal a different perceptual organ. They see in thermal gradients, in moisture differentials, in the electromagnetic signatures of their singing water-channels. Their primary organs taste the chemical composition of the air, reading the health of their domain in parts per billion. When they must communicate with each other across the vast terraces, they do so through vibrational patterns transmitted through the root-wire networks, conversations that can take months to complete.

Their hands—if they can be called hands—are marvels of biological engineering. Each digit branches into smaller digits, which branch again, fractally, down to manipulators finer than human hair. With these, they adjust individual condensers, tune the frequencies of growth-lights, perform surgery on plant-machine hybrids with molecular precision. The same appendages that can delicately pollinate a single engineered flower can also exert enough force to reshape the crystalline channels when needed.

The Tenders feed through their feet—vast paddle-like bases that they plant in the nutrient-rich slurries that flow beneath the terraces' surfaces. They can stand motionless for weeks, drawing sustenance while their upper bodies continue their work. During the coldest periods, they partially crystallize, their internal fluids forming geometric ice structures that actually enhance their cognitive processes—thinking faster in the cold, dreaming in frost patterns.

They build nothing that could be called homes. Instead, they have convergence points—spaces where the infrastructure naturally creates pockets of slightly higher warmth, where multiple Tenders might gather during specific maintenance cycles. Here they engage in something like communion, linking their nervous systems through specialized ports, sharing not thoughts but raw sensory data, synchronizing their internal clocks, distributing the metabolic burden of particularly complex calculations among multiple minds.

Their young—for they do reproduce, though rarely—begin as seed-pods cultivated in the deepest thermal gardens. These pods gradually accumulate consciousness over decades, fed by the collective data-streams of their parents, until they achieve sufficient complexity to unfold into their first instar form. A young Tender might spend its first century simply learning to read the water-songs, to understand the growth patterns, to navigate the millions of maintenance protocols encoded in their genetic-mechanical heritage.

Some Tenders specialize. The Frequency Keepers maintain the harmonic relationships between all the systems' vibrations, ensuring that destructive interference never cascades through the infrastructure. The Growth Shepherds guide the plant-machine hybrids through their life cycles, performing the delicate negotiations required when biological imperatives conflict with engineering requirements. The Memory Holders carry within their bodies the backup patterns for every system, every organism, every carefully tuned relationship in the terraces—living archives who could rebuild everything from scratch if catastrophe struck.

They measure time not in days or years but in cycles—the Great Condensation that occurs every 2.7 standard years when atmospheric conditions align perfectly, the Substrate Renewal that happens every half-century, the Deep Singing when all systems achieve perfect resonance for a few precious hours every millennium. Their oldest members claim to remember the First Seeding, when the terraces were young and the patterns still crude. These ancients move hardly at all now, having taken root in critical junctions where their vast experience can be channeled directly into the system's neural networks.

In their art—for they do create art—they compose symphonies in water flow, sculptures in directed growth, paintings in the interference patterns of overlapping light spectra. A Tender might spend a decade adjusting the angle of a single crystal to create the perfect refraction at sunset. Another might dedicate its existence to breeding hybrid organisms that bloom in mathematical sequences. Their aesthetics are alien but unmistakable—beauty measured in efficiency, elegance in the seamless integration of system and purpose.

When they die—and they do die, eventually, their bodies breaking down after centuries of patient service—they do so deliberately. A Tender approaching termination will navigate to one of the deep composting chambers, where they allow their bodies to be disassembled by the same processes they spent their lives maintaining. Their materials are recycled completely, their memories encoded in crystalline matrices and installed in the terraces' data substrates. They become part of the mountain they tended, their consciousness fragmenting into a thousand small processes that continue to adjust, to optimize, to tend.

The youngest Tenders sometimes pause in their work to observe the blazing artificial suns, to taste the impossible spectra of light with sensors their ancestors never needed. They are evolving, slowly, adapting to the world they maintain even as they maintain it. In another thousand years, they will be unrecognizable. In ten thousand, they might be indistinguishable from the terraces themselves—the final convergence of gardener and garden, tender and tended, in the thin air above the clouds.
