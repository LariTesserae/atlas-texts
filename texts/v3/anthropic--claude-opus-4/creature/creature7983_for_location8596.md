In the Woven Depths dwell the Threadkeepers—not quite octopus, not quite spider, not quite anything the upper world has a name for. They move through the water-light with bodies that seem to be made of the same substance as the architecture itself: flesh that resembles woven kelp, eight limbs that branch and re-branch into finger-fronds capable of infinite delicacy. Their skin changes not just color but texture, matching the moss-fur of the walls so perfectly they seem to vanish into the structure until they choose to move.

They are gardeners by instinct, though they never knew the original builders. Each Threadkeeper maintains a territory of perhaps thirty chambers, moving through their domain in a constant, meditative patrol. With their branching digits, they tease apart root systems that have grown too dense, encouraging circulation. They guide new shoots toward empty spaces, weaving them into existing patterns with an intuition that might be genetic memory or might simply be the recognition of one living architecture by another. When they find the ancient copper tools, they carry them for a while, turning them over with curious finger-fronds before abandoning them in a new spot, like children playing with toys they don't quite understand.

The Threadkeepers communicate through touch and water-taste, releasing complex chemical signals that propagate through the flooded halls like slow-motion songs. Their language has no words for "inside" or "outside," no distinction between "self" and "garden." Young Threadkeepers spend their first years as little more than mobile seedlings, their bodies translucent and unformed, following the adults and learning the patterns of growth by feel. As they mature, they begin to accumulate their own flora—certain mosses preferentially grow on their skin, small flowering vines take root in the joints of their limbs, until each adult is as much garden as gardener.

They feed on the boundaries of things. Where salt water meets fresh, where root meets rot, where growth meets death—these are their hunting grounds. Their metabolism requires the chemical complexity found at these interfaces. With delicate precision, they cultivate decay in certain chambers, encouraging the growth of decomposer fungi that they harvest with their finest finger-fronds. They suck the nutrients from dying wood with specialized mouths hidden beneath their limbs, then excrete a paste that new plants find irresistible.

During the lake's storm season, when currents surge through the Woven Depths, the Threadkeepers gather in the temple-heart. They anchor themselves to the crystalline root membrane and enter something like hibernation, their bodies hardening to woody consistency. In this state, they dream—or something like dream. Their neural tissue, distributed throughout their branching bodies, forms temporary connections with the root systems they touch. For weeks they exist as part of the architecture itself, their consciousness dispersed through the living walls. When they wake, they carry new knowledge of growth patterns, of where the structure is weak, of where new rooms are budding. Some say they commune with the original builders during these dreams, learning the old songs that shaped the first saplings.

The eldest Threadkeepers are almost indistinguishable from their surroundings. Their bodies grow thick with symbiotic plants, their movements slow to the pace of growth itself. They settle in the deepest chambers where the pressure is greatest and the light barely reaches, becoming nodes in the living network of the Depths. Younger Threadkeepers come to them with offerings of rare seeds or particularly beautiful examples of metallic coral, and in exchange receive... something. A touch that conveys decades of growth-knowledge. A chemical exudation that helps them better taste the health of their gardens. Or sometimes just the privilege of grooming the ancient one's flora, carefully tending the miniature ecosystem that the elder has become.

They reproduce rarely and strangely. When conditions are right—something to do with water temperature, nutrient levels, and the phase of certain flowering cycles—a Threadkeeper will begin to bud. Small copies of themselves appear along their limbs like fruit, connected by umbilical vines. These buds develop for months, fed by their parent, until they're released to drift through the flooded chambers. Most are eaten by the fish-mice or become entangled in dense root systems, but a few find suitable territory and begin the long process of becoming true Threadkeepers.

The Threadkeepers fear dryness above all things. When water levels drop, exposing upper chambers to air, they work frantically to weave barriers of moisture-holding moss, to redirect flowing water, to seal breaches where the lake has retreated. They understand, in their wordless way, that the Woven Depths exist in a delicate balance. Too dry, and the structure becomes brittle, losing its flexibility. Too wet, and the chambers flood completely, leaving no space for air-breathing symbionts. They are the maintainers of this balance, the keepers of the threshold between states.

Sometimes, rarely, an outsider glimpses them—usually a researcher or treasure-hunter who has managed to navigate the flooded maze. The Threadkeepers observe these visitors with patient curiosity, following at a distance, tasting the chemical traces they leave in the water. If the intruder damages the growth-patterns or takes more than the ecosystem can spare, they intervene—not violently, but with an inexorable purpose. Vines grow with suspicious speed to entangle equipment. Passages that were clear moments before become impassable tangles. The water itself seems to carry the intruder in circles until, exhausted and confused, they find themselves expelled back into the open lake.

The Threadkeepers know every passage, every chamber, every growing thing in their domain. They know where the ancient tools rest and what they were for, even if they cannot use them as intended. They know the songs in the water and the patterns in the growth, the secret places where air collects and the hidden springs that feed the upper chambers. They are the memory of this place made flesh—or something like flesh—carrying forward the vision of builders they never met, maintaining a paradise designed for inhabitants who never came. In their patient, endless tending, they have become what the Woven Depths were always waiting for: the perfect symbiont, the gardener indistinguishable from the garden, the dweller who exists only to dwell.
