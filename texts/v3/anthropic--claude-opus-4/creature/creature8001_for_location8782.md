**The Lithovores**

They move between the crystal fronds with the deliberate grace of sea anemones in a slow current, though they are neither plant nor animal but something altogether other. The Lithovores—as the few who have glimpsed them have come to call them—exist at the intersection of mineral and flesh, their bodies a testament to evolution's strangest compromises.

Each stands no taller than a child's waist when fully extended, though they spend most of their existence in states of partial collapse, their boneless forms pooling and spreading across the cavern floor like living mercury. Their skin—if it can be called that—resembles polished obsidian worn thin, translucent enough to reveal the slow circulation of crystalline blood through vessels that branch and merge in patterns matching the root networks beneath the floor. This is no coincidence; they are part of the garden's circuit, another form of life that has learned to subsist on heat-theft and mineral dreams.

They have no eyes. Instead, their "faces" (the term used loosely, as they shift and reorganize their features at will) are dominated by circular arrays of vibration-sensitive filaments, each as fine as spider's silk but rigid as wire. These organs can detect the slightest tremor through the crystal substrate—the crack of an expanding root, the chime of disturbed fruit, the careful footfall of an intruder. When they feed, these filaments extend and burrow into the crystalline plants, not to destroy but to commune, drawing sustenance from the same mysterious process that allows vegetation to flourish in endless dark.

Their hands—when they choose to form them—are their most disturbing feature. Too clever by half, with too many joints that bend in directions that make human observers queasy. These appendages can shift from soft as putty to diamond-hard in seconds, allowing them to shape crystal with the patience of water wearing away stone. It was they who carved the bowls, who knapped the crystal knives with techniques no human hand could replicate. The prayer beads were their invention too, each one containing a microscopic garden, a perfect terrarium that continues the cavern's strange ecology in miniature.

They do not speak, but they sing—if the subsonic thrumming that occasionally rises from their gathered masses can be called song. It resonates through the crystal substrate, setting the entire garden to gentle vibration. The plants respond, their bioluminescent pulses synchronizing, creating waves of dim light that wash through the cavern in patterns that might be language, might be music, might be mathematics. During these choruses, the Lithovores' bodies ripple and flow in unconscious harmony, their obsidian skins reflecting and refracting the ghostly light until they seem to dissolve into the garden itself.

Their lifecycle is measured in geological time. A young Lithovore—if such terms apply—might spend its first century as little more than a sentient puddle, learning the cavern's rhythms, mapping the root networks through patient listening. As they age, they begin to solidify, to hold shapes longer, to develop preferences for certain forms. The eldest among them have become almost sculptural, their bodies maintaining semi-permanent configurations that younger ones flow around and between like living architecture.

They leave no waste, shed no skin, produce no young in any way comprehensible to surface biology. New Lithovores simply begin—condensing from the cavern's strange atmosphere like dew, or perhaps budding from the root networks themselves. They may not be separate organisms at all, but temporary expressions of the garden's own consciousness, mobile extensions that tend and shape and harvest according to some plan too slow and vast for human perception.

When they encounter intruders—those rare explorers who find the hidden passages—the Lithovores do not flee or attack. They simply recede, melting back into crevices between crystal formations, becoming indistinguishable from shadows and stone. But they watch, their filaments tracking every movement, and they remember. The cairns scattered through the garden each mark such an encounter, built by Lithhovore hands to commemorate the brief intersection of their timescale with ours.

Sometimes, very rarely, a Lithovore will choose to reveal itself fully to a visitor. It will rise from the crystal substrate like a slow geyser of living glass, its filaments spread in what might be greeting, might be warning, might be curiosity. It will offer gifts—a perfectly carved crystal tool, a prayer bead warm with internal light, a fruit that chimes with unearthly music. These exchanges follow no pattern any human has discerned. Perhaps the Lithovores see something in certain visitors, some quality that resonates with their alien perceptions. Perhaps they are simply lonely, in their geological way, for contact with minds that move at speeds they can barely fathom.

Those who accept their gifts report strange dreams afterward—visions of roots spreading through endless stone, of light pulsing in patterns that almost resolve into meaning, of time stretched thin as crystal sheets. Some return to the surface changed, spending the rest of their lives working stone with an uncanny skill they cannot explain. Others never speak of what they saw, but leave their homes to seek out deep places, dark places, places where the earth's slow secrets might whisper in frequencies only they can hear.

The Lithovores continue their patient work, tending their impossible garden, measuring out existence in the slow breath of stone and the careful cultivation of beauty that no sun will ever see. They are the priests and gardeners of a place that should not exist, their lives a quiet proof that the universe harbors wonders we have not yet dreamed to seek.
