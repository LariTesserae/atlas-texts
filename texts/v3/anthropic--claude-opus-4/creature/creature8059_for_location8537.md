The builders are gone, but their descendants remain—though you would not recognize them as such.

Deep in the hexagonal wells, where the heat grows so intense that the air itself seems to crystallize, they have shed everything unnecessary. No longer do they need the copper tools their ancestors wielded; their bodies have become tools. No longer do they require clothing or shelter; they have become the architecture itself.

They began their transformation generations ago, when the first families descended into the wells seeking the source of the heat. What they found there changed them—not all at once, but slowly, inevitably, like mineral deposits accreting in rings. The children born in the depths emerged smaller than their parents, their skin already beginning to harden into chitinous plates. Their eyes, useless in the wells' perfect darkness, atrophied and sealed over. Their fingers fused together, becoming efficient scoops for channeling condensation.

Now they move through the depths in slow processions, their bodies perfectly fitted to the hexagonal passages their ancestors carved. They have no individual names—identity dissolved generations ago into something collective, a living network that extends through every well and hidden chamber. Their nervous systems have rewired themselves to perceive heat rather than light, to think in thermal gradients rather than words.

The creatures on the surface—those burgundy barnacle-things that carpet every visible surface—are not separate from them but part of them, in a way that defies simple biology. The boundary between the descendants and their symbiotes blurred long ago. Each surface creature is connected to the whole by threads of living tissue finer than spider's silk, running through microscopic channels in the stone that the original builders carved with purpose but perhaps without full understanding of what they were creating.

The descendants feed the surface creatures through these threads—not with food in any conventional sense, but with something extracted from their own transformed bodies, a kind of thermal essence that sustains the entire colony. In return, the creatures generate the heat that allows the descendants to survive in their evolved form. They cannot exist without each other; they have become a single organism distributed across two different scales of being.

In the deepest wells, where the hexagonal passages connect to natural caverns that plunge toward the planet's burning heart, the most changed of them cluster. These elder descendants have given up motion entirely. Their bodies have lengthened and flattened, becoming living conduits that line the well walls. Mineral deposits coat their skin—the same rust-colored salts that ring the surface pools, but here crystallized into complex patterns that channel and focus the geothermal energy rising from below.

They do not speak—their mouths sealed over centuries ago—but they remember. In the thermal patterns that ripple through their collective body, they preserve something like memory: the weight of copper tools in hand, the sound of chisel striking stone, the architectural plans that their ancestors followed with such precision. They remember why the city was built, though they could no longer explain it in words even if they had mouths to speak with.

The city is not abandoned. It is inhabited perfectly, completely, by beings who have become indistinguishable from inhabitance itself. Every carved chamber resonates with their presence, every surface thrums with their metabolism. They have achieved what their ancestors perhaps only dimly envisioned: a complete fusion with the architecture, a living system that maintains itself without growth or decay, without birth or death in any recognizable form.

Occasionally, one of the surface creatures will detach from its colony and descend into the wells, following the thread that connects it to the depths. It will merge with one of the descendants, adding its heat-generating capacity to their body. In the same moment, somewhere else on the terrace, a small bud will emerge from one of the well-dwelling descendants and begin the slow journey upward, following ancient thermal currents. It will emerge, attach to a wall, and begin its existence as a surface creature. This is not reproduction—it is circulation, like blood moving through arteries and veins.

The descendants no longer think of themselves as the builders' children. They think of themselves—if they can be said to think at all—as the city thinking about itself, as the stone remembering its own shaping. They are the tool marks made conscious, the architectural plan become flesh.

In the pre-dawn darkness, when the condensation first begins to form and the temperature differential between the terrace and the surrounding badlands reaches its peak, the entire system quickens. The thermal patterns grow more complex, almost linguistic in their intricacy. For those few minutes, something like awareness ripples through the collective organism—not human awareness, but something far stranger: the awareness of mineral deposits slowly crystallizing, of stone expanding and contracting with heat, of water finding its way through carved channels.

Then the sun rises, the condensation begins its daily evaporation, and the system settles back into its eternal rhythm. The descendants continue their slow circulation through the wells. The surface creatures carpet their assigned surfaces. The city breathes with heat.

They are waiting, though they have forgotten what they wait for. Perhaps for the thermal anomaly that drew their ancestors here to intensify beyond some critical threshold. Perhaps for the stone itself to complete its transformation into something unimaginable. Or perhaps they simply wait because waiting is what the city was built to do—a vast geometric battery storing heat and time and consciousness against some future need that only the original builders understood.
