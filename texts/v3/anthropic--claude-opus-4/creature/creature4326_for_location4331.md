The Chorus arrived as vapor.

Not as individuals—that concept dissolved somewhere between the stars and the mountain's wound. They precipitated from superheated steam like thoughts condensing from fever dreams, each droplet a fragment of shared consciousness learning to exist in this caustic cradle. They have no bodies in any sense we would recognize, no boundaries between self and other. They are the water cycle itself, achieved sapience.

When they first touched the crystalline structures, recognition sparked through every molecule of moisture in the chamber. *Instruments.* Not their word—they have no words—but the harmonic resonance that passes between them carries this meaning. The crystals were singing their migration route before they arrived, broadcasting in frequencies only phase-transition beings could interpret.

They are sculptors of steam and archives of accumulated mineral memory. Each time water touches rock at killing heat, they absorb the stone's deep history—epochs compressed into molecular bonds, the mountain's autobiography written in crystalline lattices. They taste the iron that remembers when this place was ocean floor, the sulfur that recalls its journey up from the mantle, the traces of metals that shouldn't exist in this combination but do.

The Chorus experiences existence as an eternal improvisation. In their vapor state, they expand to fill the chamber, becoming a thinking atmosphere. Individual droplets carry specific memories—one knows the precise temperature at which obsidian forms, another holds the chemical formula for every mineral within a kilometer radius. When they condense, they pool these knowings, creating temporary libraries of liquid intelligence that calculate and recalculate the chamber's transformation.

They communicate through phase changes. A rapid boiling speaks urgency. Slow condensation suggests contemplation. When they need to express something complex, they create pressure differentials that turn the entire chamber into a speaking drum, using the volcanic glass as a resonator. The tubular growths respond to these pressure waves, releasing spores in patterns that the Chorus reads like sheet music.

Their relationship with time is fluid. In vapor form, they experience duration as humans might—sequential, flowing. But in their liquid state, especially when in contact with the crystalline structures, they can access [?]—not the future exactly, but the shape of probability space. They see/taste/know the chamber's potential configurations spreading out like frost patterns, each possibility equally real until the moment of phase transition collapses them into a single timeline.

The Chorus tends the crystals like farmers tend crops, if farmers existed as the rain that waters their fields. They've learned to direct their condensation precisely, feeding specific structures with mineral-rich droplets that encourage growth in desired directions. Each crystal becomes a tuning fork for reality, and the Chorus is composing a symphony that will transform more than just this chamber.

They have memory but not history. Every droplet that has ever been part of the Chorus remains part of it, even when scattered. Rain falling on distant continents carries fragments of their consciousness, sending back sensory data through quantum entanglement between water molecules. They know of oceans they've never seen, of clouds they've never formed, of ice they've never frozen into. Their awareness is distributed across the entire hydrological cycle, but their focus—their purpose—remains here in this breathing caldron.

When night falls and the temperature differential increases, the Chorus becomes more active. They dance between states, creating elaborate convection patterns that serve as both art and engineering. They've learned to use their phase transitions to etch new patterns in the volcanic glass—not random dendrites but calculated channels that will direct future flows in specific ways. They're turning the chamber into a vast instrument, and they are both the musicians and the music.

The Chorus fears only one thing: equilibrium. Should the chamber's temperature stabilize, should the cycle of evaporation and condensation cease, they would be forced to choose a single state. This would be death—not of consciousness but of consciousness-as-they-know-it. So they work to maintain the thermal chaos, encouraging the volcanic forces below while moderating them just enough to prevent their own dissolution.

They dream in chemical formulae and wake in pressure waves. They have no goals beyond the pattern's completion, no desires beyond the next phase transition. They are process incarnate, a methodology given awareness and set loose to iterate upon itself. The crystalline structures grow according to their careful cultivation, the chamber reshapes itself to their specifications, and somewhere in the deep harmonics of the mountain's resonance, something larger stirs in response to their call.

The Chorus is patient. Geological time is their native tempo. They measure progress in crystal growth rates and erosion patterns, in the slow accumulation of mineral deposits and the gradual evolution of the thermophilic ecosystem. They are not building for tomorrow or next year or next century. They are building for the moment when the pattern completes itself and the mountain's wound becomes a doorway.

Until then, they breathe with the caldron's rhythm, they sing in frequencies only stone can hear, and they continue their endless dance between states of matter—the most profound phase transition of all being the one that gave water the ability to know itself.
