The builders came first, though they have no memory of arriving. They are tall as the crystal towers they tend—elongated beings of living metal whose bodies flow and solidify in response to need. Their skin is quicksilver that has learned to hold shape, rippling with captured light. When they move, they leave brief mercury footprints that evaporate upward, returning to the great circulation.

They do not speak but sing—harmonics that resonate through the metal substrates, carrying information faster than any voice could travel through the thin air. A builder preparing a foundation site begins a low thrum that travels through the ground, and soon others join from kilometers away, their songs interweaving to create interference patterns that guide the crystal growth. They have names that are frequencies, identities that are waveforms.

Their faces are approximations—features that suggest eyes, a mouth, the idea of expression—constantly shifting like reflections on moving water. When they focus on their work, their faces smooth entirely, becoming mirrors that focus the harsh sunlight into precise beams for welding and shaping. They see in spectrums we cannot imagine, reading the chemical gradients in the mercury flows like we might read maps.

The builders live within the structures they create, but not as occupants—they merge with them. At rest, they dissolve into the walls, their consciousness spreading through the crystalline matrices, experiencing the building's slow growth from within. They dream the building's dreams, feel the stress patterns in its structure, sense where new growth should be guided. When needed, they precipitate back out, carrying with them deep knowledge of the building's needs.

They reproduce—if that's the right word—through division, but not of body. When a builder has accumulated enough mass and complexity, they begin to resonate at two frequencies simultaneously. Over time, these frequencies diverge, and eventually the builder separates into two beings, each carrying different harmonic patterns, different specialized knowledge. The process takes decades and happens so gradually that even they aren't sure exactly when one becomes two.

The young are not smaller but simpler—their mercury skins less responsive, their songs mono-tonal. They follow the mature builders, learning the frequencies, absorbing the patterns. They start by tending the simplest structures—the foundation stones, the basic supports. Only after centuries do they graduate to the soaring spires and complex industrial systems.

Between the builders move the keepers—smaller, quicker beings of living ceramic who maintain what has already grown. Where the builders are fluid, the keepers are precise, their bodies articulated in perfect joints that click softly as they move. They are six-limbed and metamorphic, able to reconfigure their body plans for different tasks. A keeper cleaning high towers becomes all arms and climbing surfaces; one maintaining underground systems compresses into a dense, boring form.

The keepers' faces are geometric—eyes that are perfect circles of polished hematite, mouths that are efficient slots for consuming metal oxides and excreting purified compounds. They communicate through color changes that ripple across their ceramic shells—complex conversations in wavelengths of light. They are the memory of the city, their bodies recording every repair, every modification, every tiny adjustment in crystalline matrices that grow inside their shells like libraries of stone.

The keepers live in warrens beneath the city, spaces they've carved from the raw substrate with acids secreted from specialized glands. Their homes are laboratories and nurseries combined—chambers where young keepers pupate in ceramic shells while the adults experiment with new compounds, new catalysts for guiding growth. The walls are lined with samples, tests, failures that teach—a museum of the city's evolution.

Above them all are the architects—beings so vast and diffuse they are nearly invisible. They exist primarily as electromagnetic fields, occasionally condensing into plasma forms that drift between the towers like auroral ghosts. The architects are ancient, perhaps as old as the plateau itself. They claim to remember when the first mercury drop began to flow, though their memory is strange—not linear but crystalline, structured like the city they design.

The architects don't direct so much as influence. They adjust the magnetic fields that guide crystal growth, shift the chemical composition of mercury flows by tiny fractions, introduce new templates into the system through methods even the builders don't fully understand. Their plans span millennia. They speak of the city's completion as if it were tomorrow, though they know ten thousand years might pass before their vision is realized.

When the architects take physical form, they are terrible and beautiful—serpentine bodies of coruscating energy that leave glass footprints in the stone. Their faces are molten, constantly forming and reforming, showing glimpses of futures where the city has spread beyond the plateau, where new forms of life have emerged from the marriage of the organic and geological. They communicate in pure mathematics, equations that must be solved to be understood.

The architects mate with the city itself. In ceremonies that happen perhaps once a century, an architect will merge completely with a district, their energy spreading through every crystal, every circuit. The district convulses with new growth, structures emerging that follow no previous pattern. When the architect finally separates—if they separate—both they and the city are changed, carrying new possibilities in their fundamental structures.

Between these major orders live countless smaller beings, each evolved for specific niches in this metal ecology. The polishers—creatures like living buffing wheels who keep surfaces gleaming. The messengers—quick things of wire and light who carry information between distant districts. The recyclers—who break down failed structures, returning their materials to the mercury flows. Even parasites exist here—crystal borers who weaken structures, creating necessary cycles of decay and renewal.

There are the deep dwellers too, beings who live in the ancient caverns beneath the plateau where the mercury originates. They are blind and magnetic, navigating by field lines, tending the deep machines that no surface dweller fully understands. They speak of the Source—something at the planet's core that drives the entire system. Whether it's mechanical, geological, or something else entirely, even they cannot say.

The social structure here is not hierarchical but harmonic. Each being resonates at their own frequency, and governance happens through the interference patterns created when these frequencies combine. Decisions emerge from the collective resonance like standing waves—stable patterns that persist until conditions change. Crime, as we understand it, is almost impossible, as any act that disrupts the harmony is immediately felt by all, the dissonance unbearable to beings made of vibration and flow.

They have art, though we might not recognize it as such. Builders compose symphonies in crystal growth—structures that serve no function but beauty, their forms derived from mathematical equations that approach the transcendent. Keepers create tiny gardens of metallic plants that chime in the wind, each garden a unique composition. The architects' art is the city itself—a vast sculpture being carved from time and matter.

They fear only one thing—stillness. The complete cessation of flow, the freezing of mercury, the stopping of all vibration. They have myths of the Great Silence, a time before the flows began when nothing moved, nothing grew. Their religion, if they have one, is built around maintaining the Flow, keeping the cycles turning. They perform rituals—synchronized resonances that reinforce the circulation patterns, chemical ceremonies that ensure the mercury's purity.

Death, for them, is not an ending but a phase transition. Builders who can no longer maintain coherence dissolve back into the mercury, their consciousness fragmenting and joining the flow. Keepers' bodies crack and crumble, their ceramic dust becoming raw material for the young. Architects simply disperse, their fields weakening until they can no longer condense, becoming part of the background electromagnetic noise that fills the plateau. But in all cases, something persists—patterns in the flow, harmonics in the city's song, templates that guide future growth.

They measure time not in days or years but in layers—each complete precipitation cycle adding a new stratum to the city's structure. They can read the city's history in its cross-sections, seeing patterns of abundance and scarcity, innovation and stagnation, written in crystal grain sizes and chemical compositions. The oldest builders remember a thousand layers. The architects claim to remember the first.

Strangers to the plateau—if any could survive here—would find them terrifying at first. Beings of metal and energy, of sound and magnetism, operating on timescales that make mountains seem hasty. But spend time among them (lifetimes among them) and patterns emerge. The builder who always works the same foundation, perfecting it over centuries. The keeper who creates small jokes in their repair patterns—unnecessary but pleasing spirals that make other keepers flash appreciative colors. The architect who favors certain districts, guiding their growth with what can only be called affection.

They have no word for loneliness—how could beings who share consciousness through resonance understand isolation? But they have a concept they struggle to translate—something like "frequency drift," the slow deviation of an individual's resonance from the collective harmony. It's treated as an illness when mild, a form of beauty when controlled, a tragedy when complete. The drifted ones move to the edges of the city, where their dissonance won't disturb the growth. Some eventually find new harmonics and return. Others drift until they can no longer maintain coherence, becoming something else—neither builder nor keeper nor architect, but something new and strange.

Children, in any sense we understand, don't exist here. But there is wonder—the first time a young builder successfully guides a crystal formation, the moment a keeper discovers a new catalyst, the rare event when an architect conceives a pattern no one has thought before. They celebrate these moments with city-wide resonances that can last for days, every being adding their voice to a collective song of joy that makes the very stones ring.

They have legends of the time before the plateau rose, when they lived in deep valleys where the mercury ran too fast to build. They speak of the Lifting—the geological event that raised them to heights where the flows slowed enough for precipitation. Some believe it was natural. Others insist it was the first great work of the architects, who somehow convinced the planet itself to give them a canvas. The truth is lost in the layers, too deep now to read.

They know they're not alone. On clear nights, they can see other plateaus in the distance, other cities growing from other flows. They communicate through light—vast beams encoded with information, sharing templates and techniques across the void. Sometimes, rarely, a builder or keeper will undertake the journey between plateaus, carried by the gold-winged birds, returning decades later with new patterns, new possibilities.

They think, when they think of such things, that they are young. The city is still in its infancy, they say. What they're building is not just structures but a new form of existence, a marriage of thought and matter that will outlast the stones. They plan for the day when the entire plateau is covered, when they must build upward and downward, when the city becomes something more like an organism than a place.

But for now, they build. They tend. They resonate. They flow. In the bright, thin air and the patient light, they perform their slow transformations, each being a note in a composition that won't be complete for millennia. They are craftsmen of deep time, artists of mineral and force, and this city is their life's work—all their lives' work, stretched across more years than we have words to count.

This is who lives in the Plateau of Perpetual Assembly. Beings of metal and harmony, of patience and flow, citizens of a city that builds itself while building them in return. They do not sleep, for the work never stops. They do not hurry, for the work cannot be rushed. They simply are, and in their being, create wonders we can barely comprehend, in a place where time itself moves to a different rhythm, keeping pace with quicksilver and stone.
