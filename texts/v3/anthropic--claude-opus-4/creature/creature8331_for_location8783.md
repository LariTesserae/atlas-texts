In the depths of Thrennak's ever-birthing chambers, where stone weeps and metal breathes, the Midwives tend their impossible nurseries.

They move on too many legs—or perhaps not enough—their forms shifting with each gestation cycle. A Midwife might begin the morning walking on six articulated copper stilts, only to absorb two of them by noon, the metal flowing up into their torsos where it becomes part of their circulatory system. By evening, they've extruded new limbs entirely: tentacles of living cable, or appendages of calcified fungus that remember the shape of hands.

Their bodies are patchwork. Here, a ribcage of corroded steel shelters a garden of luminous organisms that pulse with their host's heartbeat. There, skin gives way to clusters of compound eyes—some biological, some mechanical lenses that whir and focus independently. They wear their purposes openly: pouches of transplanted flesh bulge with embryonic experiments, while external wombs of brass and membrane dangle from their shoulders like fruit.

The Midwives never speak—the cacophony would drown out any words—but they sing in frequencies that bypass the ears entirely. Their songs are felt in the marrow, tasted in the humid air, seen in the way spores suddenly spiral into new patterns. When they need to coordinate their work, they simply tune their various organs—biological and mechanical—until their bodies resonate at the same pitch. A cluster of Midwives in harmony can make the entire cavern ring like a bell.

They harvest constantly. A Midwife might pause mid-stride to pluck a particularly promising beetle-grub from the air, examine its exposed gears with eyes that see in spectrums unknown, then either consume it (analysis by digestion) or graft it directly onto their own form. They milk the Drummers for their hydraulic fluid, which they store in bladders that were once parts of the ancient pipeline system. They cultivate gardens in their own bodies—a successful strain of wire-vine might spread from one Midwife to another through touch, through spore, through deliberate surgical gift.

Their work is the cavern's work: experimentation without hypothesis, creation without blueprint. In alcoves lit by bioluminescent secretions, they maintain nurseries where no two offspring share the same kingdom of life. They'll take a promising Chorus Worm and thread it with copper wire, watching to see if it learns to conduct electricity as well as sound. They splice fungal networks into the neural systems of salamanders, creating creatures that think with mycelium minds spanning acres. They themselves are both experimenter and experiment—their own bodies the most convenient laboratories.

Some Midwives have grown enormous, no longer mobile, rooted like the fungal trees. These Ancients have become architecture themselves, their bodies hollowed out into chambers where smaller creatures nest, mate, and evolve under direct observation. Younger Midwives crawl through the Ancients' arteries-turned-corridors, learning by osmosis, feeding on wisdom-rich secretions that drip from neural clusters the size of rooms.

Others have gone the opposite direction, fragmenting into swarms. These collective Midwives exist as clouds of interconnected organisms—part school of fish, part scattered consciousness. They flow around obstacles, sampling everything they touch, their million tiny bodies each a neuron in a vast, dispersed brain that thinks in currents and chemical gradients.

The Midwives' memory is the cavern's memory. In the folds of their flesh, in the rust patterns on their mechanical parts, in the arrangement of symbiotic organisms across their backs, they carry the history of every successful adaptation. They trade these memories like currency, pressing membrane to membrane in fleeting embraces that transfer generations of encoded experience.

When a Midwife dies—though death here is a negotiable state—they don't simply decompose. They crystalize, calcify, mechanize. Their bodies become substrate for the next generation's experiments. A dead Midwife's lung might become a breeding pool for electric eels. Their metallic skeleton might be colonized by brass beetles that learn to puppeteer the remaining joints. Nothing is waste. Everything is potential.

New Midwives are born constantly, though "born" hardly describes the process. They assemble themselves from the cavern's plenty: a Drummer's discarded leg here, a colony of Chorus Worms there, sheets of fungal matter wrapped around a core of living metal. They emerge already ancient, already knowing their purpose through the inherited memories encoded in their salvaged parts.

The Midwives are Thrennak's immune system and reproductive system and nervous system all at once. They are what happens when life refuses to choose a single solution, when evolution becomes a conscious process, when the distinction between doctor and patient, creator and created, breaks down entirely. They are shepherds of chaos, gardeners of the impossible, and in their endless, soundless singing, they weave the caverns' disparate madness into something that might, eventually, make a terrible kind of sense.

They are, in essence, the caverns learning to tend themselves—evolution's fever dream of self-awareness, delivered in rust and rhizome, circuit and synapse, forever and again.
