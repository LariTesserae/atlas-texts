The Keepers emerged from necessity, not design. When the machinery first began its century-long death, those who couldn't afford passage to cleaner territories found ways to persist. Now, generations later, their descendants move through the mechanical canyons with inherited knowledge, reading the landscape like scripture.

They travel in small kinships—never more than a dozen, usually fewer. Their bodies have adapted: lungs that filter metallic dust, livers that process trace toxins, eyes that gather the scant light filtering through the industrial canopy. Children born here emerge with a faint shimmer to their skin, a nacreous quality that deepens with age as mineral deposits accumulate in their dermal layers. The eldest among them are said to ring like bells when struck.

Each kinship claims a territory measured not in distance but in complexity—how many junction boxes, how many service tunnels, how many intact catwalks. They know their sectors intimately: which pipes still hold residual coolant that can be tapped in the dry season, which transformer stations provide shelter from the caustic dust storms, where the crystal-plants grow thick enough to harvest for their faint bioluminescence.

The Keepers dress in layers—inner garments woven from vine fibers, outer shells fashioned from scavenged materials. They've learned to work the softer metals with patient abrasion, creating tools and ornaments from copper pipes and aluminum housings. The most prized possession is a respirator mask, passed down through generations, its filters replaced with crushed crystal-plant matter that somehow purifies better than the original synthetic materials.

They communicate in whispers and gestures, their language evolved for a landscape that swallows sound. Complex ideas are drawn in the dust or tapped out on metal surfaces—the entire industrial forest becomes their medium. Messages left for other kinships take the form of arranged debris, positioned crystals, or careful scratches on ceramic tiles that will last decades.

Their primary trade is in knowledge. The Keepers maintain mental maps of the ever-shifting landscape, tracking which sectors have settled, where new passages have opened, which areas have become too toxic even for their adapted physiologies. They know the migration patterns of the rust-centipedes, the fruiting cycles of the wire-vines, the locations where rare earth deposits can be chipped from defunct circuit boards.

Young Keepers undergo a ritualistic journey at maturity, traveling alone to the deepest parts of their territory where the machinery presses so close that even they must crawl. They return with relics—intact vacuum tubes, peculiar alloys, sometimes fragments of readable documentation—which are integrated into the kinship's accumulated lore. Those who discover previously unknown sectors earn the right to name them, adding to the vast oral cartography that each kinship maintains.

They've developed a complex relationship with the fauna. The bird-analogues are tracked for their ability to locate the richest mineral deposits. Rust-centipedes are harvested carefully—their shells provide abrasive material, their organs contain concentrated metals that serve as medicine or poison depending on preparation. Most mysteriously, some Keepers claim to communicate with the landscape itself, sensing shifts in the mechanical forest through vibrations in the hardpan.

At night—or what passes for night in the perpetual twilight—the kinships gather in their claimed shelters. They practice a form of music that uses the industrial infrastructure as instruments: tapping resonant pipes, drawing bows across tensioned cables, creating rhythms that propagate through the metal maze. These songs serve as long-range communication, territory markers, and oral history all at once.

The eldest Keepers speak of the Time Before with a mixture of reverence and dismissal. They maintain no illusion about returning to that lost world. Instead, they've embraced their transformed existence, finding meaning in the patient accumulation of knowledge, the careful tending of their territories, the slow adaptation to an environment that continues to evolve around them.

Death among the Keepers is marked by integration. Bodies are placed in specific locations where the crystal-plants grow most vigorously, the minerals in their accumulated tissues feeding the strange hybrid growth. Within a generation, the deceased become part of the landscape they knew so intimately, their crystallized remains indistinguishable from the industrial geology surrounding them.

In this way, the Keepers persist—not as remnants of the old world, but as the first inhabitants of the new one, shaped by the mechanical forest as surely as they shape it through their presence. They are human still, but a humanity evolved for patience, for quiet persistence, for finding life in the slow transformation of industrial decay into something unprecedented and strange.
