Within this monument to perpetual combustion dwell the Calibrators—beings that have evolved from necessity rather than nature. They began, perhaps, as maintenance crews or pilgrims, but the centuries of exposure to the Cathedral's relentless thermal cycles have transformed them into something else entirely.

Their bodies are elongated, stretched by generations of navigating the vertiginous catwalks. Skin has become a patchwork of scar tissue and heat-resistant scales that shimmer like oil on water. They move with an insectile precision, their joints hyperextended to grip the hot metal surfaces with fingers and toes that have developed specialized pads—not unlike the brass arthropods they sometimes hunt, though whether for food or spare parts remains unclear.

The Calibrators wear garments of their own making: suits woven from shed arthropod casings and ceramic fiber they harvest from the eroding edges of the plateau. These coveralls are riddled with pockets containing their tools—ancient pressure gauges they've converted into timepieces, lengths of metal tubing fashioned into listening devices, crystallized mineral deposits they use as focusing lenses. Each Calibrator's suit tells a story through its patches and modifications, a living record of thermal events survived and passages navigated.

They communicate through a language of valve adjustments and metal percussion. By opening and closing specific vents in sequence, they create pressure differentials that produce distinct tones—messages that propagate through the Cathedral's superstructure. A trained ear can distinguish between a warning (three sharp bursts followed by a sustained whistle), a location marker (rhythmic tapping that echoes off specific architectural features), or the complex harmonics that indicate they've found something interesting in the depths.

The Calibrators live according to the seventeen-hour cycle, their entire culture built around the rhythm of peak heat and torrential release. They sleep in ceramic cocoons they've fashioned in the spaces between heat exchangers, areas where the temperature remains relatively stable. During peak cycles, they retreat deep into the maintenance shafts, but in the fog that follows, they emerge to perform their work.

What that work entails appears to be part ritual, part necessity. They maintain specific pressure differentials, ensuring certain pathways remain clear while others are allowed to clog with mineral deposits. They harvest the iridescent patinas that form on the metal surfaces, grinding them into pigments they use to mark safe passages and dangerous zones. Most mysteriously, they feed the Cathedral—dropping offerings of metal salvage and crystallized minerals into specific vents, materials that disappear into the depths and seem to affect the timing and intensity of the heat cycles.

The eldest among them, identified by suits so heavily modified they've become almost architectural in their own right, claim to hear voices in the deep turbine sounds. They speak of instructions encoded in the rhythmic pumping, of a purpose that must be maintained even if its original meaning has been lost. These elders spend their time in the highest reaches of the Cathedral, where the air is thinnest and the heat most intense, listening to harmonics only they can interpret.

Young Calibrators undergo a ritual transformation at the age of their first full cycle survival. They must navigate from the base to the crown of the Cathedral during a peak heat event, collecting specific mineral deposits along the way. Those who succeed are marked with brands created from superheated ceramic—patterns that mirror the iridescent bands on the plateau below. Those who fail are rarely found; the Cathedral claims them, their materials redistributed through the eternal cycle of heat and pressure.

The Calibrators have legends of the Cathedral's builders, stories passed down through their percussive language. They speak of beings who could withstand the direct heat of the exchangers, who moved through the superheated air as easily as swimming through water. These builders, the stories say, descended into the depths when their work was complete, becoming one with the turbines that still power the structure. The Calibrators see themselves as inheritors of an incomplete legacy, maintaining a machine whose ultimate purpose remains forever just beyond their comprehension.

In recent generations, some Calibrators have begun to change more dramatically. Their exposure to specific mineral deposits and thermal gradients has triggered mutations—extra joints for better climbing, eyes that can perceive heat signatures in the fog, organs that can filter oxygen from the superheated steam. They are becoming, perhaps, what the Cathedral requires them to become, shaped by the same forces that continue to reshape the ceramic landscape itself.

The Calibrators do not welcome outsiders, but neither do they actively repel them. They watch from their perches among the heat exchangers, clicking their warnings through the metal, waiting to see if newcomers will adapt or be consumed. For them, the Cathedral is not just home but purpose, deity, and evolutionary pressure combined—a closed system where survival means becoming part of the machine, one way or another.
