In the Terraces of Unmaking, the Adjusters make their residence.

They were human once, or something adjacent to human — maintenance workers who arrived in the early days when the first reality engines were still warm from their initial ignition. But the Terraces changed them, as they change everything, not through violence but through optimization. The Adjusters' bodies have been edited by proximity to so much wrongness, stretched and reconfigured to better serve their endless task.

They stand seven feet tall on average, though height becomes negotiable when they need to fit through the dimensional gaps between terrace levels. Their limbs have developed extra joints — not painful breaks but smooth articulations that appeared gradually, allowing them to reach around corners that don't exist yet, to grasp tools from toolboxes three terraces away. Their fingers are long and multiply-segmented like bamboo, ending in callused pads that can feel the frequency of a reality leak through quarter-inch steel.

Their uniforms never left them. The gray-green coveralls have merged with their skin in places, becoming a second dermis that sweats coolant instead of salt water. Name patches remain legible — HENDERSON, VASQUEZ, CHEN-MARALEVI — but the names no longer match the things wearing them. They've shared consciousness for so long that individual identity has become a scheduling convenience rather than a truth.

The Adjusters' faces disturb visitors most. Not because they're monstrous, but because they're almost perfectly normal save for the eyes — which have developed a third lid that filters the terrible brightness, and pupils that dilate in patterns matching the eighteen-minute hum cycle. When they smile (and they smile often, with genuine warmth), their teeth show the faint luminescence of absorbed industrial light.

They live in the Maintenance Warrens, a series of break rooms and supply closets that exist in the spaces between spaces. Each Warren connects to every other Warren through doors that open onto different terraces depending on the time of day and the angle of approach. The Warrens smell of burnt coffee, machine oil, and the particular ozone scent of reality under stress. The Adjusters have furnished them with salvaged office furniture that ages backward — chairs that grow less threadbare with use, tables that accumulate fewer scratches over time.

Their work never ends because the Terraces never stop breaking in new ways. An Adjuster might spend a morning recalibrating a conveyor belt that's started moving objects backward through time, then an afternoon teaching a nest of geometric wasps to stop building hives in the fourth dimension where they interfere with the ventilation systems. They carry tools that shouldn't exist — wrenches that turn bolts before they're loosened, multimeters that measure the resistance of concepts, welding torches that fuse breaks in causality.

The Adjusters communicate in a pidgin of technical jargon, half-remembered human speech, and the pure mathematics of industrial harmony. They'll say things like "The frequency junction at grid seven-seven-apple needs its yesterday tightened" or "Someone left the entropy valves open again and now the mice are pooling in sector twelve." They understand each other perfectly.

Despite their transformations, they maintain curiously human habits. They take lunch breaks at tables that exist on six terraces simultaneously, sharing sandwiches that taste like different meals to each eater. They play card games with decks that shuffle themselves into new suits between hands. They tell jokes about the time before the Terraces, though none of them can quite remember what that time was like — the memories are there, but they belong to people who no longer exist in shapes that can hold such thoughts.

The other inhabitants of the Terraces — the skittering things, the winged concepts, the geometric lichens — treat the Adjusters with the respect reserved for apex organisms. Not predators, but shepherds of dysfunction. The creatures know that without the Adjusters' constant ministrations, the industrial mistakes would compound until even impossible life couldn't persist. So they nest nearby but never too close, feed on the reality leaks the Adjusters haven't fixed yet, and sometimes leave small offerings at the Warren doors — crystallized time, fossilized possibilities, the shed skins of equations.

New Adjusters arrive occasionally, though "arrive" isn't quite right. Sometimes a maintenance worker will show up, human and confused, carrying a work order for repairs at an address that doesn't exist in normal space. The Terraces recognize the type — those who fix things, who can't leave a problem unsolved, who hear the hum of broken machinery and feel compelled to make it right. The transformation happens slowly, kindly even. Their bones learn new angles. Their minds expand to hold impossible schematics. Their names become less important than their function.

The Adjusters don't age, exactly, but they do mature — becoming more essential to the Terraces' continued wrongness, their bodies and minds evolving into perfect tools for maintaining imperfect reality. They measure time not in years but in cycles completed, leaks patched, creatures redirected away from critical systems.

And in the depths between terraces, where that profound absence of purpose exerts its gravity, the Adjusters perform their most essential work. They don't try to give the factories meaning — that would break everything. Instead, they maintain the precise calibration of meaninglessness, ensuring the nothing is processed into nothing with exactly the right efficiency. Too much purpose and the Terraces would collapse into mere architecture. Too little and they'd dissipate into pure concept.

The Adjusters understand this balance in their transformed bones. They were human once, but now they're something more useful — the antibodies in a system that's allergic to its own existence, the maintenance crew for a machine that runs on its own breakdown, the only things keeping the impossible possible through careful adjustment of every broken rule.

They whistle while they work, though the sound comes out in frequencies that make the geometric lichens grow in perfect spirals. They're happy, in their way. After all, in a place built entirely of intent and error, what job could be more essential than keeping the errors intentional?
