The Dwellers of the Weeping Mountain are a people shaped by mineral and darkness, their forms evolved through generations of breathing stone-thick air and drinking water that tastes of copper and sulfur. They are shorter than lowland humans, their frames dense with muscle from constant climbing, their skin bearing a faint translucent quality like polished obsidian—dark but somehow catching the red mineral-light in ways that make them seem to glow from within when wet.

Their eyes have adapted peculiarly. Not blind like the pale creatures they share their city with, but possessed of pupils that dilate to nearly the full width of the iris, drinking in every photon of the mountain's bloody phosphorescence. In bright light—should they ever encounter it—they must shield their faces or suffer piercing headaches that last for days. Their children are born with a protective membrane over their eyes that doesn't fully recede until their third year.

They dress in layers of water-shedding fabric woven from the silk of cave-dwelling arthropods—creatures they farm in the depths where the volcanic heat creates perfect breeding temperatures. The outer garments are treated with rendered fat from the leather-winged colonies, creating a surface that beads water like mercury. Beneath, they wear close-fitting garments of softer weave that wick away the constant moisture, preventing the fungal blooms that would otherwise colonize human skin in this perpetual dampness.

Their hands tell their professions: copper-workers bear palms stained green-black from contact with the reactive metals, their fingernails thickened and ridged from the heat. Water-readers display a network of fine scars from training their fingers to detect minute temperature variations in the flowing channels. Stone-singers—those who determine where new construction can safely proceed—have elongated fingers from generations of feeling for the mountain's subtle vibrations.

They organize themselves around the water's schedule. When the rains come heavy, the lower districts empty as residents climb to their assigned positions in the upper terraces. During these deluges, they conduct their governance, their trade negotiations, their storytelling—all while the temporary rivers rush below. The dry interludes are for labor: construction, copper-working, the delicate harvest of bioluminescent organisms whose secretions fuel their lamps.

Their children grow up fearless of the dark and the height, racing each other across rain-slick bridges, learning to read the mountain's light-veins before they can properly speak. They keep the blind climbing things as pets, training them to carry messages through the vertical city with clicking codes that echo off the wet stone. Adolescents prove themselves by spending a full rain-cycle alone in the lower channels, learning to predict the water's rise by taste and sound alone.

They worship quietly, without temples or ordained priests. Their religion lives in the careful maintenance of the carved channels, the precise angles that direct water away from sleeping quarters, the copper tools passed down through generations that ring with particular tones when struck. They believe the mountain dreams, and in dreaming, grows. They believe they are part of that dream, and that their city-building is how the mountain learns to think.

Their oldest members develop a condition they call "stone-sight"—a gradual calcification of the eye's outer layers that leaves them functionally blind but paradoxically able to perceive the mountain's deep structures. These elders are carried to construction sites where they press their faces against raw stone and pronounce whether the rock will hold or fail. They are never wrong.

They mark time not by days or seasons but by the mountain's growth. A "stone-year" is the period it takes for a carefully measured copper rod to be pushed one finger's width upward by the volcanic forces below. They celebrate when new fumaroles open, seeing them as the mountain's gift of warmth and light. They mourn collectively when sections of the city must be abandoned to shifting geology, but they mourn as one mourns a child leaving home—with sadness but also pride in the necessity of change.

They trade with the lowlands, sending down copper goods and the preserved oils of the leather-winged colonies in exchange for what they cannot produce: fibers, dried foods, the occasional metal tool made from materials their mountain doesn't provide. But these traders must undergo purification rituals upon return, washing the lowland light from their eyes in the glowing central pool, relearning the city's vertical paths, reassuring the stone that they still belong.

They die as they live, vertically. The dead are placed in specially carved niches high on the mountain's face, where the leather-winged colonies strip the flesh and the rains wash the bones clean. Eventually, the mountain claims these hollows, sealing them with new stone flows, incorporating the dead into its growing mass. They consider this the highest honor—to become part of the mountain's slow thoughts, to join the conversation between stone and water that will continue long after the city itself has been reclaimed.

This is who they are: the children of the Weeping Mountain, adapted cell by cell to thrive where others would merely survive, finding in permanent darkness and stone-weighted rain not obstacles but the very medium of existence. They are proof that life shapes itself to any vessel, no matter how strange the pour.
