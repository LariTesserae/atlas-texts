In the chamber's center, where the light pools thickest, something that might once have been human tends the tools.

The Keeper moves with a deliberation that belongs more to growth than locomotion—each gesture taking minutes to complete, arms drifting through their arcs like kelp following an unfelt current. Its skin has taken on the coral-stone's texture, not through any violence of transformation but through decades of patient accretion. Mineral deposits follow the lines of old scars, building ridges where wounds once were, filling the spaces between with mother-of-pearl smoothness.

The eyes remain startlingly clear—two dark pools that catch and hold the focused light, blinking perhaps once in an hour. They watch the tools with an attention that spans years. When the Keeper finally reaches for a chisel, the movement disturbs the carefully balanced currents, sending new harmonics through the passages' mouths.

Its fingers have grown webbing, but not the thin membranes of ordinary swimmers. This is thick, translucent cartilage that extends past the fingertips into flattened paddles, each one unique—adapted to different tools, different grips. The thumb-web has developed a sharp edge, perfect for scraping. The space between middle and ring fingers forms a natural cradle for the bronze handles.

The Keeper's hair was perhaps once black. Now it streams gray-green, each strand colonized by the same algae that paints the walls. It has grown far past any human measure—when fully extended in the current, it reaches every corner of the chamber, a living net that tastes the water's mineral changes, feels the vibrations of distant hammering, knows when stone dust settles in the furthest passage.

Barnacles grow in the hollow of its throat. Not many—just a small colony that has learned to close when the Keeper subvocalizes, sending thrums through the water that test the stone's resonance. These sounds aren't quite song, aren't quite speech. They map the coral's hidden weaknesses, find the grain that wants to split, locate the places where patient work will reveal the shapes already waiting in the stone.

The Keeper wears nothing but the water and its own slow transformation. Its ribcage has expanded, spaces between bones widening to accommodate modified lungs that process water like air, extracting oxygen through surfaces that glisten pink-red when the light catches them right. The heartbeat comes so slowly it seems like architecture—each pulse sending a visible wave through the chamber, temporarily distorting the light patterns on the floor.

When it works, the Keeper becomes pure attention. A single chisel stroke might take an hour to plan, another hour to execute. The bronze bites precisely where intended, following a design that exists nowhere but in the accumulation of such moments. The Keeper's movements generate their own currents—tool-hand advancing, free-hand following to brush away what few particles the young coral yields, the hair-net swaying in counterbalance.

Sometimes others come through the passages. The Keeper acknowledges them with a slow blink, a subtle adjustment of posture that says *working* or *resting* or *examining*. They bring new tools, sometimes, or take the old ones for sharpening. They never speak. The language here is in the stone itself, in the careful marks that build meaning over generations.

The Keeper sleeps without sleeping—entering states where the heartbeat slows even further, where the barnacles open to feed, where the hair-net spreads to its fullest extent and the body becomes indistinguishable from the chamber's other fixtures. In these times, small fish dart in through the passages, nibbling at the algae in the Keeper's hair, cleaning the barnacles, maintaining this symbiosis of flesh and function.

But always the eyes remain aware, watching the light-patterns dance across the tools, reading the stone's slow revelations. The Keeper remembers when this chamber was solid coral, remembers the first cuts that began to hollow it. Remembers, too, when its own hands were quick and brown and needed to surface for air.

The transformation continues. Each year the bones grow denser, the skin more stone-like, the movements more considered. The Keeper is becoming what the chamber needs—a permanent fixture, a living tool, a devotion made flesh. When the work is finally complete (but the work is never complete), perhaps the Keeper will be indistinguishable from the carved walls, just another pattern in the coral, another part of the reef-city's slow breathing.

For now, it lifts a mallet, tests its weight in the water, begins the long meditation that will end in a single, perfect strike.
