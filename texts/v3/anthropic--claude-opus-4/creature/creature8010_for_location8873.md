The people of the plateau city are creatures of stone and shadow, their bodies evolved over countless generations to match their carved world. They stand shorter than lowlanders, with barrel chests that house enlarged lungs to process the thin air. Their skin carries a permanent pallor—not unhealthy, but luminous like polished marble, with a faint blue undertone from the enhanced blood vessels that web close to the surface. This internal heat network makes their skin surprisingly warm to touch, each person a small furnace against the eternal cold.

Their eyes have adapted most dramatically. Enlarged pupils dominate faces, drinking in every photon in the dim tunnels. The irises shimmer with metallic flecks—copper, silver, gold—a genetic lottery that determines subtle social hierarchies. In darkness, their eyes catch and reflect light like cat's eyes, creating an eerie effect of floating pairs of gleaming discs in the tunnels. Years of stone-work have given them the ability to dilate and contract their pupils at will, shifting from the blazing surface light to the deepest chamber darkness without pause.

Hair grows thick and lustrous here, often worn long and braided with small stones or carved bone beads that click softly as they move. The color tends toward the extreme—either pure white like fresh snow or black as the deepest caves, with little variation between. Children are born with dark hair that sometimes shifts to white during adolescence, a transformation celebrated with complex ceremonies in the phosphorescent fungus gardens.

Their hands tell their history—broad palms with shortened, powerful fingers perfect for gripping tools and finding purchase on stone. The fingernails grow thick and strong, naturally wearing into chisel-like edges from constant contact with rock. Each palm bears the characteristic callus patterns of their trade: circular whorls for water-carriers, linear ridges for stone-carvers, smooth patches for the fungi farmers who work in perpetual dampness.

Movement defines them. In the tunnels, they flow rather than walk, their bodies instinctively following the curves of walls, hands trailing along polished stone for guidance and balance. They've developed an acute spatial memory—able to navigate the complex three-dimensional maze of their city in complete darkness. Children learn to move silently early, their bare feet finding purchase on stone with gecko-like certainty. Adults wear soft leather boots soled with treated fungus-fiber that grips without sound.

Their clothing is purely functional—layers of treated leather and woven fungus-fiber that trap air for insulation. The outer garments are often passed down through generations, gaining complex patterns of repair that become family signatures. Beneath these, they wear close-fitting undergarments dyed with lichen, creating subtle patterns in grey, pale green, and rust. During ceremonies, they add cloaks of bat-fur, painstakingly collected and sewn, that shimmer with iridescent highlights.

The social structure reflects their environment—tight-knit and interdependent. Families cluster in connected chamber-complexes, sharing walls and warmth. Privacy is found in silence rather than solitude; they've developed a rich language of subtle gestures and expressions readable in dim light. Sound carries too well in stone, so children learn early to moderate their voices, and adults can conduct entire conversations in whispers pitched to travel precisely as far as intended.

They measure age not in years but in deepenings—how many new levels have been excavated during one's lifetime. The eldest remember when entire sections of the city were solid rock, and their knowledge of the stone's grain and faults is irreplaceable. These deep-dwellers rarely surface anymore, their eyes too adapted to darkness, their bodies too attuned to the stable temperatures below.

Children grow up communally, spending their early years in the mid-level nursery chambers where the temperature is most stable. They learn by touch in the dimness—feeling the texture of different stones, memorizing the vibrations of various tools, developing the precise muscle memory needed for carving. By adolescence, they begin their apprenticeships, following masters through the tunnels, absorbing decades of accumulated technique.

Their diet shapes them as much as their environment. Fungus provides the staple—dozens of cultivated varieties with flavors ranging from earthy to sweet, textures from crisp to meat-like. The blind cave fish offer precious protein, their pale flesh almost translucent when cooked. Trade brings dried fruits and grains from the lower valleys, luxuries parceled out carefully. They've developed a cuisine of subtle flavors and varying textures, meals eaten in darkness where taste and scent become paramount.

Water is worship. They've developed elaborate rituals around the cisterns, and water-carriers hold honored positions. Each person knows their daily allocation precisely, and washing is a careful art of conservation. The public baths, fed by hot springs in the deepest levels, are social centers where the normal quietness relaxes in the muffling steam. Here, people sing—wordless harmonies that resonate through the stone, songs that have grown and evolved over centuries.

Disease is rare in the cold, dry air, but when illness strikes, it spreads quickly through the interconnected spaces. They've developed a sophisticated understanding of quarantine, sealing affected chambers with shaped stone plugs. Healers work with fungal extracts and mineral compounds, their knowledge encoded in complex texture-maps readable by touch—raised dots and carved lines that preserve medical wisdom across generations.

Death, when it comes, returns them to the stone. Bodies are interred in specially carved niches in the lowest levels, where particular fungi break down flesh over years, leaving cleaned bones that are eventually ground to powder and mixed into the mortar used for new construction. Thus the dead literally become part of the city's structure, their calcium strengthening the walls that shelter their descendants.

Their arts reflect their world—sculptures that must be felt to be appreciated, their beauty in texture and form rather than visual appearance. Music consists of resonances and vibrations, instruments designed to work with the stone's acoustics. They've developed a form of echo-location performance, where skilled practitioners create complex sound patterns that map spaces in three dimensions, allowing audiences to "see" with their ears.

Children play games of vibration and balance—feeling tremors through stone to locate hidden players, walking prescribed patterns blindfolded, creating temporary sculptures from carefully balanced rocks that must stand until the morning inspection. Adults gamble with carved dice that can be read by touch, the pips drilled to different depths, and maintain complex betting pools on everything from fungus yields to the gender of unborn children.

Romance blooms in darkness. Courtship involves gifting specially cultivated fungi that glow in particular patterns, or carving private messages in hidden spots. Couples create their own secret chambers, small spaces carved together over months—the act of shared creation as intimate as the eventual inhabitation. Marriages are sealed by mixing stone dust from each family's ancestral chambers into a new mortar, literally joining bloodlines in the walls of new homes.

The mental landscape of these people is one of perpetual mapping. They think in three dimensions as naturally as others think in two, their minds constantly updating position and orientation. Dreams are filled with the sensation of stone—its coolness, its grain, its weight. They can feel instability in rock the way others might sense a coming storm, their bodies attuned to vibrations too subtle for outsiders to detect.

Language itself has adapted. They speak in tones that carry efficiently through stone, with hard consonants that click and echo. Whispers utilize frequencies that hug walls, while public announcements employ booming resonances that fill entire chambers. They've developed a parallel silent language of touches and air-pressures, allowing complex communication in the noisy workshop areas.

Their concept of beauty centers on function and endurance. A well-carved water channel that will flow for centuries is more admired than any temporary decoration. People cultivate their bodies for strength and efficiency rather than appearance—beauty is in the grace of movement through darkness, the skill of hands shaping stone, the ability to maintain warmth in the eternal cold. Scars from stone-work are worn proudly, each telling a story of the city's gradual growth.

These are not people diminished by their harsh environment but refined by it, like water that has frozen and thawed until only the purest essence remains. They have taken the limitations of their world—the cold, the darkness, the scarcity—and transformed them into the parameters of a rich, complex civilization. In their faces you see the patience of stone itself, and in their movements, the accumulated wisdom of countless generations who have learned to not just survive but thrive in the carved heart of the mountain.
