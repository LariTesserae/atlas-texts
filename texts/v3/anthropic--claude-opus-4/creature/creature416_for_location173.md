In the Singing Gorges of Keth-Marai, what passes for permanent inhabitants have learned to live between sound and silence, permanence and transience.

The Listeners dwell here—though "dwell" suggests a fixity they long ago abandoned. They are human, or were, but generations in the acoustic maze have changed them. Their ear canals have narrowed to pinpoints that they can close completely with a muscular reflex. When open, these altered ears catch and sort the canyon's frequencies with inhuman precision. Their earlobes have elongated and thickened, hanging like soft leather flaps that can be positioned to direct or deflect sound.

They dress in robes of woven grass and canyon cotton, materials that absorb and muffle rather than reflect sound. The fabric moves like water, never snapping or flapping. Their feet are always bare—shoes make too much noise, and besides, they read the stone's vibrations through their soles, sensing floods and rockfalls hours before they arrive. Their skin has taken on the canyon's palette: amber, rust, dusty rose, with patterns that shift slightly with their blood flow, an unconscious mimicry of the color-speaking lizards.

The Listeners move through the canyons like ghosts of purpose. They maintain no single home but rather a circuit of those ancient carved chambers, arriving at each just as supplies cached there run low. They know when to move not by counting days but by reading the canyon's moods—a slight shift in the harmonic pattern that says *this chamber will be uninhabitable for the next season* or *the flash floods will favor the eastern passages this year*.

They rarely speak, and when they do, their voices have adapted to slide between the gorges' frequencies rather than compete with them. Their language has evolved into something tonal and sliding, like throat-singing crossed with birdsong, though the birds here make no sound to copy. They can throw their voices in impossible ways, making words appear to come from stone itself, carrying conversations across vast distances by riding the acoustic currents.

But the Listeners are not the only inhabitants. Or rather, they share the gorges with something that may or may not be alive in any conventional sense.

The Resonance has no fixed form. It is pattern more than substance—a standing wave of consciousness that lives in the interplay between sound and stone. Those who have glimpsed it (always peripherally, always when the light is strangest) describe different things: a heat mirage shaped like a walking cathedral, a flutter of transparency that suggests wings the size of canyon walls, a moving absence where sound goes to die.

The Resonance feeds, if that's the right word, on acoustic complexity. It cultivates the canyon system like a garden, subtly altering the stone through some process that might be erosion, might be song, might be thought made manifest. Where it has passed, new formations appear: hollow columns that boom like organ pipes, knife-thin walls that keen in the slightest breeze, spherical chambers that focus whispers into roars.

The Listeners know it. They leave offerings of sound—complex whistled compositions at certain acoustically live points in the canyons. In return, they sometimes find paths through the maze that weren't there before, or wake to discover that a dangerous overhang has mysteriously collapsed in the night, leaving their route clear. Some of the older Listeners claim to have heard it speak, though they won't say what it told them. They just touch their elongated ears and smile with eyes that have seen too much silence.

There are smaller lives too: the Hush Beetles that generate tiny bubbles of silence around themselves, creating mobile pockets of quiet where they can hunt unmolested. The Membrane Bats that echolocate not with sound but with controlled variations in air pressure, their wings so thin they're translucent. Plants that have evolved leaves shaped like funnels and parabolas, focusing the canyon's sounds to vibrate their roots, helping them crack deeper into the stone toward hidden water.

And in the deepest slots, where even the wind-song barely penetrates, live the Echo Prophets. These are Listeners who have gone too far, spent too long parsing the canyon's frequencies. They've begun to hear patterns that predict not just floods and rockfalls but human events—births, deaths, the rise and fall of distant nations. They scratch their prophecies on smooth stone walls with worn fingernails, writing in a script that looks like sound waves rendered visible. Other Listeners avoid these deep places. The prophecies are always accurate, they say, but the knowledge comes at too high a price. The Echo Prophets no longer seem to recognize human faces, staring through visitors as if they were particularly uninteresting formations of wind.

The gorges take no notice. They sing on, indifferent to their inhabitants, wearing them all down grain by grain, note by note, until even the distinction between listener and listened-to becomes just another harmony in the endless composition.
