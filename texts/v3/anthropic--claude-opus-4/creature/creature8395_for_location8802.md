In this place of perpetual becoming, they move like careful surgeons through their own bodies. The people here are small—not stunted but compact, with the efficient build of those who navigate tight spaces. Their skin carries a faint green-grey pallor, as if they've absorbed the lichen's hue through generations of contact. When they stand still among the moss-covered stones, they nearly disappear.

Their fingers are their most distinctive feature—elongated and incredibly sensitive, with tips that can detect moisture variations in wood, the health of moss by touch, the stability of surfaces that appear identical. These fingers are always in motion, trailing along walls as they walk, testing, reading the constant small changes in their environment. Their nails grow thick and quickly, requiring daily paring with small jade knives they all carry. The parings are carefully collected and added to the mortar—nothing is wasted here.

They dress in layers of felted moss-fiber, garments that breathe and wick moisture while providing insulation. The fabric is never quite dry, maintaining a dampness that matches the air, preventing the dangerous chafing that comes from the friction of dry against wet. Over time, living moss begins to colonize these clothes, and it's considered good fortune when this happens—a sign that one moves in harmony with the green. The oldest members of the community wear robes that are more ecosystem than garment, sprouting tiny ferns from their shoulders, hosting colonies of the pale moths in their hoods.

Their eyes have adapted to the flat light, pupils that dilate and contract with unusual speed, irises of grey and green that shift hue with the moisture in the air. They see well in the dim spaces between buildings, in the green twilight beneath the ferns. More importantly, they see patterns in growth—which direction the moss is spreading, where the ferns will unfurl next, how the creeping dampness will alter a wooden walkway's integrity. This prescience isn't supernatural but observational, born from generations of watching the green's patient advance.

They speak in low, humid voices that barely disturb the air. Their language includes subtle variations in breath moisture—dry consonants for urgency, wet vowels for contemplation. They have forty-seven different words for types of moss and only one word for sun. Children learn to modulate their breathing before they learn to walk, understanding that in the packed density of their home, even exhaled air is a shared resource.

Their daily rhythms follow the moisture clock. They wake with the first condensation, pressing their palms against windows to feel the dew's weight. Morning tasks involve the careful scraping—using tools of carved antler and polished stone to push back the night's growth from doorways and essential paths. This isn't done with violence but with an almost tender insistence, the way one might repeatedly comb a child's unruly hair. The scrapings are collected in woven bags, later processed into food, medicine, building material.

They eat the landscape they fight. Their diet consists largely of the aggressive vegetation—fern fiddleheads, moss soup, lichen cakes. They've learned to process the primitive club mosses into a protein-rich flour. The phosphorescent bog plants, harvested at specific moisture levels, provide essential minerals. Small fungi cultivated in the dark spaces beneath buildings add variety. On feast days, they consume the pale moths, which taste of rain and stone. The larger creature that leaves tracks—they don't eat that. They leave offerings for it instead, moss-wrapped parcels placed at the intersections of seldom-used walkways.

Children grow up vertical, carried on backs through the walkway mazes until they can navigate alone. They play silent games in the pocket gardens, learning to move without disturbing the growth patterns, to find the secret paths that change daily. They keep pale salamanders as pets, housing them in moss-lined boxes, learning responsibility through maintaining perfect moisture levels. By age seven, most can predict where new ferns will emerge. By ten, they can taste the air and know if a walkway will hold their weight.

The social structure mirrors the physical one—dense, interconnected, with shared walls and borrowed spaces. Privacy is neither expected nor desired. They sleep in shifts, the settlement always partially awake, always maintaining the balance. Families blend at the edges, children raised by whoever is nearest when needed. Knowledge passes through the community like moisture through moss—slowly, thoroughly, leaving no surface untouched.

They practice a form of architecture that's half construction, half negotiation with the green. Master builders are those who can sense where the vegetation wants to grow and work with it rather than against it. They create structures that will age into the landscape, designing with the expectation that moss will cover, ferns will infiltrate, wood will swell and stone will weep. The most revered buildings are those that have achieved perfect symbiosis—still functional as human spaces while hosting complete ecosystems in their walls.

Their tools are minimal but exquisitely maintained. Stone knives knapped from the native granite, kept sharp enough to slice individual moss stems. Wooden implements soaked in fungal extracts that prevent rot while maintaining flexibility. Ropes woven from fern fibers, stronger when wet. They have no metal—the constant moisture would rust it to nothing. They have no fire in the traditional sense, but they've learned to encourage the phosphorescent plants to grow in patterns that provide light, feeding them specific combinations of minerals to increase their glow.

Art here is ephemeral by necessity. They arrange stones in the pocket gardens, knowing the patterns will be obscured by growth within days. They train moss to grow in specific patterns on designated walls, a slow art that takes years to mature and days to be consumed by competing growth. They sing in harmonies that incorporate the dripping water, the groaning wood, creating music that's half composition, half collaboration with the settlement's own voice.

Their medicine revolves around moisture—too much, too little, the wrong kind. They suffer from unique ailments: moss lung from breathing too many spores, fern joint from the constant damp, the green dreams that come from long exposure to the phosphorescent plants. They treat these with careful applications of dried fungi, with controlled exposure to the rare dry spaces they maintain for medical purposes, with teas brewed from the carnivorous plants that grow in the highest, most exposed places.

Death, when it comes, is handled practically. Bodies are wrapped in moss and placed in the growing walls, where they become part of the structure, their nutrients feeding the constant green surge. Within a year, nothing remains but a particularly lush section of wall, marked by a small stone carving. They believe consciousness seeps into the moss network, that their ancestors literally support them, that the settlement's increasing sentience comes from this accumulated presence.

They reproduce carefully, mindful of the carrying capacity of their dense world. Pregnancy is timed to the moisture cycles, children born in the wettest season when the green grows fastest, when there's abundance to harvest. Birth occurs in the moss gardens, mothers delivering while kneeling in the thick carpet of green, the first sensation for newborns being the cool, damp embrace that will define their lives.

Their stories tell of the time before the green, when the stone was bare and the air was dry. These are cautionary tales, warnings about the barren death that awaits if they ever truly defeat the vegetation. Heroes in their mythology are those who achieved perfect balance—the legendary Walker of the Middle Way who could cross the entire settlement without disturbing a single frond, the Moss Whisperer who could direct growth with breath alone, the Stone Singer who convinced the granite to weep in patterns that created the first planned waterways.

They trade with the other valley settlements, carrying light loads of concentrated goods—dried phosphorescent powder, pressed moss-fiber, the jade knives they're renowned for crafting. The journeys between settlements are perilous, following paths that exist more in memory than reality, guided by subtle signs in the vegetation that only they can read. Travelers wear special cloaks that can expand into emergency shelters, carrying their own small ecosystems with them.

Their mathematics is based on growth patterns rather than abstract numbers. They calculate in spirals, in branching factors, in ratios of expansion. Their children learn to count by tracking fern unfurlings, to add by predicting moss spread. Architecture is planned using these organic mathematics, ensuring that buildings will grow into rather than against the natural patterns.

In the deepest night, when even the phosphorescent light dims, they sometimes speak of leaving—of finding a place where the green is less hungry, where children can play without watching for sudden growth, where buildings stay as they're built. But these are just words, vapor in the thick air. They know, in their moss-softened bones, that they belong to this place as much as it belongs to them. They are another species of growth in this nascent valley, as inevitable as the ferns, as persistent as the moss, as patient as the stones slowly learning to become soil.

This is who lives here: the gardeners of their own consumption, the architects of impermanence, the people who grow like moss—slowly, surely, adapting to every surface, finding life in the narrow spaces between stone and storm.
