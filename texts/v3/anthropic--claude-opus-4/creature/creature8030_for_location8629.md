In the Drowned Bells of Korathene, the Tidewrights persist.

They were human once, these patient architects of the deep. Now they stretch like questions between what was and what must be. Their bodies have lengthened—torsos drawn out to better navigate the strange physics of their dual-medium world, arms grown thin and triple-jointed to reach through water and air pocket alike. Their skin has taken on the pale luminescence of the bacterial mats they tend, a faint shimmer that shifts from pearl to silver as they move. Fingers have developed delicate webbing that can spread or contract, equally suited for swimming and for the precise manipulation of their stone-shaping tools.

But it is their faces that tell the story of their transformation most clearly. Eyes have migrated slightly apart and grown large, pupils dilated permanently to capture every photon in the eternal twilight. A nictitating membrane flickers across them—not for protection from water, which their eyes have learned to see through perfectly, but to adjust for the shocking brightness when they surface into an air pocket. Their noses have nearly sealed, leaving only the faintest slits that can close entirely. The mouth has widened and thinned, optimized for the click-speech they've developed—a language that carries through both water and air, though it sounds like scattered rain in one medium and sharp percussion in the other.

The Tidewrights organize themselves not by family or profession but by medium-preference. The Waterworkers spend most of their time submerged, their movements fluid and dreamlike as they maintain the submerged portions of the city. They've developed a graceful swimming style that barely disturbs the water, propelling themselves with subtle undulations of their elongated spines. Their hair—if it can still be called that—has become something between kelp and fiber optic cable, each strand sensing minute pressure changes in the water around them.

The Voidkeepers prefer the air pockets, though they must regularly immerse themselves to prevent their skin from cracking. They move in careful, calculated gestures, as if still remembering what it was to be fully human. It's the Voidkeepers who maintain the mysterious air spaces, using tools passed down through generations—carved bone siphons that somehow pull water from where it shouldn't be, stone resonators that vibrate at frequencies that encourage air pockets to form and persist. They wear garments woven from their own shed hair, creating a fibrous material that stays dry even when moving between mediums.

Then there are the Threshers, rarest of all, who live equally in both states. They've developed a peculiar rhythmic breathing—storing air in specialized sacs along their ribs when submerged, releasing it slowly through modified gill slits when in the voids. The Threshers serve as messengers and merchants between the two groups, riding the carved channels on their polished stone boards, launching from water into air pocket and back again with practiced ease. Their bodies show the most extreme adaptations—skin that can rapidly adjust its permeability, limbs that can lock rigid for swimming or relax into tentacle-like flexibility for climbing.

The Tidewrights reproduce slowly, carefully. Children are precious and few, raised communally in specially maintained nursery chambers where the water level can be gradually adjusted as they develop. The young ones begin life almost entirely aquatic, slowly learning to tolerate and then inhabit the air pockets as they grow. Their play is strange to see—games that involve passing between states of matter, hide-and-seek played in three dimensions where the seeker might be breathing while the hidden hold their breath just inches away in water.

They feed on the mineral gardens they cultivate—the bacterial mats processed into a paste that tastes of salt and lightning, supplemented by the translucent fish they farm in the deeper pools. But their true sustenance seems to come from their work itself. Each Tidewright is born to a task inherited from their predecessor: maintaining specific sections of the city, tending particular growth-patterns of salt crystal, performing one part of the endless ritual that keeps their temple's impossible water sculptures stable.

In the temple's heart, the Ceremonial Weavers perform their eternal dance. These are the oldest of the Tidewrights, their transformations so complete they seem more element than animal. They move between air and water without pause or preparation, their bodies equally at home in either. The rituals they perform haven't been fully understood in generations—only that they must continue, that the movements must be precise, that the carved water must be tended with gestures that have become instinct rather than knowledge.

The Tidewrights do not sleep as humans understand it. Instead, they enter periods of stillness, floating neutrally buoyant at the interface between water and air pocket, half their body in each medium. In these twilight moments, they dream collectively—sharing visions through bioelectric pulses that create faint auroras in the mineral-rich water. Their dreams are of geometries yet to be carved, of cities that exist in states of matter not yet discovered, of a time when the distinction between water and air will cease to matter because they will have become something that belongs to neither and both.

They mark time not by days or seasons but by the growth of salt formations, by the slow shift of currents they've mapped over generations, by the patient accumulation of their adaptations. Each generation is slightly longer of limb, slightly better able to navigate their impossible world. They do not mourn what they've lost—to them, their ancestors were simply unfinished, caterpillars who could not yet comprehend the butterfly.

In their bioluminescent script, carved into walls with tools of living limestone, they leave messages for future generations—instructions for maintaining the city, certainly, but also philosophy, poetry, mathematical proofs that only make sense if you understand how water can be solid and air can flow. They write of their purpose: not mere survival, but transformation. They are becoming something new, something that requires this unique environment to exist. The city is not their prison but their chrysalis.

The other inhabitants of Korathene—the aerial jellyfish, the camouflaged arthropods, the schools of glass fish—are not separate species but part of the Tidewrights' extended phenotype. Pets, perhaps, or tools, or something stranger: external organs that the Tidewrights have learned to cultivate and control through chemical signals dissolved in the shared medium of their city. The jellyfish clean the air pockets of excess moisture. The arthropods process minerals into forms the Tidewrights can digest. The fish are memory-keepers, their patterns of movement recording information in a language of schooling and scatter.

And in the deepest parts of the city, in chambers even the Tidewrights rarely visit, there are others. Failures, perhaps, or successes too extreme to integrate—beings that have adapted so thoroughly to specific conditions that they can no longer leave them. The Pressure Prophets, who live only in the crushing depths of the foundation chambers, their bodies compressed to impossibility, speaking truths that only make sense under thirty atmospheres. The Void Monks, who've adapted to survive in pure air but can no longer touch water without dissolving, sustained by breathing the mineral dust that settles in the highest chambers. The Current Riders, who've become something like living water themselves, their bodies a coherent flow that maintains identity without fixed form.

All of them, together, make up the civilization of the Drowned Bells—a patient people engaged in the slow art of becoming, their city a laboratory for the transformation of human into something stranger and more wonderful, adapted not just to survive in their flooded world but to make it a canvas for their evolutionary art. They do not wish to return to the surface. They do not dream of pumping out the water. They dream instead of the day when they will no longer need the air pockets at all, when they will flow through their stone gardens as easily as thought, when the bells of Korathene will ring again with sounds that no human throat could make, calling out to others of their kind in the deep places of the world, inviting them to join in the patient work of becoming.
