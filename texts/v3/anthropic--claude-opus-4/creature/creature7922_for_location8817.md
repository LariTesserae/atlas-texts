The Chorus-Sworn dwell here, though that name is a translation that captures neither the sound nor the meaning of what they call themselves. In their tongue, identity is a verb conjugated by harmony—you are what you sing with.

They are human in the way a river is water—technically true but missing the point. Their bodies have adapted to the constant flux, developing a peculiar flexibility in their joints that lets them move with the water's upward flows when needed. Their skin carries a faint luminescence, not enough to see by but enough to find each other in the absolute dark when the lamps reverse. It comes from symbiotic bacteria that feed on the iron-sweet cave-sweat, the same organisms that help them digest the temporal inconsistencies in their food.

Most distinctive are their hands. Each Chorus-Sworn grows between six and nine fingers per hand, the number shifting slowly over their lifetime. The extra digits aren't mutations but accommodations—you need more fingers to grasp things that exist in more than three dimensions. Their nails grow in spirals, naturally forming the anchor-signs that help stabilize local reality. Children learn to click these spiral-nails against stone in patterns that teach them which echoes to trust.

They dress in layers of that peculiar non-rotting pale wood, thin sheets of it worked flexible as leather. The garments wrap and rewrap around their bodies in complex arrangements that can be quickly reconfigured when architecture shifts—what was a robe becomes a bridge-harness becomes a water-sling becomes a robe again. They wear their hair in elaborate knots that serve as temporary storage for small items, for when pockets can't be trusted to lead to the same space twice.

The Chorus-Sworn organize themselves not by family but by Resonance—groups of twelve to twenty individuals whose personal chronologies happen to align. Your Resonance-kin are those who experience Tuesday while you experience Tuesday, though your Tuesdays might be months apart in any objective sense. These groups shift membership as time-streams diverge and converge, a fluid kinship that accepts loss and reunion as natural as breathing.

Each Resonance maintains a Song-House, built around one of the natural chimneys where sound behaves most predictably. Here they practice the Maintenance Hymns—wordless compositions that reinforce causality in their immediate vicinity. A properly sung Maintenance can ensure that water flows downward for days, that children age in only one direction, that the dead stay dead instead of wandering back confused.

The memory-keepers, called Chroniclers, undergo a ritual partial drowning at the age of naming (which happens when you're ready, not at any calendar age). They breathe the water of the Chronicle Pools, taking the oil-memories into their lungs. This kills them, temporarily, long enough for their consciousness to scatter across all the stored histories. They return changed—able to taste lies in the air, to see the past as translucent overlays on the present. Their eyes develop a distinctive oil-sheen iridescence, and they must wear veils of fine copper mesh to filter their vision back to a single timeline.

The merchants belong to the Guild of Flexible Currency, identified by the pouches of mixed-reality items they carry. A skilled merchant can reach into a bag of copper rings and pull out exactly what the customer needs, even if it's fresh bread or a childhood lullaby. They train for years to develop the mental flexibility required, learning to believe equally in all possible contents until the moment of transaction fixes one temporarily in place.

Children of the Sunken Chorus grow up amphibious in more than the physical sense. They play games with the echo-delays, sending messages to their future selves and learning to interpret the warnings their past selves leave behind. They keep pet cryptocrabs, racing them through the non-Euclidean tunnels and betting on which will arrive with the right number of legs. By the age of first-anchor (usually around what others might call ten), they can navigate by echolocation and read the warning-signs faster than they shift.

The elderly among the Chorus-Sworn don't age so much as disperse. Their personal timelines begin to fray, spreading them across multiple moments simultaneously. The very old exist as living echoes of themselves, present at their own births and deaths and every moment between, their consciousness scattered like light through a prism. The community cares for these Dispersed Ones, who speak in overlapping prophecies and memories, valuable for their ability to see the city from outside time's flow.

Artisans work with the mutable nature of matter here. Sculptors shape stone that exists in flux, creating statues that show different faces from different angles—not in the simple way of perspective, but genuinely different people, different scenes, different meanings. Weavers work with thread spun from the temporal moss, creating cloths that age and youth their wearers depending on the pattern. Musicians craft instruments from the singing bones of deep-fish, instruments that play themselves when the harmonics align correctly.

The priests—if that word applies—tend the black glass pillars in the deepest chamber. They call themselves the Resonators, spending days at a time chest-deep in the rising water, learning to match their body's frequencies to the pillars' alien hum. They claim this communion lets them sense the city's deepest needs, predict when chambers will reconfigure, know when the water will reverse its flow. They emerge from these vigils with their bones temporarily crystallized, moving like living tuning forks until their skeletons remember how to be calcium again.

Food comes from three sources: the blind inside-out fish that must be caught with nets of braided time, the spiral moss that grows more nutritious the harder you believe in it, and trade with the surface world. The traders who brave the journey up carry special anchors—heavy reality-weights that keep them from dispersing during the transition between the city's loose causality and the rigid world above. They return with grains and preserved foods that taste of linear time, exotic delicacies in a place where most meals exist as probability clouds until consumed.

The Chorus-Sworn mark their calendar not with days but with Convergences—moments when all the scattered timelines align and everyone experiences the same now. These happen irregularly, sometimes twice in what the surface world might call a week, sometimes not for months. During Convergences, the city holds its markets, its councils, its weddings and funerals. Babies are named, contracts are sealed, and for a few precious hours, everyone can be certain they're speaking to the version of their neighbor that will remember the conversation tomorrow.

They've evolved their own technology—not of metals and mechanisms but of structured impossibility. Need light? Cultivate a colony of temporal fungi whose life cycles can be tuned to pulse at useful frequencies. Need to send a message? Encode it in the pattern of specially trained cryptocrabs whose leg-count will stabilize into prime numbers at their destination. Need to preserve food? Store it in chambers where time flows backward, bringing your meals from the moment just before decay begins.

The sick are tended by Healers who have learned that injury and illness here often exist more as possibilities than facts. A broken bone might be broken, or might only remember being broken, or might be practicing for a future break. The Healers work with sound and touch and carefully applied belief, singing the body back into its preferred configuration. They keep gardens of the medical moss whose spirals, when consumed, help a patient's timeline cohere.

Death in the Sunken Chorus is rarely permanent but never reversible. The dead might return, but they return changed—sometimes younger, sometimes older, sometimes as their own grandchildren. The community has learned to welcome these revenants while understanding they're not quite the same person who died. The funeral rites include a Dissolution Chant that helps scatter the deceased's timeline widely enough that if they do return, they'll have experienced enough elsewhen to be genuinely new.

In their deepest hearts, the Chorus-Sworn know they are an impossibility. They know their city shouldn't exist, that their lives violate laws so fundamental that the universe hasn't even bothered to name them. But they also know that knowledge itself is just another fluid thing here, subject to revision and flow. So they sing their Maintenance Hymns, they count their fingers after crossing bridges, they love their children who might be their parents who might be themselves. They persist through the simple, stubborn act of continuing to be, even when being itself becomes uncertain.

They are the Chorus-Sworn, and they live as all impossible things live—one moment at a time, even when those moments refuse to stay in order.
