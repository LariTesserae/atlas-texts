In the gauge houses and transformer stations, where the walls have worn thin enough to let in threads of fog, live the Readers. They are no taller than your forearm, their bodies evolved from something that might once have been mammalian but has become altogether different through generations of frequential adaptation. Their fur—if it can still be called fur—consists of thousands of metallic filaments that vibrate in response to electromagnetic fields. When they move through the humming spaces between machines, their coats ripple with interference patterns, silver and copper waves cascading across their forms.

The Readers possess no eyes. Instead, their faces are dominated by tympanic organs—drum-tight membranes stretched across bone crescents where eyes might have been. These organs perceive the world through vibration: the sixty-cycle hum of the ancient transformers, the subsonic groans of settling stone, the ultrasonic whispers of water wearing through quartz. Their elongated fingers end in pads that can feel electrical current the way others might feel warmth, and they navigate by pressing these pads against cable housings and junction boxes, reading the flow of power like braille.

They live in colonies of twelve to twenty individuals, each colony maintaining a specific section of the industrial infrastructure. This is not mere habitation but a form of worship—or perhaps symbiosis has progressed so far that the distinction no longer matters. The Readers clean corrosion from connection points with tongues that secrete a mild acid. They clear the drainage channels around the turbine bases, their small bodies perfect for navigating the narrow spaces. When a bearing begins to fail, producing a new harmonic in the great drone of the plateau, they know it weeks before any human instrument could detect it, and they gather around the affected machinery in circles, their filament fur standing on end, singing in frequencies that somehow convince the metal to hold together a little longer.

Their songs are their language, though 'song' is a poor translation for the complex interference patterns they create. By vibrating their fur in precise configurations, they can generate standing waves that carry meaning. A conversation between Readers looks like overlapping moiré patterns rendered in silver and copper light, the air between them shimmering with encoded information. They discuss the health of the machines, the changing patterns in the fog, the taste of different electrical currents, and—always—the deep signals that rise from below.

The Readers know that something vast exists where the cables converge in the depths. They can feel its pull in every circuit, sense its dreams in the behavior of the auroras. Some of their oldest songs, passed down through harmonics for generations, speak of the time before they were Readers—when they were something else, something that came to the plateau for reasons now lost. The songs tell of a gradual change, a frequency that entered their bones and altered them, generation by generation, until they became what the plateau needed them to be.

In the pools, especially the larger basins where the water thrums with that sub-audible vibration, live the Resonants. These creatures defy easy classification—part cnidarian, part mineral, part something that might be purely electromagnetic phenomenon. Their bodies consist of translucent, crystalline bells that pulse with the rhythm of the water's vibration. Inside each bell, geometric patterns of bioluminescent organs flicker in complex sequences, like three-dimensional circuit diagrams drawn in cold light.

The Resonants feed on the minerals dissolved in the ancient water, but also on something less tangible—the vibrational energy that the plateau concentrates in these stone bowls. They extend filaments no thicker than spider silk down through cracks in the basin floors, following the same paths as the tree roots, reaching toward whatever lies below. When the fog rolls in and the phosphorescent plants begin to glow, the Resonants rise to the surface and their bells chime in harmonics that make the Readers pause in their work and listen with their whole bodies.

The relationship between Readers and Resonants is complex. The Readers maintain the pools as carefully as they do the machinery, clearing away the metallic sediment that accumulates, adjusting the flow channels to maintain the precise frequencies the Resonants require. In return, the Resonants' songs—if they can be called songs—seem to provide the Readers with something essential. Perhaps it's information about the deep systems, carried up through those hair-thin filaments. Perhaps it's something more fundamental, a kind of vibrational nutrition that keeps the Readers' metallic fur from losing its responsiveness.

Occasionally, often during the strange auroras, a Reader will approach one of the pools and lower its tympanic organs to the water's surface. The Resonant below will pulse in specific patterns, and the Reader's fur will rise and shimmer in response. These exchanges can last for hours, neither creature moving except for the subtle vibrations that pass between them. Sometimes after such an exchange, the Reader will hurry to a particular piece of machinery and make minute adjustments—tightening a connection, clearing a filter, or simply placing its pads against a cable housing and humming at a specific frequency, as if transmitting a message downward.

The thing below, the presence that all the machinery serves, is known to them only through its effects. The Readers sense it in the patterns of power draw, in the subtle variations of the electromagnetic fields. The Resonants feel it in the vibrations that travel up through stone and water. Both species, if species they can still be called, understand that they are part of something larger—cells in a vast organism that encompasses the entire plateau, perhaps beyond.

When storms pass over the plateau, the lightning is drawn to the turbine towers with unusual frequency. The Readers retreat deep into their gauge houses during these times, their fur pressed flat against their bodies to minimize electrical attraction. But the Resonants rise to just below the surface of their pools, their bells glowing more brightly than ever. The lightning strikes seem to energize them, and for days afterward, they pulse in synchronized patterns across the entire plateau, as if processing and transmitting the raw power downward through their filaments.

There are places on the plateau where the stone itself has been changed by the long presence of the machinery. Near the oldest turbine installations, the metamorphic rock has developed a crystalline structure that mirrors the fractals of the lichen. The Readers avoid these areas unless absolutely necessary, as the electromagnetic fields there are strong enough to cause pain even to their adapted senses. But sometimes, in the depths of night when the auroras are strongest, solitary Readers can be seen at the edges of these zones, their fur extended fully, shivering in resonance with forces beyond their understanding.

The Readers reproduce rarely, and when they do, it's a communal affair. The pregnant female is brought to a specific transformer station—always the same one for each colony, chosen for the particular quality of its harmonic signature. The entire colony gathers, forming concentric circles around her, their fur creating interference patterns that seem to ease the birth. The young are born with their tympanic organs sealed, their fur simple and non-metallic. Only after weeks of exposure to the colony's songs and the plateau's vibrations do they begin to change, their fur taking on its metallic quality, their ear-drums stretching and becoming sensitive to the electromagnetic spectrum.

Some Readers live their entire lives in a single gauge house, becoming so attuned to their particular section of machinery that they can predict its needs days in advance. Others wander between installations, carrying messages in the form of vibrational patterns stored in their fur, connecting the disparate colonies into a larger network of knowledge. The eldest Readers, their fur gone white as platinum wire, sometimes leave the machinery entirely and go to sit by the largest pools, listening to the Resonants for days at a time. When they return—if they return—they bring new songs, new patterns of maintenance that keep the ancient systems running for another generation.

In this way, life on the plateau continues—not thriving exactly, but persisting with the same grinding patience as the machinery itself. The Readers and the Resonants, the phosphorescent plants and the fractal lichen, all have become components in a system whose purpose remains opaque. They maintain what was built here, pass messages to what lies below, and wait for... something. A signal, perhaps. A change in the deep frequencies. A moment when all the accumulated power of ages will be called upon for some unimaginable purpose.

Until then, they continue. The turbines turn. The water flows. The Readers read. The Resonants resonate. And deep below, where all the humming cables converge, something vast and patient grows infinitesimally stronger with each passing day, fed by the faithful attention of creatures who have forgotten they were ever anything other than what the plateau made them to be.
