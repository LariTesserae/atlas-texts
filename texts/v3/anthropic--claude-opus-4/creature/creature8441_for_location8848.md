The builders are not quite human, though they walk on two legs and shape things with their hands. Their bodies tell the story of this unfinished world.

They stand shorter than we might expect, compressed by generations of crawling through low passages. Their spines curve naturally forward, necks elongated and flexible like those of burrowing animals. But it's their skin that marks them as children of this copper-drunk place—it shimmers with a metallic sheen, not from decoration but from incorporation. The copper dust works its way into their pores from birth, creating a living patina that shifts from green to orange to deep brown depending on how the light strikes it.

Their hands are their most evolved feature: six fingers on each, with an extra joint in every digit. These fingers move like sea anemones, constantly testing textures, reading vibrations through touch. The palms are broad and padded with thick calluses that have hardened into something almost like horn. When they press these palms against the bell-stones, the calluses resonate at different frequencies, allowing for a complex vocabulary of vibration.

Their eyes have adapted to the eternal amber twilight. Pupils elongated into horizontal slits, like those of goats, maximizing their ability to gather the dim light. But more remarkable are the oil-sacs beneath their lower eyelids—small organs that produce a phosphorescent film. When they need better vision, they can squeeze these sacs, coating their eyes with glowing oil that enhances their sight for brief periods. The effect is unsettling to imagine: hundreds of workers moving through the tunnels with eyes that gleam like green-gold lamps.

They have no hair as we understand it. Instead, thin copper wires grow from their scalps, conducting the settlement's vibrations directly into their skulls. The longer the wires, the more senior the builder—elders cultivate elaborate arrangements that trail down their backs, creating personal antenna arrays that let them sense disturbances anywhere in the city. Young builders start with mere stubble of copper fuzz, earning length through years of patient growth and careful cultivation.

Their ears have evolved into complex, chambered structures, more like the inside of a seashell than human ears. Multiple channels and resonating chambers allow them to parse the constant noise, filtering out specific frequencies while amplifying others. They can focus their hearing by tilting their heads, using the chambers to create acoustic shadows that block unwanted sound.

The builders breathe in rhythm with the fissures. Their lungs have developed a secondary chamber that stores air during the long exhalations of the earth, releasing it slowly during the inhale phase. This gives them a peculiar speech pattern—words come in rushes during the exhale, followed by long pauses. Conversations ebb and flow with the geological breathing, creating a tidal pattern of communication.

They wear little clothing—the constant warmth of the proto-stone makes it unnecessary—but what they do wear serves specific purposes. Apprentices wrap their joints in strips of crystal-plant fiber, which helps dampen vibrations until their bodies learn to process the constant resonance. Masters wear elaborate harnesses of woven copper wire, creating personal networks that extend their sensory range throughout their districts. The wire clothing sings faintly as they move, adding their own notes to the settlement's endless chorus.

Their diet consists entirely of processed crystal-plant matter and concentrated oils. They've developed specialized stomach chemistry to break down the mineral-organic compounds. Their teeth are not bone but accumulated mineral deposits, constantly growing and self-sharpening as they gnaw through the tough plant fibers. Lost teeth regrow within weeks, pushing up through their jaws like slow-motion crystals.

The social structure reflects the physical reality of their world. Builders organize themselves into "resonance groups"—collections of individuals whose natural frequencies harmonize. These groups live and work together, their synchronized movements creating standing waves in the air that make their labor more efficient. Leadership isn't based on strength or intelligence but on acoustic compatibility—those who can harmonize with the most others become coordinators, their bodies serving as living tuning forks for the group.

They reproduce through a process that seems almost geological. Partners spend months attuning their frequencies, synchronizing their copper-wire hair until it resonates in perfect harmony. When the alignment is complete, they press their oil-sacs together, exchanging phosphorescent secretions that carry genetic information. The resulting offspring grows not in a womb but in a specialized chamber carved from the living rock, where the parents take turns singing specific frequencies that guide the child's development.

Children emerge from these stone wombs already adapted to the cacophony, their copper hair already beginning to sprout, their oil-sacs functional from first breath. They learn to crawl through the passages before they walk, their elongated necks and flexible spines making them natural navigators of the maze-like city.

The builders show affection through resonance—pressing their palms together to share vibrations, or intertwining their copper hair to create complex harmonies. They mourn their dead by carrying the bodies to the breathing fissures, where the oils slowly transform the remains into a special type of stone that rings with the deceased's personal frequency when struck. These memorial stones become part of the city's architecture, ensuring that every ancestor continues to contribute to the great symphony of First Breath.

In this moment of eternal becoming, the builders are themselves perpetually unfinished. Their bodies continue adapting, their copper patina deepening, their wire hair growing ever more complex. They are not separate from their city but part of it—living components in a vast instrument that plays the song of a world being born.
