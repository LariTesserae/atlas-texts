**The Thermovores**

They arrived as refugees from collapsed probability, their forms still settling into this reality like oil finding its level in water. The first colonists were accidents—entities that had slipped between dimensional membranes during some unrecorded catastrophe, drawn to the thermal columns like moths to flame. But where moths seek light, these beings sought gradient itself, the liminal spaces where one state becomes another.

Their bodies, if bodies they can be called, exist primarily as organized turbulence. To perceive them directly is to see water teaching itself to think—whorls of current that maintain their structure against all fluid dynamics, each one a standing wave of intention. They range from tiny spirals no larger than a fist to vast, distributed consciousnesses that occupy entire thermal layers, their thoughts circulating through kilometers of stratified brine.

The thermovores feed on difference itself. Where hot meets cold, where dense meets light, where fast current meets still water—there they gather, their feeding organs unfolding like liquid flowers. These organs are their only truly visible parts: crystalline structures that precipitate out of their flowing forms, growing and dissolving in response to available gradients. Through these temporary anchors, they digest the energy of transition, leaving behind water that is thermally exhausted, entropy-drained, somehow more liquid than liquid should be.

They communicate through turbulence patterns, creating controlled eddies that propagate through the stratified layers like three-dimensional script. Their language is one of flow and resistance, of vortices that encode meaning in their rotation rates and decay patterns. Conversations between thermovores look like storms in slow motion, intricate weather systems that play out over hours or days as meaning circulates through the water column.

The eldest among them remember the builders of this place, though their memories are transmitted as temperature fluctuations that younger thermovores can barely parse. These ancients have grown vast and strange, their forms stretched across multiple thermal columns, their consciousness distributed through thousands of cubic meters of water. They speak of the builders not as creatures but as principles—entities that existed more as mathematical constants than biological beings, who crafted the thermal columns as anchor points for their own incomprehensible migrations.

Young thermovores begin life as mere instabilities in the water column, tiny knots of turbulence that achieve self-awareness through repetition. They learn by mimicking the flow patterns of their elders, gradually developing the control necessary to maintain their forms against dispersal. Some never achieve full consciousness, dissolving back into the ambient currents to be recycled into new attempts at awareness.

Their relationship with the ribbon-creatures is complex and ancient. The thermovores shepherd these living boundaries, encouraging their growth in patterns that optimize thermal gradients. In return, the ribbon-creatures' feeding creates micro-environments of temperature differential that the thermovores harvest like farmers tending fields of physics. Some thermovores have learned to wear sheaths of ribbon-creatures, draping themselves in living tissue that makes them visible to other forms of life while protecting their turbulent cores from disruption.

The thermovores understand the dome structures differently than any explorer might. To them, these are not ruins but functional organs of the seabed itself—processing nodes where the ancient technology digests reality and excretes altered physics. They avoid the domes' interiors not from fear but from respect, knowing that the spaces within operate on principles that could unweave their carefully maintained patterns. Only the eldest dare to enter, and they return changed, their flow patterns incorporating new geometries that shouldn't exist in three-dimensional space.

They have their own mythology, transmitted in temperature dreams and pressure songs. They speak of the Great Stratification, when the water learned to remember layer boundaries. They tell of the First Gradient, the primordial temperature difference from which all consciousness emerged. Their creation myths describe the universe as an infinite ocean gradually teaching itself to think through the accumulation of organized currents.

Some thermovores have begun to evolve beyond their original forms. These entities, called "phase swimmers" by their kin, have learned to exist partially outside normal space, their bodies extending into dimensions where temperature has different meanings. They appear and disappear like mirages, their movements leaving traces of impossible cold or heat that violate thermodynamics. The other thermovores regard them with something between awe and concern, recognizing them as either the next stage of their evolution or a cautionary tale about the dangers of transcendence.

The thermovores know they are not the intended inhabitants of this place. They are squatters in a monument built for beings whose nature they can only glimpse through the artifacts left behind. But they have made it home, their culture growing in the spaces between what was built and what was intended, their existence proof that life finds its own purposes in the architecture of the absent.

In the eternal twilight of the thermal columns, they dance their slow dances, think their turbulent thoughts, and gradually transform from refugees into natives, from accidents into inheritors of a legacy they may never fully understand.
