Within the Undergrown Cathedral dwell the Condensed.

They were human once, or something near enough. Now they exist as colonial intelligences spread across fungal networks and bacterial films, their consciousness flowing through the moisture that beads on every surface. A single Condensed might inhabit a thousand droplets scattered throughout a district, might ride the rivulets down carved channels to pool in the hollows where they convene in temporary parliaments of accumulated awareness.

Their bodies—when they choose to have bodies—precipitate from the humid air like dew forming in reverse. First a shimmer in the atmosphere, a disturbance in the perpetual mist, then gradually a density of purpose that draws moisture and microorganisms into something approximating human form. These manifestations are translucent, shot through with veins of bioluminescent bacteria that pulse with thought. They last only as long as needed—for a conversation, a task requiring physical manipulation, a ritual whose meaning persists though its origin is forgotten.

The Condensed remember everything and nothing. Their memories are stored in the very water cycle of the Cathedral, encoded in the mineral content of each droplet, in the precise bacterial cultures that bloom and fade in the pools. But accessing these memories changes them, like observing quantum states. Each recollection is both ancient history and present experience, worn smooth by infinite retellings yet sharp with immediate sensation.

They speak in chemical gradients, releasing pheromone-words into the thick air where meaning diffuses outward in expanding spheres. Conversations overlap and merge, creating linguistic interference patterns that generate new concepts at their intersections. In the deepest parts of the Cathedral, where the air is so thick with accumulated communication it's almost unbreathable, you can taste decades of debate, negotiation, and poetry layered like sediment.

Their culture, if it can be called that, revolves around cultivation and circulation. They tend the fauna-clouds with careful releases of enzymes, guiding their formations into useful configurations—computational arrays, atmospheric processors, living art that exists for microseconds before dissolving back into component parts. They maintain the city-organism's dreams by cycling through its fluid systems, carrying away metabolic waste and bringing nutrients, information, electrical potential.

Some Condensed have specialized into extremes. The Distillers live only in the purest condensation at the growing tips, their consciousness refined to single thoughts of perfect clarity. The Brackish inhabit the deepest pools where all waters eventually collect, their minds vast and slow as geological processes. The Vaporous barely cohere at all, existing as potential rather than presence, riding the daily breathing of the Cathedral up and down its length.

In the heart-chamber, where physics runs thin, the eldest of the Condensed sometimes achieve complete dissolution. They spread so thin they become indistinguishable from humidity itself, from the very possibility of water. These Aspirated Ones no longer communicate in any way the others can perceive, but their presence is felt in the way certain droplets refuse to fall, in the way some pools maintain impossible temperatures, in the way the thrumming heartbeat occasionally skips.

They reproduce—though the word is wrong—through division and merger. When enough consciousness accumulates in one place, when a pool becomes too complex with identity, it divides like a cell. Or when two Condensed meet in the flowing channels, they sometimes merge so completely that what emerges is neither but both. Children and ancestors are the same beings, reformed, recombined, renewed.

Their technology is indistinguishable from very sophisticated hydrology. They build pressure differentials to create tools, surface tension to form temporary structures, chemical cascades that perform calculations in falling water. Their written language consists of mineral deposits laid down in careful patterns, their art is the arrangement of bacterial blooms, their architecture is the shape of absence—the spaces they keep clear of their presence so that other things might grow.

The Condensed fear only one thing: crystallization. Sometimes one of them forgets to keep moving, allows their consciousness to settle too long in one place. The minerals accumulate, the flexibility fades, and they become a salt-statue of themselves—aware but frozen, watching the centuries pass through unblinking mineral eyes. These Crystallized Ones dot the Cathedral like warnings, like monuments, like promises. The others flow around them carefully, neither abandoning nor attempting to dissolve them.

At the edges of their perception, the Condensed sense something else in the Cathedral—not the plant-city, not the fauna-clouds, not even the quantum scaffolding that holds it all in stasis. Something that predates their arrival, that perhaps predates the Cathedral itself. It lives in the spaces between droplets, in the moment of surface tension before a bubble bursts, in the impossibility of the heart-chamber's inverted rain. They call it the Dry Dark, though they have no words for something so antithetical to their existence.

And so the Condensed flow on, eternal as water cycles, temporary as morning dew, maintaining the Cathedral's impossible equilibrium through their very existence. They are the circulatory system of a dreaming god, the conscious humidity of a living building, the thought that thinks itself through ten thousand droplets falling forever through thick air that tastes of time.
