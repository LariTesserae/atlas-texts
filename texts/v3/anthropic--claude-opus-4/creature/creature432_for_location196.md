The people of Verlumm move like sap through veins—slowly, deliberately, with an economy of motion that speaks to centuries of adaptation. They are tall and attenuated, their limbs stretched thin by generations of navigating the plant's vertical chambers. Their skin has taken on the subtle properties of their host: gray-green undertones that deepen to bark-brown at the joints, a fine scaling that catches the bioluminescent light like scattered coins. When they stand still, which they often do for hours at a time, they become nearly invisible against the plant-flesh walls.

Their eyes have evolved past the need for whites—the entire orb is given over to pupil and iris, dark as the deep hollows where the Worldheart beats loudest. These eyes see in spectrums that overlap with the plant's own chemical signals, reading pheromone gradients like text, tracking the invisible currents of communication that flow through every surface. When they speak, it is in two languages simultaneously: audible words that carry meaning, and chemical exhalations that provide context, emotion, and layers of nuance that no outsider could parse.

The eldest among them, the Sap-Readers, have gone further in their symbiosis. Their hair has been replaced by thin tendrils that interface directly with the plant's neural pathways, allowing them to commune with the vast slow intelligence that underpins their world. These individuals move hardly at all, stationed at critical junctions where the plant's consciousness runs deepest. They feed through direct osmosis, their skin having developed specialized pores that align with the plant's feeding apertures. When they speak, which is rare, their words carry the weight of the substrate itself—proclamations that can redirect growth patterns, open sealed passages, or wake dormant sectors of the city.

Children are rare and precious, raised communally in nursery chambers where the plant's growth is most active. They learn to read the guide-threads before they can properly walk, their small fingers tracing the phosphorescent paths with an instinct that seems hardwired into their genes. The young ones play games that would terrify an outsider—racing through vascular channels ahead of the cleaning serpents, composing frequencies that make the Chorus Beetles respond in harmonious song, daring each other to venture into the thin zones where the plant gives way to raw stone and the air tastes of ancient machinery.

The workers who maintain the deep machines comprise their own caste, marked by the metallic sheen their skin acquires from constant exposure to the interface zones. They speak in mathematical frequencies, their voices pitched to resonate with the equipment they tend. Their bodies show signs of deliberate modification—extra joints for navigating cramped mechanical spaces, fingertips that can extrude tools formed from their own calcified blood, eyes that have developed nictitating membranes to protect against the harsh light of the machine cores. They live apart from the others, in chambers that vibrate with energies that would disturb unmodified nervous systems.

But perhaps most striking are the Dreamers—those who have given themselves over entirely to the plant's longest cycles. They root themselves in growth chambers, allowing the substrate to partially absorb them, their consciousness merging with the vegetal intelligence for years or decades at a time. Their bodies become nodes in the greater network, their dreams influencing the city's development in ways both subtle and profound. When they occasionally surface from these deep communions, they speak of vast patterns, of purposes that span geological time, of the slow conversation between the plant and the machines in the deep that has been ongoing since before humans arrived.

The hunters move in packs through the middle heights, where the floating colonies drift between spires. They wear suits of cultivated armor—living tissue that the plant grows for them in exchange for their service in controlling the jellyfish-like predators. These suits amplify their movements, allowing them to leap between surfaces with arthropod precision, trailing pheromone markers that warn others of danger. Their weapons are not separate tools but extensions of their armor, barbed tendrils that can deliver paralytic toxins or slice through the translucent flesh of their prey.

In the resonance markets, traders deal in frequencies rather than physical goods. They carry their wares in their throats—specialized vocal modifications that allow them to produce and store valuable tones. A successful merchant might possess the frequency that opens a rare feeding pore, or the subsonic pattern that calms an aggressive beetle, or the precise harmonic that causes certain crystal formations to release their stored water. They haggle in concerts of competing sounds, their negotiations creating impromptu symphonies that draw crowds of listeners who pay in secrets—chemical passwords that unlock new areas of the city.

The artists work with living medium, coaxing the plant to grow in aesthetic patterns, creating galleries that shift and evolve over years. They inject dyes into the vascular system that emerge as slowly blooming murals, or train colonies of bioluminescent organisms to create light sculptures that respond to viewers' presence. Their studios are chambers where they've established such rapport with the substrate that it responds to their emotional states, growing thorns when they're angry, blooming with phosphorescent flowers when they're joyful.

All of them share certain traits—the efficient movements, the water-conserving adaptations, the deep patience that comes from living in sync with a consciousness that measures time in centuries. They touch surfaces constantly, reading chemical information through their fingertips. They sleep standing up, leaning into the plant's embrace, their breathing synchronized with the great respiratory cycles of their host. When they die, they are not buried but absorbed, their bodies given to the feeding chambers where their nutrients can be redistributed through the system they spent their lives serving.

The mad ones, those who've lost their synchronization with the Worldheart, wander the thin zones where the plant barely grows. They scratch at the stone, muttering about the time before the merger, collecting fragments of metal and crystal that they claim are messages from the deep machines. The others avoid them but also feed them, leaving nectar deposits at the edge of settled areas. For in Verlumm, even madness is recognized as a necessary part of the ecosystem—the random elements that prevent the system from becoming too perfectly ordered, too completely synchronized.

And in the deepest chambers, where even the Sap-Readers rarely venture, there are those who claim to have seen the Gardeners—beings so completely merged with the plant that they can no longer be said to be human at all. They move through the substrate like thoughts through a mind, tending to processes that span the entire plateau, their purposes as alien as the original designers of this living world. Whether they truly exist or are merely legend, their possibility shapes the aspirations of every citizen of Verlumm, the ultimate expression of the symbiosis that defines this breathing, thinking, growing civilization.
