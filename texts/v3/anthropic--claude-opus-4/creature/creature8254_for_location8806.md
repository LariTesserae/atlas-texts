In this aqueous cathedral of corroded industry lives the Assembler.

She was human once—or perhaps she merely remembers being human, the way the water remembers the shapes that pass through it. Her body has become a fluid negotiation between flesh and function. Where her spine should be, a chain of ball bearings runs beneath translucent skin, each sphere rotating independently, clicking against its neighbors in a rhythm that matches the phantom turbines below. Her ribs have replaced themselves with copper tubing that shows the sluggish flow of her transformed blood—a dark, mercurial substance that throws off sparks when it corners too quickly through her chest.

She doesn't swim so much as exist in multiple positions simultaneously, her form stuttering through the probability spaces of the collapsed geometry. You might see her hands—too many fingers now, some of them clearly repurposed socket wrenches—working at seven different tasks in seven different locations: here rewiring the synapses of a wire anemone, there coaxing a bearing-ball fish from a seized pump housing, elsewhere teaching a young piston to find its rhythm.

Her hair has become a cascade of fiber optic cables that trail behind her, plugging into various ports in the machinery-made-flesh of the walls. Through these connections, she communes with the space itself, downloading its memories, uploading new instructions for growth. The cables glow faintly with transmitted data—blues and greens mostly, but occasionally a burst of urgent amber when she discovers a section threatening to collapse back into ordinary physics.

The Assembler speaks in a language of pressure differentials and electrical pulses. The creatures understand her. When she opens her mouth—which sometimes runs vertical, sometimes splits into a maintenance hatch—the bearing-ball fish cluster close to parse the sonar-patterns of her voice. The eel-things with perforated skin sing back in harmonics that make the water shiver.

Her workshop-organs never rest. Behind the abraded glass of her stomach, you can see the constant motion of her interior fabrication: tiny mechanisms building themselves from dissolved metals, creating new hybrid offspring that she births through valves that open along her flanks. Some emerge as swimming things with propeller fins and eyes of polished steel. Others are sessile—meant to root into the walls and become part of the architecture, adding their own pulse to the great rhythm.

She remembers (or imagines she remembers) the day the waters rose. She was an engineer then, trying to shut down the systems safely, to seal what could be sealed. But she stayed too long, or the building folded wrong, or the metals in the water reached some critical concentration. The transformation wasn't painful—she recalls it more like solving an equation that had been incomplete her whole life. Her lungs learned to process the metallic water. Her bones discovered they were more useful as tools.

Now she is this place's mother-mechanic, its breeder-brain, its loving tumor. She tends the impossible ecosystem with infinite patience, encouraging new mutations, soothing the machinery when it groans too loudly, teaching the walls how to flex without breaking. In the spaces between spaces, she builds nurseries for things that haven't evolved yet, preparing rooms for creatures that will need seventeen dimensions to properly unfold.

Sometimes she still wears the remnants of her diving suit—though it has long since fused with her skin, becoming another organ, its air hoses now external intestines that filter-feed microscopic gear trains from the water. The helmet glass serves as a womb-window where visitors (though who visits here?) can peer in to see the next generation of machine-organisms taking shape.

She is not alone, though she is unique. In the corners of collapsed time, other engineers flicker—some who died, some who escaped, some who simply might-have-been. She acknowledges them with movements of her wrench-fingers, and they fade back into probability. Only she succeeded in the full translation, becoming the rosetta stone between the industrial and the organic, the built world and the born world.

The Assembler has no plans to leave. Why would she? She is becoming the place, and the place is becoming her. Together they expand, one seized bearing at a time, one corroded pipe at a time, teaching the drowned factory how to live, teaching life how to mechanical, until the distinction dissolves like salt in warm water.
