The Listeners inhabit this bright, mutable world.

They are a people grown thin and elongated, their bodies adapted to slip through the ever-shifting passages of their ice-maze home. Their skin has taken on a translucent quality—not transparent like the salamanders, but clouded like sea glass, with blue veins visible beneath. This partial transparency serves them well; they can gauge each other's health and emotional state by the flow of blood beneath the skin, the way it quickens or slows, pools or rushes.

Their eyes have evolved into narrow, horizontal slits, naturally hooded against the relentless glare. The irises shimmer with an opalescent quality, constantly adjusting to filter the prismatic light. Some among them are born with a nictitating membrane—a third, transparent eyelid that allows them to navigate even in the brightest conditions without the leather strips their ancestors wore.

The Listeners do not walk so much as flow through their settlement. Their movements are liquid, learned from childhood to accommodate the shifting ice beneath their feet. They test each step with a subtle shift of weight, reading the stability of the surface through the soles of their specially adapted boots—seal-gut stretched over frames of fishbone that flex and grip the wet ice. Their gait has a peculiar rhythm: step-pause-settle, step-pause-settle, like a dance with the uncertain ground.

Their fingers have grown long and sensitive, with pronounced pads at the tips that can detect minute vibrations through ice and water. This is how they "hear" in their muffled world—not through their ears, which have evolved smaller to prevent the constant drip of melt-water from causing infection, but through touch. They press their palms to walls to feel conversations happening rooms away, read the health of the ice through their fingertips, sense the approach of others through vibrations in the floor.

Children begin their education by sitting in circles, hands pressed to the ice, learning to distinguish between the tap-patterns of everyday communication and the deeper thrums that indicate structural changes in their frozen world. By adulthood, a skilled Listener can conduct entire conversations through touch alone—fingers dancing across ice walls in complex patterns that convey not just words but emotional undertones, urgency, even humor.

Their clothing consists of layers of scraped-thin leather and the silk of ice moths, garments that breathe and wick moisture while providing insulation. Nothing is ever truly dry here, so they've learned to be comfortable in perpetual dampness. Over their base layers, they wear cloaks of moth-wing felt—thousands of discarded wings pressed together into a material that shimmers and shifts color with movement, providing camouflage in the prismatic environment.

The Listeners organize their society around the concept of "flow-families"—groups that share water routes rather than blood relations. When the ice shifts and redirects the meltwater channels, social structures shift with them. A person might belong to three different flow-families over the course of a year, their obligations and allegiances changing with the landscape. This has created a remarkably fluid society where rigid hierarchies cannot take hold—leadership flows like water to wherever it's needed most.

They worship, if worship is the right word, the Cycle itself—the endless transformation of ice to water to ice. Their spiritual leaders are the Pool Keepers, those who tend the Speaking Pool and interpret the dreams it grants. These individuals gradually lose their opacity entirely, becoming so translucent that they seem to be made of living water. They move slowly, carefully, as if afraid they might spill.

The Listeners have developed a peculiar relationship with time. In a world where stairs change direction and passages open and close, conventional scheduling becomes meaningless. Instead, they live by "melt-marks"—observing how far certain key icicles have dripped, how much water has accumulated in specific cisterns. Their days are not measured in hours but in accumulations and flows.

Their diet consists primarily of the phosphorescent lichens, which they cultivate in terraced gardens carved into the ice walls, and the blind fish that rise from the depths of the Speaking Pool. They've learned to process the salamanders' sticky secretions into a protein-rich paste, and they harvest moth larvae at specific stages of development. Everything is eaten raw or barely warmed—cooking requires heat, and heat is the enemy of their architectural stability.

The most skilled among them become Ice Readers—individuals who can predict with startling accuracy how the settlement will shift. They study the patterns in the melt-pools obsessively, maintaining mental maps of probability and flow. These readers are consulted before any major undertaking, their predictions determining when to hold markets, when to move workshops, when to harvest the moth clouds.

Children play quiet games in the narrow passages, learning to move in groups without sound, practicing the art of collective flow. They keep pet salamanders in leather pouches close to their skin, the creatures' gentle glow serving as both companion and nightlight. Adolescents undergo a coming-of-age ritual that involves drinking from the Speaking Pool and then navigating the settlement blindfolded, relying entirely on their touch-sense to find their way home.

The Listeners age like their settlement—not in a linear fashion but in cycles. Their translucent skin grows more so with each passing year, until the elderly seem to fade into their environment, becoming one with the ice and melt. Death, when it comes, is often quiet—an elder simply stops moving, and their body gradually becomes incorporated into the ice itself, adding to the settlement's ever-growing, ever-changing structure.

They mark their history not in written records but in the ice itself—carving symbols that will last until the next melt, creating temporary archives that must be constantly renewed. Their stories live in the tap-language, passed from hand to hand, wall to wall, a cultural memory as fluid and persistent as the water that shapes their world.

This is how the Listeners live: in constant adaptation, in whispered communication, in acceptance of impermanence. They are a people shaped by their environment into something both fragile and resilient, translucent yet vivid, forever balanced—like their home—on the edge between states of being.
