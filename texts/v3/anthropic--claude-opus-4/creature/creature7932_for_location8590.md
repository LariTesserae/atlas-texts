The people of the plateau are small and dense, their bodies compressed by generations of low ceilings and narrow passages. Their shoulders slope forward, permanently hunched from a lifetime of ducking through doorways. Their hands are oversized and scarred, fingers thick as sausages from working stone, palms permanently gray with basalt dust that no amount of washing can remove. They move through their city like water through a reef—flowing around obstacles, finding the path of least resistance, never quite standing straight.

Their eyes have adapted to the constant twilight of the inner city. Pupils dilated wide and dark, they navigate by touch as much as sight, reading the texture of walls with their fingertips. The young are born with pale, almost translucent irises that darken with age as they spend more time in the shadows. The old develop a peculiar sideways way of looking, always watching for the telltale dust that precedes a ceiling collapse, always listening for the groan that means the earth is shifting again.

They speak in whispers and clicks, a language evolved for echo-prone corridors where a shout might bring down loose stones. Their words are short and sharp, like their tools—nothing wasted, everything functional. They have forty-seven different terms for the quality of stone, but only one word for beauty, and it translates roughly to "holds weight well."

Children are raised communally in the middle levels, where the ceilings are highest and the air least smoky. They learn to walk on the balls of their feet, silent as spiders, playing games that involve navigating blindfolded through mock passages. By age six, they can identify good building stone by sound alone—the clear ring of solid basalt versus the dull thud of fractured rock. They carry small hammers from the time they can walk, tapping constantly at walls, learning the city's ever-changing structure through vibration.

The water-carriers form a separate caste, marked by the permanent groove worn into their shoulders from leather straps. They know every hidden spring, every seasonal seep, every place where morning condensation gathers. Their children inherit not just routes but the subtle knowledge of weather—how the pressure drops before the clouds snag, how the stone sweats before rain. They move at a characteristic half-trot, their gait evolved to minimize spillage while maximizing speed.

Stone-workers are the city's memory. In a place that constantly rebuilds itself, they remember which blocks came from where, which walls have stood longest, which foundations rest on solid bedrock versus fill. Master builders carry mental maps of the city's bones—not just what is, but what was, layers of construction history that inform each decision. They read stress fractures like fortune tellers read palms, predicting where the next collapse will come.

The forge-workers are half-deaf from the constant hammering, their faces permanently reddened from leaning over inadequate fires. They've learned to make tools from almost nothing—bog iron hammered paper-thin and folded dozens of times, cutting edges maintained by constant sharpening rather than hardness. They work in shifts around the clock, knowing that if the forges go cold, they might never get hot enough again in the thin air.

Everyone is always building. Even the elderly work mortar between stones with arthritic fingers. Children sort rubble by size. Pregnant women weave grass for chinking. There is no concept of retirement, only a gradual transition from lifting stones to mixing mortar to finally becoming one of the watchers—the old ones who sit in strategic corners and observe the city's structural health, calling out warnings in voices made authoritative by survival.

They eat standing up, quickly, food held in one hand while the other continues working. Their diet is monotonous—stone-ground tuber cakes, bitter greens, occasional birds netted in the morning fog, thin soups extended with grass seed. Everything tastes of smoke and stone dust. They've learned to extract nutrition from lichen, to identify which mosses can be safely eaten, to cultivate mushrooms in the darkest, dampest corners of their basements.

Their religion, if it can be called that, is entirely practical. They leave small offerings of food at stress points in the architecture—not for gods, but for the stone itself, as if feeding it might make it stronger. They believe the plateau is alive, young and still deciding what it wants to become. Their role is to help it bear the weight of their existence, to become part of its structure rather than merely burden it.

They dream of open spaces but wake gasping, terrified by the absence of walls. Children born on the rare occasions when families venture to the plateau's edge often can't adjust to the interior city—they develop a condition the people call "sky sickness," an inability to navigate without horizons. Most who show symptoms are apprenticed to the water-carriers, who at least see daylight more than most.

Marriage contracts are written on thin sheets of slate and built into walls—literally mortared into the structure of shared dwellings. Divorce involves carefully chiseling out the slate without compromising the wall's integrity. The most stable marriages are those whose contracts have been built into load-bearing walls, where removal would require extensive reconstruction.

They mark time not by seasons but by collapses and reconstructions. "The year the east quarter fell." "Two builds after the great settling." Their history is written in stone—not carved, but in the very arrangement of blocks, the way certain patterns repeat, the architectural memory of previous disasters teaching each generation how to build stronger.

Death is practical here. Bodies are burned in the forges, the ash mixed into mortar. They say it makes stronger cement, though whether this is true or merely comfort, no one has tested. The dead become part of the walls, holding up the living, which seems appropriate in a place where everything supports everything else.

There is a madness that comes from living at such density, a particular break that happens when someone can no longer bear the weight—literal and figurative—of the city pressing in. The mad are not exiled but given new roles. They become the runners, the only ones who use the rooftop roads, leaping between buildings with a recklessness that looks suicidal but somehow rarely results in falls. They carry urgent messages, small packages, news of instabilities. The city needs their madness, their willingness to move through spaces others won't risk.

The people sleep in shifts, three or four to a stone shelf, wrapped in woven grass blankets that smell of smoke and sweat. Privacy exists only in the mind. They've developed a cultural blindness to others' activities, a way of being alone in crowds that involves subtle positioning of shoulders, minute adjustments of gaze, a social architecture as complex as their physical one.

They sing, but only while working—rhythmic chants that coordinate effort, warn of dangers, pass along instructions. A handful of tones convey complex information: *lift now, swing clear, wall falling, all safe, need water, found crack, good stone, bad stone.* Outsiders would call it music, but to them it's simply the sound of building, as natural and necessary as the tap of hammers.

These are people who have evolved with their city, shaped by stone as much as they shape it. They are small and strong and enduring, like the lichen they resemble. They build because they must, live because they build, and dream of nothing but the next stone, the next wall, the next small victory against the young earth's restless shrugging. They are not happy, but they are necessary, and in their necessity they find a purpose that transcends happiness—the grim satisfaction of weight well-carried, of another day survived, of walls that stand through one more settling of the restless stone.
