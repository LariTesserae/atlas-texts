In this breathing world between worlds, she tends her garden.

The children of the village know her as the Orchid Woman, though she has been called by older names—*Guardian of the In-Between*, *Keeper of Small Hungers*, *The One Who Listens to Water*. She is neither young nor old, but something outside of time, like the grotto itself. Her skin carries the same quality as the cave walls: smooth in places from years of touch, rough where life has left its marks, faintly luminescent in the darkness as if she has absorbed decades of filtered light.

She moves through the grotto like water finding its level, never disturbing more than necessary. Her bare feet know every moss-covered stone, every slippery patch where algae blooms. The frogs announce her presence not with alarm but with a subtle shift in their chorus, a recognition. She wears simple cloth that might once have been white but now carries the colors of her world—green stains from moss, ochre from clay, silver traces where snail paths have dried.

Her work is patient and precise. She tends the carnivorous sundews, feeding them beetles when their sticky traps grow sparse. She guides the aerial roots of the orchids toward better purchase on the stone. She clears the overflow channels before the rains come, reading the humidity in her bones. The poison dart frogs bring their tadpoles to the pools she maintains, knowing the water levels will remain constant under her care.

But she is not the only one who calls this breathing cave home.

The children come and go like the butterflies—quick visitors drawn by curiosity and the sweet perfume. She teaches them which fungi glow brightest when touched gently, which frog songs predict rain, how to read the fossil spirals in the limestone walls. They bring her offerings from the world above: fruit she will not eat but will place in the composting corners, news she will listen to while working, braiding vines or crushing petals for the dyes she rarely uses anymore.

In the deeper pools lives something older. The villagers call it *Viejo Pez*, the Old Fish, but it is not quite a fish—more like the memory of one, pale and elongated, with whiskers that taste the water and eyes that have forgotten the sun. It surfaces only when she calls it with a particular sequence of ripples, accepts the grubs she offers, then disappears into the underwater passages that connect to cave systems no human has mapped. Sometimes she follows it partway, holding her breath and pulling herself along the submerged limestone, but always returns before the darkness becomes absolute. They have an understanding, she and the Old Fish: neither asks questions about what lies beyond their shared boundary.

The bats that roost in the highest crevices know her as a heat signature that moves too slowly to be prey, too regularly to be a threat. Their guano feeds her garden, and she ensures their roosts remain undisturbed when the rain-swollen waterfalls might otherwise flood them out. At dusk, when they spiral out through the entrance shaft in a living cyclone, she often climbs to watch them disappear into the purple sky, her first glimpse of the outer world in days.

There are others, less seen: the boa that sleeps for weeks in the warm stones near the geothermal vent, digesting something large; the translucent cave spiders that build webs across the narrower passages, their silk catching water droplets like strung diamonds; the colony of leaf-cutter ants that have somehow adapted to harvest the cave moss, their highways running up the walls and out through cracks too small for any vertebrate to follow.

But perhaps most remarkable is what lives in the threshold itself—that narrow squeeze where the outer world meets the inner. Here dwells a peculiar symbiosis: a moth whose caterpillars feed exclusively on a vine that grows nowhere else, its roots in the cave's humidity and its leaves reaching for daylight. The adult moths, wings patterned like the limestone walls, pollinate the night-blooming orchids before laying their eggs on the vine leaves. She protects this cycle fiercely, knowing that it exists nowhere else on earth, this precise balance of light and dark, wet and dry, earth and air.

On the fullest moons, when silver light pours down the entrance shaft like water, she receives visitors of another kind. The healers come for the orchids that bloom only in this half-light. The shamans come for the fungi that fruit in perfect darkness. The grieving come to listen to the water until their tears join the eternal dripping. She guides them all with the same quiet attention she gives to the snails and sundews, knowing that everything that enters the grotto becomes, in some small way, part of its breathing.

At night, when even the diurnal frogs quiet and only the nocturnal chorus remains, she settles into her place—a natural hollow worn smooth by generations of her predecessors, positioned where she can hear both the life of the grotto and the wind from the world beyond. She does not exactly sleep. Instead, she enters a state like the cave itself: neither quite awake nor dreaming, but something in between, breathing with those thousand tiny lungs, her heartbeat synchronizing with the patient drops of water that built this world and will, eventually, transform it into something else entirely.

In the morning, she will rise with the first shift of air that announces the warming stone. She will check the orchid buds, adjust the channels, perhaps venture deep enough to commune with the Old Fish. The children might come with questions or offerings. The rains might demand her attention. But for now, she simply exists in the breathing dark, guardian and gardener, neither fully human nor other, as essential to this grotto as the limestone bones that hold it all together.
