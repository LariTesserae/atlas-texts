**The Keepers of Continuity**

They number perhaps seven, perhaps twelve—the count changes with the decades, as some fade into the walls and others precipitate from them like mineral ghosts given form. The Keepers move through the cistern maze with a fluidity that makes them seem less like separate beings than extensions of the water itself, their long limbs finding purchase on surfaces too slick for ordinary flesh.

Their bodies have adapted over generations uncounted. Skin like wet parchment, translucent enough to show the sluggish pulse of blood beneath—if it is blood. Their circulation has slowed to match the patient drip of the cisterns, hearts beating perhaps once per minute, perhaps less. They breathe in sighs that last hours, drawing the thick air deep into lungs that have learned to extract oxygen from humidity alone.

The eldest among them—though age means little when time moves like honey—has fingers that have worn into living ladles, cupped permanently from centuries of testing water quality with ritual dedication. Her eyes have filmed over with a protective membrane that makes them appear to be filled with milk, yet she navigates the cramped passages by touch and taste alone, reading the mineral content of each droplet like scripture.

They wear nothing but the accumulated minerals of their environment—deposits that have built up on their bodies like living armor, creating ridged patterns that mirror the chamber walls. These accretions flake away and regrow in cycles tied to some calendar known only to stone. In certain lights, they appear to be wearing robes of flowing water, the minerals catching and refracting what little illumination penetrates their realm.

The Keepers do not speak—their vocal cords have atrophied to whispers that blend seamlessly with the sound of water on stone. Instead, they communicate through the cisterns themselves, tapping complex rhythms on the bronze fixtures that propagate through the water-filled channels. A conversation might take days to complete, each participant adding their percussion in turn as they pass through different chambers.

Their work is mysterious, even to themselves. They maintain the ancient waterways through movements so subtle they border on the geological—a finger drawn along a channel over decades to prevent mineral blockage, a shift in posture that redirects a drip to wear away an obstruction. They are gardeners of erosion, shepherds of seepage.

The youngest Keeper—if youth can be measured in mere centuries—still remembers sunlight as more than abstraction. She tends the pale cave vines with special devotion, coaxing them through cracks toward the surface, though she will never follow them there. Her fingernails have grown into specialized tools, shaped by use into perfect instruments for scraping specific mineral deposits, clearing particular channels.

They feed rarely and strangely. The pale mosses are both food and companion—they cultivate certain patches with their own saliva, which has become a potent growth medium. They sip from specific points in the water system where minerals concentrate, their bodies processing limestone and copper salts as sustenance. Some have developed symbiotic relationships with the cave salamanders, which clean parasites from their mineral-encrusted skin in exchange for access to the choicest moss gardens.

In the deepest chambers, where the strange formations grow, the Keepers perform their most arcane duties. They shape these accretions with infinite patience, guiding their growth through mysterious means—perhaps through the warmth of their bodies, perhaps through secretions from glands that have evolved for this sole purpose. The formations serve some function in the water system's supernatural longevity, though whether as filters, reservoirs, or something stranger, even the Keepers may not fully understand.

Death among them is a gradual mineralization. The eldest sometimes simply slow their movements until they become indistinguishable from the stone, their bodies becoming new formations for water to navigate around. The others honor these transformations with rituals of moistening—keeping the mineralizing forms wet for decades until the transformation is complete.

New Keepers arise through a process they call "the seeping birth." Children—if they ever were children—discovered in the abandoned districts above, drawn by dreams of water and depth. Those who find their way to the cisterns and choose to remain begin their slow adaptation. The process takes generations. First the eyes adjust to the dimness, then the skin to the constant moisture. Eventually, they forget they were ever anything but Keepers, ever anywhere but here.

They are not unhappy. In their glacial way, they experience profound satisfactions—the perfect note of a drop hitting stone, the successful clearing of a centuries-old blockage, the first pale shoot of a new moss colony. They know every whisper of their domain, every trickle and seep. In their minerals-slow consciousness, they dream of the water cycle on a geological scale, seeing themselves as merely another phase of precipitation and flow.

The city above has forgotten them, but they have not forgotten the city. Through the water system, they sense its changes—new constructions that alter drainage patterns, settlements that shift the water table. They adjust their work accordingly, ensuring the ancient cisterns continue their function even as their purpose grows ever more mysterious.

Sometimes, very rarely, someone from above discovers an entrance to the Cistern Quarter. If they stay long enough to let their eyes adjust, they might glimpse a Keeper in the distance—a shape that could be human but moves like water finding its level. The wise ones leave quickly. The unwise ones sometimes stay, beginning their own slow transformation, drawn by the promise of purpose measured in millennia and the strange peace of the ever-weeping stone.
