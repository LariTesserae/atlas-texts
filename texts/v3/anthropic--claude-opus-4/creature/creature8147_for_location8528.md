In the Vapor Gardens, the Tenders move like ghosts through their own exhalations.

They are perhaps human, or were once. Their forms are draped in layers of oiled canvas and rubber, garments that have taken on the characteristics of the garden itself—streaked with algae, pocked with small tears where thorns have caught, permanently damp with condensation that never quite evaporates. Their faces, when glimpsed, are obscured behind masks of brass and glass—not the crude filters of industrial workers, but delicate constructions that seem grown rather than made, their surfaces etched with patterns that mirror the venation of leaves.

The Tenders do not speak. Whether they cannot or simply choose not to is unknown. They communicate through gestures that are both precise and languid—a hand raised to indicate a valve that needs adjustment, fingers tracing the air to show where new growth should be guided. Their movements have the quality of underwater dancing, slow and deliberate, as if the thick air offers resistance to every motion.

Each Tender carries tools that blur the line between gardening and engineering. Pruning shears designed to cut both stem and copper pipe. Watering cans that double as pressure gauges. Their belts hung with vials of nutrient solutions and industrial lubricants, seed packets and spare gaskets, the organic and mechanical given equal reverence. In their wake, they leave subtle adjustments—a valve opened half a turn, a vine redirected to better support a sagging pipe, dead blooms removed with the same care as rust spots treated with protective oils.

They live within the garden's infrastructure, in spaces that exist between the designed and the discovered. Behind certain panels, through maintenance hatches grown over with vines, there are hollows where they have made their quarters. These spaces are lined with the same plants that overwhelm the public garden, but arranged with an intimacy that suggests long cohabitation. Sleeping alcoves curtained with living moss. Collections of salvaged brass fittings arranged like altars. Books—if they can still be called books—their pages so suffused with moisture that the text has bled into abstract patterns, yet still consulted with the reverence of scripture.

The Tenders' bodies have adapted. Their skin, where it can be seen, has a peculiar sheen—not quite perspiration, not quite oil, but something that seems to repel excess moisture while allowing them to move through steam clouds that would scald others. Their fingers are long, joints pronounced, equally suited to delicate botanical work and the manipulation of hot metal valves. Some say they no longer need to eat solid food, that they subsist on nutrient-rich condensation and the occasional flower petal consumed in what might be ritual or simple sustenance.

They know every plant by touch, every pipe by its particular hiss. When a new orchid blooms—one of the engineered varieties whose flowering is so rare it happens perhaps once in a decade—they gather in silence to witness it, standing vigil through the twelve hours of its glory before it collapses into engineered senescence. They mourn corroded pipes that finally fail after centuries of service, marking their removal with the planting of particularly vigorous vines that will eventually obscure the absence.

New Tenders arrive occasionally, though no one sees them enter. They appear one day simply as part of the garden's ecology, already moving with that distinctive underwater grace, already knowing which valves control which sectors, already attuned to the rhythms of steam release and condensation collection. Whether they are trained elsewhere or simply recognize their calling and arrive, learning through observation and absorption, is another mystery the garden keeps.

The oldest Tenders are almost indistinguishable from the garden itself. Their canvas wrappings have taken on the colors of rust and verdigris. Moss grows in the folds of their garments. Their movements have slowed to match the geological patience of the machinery they maintain. One ancient Tender tends only a single sector near the Accumulator, moving perhaps once an hour to adjust a single valve or redirect a single root, spending the rest of the time in absolute stillness that makes them easy to mistake for a peculiarly shaped assemblage of pipes and foliage.

They leave offerings at certain junctions—small arrangements of particularly perfect leaves, vials of collected condensation from specific pipes, flowers placed in the path of steam vents where they will be cooked into fragrant essences. Whether these are devotional practices, practical communications, or simply expressions of aesthetic pleasure is known only to them.

At night—though in the garden's eternal twilight of steam and filtered sunlight, night is more a consensus than a reality—they sometimes gather in the depths beneath the grated floors. Down there, among the primary pipes and ancient boilers, their silhouettes can be glimpsed through the flooring as they perform maintenance that seems equal parts engineering and choreography. The sound of their work rises with the steam—the occasional clang of metal on metal, the whisper of files on brass, the subtle splash of water being redirected, all of it forming a kind of industrial lullaby that the plants above seem to recognize and respond to with minute adjustments of leaf and stem.

The Tenders are the garden's memory and its immune system, its aesthetes and its engineers. They ensure not just that the machinery continues to function and the plants continue to grow, but that the impossible balance between them is maintained—that neither fully conquers the other, that the decay and the growth remain locked in their productive tension, that the garden remains forever both dying and being born.
