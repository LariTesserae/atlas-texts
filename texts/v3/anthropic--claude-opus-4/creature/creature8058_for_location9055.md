The Keepers move through the glass prairies like living contradictions—solid enough to leave tracks in the wet clay, yet translucent as the steam that rises around them. They emerged perhaps a century after the basin cracked open, though emerged isn't quite right. They condensed, maybe. Precipitated. Found the frequency between the obsidian's quantum wells and the moss-analogues' infrared whispers and tuned themselves into existence there.

Each Keeper stands four meters tall when fully extended, though they rarely achieve such heights. They prefer to flow low across the clay, their bodies spreading like heated oil into configurations that maximize contact with the warm, weeping earth. What passes for their flesh is a complex colloid—neither liquid nor solid but something that phases between states according to needs we can only guess at. Their surfaces ripple with the same iridescent sheen as the mercury-like pools, and like those pools, they can hold impossibly still for hours, maintaining standing wave patterns across their skin that seem to encode information in three-dimensional moiré.

They have no fixed anatomy. A Keeper might extrude what could generously be called limbs—temporary extensions that probe into the root networks of glass grass, sampling the stored moisture data through direct molecular interface. These appendages branch and merge like flowing fractals, following mathematical rules that govern both their movement and their thought. Sometimes they'll fashion eye-analogues—concave depressions that collect and focus the strange filtered light—but just as often they navigate by reading thermal gradients through their entire surface, seeing the world as a topology of heat differentials and information flow.

The Keepers tend the glass prairie with movements so slow they border on the geological. One might spend a month positioned around a single cluster of obsidian shoots, its body formed into a perfect ring that regulates the temperature of the clay beneath. They seem to be gardeners, or perhaps midwives, facilitating the impossible thermodynamics that keep this landscape in balance. Through patient manipulation of heat flow and moisture content, they coax new glass grass from the volcanic nodes, guide the growth of moss-analogue curtains, maintain the precise salinity of the collecting pools.

They communicate through the medium they inhabit. A Keeper can introduce subtle chemical signals into the water cycle—complex molecules that ride the evaporation and condensation between glass blade and clay, carrying messages that might take weeks to fully propagate across the basin. These molecular letters overlap and interfere, creating an ever-shifting chemical literature that permeates the humid air. To breathe here is to read their correspondence, though human lungs can parse only the faintest sense of meaning—something between urgency and patience, between question and observation.

When Keepers meet—a rare event given the vast spaces between things—they merge. Not metaphorically, but literally, their colloidal forms flowing together into a single larger entity that pools and eddies with the combined patterns of both beings. In these temporary unions, information cascades between them in ways that bypass language entirely. They share direct molecular experience: the precise mineral content of distant clay deposits, the quantum states of water molecules in specific glass clusters, the bacterial spore-patterns in moss colonies kilometers apart. After hours or days, they separate again, each taking some indefinable portion of the other with them.

Their relationship with time is as fluid as their bodies. A Keeper can enter something like stasis, reducing its metabolism (if that word even applies) until its movements become imperceptible even to time-lapse observation. In this state, they merge partially with the clay itself, becoming a lens of specialized minerals that focuses the basin's slow energies. Years might pass before they reconstitute, carrying forward some subtle adjustment to the local system—a new channel carved in the clay, a shift in the angle of glass grass growth, a change in the chemical composition of a pool.

They build nothing recognizable as structures, yet the entire glass prairie bears evidence of their work. The spacing between obsidian clusters follows patterns too regular for chance but too complex for simple mathematics. The drainage channels form networks that optimize flow in ways that account for rainfall patterns decades in the future. Even the moss-analogues grow in configurations that suggest aesthetic preferences—if aesthetics can exist for beings that see in thermal gradients and think in molecular exchange.

At the deepest pools, where the water glows with that soft pre-dawn bioluminescence, Keepers gather in groups. They don't enter the water but surround it, their bodies formed into complex geometric arrangements that seem to focus or amplify whatever process creates the glow. These gatherings can last for months, the Keepers barely moving except to maintain precise distances from each other and the pool's edge. Sometimes, when they finally disperse, new patterns appear in the hexagonal cracks of the blue-black clay—patterns that persist until the next gathering, gradually fading under the basin's patient entropy.

The youngest Keepers are barely distinguishable from the natural phenomena of the basin—perhaps just a persistent eddy in a warm spring, or a patch of clay that maintains a temperature slightly different from its surroundings. But given decades, these proto-beings accumulate complexity, drawing materials and information from their environment until they achieve true motility. The oldest have grown vast and strange, their bodies incorporating veins of volcanic glass, networks of optical fibers from the moss-analogues, even colonies of engineered bacteria that perform specialized functions within their colloidal matrix.

Death, for Keepers, is less an ending than a change of state. When one reaches whatever endpoint drives them, it doesn't simply cease but disperses—its molecules joining the water cycle, its accumulated information encoding itself into the growth patterns of nearby glass grass, its mineral components enriching the clay. In this way, each dead Keeper becomes part of the landscape it tended, indistinguishable from the natural processes except for subtle improvements in local efficiency, tiny optimizations that accumulate over centuries.

They show no interest in the world beyond the basin's rim. When storms blow in from the normal lands beyond, Keepers retreat into the deepest pools and thickest glass clusters, waiting for familiar conditions to return. They are as much prisoners of this landscape as they are its guardians, limited by the same impossible thermodynamics they help maintain. Or perhaps they simply have no conception of elsewhere—born from this place's unique conditions, they might perceive the normal world as we would perceive hard vacuum: a void where the fundamental prerequisites for existence are absent.

In the morning, when mist rises thickest from the warm clay and the glass grass sweats its stored moisture, you can sometimes spot a dozen Keepers scattered across a single square kilometer. They move like living meditations on the nature of boundaries—between states of matter, between organism and environment, between intention and instinct. They are this place's slow thoughts thinking themselves, its self-regulating dreams flowing patient and purposeful through the wet heat and crystalline emptiness, tending a garden whose ultimate harvest remains forever unclear.
