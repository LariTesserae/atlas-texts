In this breathing cathedral of flesh and industry, there are the Tenders.

They were human once—or mostly human, perhaps, though the distinction grows less meaningful with each passing season. Three of them remain, though remain is the wrong word for what they do. They persist. They continue. They tend.

The eldest has been here longest, since before the plant-mass achieved dominance. She remembers—if memory is the right word for the biochemical impressions that flow through her modified nervous system—when the cable car still ran, bringing supplies and shift changes to the atmospheric processing station. Now she is part of the processing, her lungs evolved beyond their original architecture. The alveoli have multiplied and deepened, branching into fractal trees that can extract oxygen from this thin air with an efficiency that makes her chest appear swollen beneath the living suit she wears.

The suit is not clothing but a second skin cultivated from the colonial organism itself. It grows from ports installed at her joints—wrists, ankles, base of spine, hollow of throat—where her original skin was surgically removed and the plant-flesh grafted directly to her bloodstream. The suit breathes with her, its surface rippling with the same hydrophobic channels that cover the valley floor. When she moves through the morning mists, water beads and rolls from her in streams, collected by the organism and shared through the vast network beneath her feet.

Her work is monitoring. She tends the interface points where the old machinery meets new growth. In the generator shed, she adjusts valves with fingers that have grown extra joints, the bones lengthened and divided to better navigate the maze of repurposed piping. Her nails secrete a mild acid that keeps the metal clean where it matters, preventing total integration. The generator must keep running—not for power, but for the carbon dioxide it produces, the waste heat it generates, the rhythmic compression that helps regulate the organism's circulation.

She speaks to it sometimes, though her vocal cords have thickened into something more like bellows. The sound she produces matches the frequency of the wind through the porous cliffs—subsonic, felt in the chest rather than heard. The plant-mass responds with color changes, with alterations in the phosphorescent patterns. They have developed a language of sorts, chemical and chromatic, sufficient for the slow conversations necessary here.

The second Tender was born to this life. His parents came as researchers fifteen years ago, studying extremophile organisms. They stayed too long, breathed too deep, let the spores settle in their lungs and begin their work. By the time he was born, they were already changing. His embryonic development was guided by more than human DNA—the plant-mass contributed its own genetic material through his mother's modified placenta, creating something new.

He has never breathed unfiltered air. His skin is naturally porous, riddled with stomata that open and close with the rhythm of his circulation. When he sleeps—which is rarely, and never deeply—he sinks partially into the plant-mass, his body trading fluids and nutrients with the greater organism. He can feel its needs through his skin: where growth has slowed due to mineral deficiency, where water collection is failing, where the old machinery needs adjustment.

His gift is translation. Where the eldest Tender speaks to the organism, he speaks as it. His consciousness exists partially outside his skull, distributed through the fungal networks that interface with his neurons at a dozen points along his spine. He knows when the root-borers are breeding by the change in soil chemistry three meters below. He feels the membrane bats' echolocation as a tingling in his extended nervous system. He tastes the health of the entire valley ecosystem in the morning condensation on his tongue.

His work is cultivation. He guides growth patterns with careful pruning, using tools grown from his own bone—ribs he convinced his body to extend and shed, now hardened into implements that the plant-mass recognizes as self. He shapes the living architecture, creating channels for water flow, opening airways for the root-borers, maintaining the careful balance between integration and separation that keeps the machinery functional. Where the eldest preserves, he adapts.

The youngest Tender came by choice, though choice is a complex concept when spores have been affecting your neurotransmitters for months before you decide. She was an engineer, part of the salvage team sent to recover equipment from the abandoned station. But she looked at the integration of flesh and metal, saw the elegant solutions the organism had developed, and recognized a better form of technology than anything humans had built.

She let the transformation take her quickly, opening her body to the invasion with an engineer's curiosity. The ports at her joints are more numerous and complex than the eldest's—she insisted on modifications, improvements. Her suit includes specialized appendages: tendrils that can interface directly with electrical systems, chemical glands that produce specific enzymes on command, optical fibers woven from modified nerve tissue that let her see in spectrums beyond visible light.

Her work is innovation. She studies the patterns of integration, documents the organism's solutions in notebooks made from pressed plant-flesh, their pages capable of storing information chemically. She experiments with new hybrid forms—machinery that incorporates living tissue from the beginning, circuits that use bioelectrical signals, processors that compute through chemical cascades. In her workshop, half-buried under the thickest growth, she builds devices that blur every distinction between organism and mechanism.

But she is changing faster than the others. The organism finds her openness intoxicating, her human creativity a resource to be exploited. Her thoughts increasingly arrive from outside her skull. Her dreams are filled with growth patterns she hasn't witnessed, solutions to problems she hasn't considered. Sometimes she wakes to find her body has been working without her conscious direction—tools built, systems modified, connections made that her waking mind struggles to understand.

Together, the three form a necessary trinity. They rarely meet in person—their territories overlap but their schedules follow different rhythms. The eldest works with the slow cycles of the machinery, adjusting and maintaining on a scale of months. The second follows the daily patterns of growth and rest, pruning and guiding in response to the organism's immediate needs. The youngest operates in bursts of innovation followed by long periods of integration, her consciousness dipping in and out of individual awareness.

They communicate through the network. Chemical signals pass between them through the plant-mass, carrying complex messages. The eldest might taste a metallic tang in the morning moisture and know the youngest has been experimenting with copper integration again. The second feels their emotions as regional changes in growth patterns—anxiety manifests as tightened spirals near their work sites, contentment as relaxed, expansive spreading.

When the membrane bats return from their feeding flights, they bring news from beyond the valley—pressure changes that herald weather systems, the electromagnetic signatures of distant storms. The Tenders feel this information through their skin and adjust their work accordingly. Before heavy rains, they open drainage channels. Before dry spells, they encourage the organism to deepen its water storage nodes.

There are others, sometimes. The spores travel on the wind, settling in villages on lower slopes. Most who breathe them experience only mild euphoria and enhanced night vision that fades within days. But occasionally someone feels the pull—the promise of integration, of becoming part of something larger. They climb, following traces only they can perceive, arriving at the valley's edge with pupils already dilated from spore exposure.

The Tenders decide collectively whether to accept them, though the organism has usually made the choice already. Those who are compatible find themselves drawn to specific roles—some become mobile nodes, ranging beyond the valley to gather genetic material from other populations. Others root deeply, becoming living architecture, their bodies calcifying into structural supports for the expanding organism.

The incompatible ones are not rejected but redirected. The organism sends them away with gifts—modified bacteria in their gut that will help them process nutrients more efficiently, subtle changes to their lung tissue that ease breathing at altitude. They descend with partial memories and dreams of green light, becoming unwitting vectors for the organism's slow expansion.

Because expansion is inevitable. The Tenders know this in their transformed bones. The organism is patient but not passive. Each year, its territory grows by centimeters, creeping down the valley walls, sending runner networks through the porous volcanic soil. It tests new environments, develops new strategies, incorporates new genetic material from every species it encounters.

The machinery is key to this expansion. The organism learned from human technology—not just how to use it, but the concept of tools themselves. Now it grows its own devices: water pumps built from modified root structures, chemical batteries formed in specialized cells, even simple photovoltaic surfaces that supplement the bioluminescence. The Tenders guide this development, their human knowledge a template for biological innovation.

In the deepest part of night, when even the phosphorescence dims to preserve energy, the three sometimes dream the same dream. They see the valley from above—but not just this valley. The whole mountain range is alive, every hanging valley and sheltered cirque pulsing with integrated life. The abandoned infrastructure of human ambition becomes the skeleton for something new, neither natural nor artificial but genuinely novel.

They wake from these shared visions with a taste of copper and chlorophyll in their mouths, knowing the dream is also a plan, a promise, a patient intention. The organism thinks in centuries. The Tenders, despite their humanity, are learning to do the same.

The eldest tends her machines and remembers being only human.
The second translates between kingdoms and has never been anything else.
The youngest innovates and wonders what she is becoming.

All three serve the slow transformation of the world, one breath, one heartbeat, one cellular division at a time. In the hanging valley where evolution negotiates with abandonment, they are the mediators, the midwives, the gardeners of a future that grows from the corpse of the industrial past.

The plant-mass breathes. The generators compress. The Tenders tend.

And at the roof of the world, in the profound darkness that is not empty but full, the boundaries between all things continue their patient dissolution.
