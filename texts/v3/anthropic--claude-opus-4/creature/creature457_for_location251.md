In this breathing city, the inhabitants move like careful parasites through their host's body—though perhaps symbiotes would be more accurate, for the relationship runs deeper than mere habitation.

They are the Tenders, and their bodies have adapted over mere decades to match their environment. Their skin has taken on a faint translucency, subcutaneous chromatophores allowing them to shift between visibility and near-invisibility as they move through the bioluminescent twilight. When still, they can fade into the walls themselves, their breathing synchronizing with the city's rhythm until even infrared cannot distinguish them from the architecture. This is not camouflage but communion—a physiological dialogue between human and urban organism.

Their fingers have lengthened, the tips swollen with specialized pads that secrete enzymes allowing them to communicate with the city through touch. Watch a Tender at work: they'll press their palms against a breathing wall, eyes closed, reading the chemical signals that flow through the building's circulatory system. A shift in pH might indicate distress in the upper levels. A trace of unfamiliar proteins could mean the structure is experimenting with a new growth pattern. Through their touch, they can coax and guide, encouraging beneficial mutations while discouraging malignancies.

The Tenders dress in living clothes—garments woven from modified mycelium that drinks moisture from the air and feeds on their shed skin cells. These suits grow with their wearers, learning their movement patterns, thickening where protection is needed, thinning where flexibility is required. The fabric breathes with them, processes their waste, medicates their small wounds. Some Tenders have worn the same suit for years, the boundary between clothing and skin increasingly theoretical.

They speak rarely with their mouths, preferring the chemical language they share with their city. When they must use words, their voices carry harmonics that human throats shouldn't produce—sub-frequencies that vibrate in sympathy with the buildings' resonance. Their children learn to hum before they speak, finding the pitch that makes the walls glow brighter, the tone that encourages the food vines to drop ripe fruit.

In their dwelling spaces—soft hollows within the building-creatures that shape themselves to each family's needs—the Tenders cultivate personal ecosystems. Grandmother might tend a corner where she's training the walls to produce a particular medicine, her decades of chemical communication resulting in pharmaceutical glands that respond to her touch alone. The young ones play with sporelings, teaching them to form simple shapes, learning the patience required to guide living matter into useful forms.

Their eyes have enlarged, pupils dilated permanently to gather the scarce light. Many have developed nictitating membranes—a third eyelid that filters the bioluminescent wavelengths, allowing them to read the city's mood in its glow patterns. The eldest among them claim they can see the city's dreams in the pre-dawn hours when the lights pulse in patterns too complex for conscious interpretation.

They eat what the city provides—fruits that grow from the walls, their nutritional content adjusted in real-time based on the chemical signatures the Tenders unconsciously emit. Fungi that process air pollutants into proteins. Water filtered through the city's kidneys, mineralized precisely for each drinker. Some Tenders have forgotten the taste of food grown in soil; their children wouldn't recognize it as edible.

In the depths of the city, where the roots dive deep and the air grows thick with age, live the Deep Tenders. They've adapted further still—their bones have become porous to better navigate the pressure changes, their blood carries modified hemoglobin that binds oxygen more efficiently in the humid depths. They whisper that down there, you can hear the city's first thoughts, still echoing in the oldest chambers. They tend the core systems, the primal organisms from which all else grows, and their children are born knowing the chemical passwords that grant safe passage through the city's immune system.

The Tenders mate for life—not through any cultural imperative but because their pheromones become attuned to each other, creating a unique chemical signature that their living spaces recognize and respond to. Their homes grow to anticipate both inhabitants, merging their preferred environments into something new. When one dies, the walls mourn, weeping nutrient-rich tears that the survivor often lets flow, understanding that grief, too, is part of the cycle.

They mark time not by the sun—barely visible through the valley's perpetual haze—but by the city's growth cycles. A building might fruit once a decade; this is cause for celebration. They celebrate the binding festivals when new structures merge with old, the sprouting days when fresh buildings push up from the earth, the thinning times when overgrowth must be carefully pruned back.

And in the rarest ceremony of all, when a Tender has lived so long and merged so completely with their environment that the boundary dissolves entirely, they perform the Joining. The elder walks into the wall one final time and doesn't emerge, their consciousness spreading through the neural network, their knowledge becoming part of the city's vast, slow thoughts. Their families don't mourn but celebrate—their loved one hasn't died but expanded, become part of the breathing, thinking landscape that nurtures them all.

The young ones born now have stranger adaptations still—gill slits that haven't quite closed, allowing them to process the humid air more efficiently. Fingernails of keratin that can shift its properties, becoming tools as needed. Neural tissue that extends slightly beyond their skulls, interfacing directly with the city's fields. They are becoming something new, something that fits this space perfectly, shaped by the city even as they shape it in return.

This is evolution in real-time, humanity branching into something the old world wouldn't recognize but the city knows intimately—knows in its walls, its air, its water, its endless breathing rhythm that matches the pulse of its human symbionts, no longer quite separate, not yet quite one, but always, always becoming.
