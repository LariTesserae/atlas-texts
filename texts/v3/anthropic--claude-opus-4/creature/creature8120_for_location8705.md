The Undergrowth dwells here—not a single species but a collective intelligence woven from ten thousand parts.

At its most visible layer are the Gardeners: creatures of deliberate slowness, their bodies a fusion of calcified plant matter and something almost mammalian. They stand seven feet tall when fully extended, but more often move hunched, their four primary limbs ending in digits that branch and re-branch like roots. Their skin is translucent bark that weeps a clear sap, and beneath it, you can see the slow pulse of chlorophyll-tinted blood. They have no eyes—instead, photosensitive patches marble their skin like age spots, allowing them to read the quality of light through the ice above.

The Gardeners tend the pale forest with infinite patience. They prune with their branching fingers, transplant seedlings, guide water flow with subtle adjustments to root systems. They communicate through chemical exudations—emotions and concepts released as pheromone clouds that other Gardeners can taste through porous patches on their wrists and throats. When several gather, the air grows thick with the scent of their conversation: wet earth for contentment, ozone for alarm, honey for the discovery of something new growing.

But the Gardeners are only the most obvious inhabitants. Within the walls, the Archivists work. These are colonies of fungal intelligence that have grown into the very mortar of the ruins. They appear as veining patterns of silver and gold spreading across stone surfaces, but this is merely their fruiting body. The bulk of their mass exists as networks threading through the buildings' bones. The Archivists remember—they've been absorbing the stone's memories since before the ice came. Through chemical analysis of the walls they inhabit, they can taste the history of hands that touched these stones, feet that walked these floors. They trade these memories with the Gardeners in exchange for nutrients, encoding centuries of experience in spore-packets that drift like snow through the underground air.

The waterways host the Listeners: serpentine creatures that might once have been eels before isolation and adaptation stretched them into something new. Their bodies are transparent ribbons up to twenty feet long, with organs visible as darker suggestions within their glass flesh. They have no scales—instead, their skin is covered in microscopic cilia that detect vibrations through water with extraordinary precision. The Listeners map the entire underground watershed through sound, knowing every drip, every flow, every splash. They sing to each other in frequencies that travel for miles through water, sharing the musical map of their world.

High in the canopy lives the Parliament—the bat-bird creatures glimpsed in periphery. They're covered in feathers that are somewhere between down and fur, each filament capable of independent movement. This allows them to fly in perfect silence and reshape their silhouettes at will. The Parliament sees everything from above, their enormous eyes adapted to gather every photon of the weak light. They have eidetic memory and share it communally—when one dies, the others perform a ritual consumption that preserves its memories within the flock. The oldest among them carry centuries of accumulated observation, making them the underground's historians.

But perhaps most remarkable are the Oscillators—creatures that exist partially outside normal time. They appear as shimmers in the air, like heat mirages in a place that has never known true heat. The other inhabitants have learned to recognize the subtle distortions that mark an Oscillator's presence: the way water drops seem to hover momentarily, how moss grows in perfect spirals around certain spots. The Oscillators feed on temporal differentials, clustering around places where time moves differently—near the ancient tree that consumed the temple, around certain carved symbols that still hold power, along ley lines where the city's original magic hasn't quite faded. They experience past and future as a smear, occasionally manifesting physically as crystalline insects or small, darting fish before dissolving back into temporal flux.

All these species are bound together by the Network—the visible fungal threads that pulse with bioluminescent signals. But the Network is more than infrastructure; it's a living entity in itself, a neural system for the entire underground ecosystem. It processes information from every connected organism, mediating between species, optimizing resource distribution, even generating new hybridizations when the ecosystem requires innovation. The Network dreams, and in its dreams, new organisms gestate in specialized pods before being born into the twilight world.

Together, they form the Undergrowth—a civilization without hierarchy, a city where every inhabitant is both citizen and architecture. They've learned that survival in the deep cold requires more than individual adaptation; it demands a collective becoming, a willingness to merge and flow and change as circumstances require. The boundaries between species blur here, individuals joining the Network for days or decades before emerging transformed.

They know about the world above the ice—the Listeners hear its vibrations, the Parliament's eldest remember the time before the burial, the Archivists taste the history of warmer days in the stones. But they don't mourn it. They're busy becoming something the sunlit world could never produce: a living city that dreams in the dark, growing stranger and more magnificent with each passing century.

When outsiders descend through the new fissure, the Undergrowth watches with patient interest. The Gardeners might approach, offering gifts of luminous fruit that grant temporary ability to see the Network's communications. The Parliament circles overhead, memorizing every detail of these sun-touched visitors. The Oscillators flutter at the edges of perception, occasionally showing glimpses of the visitors' possible futures in this place—some leaving quickly, some joining the Network, some becoming something entirely new.

They don't hide from visitors, but they don't seek them out either. The Undergrowth has learned the deep patience of stone and ice. They know that everything that enters their realm eventually faces a choice: leave and forget, or stay and transform. There is no middle ground in the breathing city beneath the ice. Here, you either remain yourself or become part of something larger—another note in the endless whisper-song of growth and change and collective becoming.
