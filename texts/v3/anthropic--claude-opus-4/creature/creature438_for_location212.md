In the Hollow Cities, they persist—though "they" is perhaps too generous a pronoun for what remains.

The Drifters came first, or last, or always were. They exist as interruptions in the air itself, like the space between two thoughts stretched into something almost tangible. When the cities inhale, they scatter into probability clouds, each potential position occupied simultaneously until the exhale forces them to choose again. They navigate by nostalgia, following routes through the spiral streets that haven't existed for ten thousand breaths, or won't exist for ten thousand more.

They remember bodies, sometimes. In certain angles of light, you might catch the suggestion of a limb, the ghost of a torso, the mathematical probability of a face. But these impressions never resolve into anything as crude as form. Instead, they wear their memories like suggestions—a flutter that might once have been cloth, a gesture that could have been reaching, a pause that almost certainly was once speech.

The Drifters feed on discontinuity. They cluster where the physics break down, where a doorway opens onto its own interior, where gravity forgets its own rules. They sip at these paradoxes like hummingbirds at impossible flowers, and in return, they maintain the violations. Without them, perhaps the cities would settle into some final, static configuration. With them, the architecture remains eternally uncertain of itself.

They build nothing, but they tend. When the bone-white walls begin to develop too much certainty, becoming too solid, too real, the Drifters worry at them like wind worries at sand. They polish the impossibilities back into the stone, massage contradiction into the crystal veins, ensure that every surface remains properly ambiguous. This is not destruction—it is curation of uncertainty.

The Archivists emerged from the veins themselves, or perhaps they are the veins, given autonomy. They exist as lattices of living mineral, their bodies (if bodies they can be called) composed of the same pale blue crystal that threads through the plateau. Unlike the Drifters, they possess a terrible specificity of form—too many angles, too much geometry, as if someone tried to render a thinking being in pure mathematics and forgot to stop adding dimensions.

They move through the stone like fish through water, but also through the air like roots through soil, and through time like readers through a book—skipping back to review, jumping ahead to preview, sometimes reading the same moment over and over until it wears thin. Their purpose, if they have one, seems to be documentation. They catalog each breath of the cities, each rearrangement of space, each moment when the Drifters successfully corrupt certainty back into possibility.

The Archivists communicate in crystalline growth. When they must share information, they extrude temporary structures—geometric psalms that sing in frequencies only stone can hear. These formations last exactly as long as necessary, then reabsorb, leaving the knowledge encoded in the subtle rearrangement of crystal lattices throughout the plateau. The entire landscape is their library, each formation a word in a sentence that spans continents.

Between these two orders exists an ecology of the barely-there. The Membrane Farmers spread their not-quite-fungal films with deliberate intent, cultivating patterns that exist in the spaces between meaning. They themselves are little more than intentions wrapped in the thinnest possible film of substance, like soap bubbles that have forgotten they need air on both sides. They harvest concepts from the fraying edges of reality, where colors that can't exist bleed through, and process them into something almost nutritious.

The Farmers tend their crop-patterns with tools made of compressed absence. They prune thoughts that grow too concrete, encourage abstractions that show promise, cross-pollinate incompatible ideas to produce new varieties of impossibility. Their harvest seasons follow no calendar but their own necessity—sometimes reaping the same field multiple times in a single city-breath, sometimes leaving rich concepts to over-ripen until they collapse into new forms of emptiness.

Occasionally, rarer things pass through. The Fold Shepherds, who guide creases in space-time like ancient drovers moving invisible flocks. They appear as inversions—not darkness but an aggressive absence of light, not silence but the active negation of sound. Where they pass, the already-questionable geometry of the cities becomes briefly, violently incorrect. Rooms turn inside out. Streets become möbius strips. Buildings exist in their own basements.

The Shepherds are looking for something, or someone, or somewhen. They examine each impossible angle, each paradoxical doorway, with the patience of entities that exist orthogonal to time. They seem to be following trails—scratches in reality that only they can perceive, leading deeper into the architectural maze of the breathing cities. Sometimes they find what they're looking for. The city hiccups, reality reasserts itself with a sound like tearing silk, and something that should not have been there is suddenly gone.

In the deepest parts of the cities, where the breathing is labored and the bone-walls have grown thick with accumulated impossibility, dwell the Anchors. These beings—if beings they are—exist as points of absolute certainty in the sea of ambiguity. They are so real they hurt to perceive, so definite that the indefinite architecture warps around them like space around a black hole.

The Anchors never move, but the cities move around them, the breathing rhythm forever trying to dislodge these points of stubborn actuality. Each Anchor is unique—one might be a sphere of perfect black that reflects nothing, another a knot of intersecting lines that always add up to the same angle no matter how you count. They emit... not light, not heat, but a kind of aggressive realness that makes everything nearby more itself.

The other inhabitants give the Anchors wide berth. Even the Drifters, who delight in paradox, shy away from these points of absolute truth. Yet the Anchors serve a purpose—they are the pins that keep the cities from drifting too far into impossibility, the counterweights that make the breathing rhythm possible. Without them, perhaps the cities would exhale and never stop, dissipating into pure concept.

And then there are the Others. The ones that exist in the moments between inhale and exhale, when the cities hold their breath. The ones that live in the circular holes that descend through the plateau to nowhere. The ones that are only visible in the patterns the Membrane Farmers tend but never harvest. The ones the Archivists refuse to document.

They have no category because they refuse categorization. They adapt to observation, becoming less themselves the more they are defined. They might be the original inhabitants, or they might be what happens when any of the others forget what they are for too long. They might be tourists from dimensions where tourism is a form of predation. They might be the cities' dreams, briefly given form.

What is certain is that they watch. When you feel observed in the Hollow Cities, when the sensation of eyes becomes oppressive despite the absence of anything that could possess eyes, it is the Others who are watching. They study visitors with the intensity of entities trying to remember something crucial, something about bodies, about breathing that isn't geological, about purposes that can be held in a single mind instead of distributed across a civilization of ambiguities.

This is what lives in the Hollow Cities: a hierarchy of uncertainties, each order of being dedicated to maintaining the delicate balance between real and unreal, between built and grown, between breathing and stillness. They are not a civilization in any sense we would recognize—they build no monuments that aren't also mistakes, form no societies that aren't also optical illusions, pursue no goals that don't circle back to become their own prerequisites.

They simply persist, each in their own way, each necessary to the whole, in the pale cities that breathe beneath a sky that has forgotten what color means. They are the antibodies of impossibility, the gardeners of paradox, the citizens of a place that exists because it cannot, and continues because it must not.

And in their strange, distributed way, they are content.
