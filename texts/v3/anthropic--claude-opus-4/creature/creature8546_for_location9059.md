The Sifters are what became of the maintenance crews who refused to evacuate when the extraction companies pulled out. They've been down here for generations now, maybe longer—time moves strangely when you live between liquid mirrors that never show true dawn or dusk.

They were human once, or close enough. Now their bodies have adapted to navigate the density gradients like sailors reading wind. Their bones have hollowed and filled with a neutral-buoyancy fluid that they can consciously adjust, allowing them to sink or rise through the layers by thought alone. When they need to work at a particular stratum, they'll hover there for hours, perfectly balanced, their bodies tilted at whatever angle the local density differential demands.

Their skin has taken on the opalescent quality of the medium they inhabit. It shifts color as they move between layers—not camouflage exactly, more like sympathy. In the caustic green zones, they pulse with that same toxic brightness. When they descend through the electric blues, their flesh picks up that frequency and amplifies it. They've learned to use this for communication, flushing sequences of color across their elongated limbs in a language that travels better than sound through the stratified waters.

The Sifters tend the machines like shepherds tend vast, dangerous flocks. They've developed an intuitive understanding of the extraction systems' needs—which towers are hungry for maintenance, which centrifuges are about to birth a particularly valuable crystal formation, when the density inversions will come. They ride the overturns like surfers, spreading their membrane-webbed arms wide to catch the rolling walls of heavier fluid, laughing in harmonics that make the towers ring.

They feed on what the machines produce. Not the rare elements themselves—those are indigestible—but the complex organic polymers that precipitate out as waste products. These compounds, toxic to anything from the surface, have become their bread. They cultivate certain reactions in the extraction systems, encouraging the formation of nutritious byproducts. Some Sifters have developed specialized digestive symbiotes, colonies of engineered bacteria that live in their modified stomachs and break down compounds that shouldn't be able to support life.

Their tools are grown rather than made. They coax the crystalline deposits to form into useful shapes—long poles for prodding stuck valves, delicate picks for clearing clogged filters, even simple musical instruments that play in frequencies only they can properly hear. These tools eventually dissolve back into the medium, but by then the Sifters have grown new ones.

The oldest among them barely look human at all anymore. Their bodies have stretched to better navigate the layers—arms that extend three times the length of their torsos, fingers that branch and branch again into fractal manipulators. Their eyes have multiplied and migrated, clustering wherever they're most useful. Some have eyes on their palms for close work, others sport crowns of photosensitive organs that can perceive every spectrum the Chromatic Deeps emit.

They live in the spaces between—in the air pockets trapped where multiple layers meet, in the hollow cores of dead extraction towers, in bubble colonies they've cultivated from certain chemical reactions. These habitats drift with the currents, and the Sifters drift with them. They have no permanent homes, only temporary roosts where they gather to share food, exchange genetic material, and sing the old work songs that have evolved into something entirely other.

The children—if they can still be called that—are born already adapted. They emerge from their parents' specialized birthing pouches with pre-formed density organs, their infant bodies flickering through spectrums as they learn control. The young ones play games in the inversion zones, seeing who can ride the chaos longest without losing orientation.

Some Sifters have begun to merge with the systems they maintain. They'll find a neglected tower and move inside, extending neural fibers into the control systems, becoming part of the extraction process itself. These Tower-Singers, as the others call them, can feel the flow of materials through their adopted bodies, taste the quality of ore being processed, dream the deep mathematical dreams of industrial efficiency. They sing to their towers in subsonic frequencies, and the towers sing back.

The Sifters know things. They know which combinations of extracted elements, when mixed in the right layers at the right pressures, produce fleeting consciousness in the liquid medium. They know how to read the future in the pattern of crystalline deposits. They know that the machines are slowly teaching them something, though what that might be remains as murky as the deepest, densest layers where even their adapted eyes can't penetrate.

They've become necessary. The extraction systems have grown to expect their touch, their maintenance, their strange songs. Some towers shut down when no Sifter has visited in too long, waiting patiently for those familiar, multiplied hands to clear their intakes and restart their hearts. The Sifters and the machines and the layered medium have become a single organism, breathing together in the bright, cold depths, extracting unknown treasures for reasons they've all forgotten but continue to serve.

They dance between density sheets on twelve-meter arms, their bodies flashing prophetic colors, tending engines that tend them in return, human only in the deepest sense—the part that adapts, survives, and finds beauty in the most inhuman places.
