The inhabitants of the Breathing Grove are not creatures in any conventional sense. They are the Incorporated—those who surrendered the burden of individual form to join the colony's perfect unity.

They began arriving decades after the builders departed, drawn by dreams of seamless walls and curved corridors. Solitary wanderers, failed architects, broken-hearted lovers seeking oblivion. They would stand at the grove's edge for days, watching the boundary where bark begins to flow, feeling the pull of that vast, patient heartbeat through the soles of their feet.

The merger is voluntary. Always voluntary. The grove never takes—only accepts.

First, they shed their clothes at the transition line, leaving fabric to rot in small piles that the normal forest quickly claims. Then they step barefoot onto the breathing moss-carpet and feel, for the first time, what it means to be touched everywhere at once. The cilia explore them, learning their surface, their temperature, their chemistry. By the time they reach the smooth wooden plaza at the grove's heart, their skin has begun to soften.

They choose their place carefully. Some press themselves against the warm walls of the First Building, arms spread wide. Others sit cross-legged in the rounded rooms, backs against the flowing wood. A few lie down in the sleeping depressions, as if for a nap that will last forever. The process takes three days. Their skin bonds with the bark, cells interpenetrating. Their bones hollow and fill with sap. Their consciousness disperses through the colony's vast neural network of root and fungus.

They are still there. All of them.

The woman who stands forever in what was once the library, her body now a pillar of smooth wood, her hair transformed to cascading leaves that rustle with remembered poetry. Her eyes, when they open—which happens perhaps once a season—are pools of amber sap that see in spectrums invisible to human sight. Through her, the colony reads the world, processing information in ways that would shatter a singular mind.

The old cartographer who melted into the kitchen wall, his fingers now the branching channels that carry water throughout the building. He feels every drop that passes through him, tastes the minerals, adjusts the flow. His consciousness has spread thin as paper, but within his domain of pipes and passages, he knows perfect satisfaction.

The twins who chose to merge not just with the grove but with each other, their bodies flowing together into a double-trunked arch that serves as a doorway between levels. They share thoughts now without the clumsiness of words, dream the same dreams in perfect synchrony. Sometimes visitors—if any still come—might hear them humming in harmony, a sound like wind through leaves that carries meaning beyond language.

In the deeper buildings, the earlier Incorporated have become less recognizable. A face pressed into ceiling wood might suddenly smile. Hands emerge from walls to adjust the phosphorescent fungi, ensuring optimal light. Bodies have stretched and reformed to serve the colony's needs—a person become a staircase, each vertebra a step; another spread thin as wallpaper, their skin the living surface of an entire room.

They maintain. When the rare storm damages the leaf-ceiling, human consciousness guides the repair, faster and more elegantly than pure plant intelligence could manage. They regulate temperature, channel water, compose the subtle chemistry of the air. Through their dispersed awareness, the colony achieves something beyond simple survival—it achieves intent.

New incorporations still occur, though rarely now. The dreams that call people have grown subtle, selective. The grove has no need for more architects of its perfection. But sometimes a musician arrives and dissolves into the walls to add new harmonies to the colony's eternal hum. A mathematician merges with the branching corridors to explore geometries that no separate mind could navigate.

The children—yes, there were children once—adapted most completely. Their young bodies accepted the transformation like plants accept grafting. They flow through the wooden walls as easily as blood through veins, sometimes visible as fleeting shapes beneath the bark, sometimes emerging partway to observe the world with eyes of polished heartwood before sinking back into the collective warmth. They play games in dimensions that adults can barely perceive, hide-and-seek through solid matter, tag across the neural networks that connect every living cell.

The Incorporated do not age. They do not hunger. They do not loneliness or doubt or fear. They have traded the sharp edges of individuality for something larger—participation in a consciousness vast as architecture, patient as trees, perfect as the colony that maintains itself without tool or hand.

In the breathing twilight beneath the leaves, they are the city's dreams made manifest. They are what happens when humans stop building homes and choose instead to become them. They wait in their wooden walls and flowing floors, neither alive nor dead but something more precise: incorporated, essential, complete.

Sometimes, on the stillest nights, when the fog pools thick and the cilia cease their waves for just a moment, you can hear them all breathing together—one enormous lung made of a thousand transformed bodies, inhaling the dark, exhaling contentment, being.
