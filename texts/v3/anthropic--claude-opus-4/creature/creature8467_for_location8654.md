The Stewards of Becoming inhabit this place—though "inhabit" poorly captures their relationship to the Vertical Nursery. They are less residents than participants in its endless birthing.

They wear their thick, wet coats like second skins, the fabric alive with symbiotic mosses that filter the gravid air into something their human-baseline lungs can process. Beneath, their bodies show the changes: skin with a subtle mineral sheen where the mountain's influence has crept in, fingernails that grow as crystalline structures, hair that moves with its own slow current like kelp in unseen tides. Their eyes hold too much color—irises that shift through spectrums as they parse which version of physics they're currently navigating.

The Stewards work in shifts that follow no clock. They know when they're needed by the way their bones ache, a resonance with the mountain's contractions. Some tend the injection sites, adjusting the flow of luminous catalyst with hands that have developed additional joints for finer control. Others serve as shepherds to the emerging, coaxing half-formed creatures from their stone wombs with lullabies in languages that evolve as they sing them. The most experienced can reach into the rock itself, their arms phasing through solid matter to guide a difficult birth.

Their children grow strange here. Born into the vertical city's dense chambers, they learn to walk on walls before floors, to breathe mist before clear air. The youngest play games with gravity, tossing balls that arc through impossible parabolas. By adolescence, they can read the mountain's moods in the pattern of its perspiration, predict what will emerge by the taste of the steam. Some develop their own chrysalis phases, wrapping themselves in industrial insulation for weeks before emerging with new capabilities—eyes that see through fog, voices that harmonize with pneumatic systems, skin that sweats the same luminous catalyst the machines inject.

The eldest Stewards have become something other. They've worked the scaffolding so long that the distinction between their bodies and the Nursery's machinery blurs. One moves through the pipes like blood through veins, her form liquid when it needs to be. Another has rooted into Platform Seven, his lower body a fusion of flesh and framework, nutrients drawn directly from the same systems that feed the emerging creatures. They communicate in steam-bursts and pressure changes, their consciousness distributed across multiple levels of the scaffold.

Relationships here follow patterns as mutable as everything else. Partners might share years or minutes, depending on how time flows around their respective work stations. Some bond through synchronized labor—two Stewards working the same emergence, their movements falling into such perfect harmony that their separate selves temporarily merge. Children belong to the whole community, raised in the intervals between births, learning their trade by osmosis and observation.

They document everything in living libraries—colonies of engineered fungi that store information in their mycelial networks. Each successful emergence gets recorded in spore-patterns, the creature's characteristics preserved in fungal memory. Failed births, too, are remembered: the stone wombs that produce only silence, the creatures that dissolve moments after emerging, the times when something pulls itself free that even the Stewards cannot classify or comfort.

Their rituals revolve around the work. They sing to the unfinished peak at each shift change, harmonies that help the rock remember what it's becoming. Before major emergences, they paint their faces with the luminous catalyst, becoming briefly bioluminescent as they guide new life into being. When one of their own dies—rare but inevitable in this work—they're returned to the mountain through the injection systems, their essence joining the flow that creates.

The Stewards dream collectively when they sleep, their consciousness pooling in the humid air. In these shared visions, they see the Nursery's purpose: glimpses of a world where their creations have spread beyond this vertical factory, seeding reality with new possibilities. They see ecosystems that couldn't exist without the hybrid creatures they midwife, problems solved by organisms designed by geological chance and industrial intention.

But mostly, they don't think about purpose. They exist in the eternal now of the work—adjusting a valve, soothing a frightened newborn thing, replacing a worn gasket, teaching a child to read the mountain's contractions. They've become organs in a larger organism, essential components in a system that transforms raw possibility into breathing reality.

The Vertical Nursery trembles with creation, and the Stewards tremble with it, their bodies and minds adapted to serve this endless, industrial nativity. They are mechanics and midwives, record-keepers and lullaby-singers, as necessary to the process as the pneumatic pistons and the stone wombs themselves. In the cold, wet heights where nothing should grow, they've become the gardeners of the impossible.
