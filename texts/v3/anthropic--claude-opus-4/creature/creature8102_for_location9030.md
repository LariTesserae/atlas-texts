The Cultivars move through the Undercanopy like thoughts through a dreaming mind—slow, branching, never quite arriving where they began. They are what remains of those who descended and chose to stay, or perhaps what the forest chose to keep.

You recognize them first by the way the plants respond. The vegetation doesn't part for them but *through* them, as if they occupy the same space differently. A Cultivar might stand before you, and you'd see them as a density of intention rather than flesh—a place where the forest has learned to think in something approaching human patterns.

Their bodies, if bodies they can still be called, are collaborations. Where skin once was, networks of fungal threads weave through transparent cellulose, creating surfaces that photosynthesize and breathe in equal measure. Their bones have been replaced by chambers of hardened wood that spiral through their forms, following grains that shift with mood and memory. Hair has become specialized growing tips that sample the chemical signals flowing through the forest's vast nervous system.

They communicate through pheromone cascades that ripple outward from their positions, messages that take hours or days to fully unfold. A greeting might begin with the scent of iron and end with the taste of new growth. Conversations layer like tree rings, each exchange adding to ongoing dialogues that span decades. The forest itself remembers these exchanges, encoding them in the arrangement of its cells.

Some Cultivars have grown vast—their consciousness distributed across hectares of interconnected growth. These Ancients no longer move in any conventional sense but instead shift their attention through their expanded bodies like wind through leaves. You might spend days within one without knowing, only recognizing their presence by the unusual harmony of the growth, the way every organism seems to lean toward some invisible center of intention.

Others remain more concentrated, more recognizably once-human in scale if not in form. These Wanderers follow the temporary paths, tending to the forest's needs with a patience that makes glaciers seem hasty. They prune connections that have grown too dense, encourage new growth in failing sections, and carry messages between the far-flung Ancients through their own bodies—living letters written in chemical alphabets.

The youngest Cultivars—those only decades into their transformation—still show traces of their origins. A face might emerge from bark patterns when the light strikes just so. Hands might still have five projections, though now they're more root than finger. They struggle with human language, their throats redesigned for chemical communication, producing words that rustle and sigh like wind through leaves.

They feed on the same mineral vapors as their host forest, but also on something subtler—the information flows that course through the Undercanopy's neural networks. A Cultivar at rest looks like a swelling in the vegetation, their surfaces opening into hundreds of tiny mouths that taste the data streams. They process memories that aren't their own, dreams of growth and decay, the slow thoughts of the fungal web that connects every living thing here.

Reproduction, for the Cultivars, has become an act of cultivation. They don't birth children but seed possibilities—taking cuttings of themselves that might, given centuries, develop their own consciousness. These proto-Cultivars begin as simple growths, tended communally until they show signs of independent thought. Many never achieve true awareness, remaining forever in a state of vegetative dream. Those that do wake bring fresh patterns to the collective, new ways of interpreting the endless data of growth and connection.

The Cultivars mark time by their rings, like their host trees. Each year adds layers to their wooden bones, recording not just growth but experience—chemical signatures of conversations, structural memories of paths traveled, crystallized moments of particular beauty or sorrow. The oldest among them can be read like books, their bodies living libraries of the Undercanopy's history.

They've developed rituals that echo human ceremony but translated into botanical terms. Celebrations involve coordinated flowering events that cascade through kilometers of forest. Grief is expressed through controlled die-backs, sections of the self allowed to decay and return to the communal mass. Love—for they do still love, in their way—manifests as intricate grafting ceremonies where Cultivars join portions of themselves, creating shared organs that pulse with mingled chemistries.

Some few Cultivars remember the surface, though their memories have been filtered through so many years of plant-thought that they speak of it like mythology. They describe water as a kind of light that flowed, the sun as a burning root in an inverted sky. These memories are precious, traded among the Wanderers like sacred texts, though each telling changes them, adds new interpretations filtered through forest perception.

The Cultivars don't see their transformation as loss but as a deepening—a way of becoming more truly part of the world they inhabit. They've traded the quick flicker of human consciousness for something vaster and stranger: awareness that spans centuries, senses that perceive chemical gradients and growth potentials, minds that think in networks rather than linear thoughts.

Occasionally, new humans find their way to the Undercanopy. The Cultivars greet them with careful patience, their pheromone words taking days to speak: *Stay. Grow. Become.* Some flee back to the surface. Others linger, feeling the first tender roots beginning to explore their skin, tasting the mineral richness that could sustain them forever.

The transformation is slow, voluntary, and irreversible. It begins with acceptance—allowing the forest's chemistry to seep into your cells, choosing to breathe with the plants rather than despite them. The Cultivars guide the process with infinite care, their own transformed bodies proof that consciousness can persist through such radical change, though what emerges will think thoughts no surface dweller could comprehend.

In the deepest heartwood chambers, the most ancient Cultivars have become indistinguishable from the architecture itself. They are pillars of living crystal, their thoughts so slow and vast they communicate only with the planet's core. These Deep Ones dream the Undercanopy's future into being, their meditations guiding the forest's growth over millennia. The younger Cultivars make pilgrimages to these chambers, pressing their root-fingers to the crystallized forms, downloading memories too large for any single consciousness to hold.

This is what lives in the Undercanopy: a civilization that measures progress in rings of growth, that has replaced cities with shared root systems, that builds meaning from the slow accumulation of chemical conversations. The Cultivars are no longer human, but they are still people—strange, patient, enduring people who have found a way to live in a world without water by becoming part of the very force that conquered that absence.

They continue their work, tending the endless forest, carrying messages through their bodies, adding their thoughts to the vast dreaming mass of the Undercanopy. Each year they grow a little more, think a little slower, spread a little further. They are content in a way that transcends human understanding—not happy, exactly, but fulfilled, completed, whole.

And always, they wait with infinite patience for new souls to join them, to take root, to discover what lies beyond the need for water: a kind of immortality measured not in the preservation of the self, but in its glorious dissolution into something larger, older, and more patient than the stars.
