The people of the mist city move like water finding its level—slowly, deliberately, always choosing the path of least resistance through their vertical world. They dress in layers of oiled wool and waxed leather, garments that shed moisture like duck feathers. Over generations, their bodies have adapted: barrel chests that process the thin air more efficiently, thick fingers that grip wet stone surely, eyes the pale grey of winter sky that see better in the perpetual twilight than in harsh sun.

They are a quiet people by necessity. The mist swallows loud voices, so they speak in low tones that carry better through the thick air. Children learn early to whistle—not melodies, but a complex system of calls that pierce the fog. Each family has its own whistle-name, each profession its own urgent summons. The stone-workers' call drops low and rises sharp; the cistern-keepers' trill wavers like water over rocks.

Their trades have evolved with their environment. The gill-cleaners wade thigh-deep in the cisterns, cleaning the filters that keep the city's water flowing. They wear suits of salamander leather that never quite dry, and over years of work, their skin develops a faint shimmer—not scales exactly, but a smoothness that repels water. The thermal-tenders maintain the deep pipes that bring heat from below, their guild passing down knowledge of every twist and joint in the system through apprenticeships that last decades. They know the mountain's moods by the temperature of the water, can predict avalanches by the way the springs gurgle.

The bat-bird keepers live in the highest towers, their profession somewhere between shepherd and musician. They play bone flutes at dawn and dusk to call their flocks home, each keeper's melody unique to their colony. The birds recognize their keeper by scent—these men and women rub rendering from the midges into their hair and clothes until they smell like the city's particular blend of stone and prey. Children born to keeper families often display a peculiar trait: they hear in the ultrasonic range, able to perceive the bat-birds' echo-location as a kind of music.

In the market squares, vendors huddle under the calcified awnings selling goods adapted to the climate: dried fungi that reconstitute in water, fermented fish paste that never spoils in the cold, textiles woven from the horizontal trees' fibrous bark. The mushroom farmers bring their harvest up from the deeper caves—pale, luminescent varieties that glow faintly in the dark, prized for both food and light. The salamander-milkers extract antifreeze secretions that the alchemists refine into medicines and preservatives.

The city's spiritual life has grown around its water. They worship not gods but patterns—the spiral of moss growth, the branching of water down stone, the formations of mist at certain hours. Their priests are more meteorologists than mystics, reading meaning in the interplay of temperature and moisture. Sacred sites cluster around the old quarry pools where those ancient not-quite-creatures surface. Young adults undergo a ritual where they must spend a night by these pools, returning with visions induced by the cold and the strange emanations from the water. Some claim to understand the language of whatever lives in those depths. Some never speak again.

The social structure flows like the water itself—finding its level through the path of least resistance. Authority pools in natural gathering places: the keepers of the thermal springs hold sway in winter, the cistern guards in the dry weeks of late summer when every drop matters. The city has no single ruler but rather a fluid council that expands and contracts based on the season's needs. During the spring melt, the bridge-engineers join the council; during the autumn spawn when the blind fish breed, the fishery guilds take precedence.

Marriage customs involve the exchange of water rights—each family maintains ancestral claims to specific gutters and collection points. Love tokens are carved from the soft stone that lines the thermal springs, worn smooth as worry stones. Children play games with salamander shells and midge-wing fans, their rhymes keeping time with the constant drip of water: *"Moss grows left, spiral right, bat-birds home before the night."*

The elderly don't retire but transform. As joints stiffen in the damp, they take up sedentary trades—becoming memory keepers who maintain the oral maps of the pipe systems, or dream-readers who interpret the visions brought back from the quarry pools. The very old often move to the lowest levels, claiming the warmest spots near the thermal vents, where they tend the bacterial mats that help purify the hot water before it rises through the city.

Death, when it comes, returns them to the water. Bodies are placed in special chambers where the stone salamanders strip them clean, the bones then ground and mixed with mortar to repair the endless small cracks in the city's infrastructure. They say you can read the city's history in its walls—that each repair contains the essence of those who came before, that the whole city is built from the bones of its dead, forever cycling between flesh and stone.

This is how they endure—not by conquering their harsh home but by becoming part of it, their culture as adapted to the mist and stone as the horizontal trees and spiral moss. They persist through a kind of stubborn flexibility, bending but not breaking, flowing but not dispersing, generation after generation learning the mountain's moods and teaching them to their children in turn.
