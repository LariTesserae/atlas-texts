In the deepest chambers, where the stone roots branch into darkness, there are the Attendants.

They are difficult to see at first—your eye passes over them as it would pass over a peculiarity in the stone's grain. But once noticed, they become unmistakable: figures that stand motionless at the junctions where walls meet, at the lips of water-collecting basins, at the spiral stairs' landings. They are roughly human in proportion but made of the same stone as the city, their surfaces bearing the same fresh-cut edges, the same crystalline grain. The moss has not yet claimed them, though it creeps closer each day, sending exploratory tendrils across their feet.

The Attendants do not move—or rather, they move as the city moves. In an hour, one might turn its head a fraction of a degree. In a day, an arm might lift imperceptibly. They are synchronized with the city's growth, operating at its tempo, performing maintenance that takes years to complete. One stands by a channel where water flows wrong, and over the course of months, its hand descends to adjust the stone, to correct the flow. Another tends to a crack that shouldn't have formed, sealing it with infinitesimal movements of its fingers that deposit new mineral material.

They have no faces—just smooth depressions where features might be, collecting shadows and dew. Yet they seem aware. Stand near one long enough and you feel observed, evaluated, catalogued. They know where every drop of water flows, where every spore settles, where each new wall will emerge. They are the city's autonomic nervous system, making tiny corrections to ensure the grand pattern continues.

Sometimes, in the pre-dawn darkness when the moss glows brightest, you might catch one in what passes for rapid movement—a figure crossing a plaza in the span of an hour, its form blurring like a long exposure photograph. These are the emergency responses, when something threatens the city's growth. A tree root penetrating too deep. A crack that might become a fissure. A channel blocked by debris. The Attendant moves with geological urgency to correct the error, then returns to stillness.

They multiply like the city multiplies. In certain chambers, you find them half-emerged from walls, their forms still crystallizing from the stone. These nascent Attendants will take months to fully separate, years before they begin their slow patrol. They emerge where needed—a new section of city requires new maintenance, new attention to the flow of water and the spread of moss.

There are older ones too, down in the root chambers. These have been claimed by the moss entirely, their forms now indistinguishable from particularly thick growths of vegetation. But they still move, still perform their functions, now as hybrid entities—part stone, part plant, mediating between the mineral and vegetal aspects of the city. They tend to the deepest channels, where water collects in vast reservoirs. They monitor the foundation's spread through the earth.

The Attendants never acknowledge each other, never coordinate except through the city itself. Each knows its role through some communion with the stone, receiving instructions through vibrations too low to hear, through chemical signals carried in the water, through the electrical potentials that build up in the crystalline structure. They are not servants—they are organs, components of the city-organism as essential as the walls or the moss.

In the highest towers, where new stone still crystallizes from the air itself, the youngest Attendants keep watch. These are barely formed, translucent, their bodies still deciding on their final shape. They monitor the growth, ensuring each new crystal aligns properly, each new chamber connects correctly to the water system. They will descend as they solidify, taking up positions lower in the city as newer ones emerge above them.

Some chambers contain what might be mistakes—Attendants that crystallized wrong, their proportions inhuman, their forms twisted or incomplete. But even these have functions. They tend to the strangest spaces, the chambers that don't quite fit the pattern, the channels that flow upward, the moss that grows in impossible spirals. Perhaps they are not mistakes but specializations, evolved to maintain aspects of the city that follow different rules.

The Attendants are not alive in any biological sense. They have no metabolism, no respiration, no reproduction beyond their emergence from stone. Yet they are not dead either. They exist in the same liminal state as the city itself—not built but grown, not living but growing, not conscious but aware. They are the city's dream of inhabitants, as necessary and as empty as the chambers they tend.

Watch one long enough—hours, days, weeks—and you begin to synchronize with its tempo. Your breathing slows, your heartbeat spaces itself out, your thoughts crystallize and reform at geological pace. You begin to understand the urgency of a misaligned stone, the crisis of a channel flowing wrong, the satisfaction when a new section properly integrates with the whole. 

You are not welcome here, but you are not unwelcome. You are simply irrelevant, operating at a tempo the city cannot perceive except as a blur, a momentary perturbation in the air. The Attendants note your presence as they note a falling leaf or a shifting shadow—something that passes through but does not remain, does not matter to the real work of growth and maintenance and the patient collection of dew.

When you leave, you carry the city's tempo with you for days. The human world seems frantic, pointless, a chaos of unnecessary motion. You find yourself standing still for hours, watching walls for signs of growth, listening for the whisper of water on stone, waiting for the moss to tell you what comes next. The feeling fades, eventually. But sometimes, in quiet moments, you still feel it—the pull of that deeper rhythm, the call to return and take your place among the Attendants, to crystallize from stone and join the eternal maintenance of something that builds itself for no reason except that it must.
