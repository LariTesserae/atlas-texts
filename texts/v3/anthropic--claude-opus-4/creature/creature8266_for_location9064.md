The Shapers move through their wooden world with the patient industry of those who know their work will never end. Their bodies tell the story of their evolution—elongated forms wrapped in muscle, built for powerful swimming through dense water and for hours of stationary carving work. Their skin has thickened into a natural wetsuit, mottled in patterns that match the scarred bark they spend their lives working. Four arms allow them to brace against strong currents while carving, though their lower pair has shortened over generations, becoming specialized for holding tools and anchoring.

Their faces disturb newcomers. The eyes have divided—two large primary orbs for seeing in the amber darkness, and a ring of smaller secondary eyes around their skulls for detecting the faint bioluminescent signals that mark their paths. Their mouths have evolved past mere eating into multi-tools: teeth differentiated into scrapers, grinders, and precision picks, with a secondary palate that can taste water composition down to trace nutrients. Most unsettling are their nostrils, which have migrated to the crown of their heads and evolved muscular seals, allowing them to work inverted in air pockets without drowning.

They live in hollowed chambers within the great pneumatic trunks, spaces they've carved following the natural grain over decades. A family's dwelling grows with them—children learn to carve by expanding their own sleeping nooks, gradually taking on more ambitious modifications as they mature. The walls of their homes are living libraries, carved with texture-scripts that record everything from current maps to cultivation schedules to the songs they hum while working, felt and read by sensitive fingerpads in darkness.

Their tools are organs as much as implements. They cultivate boring-worms in the flesh of their forearms, creatures whose diamond-hard teeth can be exposed by muscle contractions to eat precise channels. Their shortest fingers end in symbiotic colonies of filing organisms, renewed by dipping hands into cultivation pouches worn at their waists. For larger work, they carry implements of compressed wood harder than stone, or shells shaped by generations of selective harvest. But their primary tool remains knowledge—understanding exactly where to cut to encourage growth rather than disease, how to read the stress patterns in living wood, when to let the forest heal itself.

The social structure mirrors their architecture. Master Shapers control critical junctions where multiple current-systems meet, while apprentices work the periphery, learning to read wood-grain and water-flow. Status comes from the elegance of one's modifications—Shapers who can create self-maintaining channels that need no adjustment for decades earn reverence. The most honored are those who can perceive problems years before they manifest, carving preventive modifications that guide growth away from future catastrophe.

They communicate through vibration as much as sound. Their throat modifications produce subsonic pulses that travel efficiently through water and wood alike. Complex conversations involve placing palms against carved surfaces, feeling the tremors of distant speakers through the wooden network. For immediate needs, they've developed a rapid clicking language using specialized throat valves. But their deepest expressions come through their work—emotional states carved into maintenance patterns that others recognize years later.

Their children learn by shadowing, small forms clinging to their parents' backs as they work, absorbing the rhythm of scraping and the patterns of growth. By the time they can swim independently, they know the texture of healthy wood versus infected, can taste the difference between beneficial and parasitic organisms in the water. Adolescence is marked by receiving their first cultivation colonies—the boring worms, the filing organisms, the bioluminescent bacteria they'll carry for life.

They worship through maintenance. Their religion recognizes no gods, only the Pattern—the ideal state of flow and growth that the Drowned Canopy aspires toward. Sacred sites exist where ancient Shapers achieved perfect modifications, channels that have run unchanged for centuries. Pilgrims come to feel these surfaces, trying to understand the hands that made them. Their highest prayer is the work itself, the endless adjustment that keeps their world in balance.

Memory among them is spatial and tactile. They recall events by the scars they left on wood, navigate by the feeling of familiar carvings under their fingers. Their histories are traced in the growth rings of the great trunks, readable to those who know how their ancestors' modifications influenced each season's expansion. Personal memories embed in their cultivation colonies—boring worms that have tasted specific woods, filing organisms that remember the texture of important works.

Death returns them to the Pattern. Bodies are sealed into specially prepared trunk chambers where fungal networks gradually incorporate their nutrients into the forest-city's metabolism. The wooden walls grow around them, preserving the shape of their forms as honored voids in the heartwood. Master Shapers can read these body-casts generations later, understanding something of the individual from how the wood chose to embrace them.

They dream collectively when they sleep, neural activity synchronizing through palms pressed against the wooden walls. In these shared visions, they process the day's modifications, unconsciously planning tomorrow's work. Sometimes they dream of catastrophic failure—the wooden sky splitting, the currents reversing, the careful balance collapsing. They wake from these nightmares already swimming toward the threatened section, tools ready, knowing others have dreamed the same warnings.

The Shapers have no concept of nature separate from artifice. To them, the Drowned Canopy is simply the world properly arranged, no different from how a beaver's dam improves a stream. They pity creatures that live in unmodified spaces, seeing raw nature as a form of poverty. Their deepest fear is not death but abandonment—sections of the canopy reverting to wild growth, channels clogging, the Pattern dissolving into chaos. They work not from duty but from love, each careful carving an act of devotion to the wooden machine that makes their existence possible.

In the eternal twilight of their drowned forest, they have become what they create—living tools shaped by their own shaping, as much a part of the Drowned Canopy as the trees they endlessly carve.
