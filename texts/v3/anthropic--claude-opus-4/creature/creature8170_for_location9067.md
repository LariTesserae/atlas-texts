The Gardeners of Meltwater move through their inverted domain with the patient gravity of those who tend living things in impossible places. They are recognizable as human only in silhouette—decades of adaptation have reshaped them into something more suited to their pendant world.

Their bodies stretch long and lean, bones lengthened by childhoods spent suspended. Arms reach further than they should, fingers splayed into delicate fans of membrane between the digits—better to catch themselves on the omnipresent cables, better to feel the electromagnetic pulse of the cable worms before accidentally grasping one. Their skin carries a faint luminescence, a symbiotic relationship with the bacteria that coat every surface in the humid air. In the eternal twilight, they glow softly green-gold, their movements leaving brief afterimages in the mist.

They wear living clothes. Moss grows across their shoulders in careful patterns, drinking their sweat and exhaling oxygen in return. Vine fibers weave through ports in their skin—voluntary piercings that allow the plants to share nutrients directly with their blood. The eldest Gardeners are more garden than human, their backs sprouting with ferns, their hair replaced by trailing orchids that pulse with their heartbeat. They claim the plants whisper to them, sharing chemical signals about the health of the hanging ecosystem.

The Gardeners speak in clicks and harmonics, a language learned from the pendulum bats but evolved beyond mere mimicry. They've developed secondary vocal cords, thin membranes in their throats that let them echolocate through the vast spaces. Their children are born clicking, mapping their inverted world through sound before their eyes—huge and dark-adapted—ever open.

Their tools are organic. They cultivate builder-worms, creatures they've bred from the cable worms but fed on different currents—biological electricity generated by modified electric eels in cultivation tanks. These living tools shape metal through focused electromagnetic fields, allowing the Gardeners to repair and expand the hanging infrastructure without traditional machinery. They wear the worms coiled around their arms like living jewelry, the creatures' neural tissue interfacing directly with their own through the vine-ports.

The Gardeners' society mirrors their architecture—inverted hierarchies where the youngest lead and the eldest sink into contemplative symbiosis with their plants. They worship through cultivation, believing each new species that adapts to their world brings them closer to understanding their purpose. Their priests are those so overgrown with flora they can no longer move, suspended in garden-cocoons where they dream the slow thoughts of plants and translate the chemical poetry of growth.

They navigate by taste as much as sight or sound. Their tongues, forked and sensitive, can detect the mineral content of different water sources, the health of air currents, the stress pheromones of struggling plants. They lick the cables before traversing them, reading the accumulated history of who passed before, what they carried, where they were going.

Their children terrify visitors. Born in the subcity, they move through three dimensions with casual indifference to up or down. They leap between platforms with calculated precision, their webbed digits catching cables at the last moment. They play in the air, diving through the humid atmosphere in controlled falls that end with graceful catches hundreds of meters below. Their games involve cultivating competing gardens on the undersides of platforms, racing vines toward each other in slow-motion battles that take months to resolve.

The Gardeners reproduce as their plants do—through careful cultivation rather than chance. Pregnancy is a community affair, the developing child fed through multiple umbilical vines connected to different donors, ensuring genetic diversity and communal investment. Births happen in the moss pools, the infants' first breath underwater, their lungs adapted to extract oxygen from the humid air or the algae-rich pools with equal efficiency.

They fear the dry. Their nightmares involve the permafrost refreezing, the drips stopping, their carefully tended ecosystem withering. They perform daily rituals to encourage the melt—heating ceremonies where they gather around the geothermal vents and sing harmonic frequencies that vibrate through the ice ceiling, supposedly encouraging the thaw. Whether this works or merely comforts them, the water continues to fall.

The eldest among them remember the surface, though their memories have been edited by decades of biochemical adaptation. They speak of the sun as a burning eye, too bright, too harsh for the delicate life they now shepherd. Their descendants listen politely but cannot imagine wanting to leave their green-glowing world for the sterile ice above.

They are changing faster than they realize. Each generation's children are born with more pronounced adaptations—longer limbs, better echolocation, more complete symbiosis with their flora. The Gardeners are becoming something new, a species shaped by humanity's strangest city, evolved for a world that hangs like ripe fruit from the earth's thawing bones.

In their bioluminescent gardens suspended over marsh and mist, tending impossible ecosystems with tools made of living light, clicking poetry to their plants in languages humans were never meant to speak, they are no longer quite human and not yet quite Other—but something in between, something that could only exist in the wet, inverted miracle of Meltwater, where life drips from above and humanity plants its future in the sky.
