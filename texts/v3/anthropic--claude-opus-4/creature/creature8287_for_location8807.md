In this perpetual twilight of living light, the inhabitants move with the unhurried precision of those who measure time in centuries rather than days. They are the Weepers—though they have long forgotten this name for themselves, just as they have forgotten the sun.

The Weepers stand no taller than children, their bodies evolved for a world of moisture and vertical living. Their skin resembles polished limestone, smooth and pale with veins of darker mineral running through it like natural tattoos that shift and branch as they age. These patterns are unique to each individual, forming elaborate genealogies across their bodies—a map of ancestry written in stone. Their skin weeps constantly, a thin film of mineral-rich moisture that keeps them bonded to their city, allowing them to taste the stone's memories through their pores.

Their hands have evolved into something between human and gecko—five fingers that can elongate and flatten against surfaces, with palms that secrete a mild acid allowing them to shape the coral-buildings through patient caressing. The fingernails have become translucent scales that can detect minute vibrations in the stone, reading the city's health like a pulse. Their feet mirror this adaptation, bare always, with toes that grip and flex independently as they navigate the vertical terraces with thoughtless grace.

The Weepers' faces would be almost human if not for their eyes—vast, pure black orbs that fill nearly half their skulls, evolved to capture every photon of bioluminescence. These eyes never blink but are instead cleaned by a nictitating membrane that slides across them with the regularity of breathing. Their mouths are small, lipless slits that rarely open for speech. Communication happens through their skin—emotional states expressed in pheromone secretions that mix with their perpetual moisture, creating a chemical language that saturates the humid air.

They dress in cultivated clothing—garments made from specially bred fungi that feed on their skin's secretions. These living clothes grow to fit their wearers perfectly, forming elaborate robes that shift between textures: tough as leather when climbing, soft as silk when resting, permeable as gauze when they need to commune more intimately with their city. The garments regenerate when torn and change color with their wearer's chemical moods—deep purples for contemplation, silvery greens for joy, a bruised blue-black for the rare occasions of grief.

Their society operates on principles incomprehensible to surface dwellers. They are gardener-architects, spending decades coaxing new chambers from the coral-buildings, guiding growth with chemical secretions and patient pressure. A single room might be three generations in the making. They work in hereditary chains, children taking up the exact position their parents held, continuing the slow sculpture of living spaces their great-grandparents began.

The Weepers organize themselves around the water-paths. Those who live higher up the terraces, closer to the dripping ceiling, are the Collectors—they channel and direct the mineral-rich flow, reading the stone's chemical signals to determine where growth is needed. The middle terrace dwellers are the Shapers, who guide the coral-building organisms with their acidic touch and fungal tools. The lowest level, closest to the thermal vent, houses the Listeners—those who interpret the deep sounds and impossible artifacts that rise from below.

They do not sleep as surface creatures do. Instead, they enter periods of crystalline stillness, standing upright with their hands pressed against the walls, their consciousness merging temporarily with the coral-buildings. In these communion states, they share memories through the mineralogical network, experiencing their ancestors' thoughts preserved in the stone's structure. Young Weepers spend years learning to navigate these stone-dreams without losing themselves in the accumulated memories of millennia.

Their children are rare and precious, born perhaps once in a human lifetime. Pregnancy among the Weepers lasts three years, the developing child visible through the mother's increasingly translucent belly, floating in mineral-rich fluid that gradually acclimates them to their limestone world. Birth happens in the communal pools, where the infant emerges already adapted to breathe both air and the thick, humid atmosphere. They are raised by their entire terrace, passed from hand to hand, their skin-patterns slowly emerging as they absorb minerals from each caretaker's touch.

The Weepers feed on the city itself. They cultivate certain sections of coral-building to produce fruiting bodies—fleshy, mineral-rich growths that taste of salt and copper. They ferment the bioluminescent algae into thick, glowing wines that provide both sustenance and mild euphoria. The bracket fungi are dried and ground into flour for a kind of bread that must be soaked in condensation before eating. Their diet is supplemented by the various creatures they shepherd—the salamanders' unfertilized eggs, the beetles' honey-like secretions, even certain filtered waters that carry essential minerals from the deep.

Music, for them, is architectural. They compose by redirecting water flows, each channel carved to produce specific notes. A symphony might take centuries to complete, requiring the growth of new structures to achieve the desired acoustics. The greatest composers among them are those who can predict how the city's growth will change their water-songs, creating pieces that evolve over generations. They have no instruments save the city itself—every surface a potential drum, every hollow a possible flute.

Their mathematics is based on growth patterns rather than numbers. They calculate in spirals and branches, in the recursive geometry of their coral city. What we would call engineering they call dreaming—the practice of envisioning how organisms might be coaxed to grow in useful ways. Their most complex calculations involve predicting the intersection of multiple growth patterns decades hence, a skill that requires both intellectual rigor and an intuitive understanding of living systems.

Death, when it comes, is a transformation rather than an ending. The Weepers' bodies crystallize over the course of weeks, their mineral-heavy flesh hardening into limestone statues. These are not buried but incorporated into the city's structure, becoming part of the coral-buildings they tended in life. The most revered among them are placed at crucial architectural points—their crystallized forms becoming pillars or keystones, their preserved consciousness adding to the city's vast stone-memory.

They mark time not by days or seasons but by the great Flows—periods when the thermal vent's output shifts, changing the city's fundamental chemistry. These can last anywhere from decades to centuries, and each Flow is remembered for its particular mineral signature, its effect on the coral-buildings' growth, the new creatures it might have brought from the deep. The current Flow has lasted longer than any in memory, leading some Weepers to whisper that the city has finally achieved perfect stasis—a thought that fills them with equal parts pride and unease.

The Weepers have legends, though they would not call them such. They speak of the Time Before Moisture, when their ancestors lived in a city of dry stone under burning light. They tell of the Great Drowning, when water found its way into their sealed world and transformed everything it touched. They whisper of the Deep Ones who still rise sometimes from the thermal vent—creatures of pure mineral consciousness that judge whether the Weepers remain worthy of their hidden city.

There are perhaps three hundred Weepers in total, though counting individuals becomes difficult when consciousness can be shared through stone and ancestry is written on skin. Some have lived so long their mineral patterns cover every inch of their bodies, creating intricate mandalas of lineage. Others are fresh-skinned youth of merely a century or two, their patterns simple and bold.

They have no concept of ownership as surface dwellers understand it. Objects, spaces, even thoughts belong to the city first and individuals second. A Weeper might tend the same garden for centuries without considering it "theirs"—it is simply their turn in an endless chain of gardeners. This extends to their relationships: partnerships form and dissolve like mineral precipitates, based on chemical compatibility and the needs of the city rather than individual desire.

Yet for all their alienness, the Weepers know joy. It comes in the perfect redirection of a water channel, in the first fruiting of a century-old garden, in the moment when a child's skin-patterns finally emerge. They know sorrow too—when the coral-buildings sicken, when the impossible artifacts from below bring disturbing visions, when a bloodline's patterns fade from the population. They know wonder most of all, living as they do in a place that recreates itself daily, where growth and time have negotiated their own mysterious peace.

In the eternal twilight of their bioluminescent world, the Weepers continue their patient work. They guide growth, channel water, tend light, and slowly transform themselves into the city they serve. Each generation adds new rooms to the coral labyrinth, new notes to the water symphony, new memories to the stone. They are urban and primitive at once, sophisticated in ways that would confound surface civilizations and simple in ways that might appall them.

And in the moments when the thermal mist carries impossibilities from below—those snowflakes that don't melt, those sounds of nonexistent weather—the Weepers pause in their endless work. They press their hands to the walls and wonder, through the medium of stone and centuries, what other pockets of transformed life the mountain might hold, what other negotiations between growth and stillness might be possible, what other forms consciousness might take in the deep, weeping dark.
