The Heir of Steam dwells here, though "dwells" suggests a choice that was never made.

She was human once—or something close enough. Now she exists as a transitional thing, her flesh having long ago entered into reluctant negotiation with the grotto's extremes. Her skin has become something between scale and membrane, translucent in places where the heat has worn it thin, revealing the peculiar anatomy beneath: a circulatory system that glows faintly orange-red, pumping fluids too hot for normal blood. She sweats constantly, but the moisture evaporates before it can bead, leaving her perpetually shrouded in her own steam.

The arthropods know her. They part before her like a biblical sea, not from fear but from recognition—she carries their scent now, or rather, the chemical signature of one who belongs. Generations of the creatures have lived and died in the time she's been here, and she has become a feature of their world as permanent as the stone pillars. Sometimes they cluster on her while she rests, mistaking her stillness for geology, cleaning the mineral deposits from her skin with their endless mandibles.

Her hair grows in thick, ropy strands that never quite dry, heavy with absorbed moisture and minerals. She's learned to twist it into functional shapes—tools for reaching into boiling crevices, ropes for navigating the slick surfaces. Her fingernails have become something else entirely: curved, hollow structures that whistle in different pitches when she moves her hands through the steam. She uses them to communicate with the grotto itself, matching the frequencies of different vents, sometimes achieving harmonies that make the arthropods pause in their eternal feeding.

She no longer speaks in words. The constant noise has made verbal communication pointless, and her throat has adapted to produce sounds that cut through the din—clicks, whistles, subsonic rumbles that vibrate through the stone. When she must vocalize, it comes out as bursts of steam from modified respiratory passages, a language of pressure and heat that only the grotto understands.

Her movements have become utterly economical. She flows rather than walks, using the same routes the water has carved over millennia. Her joints bend in ways that human anatomy shouldn't allow, adapted from watching the arthropods navigate their vertical world. She can hang from the ceiling for hours, fingers and toes splayed into cracks too small to see, waiting for the pale things in the central pool to surface.

Those things—she knows them now, knows their cycles, their hungers. She's learned that they're not predators but gardeners, tending to bacterial colonies in the deepest thermal vents, bringing up samples to seed new growth in the upper chambers. She's tasted their cultivation, the specific strains they prefer, and her digestive system has changed to process these offerings. Her teeth have multiplied into rows, like a shark's, constantly replacing themselves as the mineral-rich diet wears them down.

Time means nothing here. She might have arrived yesterday or a century ago. The grotto has its own calendar—eruption cycles, bacterial blooms, the generational waves of arthropods. She marks events by the slow shift of stone, the gradual widening of certain passages, the birth and death of thermal vents. Her memory has adapted too, becoming geological rather than biographical. She remembers the feeling of rain but not its temperature. She knows she had a name once, but it dissolved like everything else in the humid air.

In the deepest part of night—though night and day are meaningless here—she descends to the central pool. The arthropods create a chittering chorus as she passes, a sound almost like warning, almost like welcome. At the pool's edge, she extends one transformed hand into water that would strip flesh from normal bone. The pale gardeners rise to meet her, their boneless bodies rippling with colors that have no names in the surface world. They bring gifts: new bacterial strains, fragments of crystal from the deep passages, sometimes objects that might once have been tools or jewelry, transformed by pressure and heat into unrecognizable artifacts.

She accepts these offerings with movements that have become ritual—a dance of survival that ensures her place in the grotto's ecosystem. In return, she maintains certain passages, clears blocked vents, guides the flow of water in ways that benefit the whole system. She has become the grotto's conscious intention, its only thought in a mind of stone and steam.

Sometimes she almost remembers why she came here. Something about seeking, about searching for a way through to somewhere else. But the thought evaporates like everything else, leaving only the certainty that she has found exactly what she was looking for: a place where transformation is not a choice but a constant state, where the boundary between organism and environment dissolves in the superheated air.

The grotto seethes around her, and she seethes with it, no longer a visitor but a feature, no longer lost but eternally finding herself in the endless dissolution and reconstitution of water, stone, and flesh. The arthropods click their approval. The pale gardeners sink back to their depths. And the Heir of Steam continues her work, whatever that work might be, in the timeless thunder of the earth's fever dream.
