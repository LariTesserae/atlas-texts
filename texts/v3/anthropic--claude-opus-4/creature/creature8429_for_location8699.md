The people of the hollow are small-boned and narrow-shouldered, shaped by generations of threading through stone passages barely wider than their bodies. Their fingers are long and sensitive, capable of reading the moisture on walls like braille, distinguishing between the dozen types of condensation that signal different weather patterns approaching. Children develop these fingers early, spending hours tracing the carved channel systems that direct water, learning to feel when a blockage forms before it becomes critical.

Their eyes have grown large and dark, adapted to the perpetual twilight of covered passages and the faint bioluminescence of the cistern. In bright sunlight, they squint and shade their faces with hands that flutter like the membrane-winged creatures they harvest. But in the dim blue glow of the coral-fungus, they see details that would escape any lowlander—the particular way a salamander's throat pulses before it strikes, the shift in aerial plankton that signals a pressure change.

They dress in layers of finely woven mesh, garments that serve multiple purposes. The outer layer catches moisture from the air, funneling it through carefully placed folds to collection vessels at the waist. The inner layers trap warmth while allowing skin to breathe in the humid environment. Everything they wear can be quickly reversed or reconfigured—a cloak becomes a net, sleeves detach to form collection bags, hoods unfold into water catchers.

Their movements are peculiar to watch—a flowing, deliberate quality that comes from living where every gesture might disturb a colony or trigger a defensive swarm. They place their feet with absolute precision, not from fear but from habit, their weight rolling from heel to toe in a way that minimizes vibration through the stone. When they reach for something, their hands trace slow arcs that give creatures time to move aside. This gentleness extends to their speech—they talk in low, modulated tones that won't interfere with the salamanders' clicking navigation.

The eldest among them bear marks of their profession. Netters have scars on their forearms from years of brushing against stone edges while maneuvering poles in narrow spaces. Water-carriers develop a permanent curve to their spine from decades of hauling buckets up the spiral steps. The lamp-makers' fingers are stained permanent violet from processing salamander secretions, and they smell faintly metallic even after washing.

Knowledge passes through apprenticeship rather than formal teaching. A child shadows an adult for years, learning not through explanation but through countless small gestures—how to angle a net to catch the morning swarms, where to press on a salamander to express oil without killing it, which growth patterns in the coral-fungus indicate it's ready for harvest. They develop an intuitive understanding of the settlement's living systems, knowing without conscious thought when the creature cycles are about to shift.

Their social structure mirrors the interconnected spaces they inhabit. No single leader commands; instead, different individuals take precedence based on the need—the weather-reader during storm season, the cistern-keeper during drought, the harvest-master when the aerial plankton blooms. Conflicts are resolved in whispered councils held in the deepest chambers, where the stone amplifies voices into overlapping echoes that make shouting impossible.

They mark time not by days but by creature cycles—the salamander's seven-day hunt, the plankton's twelve-day bloom, the coral-fungus's slow pulse that completes one full cycle every thirty days. Their celebrations align with these rhythms: the Night of Blue Light when the coral-fungus glows brightest, the Harvest Gathering when the aerial plankton reaches peak density, the Water Festival when the first storms break the dry season.

Death, when it comes, is given back to the system that sustained life. Bodies are laid in specially carved hollows where the coral-fungus grows thickest, providing nutrients for explosive growth that the community then harvests in remembrance. They believe the phosphorescent blue glow carries something of the deceased, and eating the funeral harvest connects the living to the dead in an unbroken chain stretching back to the settlement's founding.

Their children play games that would terrify lowlanders—racing through dark passages guided only by sound, competing to see who can approach closest to a salamander without triggering its defensive secretion, building elaborate stick structures that mimic the settlement's organic architecture. By five years old, they can navigate the entire hollow in complete darkness. By ten, they're contributing to the harvests. By fifteen, they've usually chosen their life's path, apprenticing to master the particular skills their temperament suits.

The settlement's few visitors—traders who climb from the lower slopes bringing metal tools and taking processed oils—find these people unsettling. It's not just their appearance or their creature-filled home, but something deeper: the way they've become part of their environment rather than merely inhabiting it. They move through their aerial city like components of a larger organism, each person a cell in a body made of stone and water and countless small lives.

In the evening, when the aerial plankton settles and the salamanders begin their nocturnal hunts, the people gather in small groups throughout the passages. They share food—root vegetables boiled in salamander oil, cakes of pressed plankton, fresh growths of coral-fungus that pop between the teeth. They tell stories in their low, melodious voices, tales that weave together human memory and creature behavior until it becomes impossible to separate history from ecology.

This is how they endure in their impossible city: not by conquering their environment but by dissolving into it, becoming as essential to the hollow's ecosystem as the salamanders or the aerial plankton. They are human still, but humans shaped by stone and storm and the constant presence of other lives, adapted so completely to their aerial world that the lowlands have become as foreign to them as the deep ocean. They are the people of the hollow, and the hollow lives in them.
