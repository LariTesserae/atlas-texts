In the Cistern Cathedral, the Keepers move with deliberate slowness through their inherited domain. They are neither fully human anymore, nor something else—descendants of the original builders who stayed too long in the darkness, breathing the sharp air and drinking the mineral-heavy water until their bodies adapted to this underworld.

Their skin has taken on the pallor of cave fish, not quite translucent but thin enough that veins show through like blue tributaries. Their fingers have lengthened over generations, developing extra joints that allow them to read the carved surfaces like braille, tracing the water channels and symbol patterns with spider-like precision. Their eyes, enlarged to catch the faintest phosphorescence, reflect the blue-green glow when light finds them, giving them an aquatic appearance even in air.

The Keepers communicate in whistles that harmonize with the salamanders' calls, their larynxes evolved to produce sounds that carry efficiently through the water-filled channels. They've developed a tonal language that maps perfectly to the acoustic properties of the carved chambers—certain pitches only audible in specific rooms, creating a geographic vocabulary where location and meaning intertwine.

They wear garments woven from the silk of the pearl-pale spiders, harvested with infinite patience and spun into fabric that collects moisture from the air. These robes help regulate their body temperature and provide sustenance—the condensation that gathers on the spider silk carries trace minerals that supplement their diet. Their primary food comes from the stone-eaters themselves, which they cultivate in certain chambers like slow gardens, scraping off the crystalline growth in careful harvests that don't kill the colonies.

Each Keeper maintains a section of the cathedral, though "maintains" hardly captures their relationship to the space. They are more symbionts than custodians, their movements through the chambers following patterns laid down by centuries of predecessors. They clear blocked channels with their elongated fingers, guide the growth of stone-eater colonies away from crucial water paths, and most mysteriously, tend to the symbol-carved walls.

The Keepers' relationship with these carved symbols defies easy explanation. They don't read them in any conventional sense, but rather perform a kind of ritual interaction—tracing specific patterns at specific times, adding their own marks in response to changes in water flow or stone-eater growth. Some observers might call it maintenance, others prayer. The Keepers themselves make no such distinctions. They simply know that when certain symbols are touched in certain ways, the water behaves differently, the stone-eaters retreat or advance, and the deep structure of the cisterns shifts in subtle but crucial ways.

Their social structure mirrors the architecture they inhabit—dense, layered, difficult for outsiders to parse. They organize themselves around water flow patterns, with hierarchies that shift based on seasonal variations in the moisture levels. A Keeper who controls a chamber where three major channels converge holds more influence during the dry months, while those who tend the deepest pools rise in prominence when the water table rises.

They reproduce rarely and with great ceremony. Children are born in the darkest chambers and kept there for their first years, their developing eyes shielded from even the faint phosphorescence until their pupils have grown large enough to handle the strain. The young ones learn the whistle-language by echo, playing games where they must navigate the chambers by sound alone, their elders calling from hidden places while the children trace the acoustic patterns to find them.

The Keepers' memories stretch back through oral traditions that predate the city above. They remember—or claim to remember—when the cisterns were new, when the builders first carved the channels and discovered the properties of the stone-eaters. Their histories are encoded in their whistled songs, in the patterns they trace on the walls, in the very paths they walk through their inherited maze.

But perhaps most unnervingly, the Keepers seem to be waiting for something. Their patient tending has the quality of preparation, as if the cisterns aren't simply water storage but something more complex—a mechanism or organism that hasn't yet reached its full development. They speak in their whistling tongue of the "Opening Season," though whether this refers to a time when the cisterns will open to somewhere else, or when something within them will open, remains uncertain.

Visitors who stumble into the Cistern Cathedral (and survive the disorientation, the strange air, the easy lostness of the place) report that the Keepers are not hostile, merely indifferent—unless the intruders threaten the water flow or disturb the stone-eaters. Then the Keepers move with startling speed, their elongated limbs carrying them through the channels like salamanders, their whistles rising to pitches that seem to make the carved stone itself vibrate in warning.

They are the living memory of this place, evolved to match its strangenesses, speaking in languages of water and mineral growth, tending patterns whose ultimate purpose perhaps even they no longer fully understand—only that the patterns must be maintained, the water must flow, and the slow transformation of the Cistern Cathedral must continue uninterrupted toward whatever completion awaits in the darkness.
