The Rootworkers emerge from the green depths like creatures born from the chamber itself. They don't enter this space so much as unfold from it—bodies that have adapted over generations to navigate the dense arterial networks of the vascular chambers.

First, you mistake them for part of the growth matrix. Their skin carries a faint green undertinge, chlorophyll bonded at the cellular level through decades of symbiotic exposure. They photosynthesize, just slightly—not enough to sustain themselves, but enough to extend their working hours in the depths without surface food. Their bodies are elongated, joints hyperflexible from childhoods spent threading through root networks that would trap normal human frames.

They wear nothing but the living suits—second skins of modified lichen that process their waste, regulate temperature, and most importantly, communicate with the chamber itself through chemical signals. The suits grow with them, renewed by spores they cultivate in the dormitory levels, each one unique as a fingerprint, carrying the history of every chamber worked, every crisis navigated.

Their eyes have adapted too—pupils that dilate far wider than baseline human, irises shot through with gold filaments that help them perceive the subtle bioluminescent signals the root networks use to indicate distress, optimal growth, or system failures. In the near-darkness between the growth lights' cycles, they navigate by these signals, reading the health of the city's foundation in glows too faint for unmodified eyes.

The eldest among them, the Rootmasters, have gone further in their symbiosis. Fungal networks thread through their nervous systems—carefully cultivated, benign parasites that let them interface directly with the chamber's consciousness. They think with it, dream its slow vegetable dreams, feel the pulse of nutrients through vast networks as sensation in their own bodies. When they speak—in the rare moments when the chamber's thunder quiets enough for speech—their voices carry harmonics that resonate with the frequencies of growth itself.

Their children begin training young, brought down in groups to acclimate to the pressure, the sound, the overwhelming aliveness of the deep chambers. They learn to read growth patterns like language, to taste the chemical signatures of different subsystems on the air. By adolescence, they can diagnose a failing filtration node by the subtle sweetness in the humidity, detect a bacterial infection by the way certain roots curl.

They live in hollows carved into the chamber walls—not carved, exactly, but convinced to grow that way, spaces shaped by generations of careful chemical coaxing. Their homes are indistinguishable from the chamber itself: sleeping alcoves lined with soft moss that glows gently through the night cycle, furniture of shaped and hardened cellulose that still carries nutrients in its veins, kitchens where modified pitcher plants process waste and synthesize basic proteins.

The Rootworkers rarely surface. When they do, they move through the tourist levels like deep-sea creatures brought to shallow water—blinking in the brightness, overwhelmed by the sharp edges and dead materials of the upper city. They're paid well for their work, but most of the currency flows to families who live in the mid-levels, relatives who understand that their kin have become something both more and less than human in service to the city's living heart.

In the depths, they dance with the growth, guiding it with touches that are half-maintenance, half-worship. They whisper to particularly stubborn root clusters in frequencies that bypass the ear, felt instead in the bones. Their movements match the chamber's rhythm—when it contracts, they contract; when it reaches, they reach. They've learned that fighting the chamber's nature leads to failure, that success comes from becoming part of its patterns, another organ in its vast body.

The youngest ones still dream of the surface sometimes, of lives lived in spaces that don't pulse and breathe. But the elders know better. They've felt the city's vast consciousness through their fungal threads, experienced the profound peace of synchronization with something so much larger than themselves. They know that someone must tend the depths, must speak the language of roots and growth, must ensure the great wet heart keeps beating.

When they die—and they die younger than surface dwellers, their bodies paying the price for symbiosis—they're composted into the chamber walls. Their nutrients feed the very systems they tended, their genetic material occasionally expressing itself in new growth patterns, their memories preserved in the chemical signatures of the roots they touched. The chamber remembers them, in its way, and the other Rootworkers can sometimes taste echoes of the departed in the moisture that rains down during peak cycles.

This is their gift to the vertical city: not just maintenance, but translation between the human and the botanical, a living bridge between what the city was built to be and what it needs to survive. In the thunderous green depths, they perform their slow devotions, keeping the dialogue between species open, ensuring the city's heart never stops its ancient, engineered beating.
