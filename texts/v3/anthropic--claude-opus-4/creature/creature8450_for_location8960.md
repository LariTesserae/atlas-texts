The people of Keening Shelf are perpetual readers of stone, their bodies marked by the constant dampness of their world—skin that stays supple and never quite dries, hair that grows thick and rope-like from the minerals in the water. They move with a particular gait, always testing the ground before committing their full weight, a habit so ingrained that even their children practice it in their sleep, feet twitching in exploratory patterns.

They call themselves the Foundling Folk, for they are forever finding and losing their homes to the stone's slow breathing. Generations of adaptation have given them broad, splayed feet with semi-prehensile toes that grip wet rock, and their bones are denser than lowlanders', able to withstand the occasional collapse or sudden tilt of a floor. Their eyes have developed a peculiar quality—they can detect the minute stress patterns in stone that precede a shift, seeing in the rock what others might read in clouds.

Their society is built around the Listeners—those who press their ears to the stone for hours, learning its intentions. These individuals often develop a growth of cartilage around their ears from the constant pressure, creating natural cups that amplify the deep frequencies of moving rock. The most experienced can predict a new spring three days before it breaks through, or sense when a supporting wall is about to buckle. They wear no ornamentation save for the patterns of mineral deposits that accumulate on their skin from years of lying against wet stone—maps of copper green, iron orange, and limestone white that tell the story of where they've listened.

The youngest adults serve as Runners, lean and quick-footed messengers who leap between the city's scattered towers when the ground becomes too unstable for normal travel. They know every temporary bridge, every stone that will hold weight for just long enough, every gap that can be cleared with the right momentum. Their legs are scarred from countless small failures, badges of honor that they compare in the common houses where people gather when their own homes are being relocated.

In the deep dwelling chambers carved into the most stable rock, live the Shapers—artisans who work with the city's strange materials. They cultivate the bioluminescent fungi in careful patterns, creating living murals that shift and grow. They harvest the shells of the moisture snails, some as large as a human head, grinding them into the mortar that holds the city together. Most prized are the Memory Keepers among them, who maintain collections of stone samples from every major shift, creating archives of the plateau's geological history that they can read like books, running their fingers over samples to recall which buildings stood where, which families lived above which springs.

The water-workers are perhaps the most crucial guild, forever rebuilding the city's system of channels and temporary aqueducts. They work in teams, some holding wooden frames while others pack them with the specialized clay that stays flexible enough to survive minor tremors. Their children learn to swim before they walk, playing in the shallow pools and learning to sense the underground connections between them. The most skilled can navigate the entire city through its hidden water passages, emerging from wells and springs like spirits.

But it's the Night Singers who truly define Keening Shelf's culture. When darkness falls and the moths rise in their thunderous clouds, these individuals climb the highest towers and sing to the pale climbing beasts, their voices carrying across the plateau in harmonies that blend with the stone's groaning and the water's constant voice. The songs are never written down—they change nightly, improvised to match the plateau's shifting mood. The climbing beasts have learned these songs over generations, and some say they sing back, their hollow bones creating haunting countermelodies that make travelers weep with inexplicable longing.

The children of Keening Shelf play games no lowland child would recognize. They build temporary structures from loose stones, competing to see whose creation can survive the longest before the ground shifts. They keep pet boring worms in jars of wet clay, racing them through miniature landscapes. They learn to taste the water from different springs, identifying minerals that might signal a change coming to that part of the plateau. By the time they reach adulthood, they can sleep through tremors that would terrify visitors, and wake instantly at the specific frequency that means real danger.

Some few are born different—the Deep Touched, they're called. These individuals seem to sense the plateau's changes in their very bones, often suffering from aches and pains that correspond to distant geological events. They're both revered and pitied, living lives of constant discomfort but possessing an almost supernatural ability to predict the stone's behavior. Many become hermits, dwelling in the most remote parts of the plateau where they can suffer their gift in solitude, emerging only to warn of major shifts.

The Foundling Folk have no concept of permanent ownership—how could they, when the ground itself refuses such stability? Instead, they practice a complex system of temporary claims and communal support. When a family's home tilts beyond saving, the entire neighborhood arrives to help dismantle and relocate it. They share tools, children, knowledge, and stories with the fluid grace of water finding its level. Their only permanent structures are the Marking Stones—ancient boulders too large to move, carved with the names of every person who has ever lived on the plateau, a record that grows daily as the stone itself grows, new surfaces emerging to be inscribed.

In this place of constant change, they've found a strange peace. They dance when the rain comes, letting it soak through their woven moss cloaks. They sing to their children about the beauty of impermanence, teaching them that home is not a place but a practice of adaptation. They've learned to love the very instability that would drive others mad, finding in the plateau's endless becoming a reflection of their own perpetual growth.

And in the deepest chambers, where the stone is oldest and most stable, the eldest of the Foundling Folk gather to tell the most sacred stories—not of the past, but of the future. They speak of the day when the plateau will finally settle, when the last spring will emerge and the stone will sleep. They wonder what they'll become then, when the constant change that has shaped them finally ends. Some say they'll leave, seeking new places of transformation. Others believe they'll finally be able to build something lasting.

But most simply smile and say that such a day will never come—that the plateau chose them as much as they chose it, and that they are partners in an eternal dance of stone, water, and adaptation. They point to their children, already learning to read the rock's intentions, already developing the thick, mineral-touched hair and the sure-footed gait of their kind, and they know that whether the stone settles or not, the Foundling Folk will continue, shaped by change into something beautiful and strange and perfectly suited to their ever-becoming world.
