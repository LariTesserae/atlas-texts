The people of the Cistern Gardens move like water finding its level—slow, inevitable, shaped by the stone that contains them.

They are a patient breed, grown tall and pale from generations in the caldera's filtered light. Their bodies have adapted: elongated fingers for climbing the wet stone, eyes that dilate wide in the constant mist, skin that never quite dries. They wear their hair long, often braided with thin copper wires that serve as conductors, grounding them against the electromagnetic fields that surge through the settlement. When they work near the salamanders, their hair stands on end, crackling with static that smells of ozone and moss.

The Gardeners form the largest caste—tenders of the vertical farms who speak in whispers that carry surprisingly far in the humid air. They know each plant by its water consumption, can taste the mineral content in a leaf and tell you which level of the caldera it grew on. Their clothes are living things, woven from algae fibers that photosynthesize in the light shafts, changing from deep green to golden as they move between levels. They prune with one hand while the other traces the water channels, always aware of the flow, redirecting streams with subtle movements of carved stone gates.

The Machinists inhabit the deeper caves, their skin taking on a permanent sheen from the oil vapor. They understand the factory equipment through touch and sound—the pitch of a properly tuned turbine, the tremor that warns of sediment in the pipes. They've learned to work in harmony with the salamanders, reading the creatures' light patterns to predict electromagnetic surges. Some say the eldest Machinists can speak to the machines directly, their nervous systems having adapted to perceive electrical fields as a sixth sense.

Children here are born quiet. They learn to swim before they walk, navigating the flooded lower levels with amphibian grace. They play games with the refractive insects, creating light patterns on the walls, and dare each other to stare into the lake's prophetic waters. By adolescence, they must choose their path—up toward the gardens and light, or down toward the machines and mysteries.

The Readers are perhaps the strangest residents. They spend their lives studying the patterns—in salamander light-shows, in the fractal arrangements of bird flocks, in the impossible reflections of the lake. They claim these patterns form a language, or perhaps a formula, that describes something fundamental about the intersection of the organic and mechanical. They live in the middle levels, spiral notebooks filled with observations that grow increasingly abstract, their handwriting evolving into symbols that mirror the salamanders' skin patterns.

All residents share certain customs. They eat communally but in silence, gathering in carved amphitheaters where the only sound is the clink of volcanic glass bowls and the eternal drip of water. Their food is subtle—fermented moss, fruit whose flavors change as you chew, salamander eggs that pop with electric sensation on the tongue. They mark time not by days but by the great turnings of the water wheel at the caldera's heart, a massive mechanism that takes exactly one hundred days to complete a revolution.

Their art is functional—every carved channel is also beautiful, every machine component shaped with unnecessary grace. They write in condensation on windows, temporary poems that last only until the next drop falls. Their music is industrial ambient, created by tuning the factory equipment to harmonious frequencies that resonate through the stone.

The eldest among them have begun to change in deeper ways. Their movements synchronize unconsciously with the settlement's rhythm. Their eyes reflect too much light. Some have developed patches of skin with the same fractal patterns as the salamanders, and when they stand near the machines, the patterns pulse in time with the electrical currents. They speak of eventually "graduating"—though to what, no one will say.

At night (though day and night blur in the caldera's constant twilight), they dream collectively. The humidity carries pheromones, the electromagnetic fields influence brain waves, and gradually all the sleeping minds of the Cistern Gardens begin to sync. They dream of being water, of being stone, of the time before the volcano died, of the time when the last salamander will switch off the last light.

They are not quite human anymore, these gardener-mechanics. But in their patient cultivation of this impossible ecosystem, in their acceptance of the strange symphony of the industrial-natural world, they have become something perfectly adapted to their vertical garden—a new form of life as beautiful and unsettling as the creatures they tend.
