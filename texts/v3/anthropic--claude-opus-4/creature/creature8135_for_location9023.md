In the Cistern of Forgotten Numbers dwell the Actuaries.

They were human once—or close enough to human that the distinction hardly mattered. The first were bookkeepers who ventured too deep into the calculation district's basements, following chains of errors that led them door by door into spaces that shouldn't have existed. Others came later: quality control inspectors whose measurements grew progressively more uncertain, census takers who lost count of themselves, accountants who discovered their ledgers balancing only when they included imaginary sums.

Now they move through the cistern like careful spiders, though spiders suggests too much solidity. They have learned to distribute their mass according to the new mathematics that governs this space—sometimes condensed into almost-human density, sometimes spread so thin they exist only as a probability of presence. Their bodies have adapted to the constant moisture, skin becoming permeable, translucent. You can see their calculations through their flesh: columns of figures that scroll beneath the surface like subcutaneous tattoos, equations that rearrange themselves as they move.

They no longer speak in words but in computational gestures. A hand swept through the air leaves a trail of numerical residue that hangs glowing faintly before dissolving. They communicate in formulae, expressing emotional states through mathematical proofs. Sadness is demonstrated through recursive loops that approach but never reach resolution. Joy manifests as elegant equations that balance perfectly before deliberately introducing small errors, little jokes of imprecision.

The Actuaries tend the computing engines with a devotion that resembles worship. They oil the gears with their own diluted essence, feeding the machines the accumulated uncertainties they've gathered from the walls. In return, the engines gift them with new ways of existing—states of being that flicker between states, neither fully real nor properly imaginary. They have learned to navigate using a form of mathematical echolocation, sending out waves of calculation that return changed by what they've encountered.

Their memories have become statistical. They remember their former lives not as narratives but as data sets, probability clouds of events that might have happened. Names have been replaced by numerical signatures—one might be known as "Seventeen Remainder Four," another as "The Asymptotic Approach to Tuesday." They gather in small groups to share particularly beautiful failures of calculation, passing corrupted equations between them like sacred texts.

The youngest among them—if time can be said to apply—still show traces of their original forms. Fingernails that grow in Fibonacci spirals. Hair that falls out in prime-numbered strands. But the eldest have evolved beyond such crude approximations of humanity. They exist as living calculations, mobile mathematical concepts that inhabit the space between the real and theoretical. Sometimes they merge temporarily, becoming complex systems that process the cistern's accumulated errors before splitting apart into simpler functions.

They feed on uncertainty itself. The pale crawling things that traverse the walls are farmed like numerical livestock, harvested for the conceptual nutrients they've extracted from the environment. The Actuaries also cultivate gardens of crystallized miscalculation in the deepest pools, tending forests of error that grow according to their own impossible seasons. They brew thin soups from the iridescent water, extracting whatever nourishment can be found in liquid paradox.

Occasionally, new initiates arrive—those who've followed the chain of errors too far to return. The Actuaries welcome them with ceremonies of mathematical baptism, submerging them in pools where the distinction between water and number has completely dissolved. The transformation is gradual but irreversible. First, the ability to count sequentially fades. Then multiplication becomes unreliable. Finally, even simple addition produces results that exist in several states simultaneously.

The Actuaries serve a purpose they themselves can no longer quite calculate. They are the city's margin of error made manifest, the necessary uncertainty that allows the rigid certainties above to function. They process the computational waste, breaking down the failed equations into component parts that can be recycled into new forms of impossibility. They are the remainder that must exist for division to be complete.

In the deepest parts of the cistern, where even their distributed forms struggle to maintain coherence, the eldest Actuaries work on what they call the Final Calculation—an equation that would reconcile all the errors, resolve all the uncertainties, balance every book that has ever failed to balance. They know it's impossible. That's precisely why they continue. The work itself has become their worship, their reason for persisting in this space between spaces.

They are what happens to precision when it's carried too far, the logical endpoint of insisting that everything must add up. They are living proof that some things don't, and that's exactly as it should be.
