The Weavers have evolved beyond human recognition, though something in their movements still echoes their ancestry. Their bodies stretch like living rope—torsos elongated to twice human length, limbs extended and jointed in too many places. Their fingers have multiplied and lengthened into dozens of specialized appendages: some thick as thumbs for hauling heavy cord, others fine as spider silk for detail work, all of them constantly moving, testing tension, reading the vibrations in the weave like a language.

Their skin has become something between leather and bark, deeply furrowed and patterned with the same spiral grain as twisted rope. It weeps a constant sheen of moisture that keeps them from desiccating in the merciless sun. Where scars cross their bodies—and they all bear countless scars from a lifetime of cuts and rope burns—the tissue has healed into raised cords that mirror the very materials they work with. Some deliberately scarify themselves in patterns that mark their lineage, their specialty, their greatest works added to the continent.

They move through the lattice like vertical spiders, all four limbs and their prehensile spine working in concert. Their feet have become hands, their hands have become something more complex—radiating digits that can grip a dozen strands at once, testing each for weakness, feeling for the perfect tension. They think in three dimensions always, understanding load and stress through their bones. Children learn to braid before they learn to speak.

Their eyes have adapted to the constant glare above and shadow below—pupils that contract to pinpricks in the noon sun, irises that dilate until they swallow the sclera when working in the deep hollows. Many develop a nictitating membrane, translucent and tough, that lets them work through the burning afternoons without pause. They see into spectrums that reveal moisture content in the fibers, spot weakness in a cord from a hundred meters away, track the heat signatures of small prey moving through the lower levels.

The constant sound has shaped their communication into something beyond speech. They sing to each other across vast distances, their voices pitched to carry through the specific resonances of the rope-world. Close up, they speak as much through vibration as sound—pressing their throats against the strands to send messages drumming through the network. Their language has words for ten thousand different qualities of fiber, for every possible knot and weaving pattern, for tensions and textures that no other tongue could name.

In the settlements, the very old ones have begun to root. Their skin puts out tendrils that wrap around nearby cords, drawing moisture and nutrients directly from the weave. They become living fixtures, their knowledge accessible but their bodies increasingly part of the structure itself. The young bring them offerings of rare fibers and new specimens, and in return receive instruction in the deepest mysteries of the craft—how to read the health of the whole through a single strand's vibration, how to predict where the weave will fail before it shows any sign of weakness.

They dream collectively when they sleep, all those touching the same section of rope sharing visions of new patterns, new expansions. In these dreams they see the continent as it truly is—not solid but breathing, growing, its billion individual strands functioning as neurons in a vast mind they're both creating and becoming part of. Some say the original weavers achieved this unity completely, their consciousness fully merged with their creation. Others insist that's just metaphor, though they can't explain the moments when the entire structure seems to anticipate stress, shifting and adjusting before any human eye could spot the need.

Their tools have become extensions of their bodies. The stone knives fit perfectly into palm-hollows worn by generations of use. The bone needles are carved from their own dead, each one unique to its user's grip. They oil their implements with their own secretions, maintaining them with the same instinctive care they show their children. Some bloodlines pass down tools for centuries, the handles worn smooth as water stones, the working edges maintained atom by atom.

They eat what the weave provides—the starchy cores of young rope-plants, the protein-rich grubs that infest old fibers, fermented pastes made from the bioluminescent fungi. Water is sacred; they've developed rituals around its collection and distribution that approach religious significance. During the storms, everyone who can move joins the water harvest, spreading their bodies flat against the mesh to channel runoff into communal vessels. They can survive on drops per day when necessary, their metabolism slowing to near-hibernation during the worst droughts.

Their reproduction has adapted to their vertical world. Infants are born with gripping reflexes so strong they can support their own weight immediately. Mothers weave elaborate harnesses that distribute children across their bodies while they work, the young ones learning the rhythms of construction before they can crawl. Coming-of-age involves a solitary descent into the deepest accessible levels, where initiates must harvest materials and return with something novel—a new fiber, an undiscovered knot, proof they can contribute to the eternal expansion.

Some among them have gone farther in their adaptations. The deep workers who maintain the lowest levels have lost pigmentation entirely, navigating by vibration and echolocation in the permanent darkness. The high masters who oversee the edge construction have developed photosynthetic patches on their skin, supplementing their diet with direct solar energy. The bridge builders who span the gaps between rope-mountains have arm spans that exceed their height threefold, their bones hollow as birds' to reduce weight on the swaying cables.

Yet for all their changes, something essentially human persists in their drive to build, to extend, to create meaning through making. They name their children after famous knots. They compose epic songs about legendary weavings. They compete in speed-braiding and strength-testing, their championships drawing crowds to arenas woven for the purpose. They fall in love with those whose rhythm matches theirs, whose tension preferences complement their own, whose vision of the pattern aligns with theirs.

They know, in some deep part of their collective understanding, that they're creating more than a structure. Each new layer adds to something incomprehensibly vast, a project beyond any individual comprehension. They build because their ancestors built, because their children will build, because the building itself has become the purpose—world without end, rope upon rope, the continent growing beneath the merciless sun while they swarm across its surface, singing their work songs into the endless, echoing deep.
