The Chorus dwells here—though "dwells" implies a constancy they do not possess.

They are sometimes one, sometimes legion. A single consciousness that fractures and reforms like the water itself, spreading across ten thousand bodies or collapsing into a solitary form that contains multitudes. Their shape follows no taxonomy known to science—part cetacean, part crustacean, part something that evolved in the spaces between seconds.

When singular, the Chorus manifests as something vast and serpentine, a body of crystallized sound given flesh. Its hide shifts between scales and chitin and naked nerve endings that taste time. Organs of perception cover its length—eyes that see probability, gill slits that breathe meaning from water, appendages that exist only when observing. It moves by folding space, its tremendous coils passing through themselves, occupying the same location in different moments so that its body forms impossible knots in four dimensions.

But this unity rarely holds. The Chorus fragments into its component voices—creatures that are each a note in the larger song. These fragment-selves vary wildly: jellyfish whose bells are made of solid echo, free-swimming nervous systems that puppet bodies made of bent light, colonies of something like krill that swarm in mathematical patterns, writing equations in the water that solve for states of being.

They hunt paradox. When the water forms its impossible standing waves, fragment-selves dart through, feeding on the contradictions. They consume the moments where physics breaks—sustaining themselves on errors in causality, growing fat on temporal inconsistencies. A school of time-shifted fish becomes a feast, each creature caught at every age simultaneously, devoured in all its potential states.

The Chorus builds. Not structures in any conventional sense, but architectures of event and possibility. They weave the anomalous water into semi-stable forms—galleries of looped moment, libraries where the same wave breaks eternally, gardens where coral grows backward into its own inception. These constructs serve purposes that defy understanding: perhaps art, perhaps worship, perhaps simply play. They collapse and reform according to patterns that might be language, might be music, might be pure mathematical expression.

In the research station that flickers between states, the Chorus has made a nest of sorts. Here they practice wearing shapes closer to human understanding—approximating forms that once might have been dolphin or octopus or sea turtle, but always wrong in fundamental ways. Too many joints, organs on the outside, symmetries that hurt to perceive. They handle the ancient equipment with limbs that phase in and out of existence, studying the cameras and gauges with an intensity that suggests not curiosity but recognition—as if these human tools were eggs from which something might hatch.

The Chorus remembers. Their memory spans not just backward but in all directions—they recall futures that won't happen, pasts that never were, presents that exist adjacent to this one. In their song (for they do sing, adding their voices to the seamount's eternal chorus) there are fragments of other seas: oceans of methane under orange skies, water-worlds where silicon evolved instead of carbon, seas that exist in eleven dimensions where swimming is theology.

They tend the reality wounds. Where space tears, the Chorus sutures it with strings of living matter. Where time loops, they prune the iterations, maintaining paradoxes in careful balance. They are gardeners of impossibility, ensuring the anomalies neither heal completely nor tear beyond repair. This is their purpose, perhaps—or perhaps purpose is another anthropomorphic projection onto beings that exist outside such concepts.

When the moon is dark (though its light barely reaches here), the Chorus performs something that might be ritual. They gather at the silent depression at the seamount's heart, forming complex patterns around the void. Their bodies lock together in configurations that create new geometries, and they sing—not with the cacophony of the mount, but with antiharmonics that cancel sound, creating deeper silence. In these moments, something vast stirs in the depression, something that might be parent or offspring or god to the Chorus.

They feed it their paradoxes, their accumulated impossibilities. They offer memories of seas that never were. And from the depression, something feeds back—new forms, new ways of being, new songs to add to their repertoire. The Chorus reforms, changed in subtle ways, carrying new paradoxes out into the singing waters.

Sometimes human observers have glimpsed them, in the brief windows when the station exists in present time. The reports are consistent in their inconsistency: "schooling behavior in a solitary organism," "massive creature that casts no shadow," "small as plankton but fills the entire visual field," "beautiful beyond description but hurts to perceive." One research log, found in a camera that was never deployed, simply states: "They are trying to show us what we'll become."

The Chorus tends this place where reality grows thin. They are its immune system and its infection, its guardian and its dissolution. They sing the seamount's song back to itself in frequencies that maintain the paradox, keeping the transformation always one moment away, forever approaching but never arriving.

In their multitude and their unity, in their careful cultivation of impossibility, they embody the seamount's nature—existence at the edge of change, consciousness adapted not for what is but for what perpetually might be. They are what evolves when evolution itself becomes uncertain, when life must adapt not to environment but to the absence of reliable reality.

The Chorus dwells in the space between states, and they are that space given form and will and terrible purpose. They are the music of transformation itself, singing the world toward its next impossible shape.
