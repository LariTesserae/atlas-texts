The Root-Dwellers move through their world with the unhurried certainty of sap. They are a people shaped by centuries of filtered light and close air, their bodies adapted to navigate the eternal twilight of the Sprawl.

Their skin carries the pallor of deep mushrooms, not sickly but luminous, as if they've absorbed the faint phosphorescence of the lichens they cultivate. When they move through darker passages, this subtle glow becomes visible—a gentle radiance that pools in the hollows of throats, the curves of palms, the spaces between fingers. The children call it "wood-light" and play games in the deepest corridors, pressing their hands against root-walls to leave brief, glowing prints.

They are tall but bent, shaped by generations of ducking through low passages, of sleeping in curved hollows. Their spines have developed a natural curl that fits perfectly into the swooping architecture of root-halls. Even the elderly move with fluid grace through spaces that would confound surface-dwellers, their bodies knowing every irregular step, every low-hanging arch, through a kind of ancestral memory written in bone and muscle.

Their hands are their most distinctive feature—long-fingered, with palms hardened from gripping rough bark, but fingertips kept soft and sensitive. These are hands that read wood like text, that can feel the health of a root through its skin, detect the first signs of beneficial fungus or harmful rot. The Tappers especially develop calluses in precise patterns, marks of their trade that they wear proudly, comparing the whorls and ridges like badges of honor.

Hair grows long and thick among the Root-Dwellers, often reaching their waists or beyond. They rarely cut it, instead braiding it with thin root-fibers and fragments of shed moth-silk. The style of braiding indicates profession and family line—water-carriers weave blue-black beetles' shells into their plaits, while the growth-guides incorporate living shoots that continue growing, creating crowns of green that must be tended daily. Children wear their hair loose until their apprenticeship begins, when they receive their first professional braid from a master of their chosen craft.

Their clothing grows as much as it is made. The silk-moths' produce is gathered carefully, spun with plant fibers into a textile that breathes like skin. Over time, these garments develop their own ecosystems—colonies of beneficial bacteria that clean sweat, tiny fungi that repair small tears, microscopic mites that eat dead skin cells. A Root-Dweller's robe becomes more valuable with age, more perfectly adapted to its wearer's body chemistry. They pass these living garments through generations, each wearer adding their own biological signature to the fabric.

They speak in liquid syllables, their language evolved to carry in the sound-absorbing environment. Consonants are soft, vowels prolonged and resonant. They've developed a complex system of harmonics—conversations between three or more people create intentional chord progressions, with meaning carried not just in words but in the interplay of tones. In the deep halls where sound behaves strangely, they switch to a visual language of hand movements, fingers dancing in patterns that mimic root growth, water flow, the movement of beetles across bark.

Their eyes have grown large over generations, pupils dilated permanently to gather every fragment of green-filtered light. But they rely as much on touch as on sight, navigating with trailing fingers, reading the health of their world through their palms. Children learn to close their eyes and find their way home by the texture of roots alone—this smooth patch where grandfather carved his marriage poem, that rough section where the water-beetles congregate, the warm spot where steam rises from a deep spring.

The Root-Dwellers organize themselves around the nodes, each major intersection supporting a population that ranges from dozens to hundreds. But they are not sedentary. Families maintain circuits through the Sprawl, following seasonal patterns older than memory. When the upper roots bloom, certain families journey upward to harvest petals for medicine. When the deep springs run cold, others descend to the mineralized halls to gather crystal-water for their most sacred ceremonies.

They mark time not by sun or stars but by the growth rings in the roots around them. A "year" is the time it takes for a taproot to add a ring of growth—slightly longer than a surface year, as they've discovered through trade with the overworlders. Births are recorded by scarification marks on designated "memory roots," deaths by the planting of new shoots that will eventually join the great network.

Their medicine is the medicine of symbiosis. They cultivate beneficial fungi on their own bodies—white webs that grow across shoulders and backs, processing toxins, producing antibiotics, creating a living pharmacy that each person carries. The healers know which beetles to encourage, which moths to avoid, how to read illness in the pattern of lichens that grow on a sick person's dwelling walls. They treat the root-system itself as a patient, knowing that the health of their world and their bodies are inextricably linked.

Music among the Root-Dwellers is architecture. They shape growing chambers specifically for sound, training roots to create acoustic spaces that amplify certain frequencies. Their instruments are hollowed gourds, stretched moth-silk, the shaped cavities of living wood that resonate with breath or touch. But their greatest performances involve the roots themselves—careful scarification that causes the wood to grow into natural flutes, percussion surfaces that develop over decades, entire groves that sing when the wind moves through the canopy far above.

They dream collectively. Whether through some property of the air they breathe, the water they drink, or simply the closeness of their living, Root-Dwellers often share sleep-visions. Families wake having experienced the same wandering through unmapped root-halls. Lovers separated by the slow travel of the Sprawl find each other in dreams, exchanging information that proves accurate upon reunion. The deepest dreamers, those who sleep for days in the mineralized chambers, claim to hear the thoughts of the roots themselves—slow, vast, almost mineral in their contemplation.

Death among the Root-Dwellers is a return. Bodies are placed in specially prepared chambers where the roots are young and hungry. Over months, the flesh becomes wood, the wood becomes part of the greater organism. They believe consciousness persists in some form within the root-network, that their ancestors' thoughts move with the sap, manifest in the occasional whisper of sound from walls that should be silent. The oldest families claim they can recognize their dead in the grain of certain roots—a curl that mirrors grandmother's smile, a knot that resembles father's worried brow.

Children grow slowly in the Undergrowth, taking twenty years or more to reach what surface folk would call adulthood. They spend their early years in the common nurseries, tended by whoever is present, learning the basic skills of navigation, water-finding, growth-reading. By ten, they begin following different adults on their rounds, discovering their affinities. Some show talent for the delicate work of moth-keeping, others for the patience of growth-guiding, still others for the deep listening required of water-singers who map the underground flows by sound.

Their technology is biological mastery disguised as simplicity. They've bred beetles that excrete specific pheromones to guide root growth. They've developed fungi that can dissolve stone, allowing them to expand the Sprawl into new territories. Their understanding of hydraulics, expressed through living wood channels, rivals any surface engineer's. But all of this appears as natural as breathing, technologies so integrated into daily life that they seem less like tools than like properties of existence itself.

The Root-Dwellers trade with the surface, but carefully, at specific sites where roots break through into caves or hollows. They exchange silk, medicine, the precious crystal-water for metal tools, specific seeds, news of the wider world. But many have never seen the sky, and few wish to. They know that their world extends far beyond what any individual could explore in a lifetime—always another passage, another node, another depth where the roots grow strange and the water tastes of deep earth.

They are not a numerous people—the Sprawl could support millions, but they number perhaps a hundred thousand, spread through the vast network. They prefer it this way, moving through their world like sparse lymph through a body, numerous enough to maintain the system but never so many as to strain it. Population control is achieved through custom rather than stricture—large families are seen as gauche, greedy, too noisy for the quiet depths.

In their art, they celebrate the intermediate: the moment when water becomes sap, when wood becomes stone, when sound becomes silence. They carve masks from fungus-softened wood that continue changing after the carving is complete. They compose poems that rely on the echo patterns of specific chambers, meaningless if spoken elsewhere. Their greatest artists are those who work with time itself—planning installations that will not mature for generations, collaborating with growth and decay across centuries.

The Root-Dwellers know they are not the first to inhabit the Sprawl. They find artifacts in the deepest places—tools of unknown metal, carvings in scripts they cannot read, chambers whose purpose remains mysterious. But they feel no urgency to solve these puzzles. Like the roots themselves, they understand that time moves in long cycles, that what is hidden will eventually be revealed, that their own artifacts will someday puzzle future inhabitants who will make their own slow way through the breathing dark.

They call themselves, in their liquid language, something that translates roughly as "Those Who Move Between." Between root and stone, between water and wood, between the made and the grown. They are the intermediaries, the connective tissue of a world that exists in the spaces between what surface-dwellers think of as real. And in their patient cultivation of this intermediate realm, they have achieved something remarkable: a civilization that grows itself, that dreams itself, that continues itself not through conquest or expansion but through the simple, endless act of being present in the living dark.
