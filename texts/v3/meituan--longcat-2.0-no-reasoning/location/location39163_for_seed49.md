# The Drown

---

You stand at the edge of a city that has no edge.

The ground beneath you is not ground. It is a membrane — a living, breathing, sweating skin of compressed matter so dense that your feet sink only a millimeter before the surface pushes back, firm and warm, like standing on the flank of something enormous that has decided, for now, to hold still. The membrane is dark, almost black, but shot through with veins of bioluminescent circuitry that pulse in slow arterial rhythms. It is wet. Everything here is wet. The air itself weeps. Droplets form on your eyelashes, on the back of your hands, on every surface, and they do not fall — they cling, trembling, before being reabsorbed into the skin of the world.

The city rises from this membrane in every direction, and it is *packed*. Not packed the way a human city is packed, with buildings and streets and the illusion of space between them. Packed the way a circuit board is packed. Packed the way a lung is packed with alveoli. Structures press against structures press against structures, and there is no gap, no alley, no breath of open air. The buildings — if they are buildings — are grown rather than built, extruded from the membrane in forms that suggest towers, spirals, honeycombs, cathedral ribs, and geometries that have no name in any human language. They are made of a material that looks like wet obsidian but moves like muscle, slowly contracting and expanding, and where two structures meet they *fuse*, sharing walls, sharing systems, sharing something deeper than architecture. The city is one organism wearing the mask of ten million buildings.

And it is *vast*. You cannot see the end of it. You cannot see a horizon. The structures rise and fall in waves that extend beyond sight, beyond the curve of the world itself, because the world here is not a sphere — it is a body, and you are standing on its skin, and the skin goes on and on, folding into itself at distances where geometry starts to stutter. Weather forms in the middle distance: not clouds, but *events* — columns of superheated vapor that rise from vents in the membrane and collide with the wet air, producing storms that are localized, furious, and brief. You watch one now, perhaps two kilometers away: a white pillar of steam that blooms into a roiling cap, crackling with discharges of light that are not lightning but something more deliberate, more *coded*. The storm lasts eleven seconds. It collapses. The membrane drinks the water back.

The sound is the first thing that breaks you.

It is *deafening*, but not in the way a machine is deafening, or a waterfall, or a war. It is the sound of *everything happening at once, everywhere, all the time*. The membrane hums at a frequency you feel in your molars. The structures groan and click as they shift against each other, a constant tectonic negotiation. The fauna — and there is so much fauna — contributes its own impossible chorus. Things move through the fused corridors of the city: creatures that are not quite animals, not quite machines, not quite anything. They are dense with purpose. Some are small, skittering along the wet surfaces on limbs that end in fractal brushes, leaving trails of light. Some are large, slow, elephantine, their bodies segmented and translucent, and inside them you can see *other things moving* — passengers, parasites, organs that might be engines. They communicate in bursts of sound that overlap and interfere, creating standing waves of noise that make the air thicken and thin in patterns you can almost read. The sound is not random. It is *language*, or it is *traffic*, or it is the sound of a city that has been talking to itself for so long it has forgotten how to be quiet.

There is no plant life. There is no soil, no leaf, no root, no green. The ecology here runs on different principles. The base of the food chain — if there is a food chain — appears to be the membrane itself, which exudes a warm, mineral-rich fluid that pools in the crevices between structures. Things feed on this fluid. Other things feed on those things. The waste products are reabsorbed. The cycle is closed, total, and it has been running for so long that the system has achieved a kind of terrible perfection. Nothing is wasted. Nothing is lost. The city eats itself and is never full.

The water is everywhere and it is *warm*. It seeps from the membrane, condenses on every surface, runs in rivulets down the fused walls of the structures, collects in basins that form and unform as the city breathes. The humidity is absolute. The air is a liquid you can barely breathe. It tastes of iron and ozone and something sweet and organic that you do not want to identify. Pools of standing water dot the surface, and in them you can see reflections that do not match the sky above — because the sky here is not a sky. It is a ceiling of membrane, far overhead, dark and veined with light, and it is *close*. The world is enclosed. You are inside something. The reflections in the pools show a different city, or the same city from a different angle, or the city as it was, or as it will be. The water remembers.

The light is normal — or it would be, if anything here were normal. It comes from everywhere and nowhere: the bioluminescent veins, the discharges in the distance, the translucent bodies of the creatures, the faint glow of the membrane itself. It is enough to see by. It is enough to see that the city extends in every direction without limit, that it is alive, that it is *aware* of you in the way that a body is aware of a splinter — not with malice, not with curiosity, but with the slow, inevitable attention of an immune system that has detected something foreign.

And the city is *changing*. Constantly. Turbulently. Structures that were fused an hour ago separate and reconfigure. New extrusions push up from the membrane like blisters, already fully formed, already occupied. The fauna flows through the shifting architecture like blood through capillaries, never pausing, never lost, navigating a city that is never the same twice. The storms come and go. The membrane sweats and drinks. The sound never stops. The water never dries. The light never fades.

You are standing in a place that is not a place. You are standing on the skin of something that is not a world. The city does not care that you are here. The city does not care about anything except the continuation of its own impossible, saturated, deafening, wet, warm, packed, turbulent existence.

The membrane shifts beneath your feet.

Something underneath you *moves*.
