# The Hanging Garden of Vael

---

You come upon it from below, climbing.

The trail — if you can call it that — is a series of iron eyelets driven into basalt, each one a foot above the last, vanishing upward into a haze that is not fog but *thinness*, the air itself going transparent as it climbs. The rock face is wet. Not streaming, not pooled, but *damp*, perpetually — beaded with condensation that never quite drips, clings instead in a skin of moisture like something breathing through its pores. Your fingertips come away slick. The iron rings are cold enough to sting.

This is a high place. Not merely elevated — *extreme* elevation, the kind where the world below has been not abandoned but *withdrawn*, pulled back like a tide, leaving you stranded on a narrow shelf of rock that should not be able to hold what grows here.

And what grows here is *lush*.

That is the first wrongness. At this altitude, on this cold — a cold that seeps through your clothing and into the marrow, a dry cold that exists alongside the wetness without resolving it — there should be nothing. Lichen, perhaps. Maybe a tenacious moss. Instead: *abundance*. The shelf widens as you climb, and the vegetation thickens with it, and you realize you are not approaching a cliff face at all but entering a *structure*, a broad ramp spiraling inward toward a central hollow, like the inside of a nautilus shell.

The plants are not familiar. Or rather, they are *almost* familiar, the way a word on the tip of your tongue is almost known. Something like ferns, but silver-blue, their fronds curling upward rather than unfurling, each one tipped with a bead of moisture that catches the light and holds it — actually *holds* it, glowing faintly even when you step into shadow, as though the plants have borrowed illumination and are slow to return it. Something like moss, but upright, finger-height, dense in mats that carpet the stone and pulse with slow peristaltic motion when you are not looking directly at them. Something like lichen, but it *moves* — a crustose bloom spreading across the wet basalt in fractal patterns of pale gold, advancing by millimeters per hour, and when you watch it, really watch it, you can see the individual hyphal tips reaching, testing, withdrawing, reaching again.

The light is extraordinary.

It comes from above and everywhere and nowhere. The sky here is not blue but *white*, a luminous pale glare that seems to emanate from the air itself, as though the atmosphere at this height has become its own source. There are clouds, but they are *below* you — you can see them through gaps in the shelf, an ocean of them, rolling and breaking against distant peaks that rise from the cloud-sea like dark islands. Above: nothing but brilliance. You squint. Your eyes water. The silver-blue plants do not squint. They lean into it, every frond oriented upward, and the beads of moisture at their tips flare like tiny lenses.

The air is thin but it carries sound strangely. You hear your own breathing, your footsteps on the wet stone, the creak of the iron rings. And beneath that — beneath the threshold of attention — a *drone*. Low, subsonic, felt in the sternum more than the ear. It is not mechanical. It is not wind. It is the sound of *life at scale*, the aggregate respiration of thousands of organisms breathing in unison, and it rises and falls with a rhythm that is not quite regular, not quite irregular, like a heartbeat that is thinking about whether to continue.

The fauna announces itself in pieces.

A sound like a stone being tapped with a smaller stone — *tik, tik, tik* — and you turn to see something the size of your fist perched on a silver frond, a creature with too many legs and a carapace the exact color and sheen of the wet basalt. It is looking at you. You are certain it is looking at you, though it has no eyes that you can identify. It taps again. The frond does not bend under its weight.

Further along the spiral, the mats of upright moss part, and you see them: small, pale, segmented things moving through the vegetation in lines, following chemical trails you cannot perceive. They are blind. They are *numerous*. They move with the slow confidence of creatures who have never had a predator. Their bodies are translucent, and you can see the fluid moving inside them, and it glows faintly the same gold as the lichen, and you understand that these creatures *are* the lichen, or the lichen is them, or they are both expressions of something else that does not distinguish between plant and animal.

A shadow passes over you. You look up. Something vast and quiet is drifting across the white sky, too high to resolve into detail — only a shape, a suggestion of wings or fins or something that is neither, leaving a wake in the brilliant air like heat shimmer. It does not circle back. It is not interested in you.

The built things appear gradually.

First: a cairn. Just stones, stacked with obvious intention, placed at the junction where the spiral shelf branches — one path continuing upward, the other cutting inward toward the central hollow. The stones are not local. They are granite, banded with quartz, and they have no business being here. Someone carried them. Someone placed them. The cairn is old — old in the way that things are old when they have been tended, when hands have maintained them across generations — but the *stones* are older. Ancient. They predate the shelf, the plants, perhaps the mountain itself.

Further in: a shelter. Or the remains of one. Three walls of stacked basalt, dry-fitted, no mortar, open to the sky. The fourth wall is collapsed, and the silver ferns have colonized it completely, growing from the cracks between stones, threading through the structure until it is impossible to say where the wall ends and the vegetation begins. Inside: a flat stone, worn smooth, with a depression in its center — a basin, a seat, a bed, something used so repeatedly that the rock itself has yielded. Beside it: a tool. It is made of bone — not carved, *grown*, shaped by some process you do not understand into a blade-like form with a handle that fits a hand larger than yours. It is cold to the touch. It is sharp. It has been here a long time and it has not dulled.

The central hollow is the heart of the place.

You reach it after what feels like hours of climbing, though the distance is not great — perhaps two hundred meters of spiraling ascent. The shelf opens into a bowl, a concave depression in the summit, perhaps thirty meters across, walled on all sides by the rising rock. The floor is stone, bare, polished smooth by water that no longer flows but whose memory is inscribed in every curve and channel. It is wet. It is always wet. Water seeps from a hundred hairline cracks in the walls, meets in rivulets, joins in channels cut deep into the stone floor — channels that form a pattern, a design, a *map* of somewhere else, or a language, or a circuit. The water pools nowhere. It moves constantly, finding its way through the channels toward the lowest point of the bowl, where it simply... *goes*. Into the stone. Into nothing.

The vegetation here is sparse. The silver ferns do not grow in the bowl. Instead: *towers*. Vertical structures of living moss, each one as tall as your shoulder, densely packed, shaped by wind or instinct or some slow intelligence into columns and buttresses and arches. They form a miniature architecture — a grove, a nave, a cathedral made of living things that grow and repair and reshape themselves on a timescale just beyond human perception. Between them, the pale segmented creatures move in their lines, and the drone is louder here, richer, layered — as though the towers are resonating, amplifying, turning the collective breath of the colony into something almost musical.

At the exact center of the bowl: a single stone. Flat. Circular. Raised slightly on a pedestal of living rock. And on it — water. Not pooled, not contained. A sphere of water, hovering, rotating slowly, held by nothing visible. It is the size of your two fists together. It catches the white light from above and splits it — not into a rainbow, but into *images*, brief flashes of color and form that resolve and dissolve too quickly to hold: a forest, a coastline, a face, a pattern of stars.

It is not magic. You can feel that it is not magic. It is something with rules, with mechanics, with a physics that is *almost* the one you know. The water sphere responds to proximity. When you approach, it shivers. The images come faster. You see — for just a moment — the spiral shelf from above, and you on it, climbing, and the vast shape crossing the sky, and the cloud ocean, and the peaks, and something *beneath* the clouds, something vast and slow and old, sleeping or thinking or waiting.

Then you step back and the images slow, and the sphere settles, and the drone returns to its steady pulse.

---

There are rules here. You begin to sense them.

The water is not abundant — it is *precious*, rationed, moved through the channels with intentionality. Every drop is accounted for. The condensation on the basalt, the seep from the walls, the slow bleed of moisture from the plants themselves — it all flows inward, toward the center, toward the sphere. The sphere is not decoration. It is *collection*. It is the reservoir, the memory, the heart of the water cycle, and everything else is tributary.

The warmth — because there *is* warmth, despite the cold, a steady low-grade heat that rises from the stone and keeps the shelf above the freezing line — comes from below. From the mountain itself, or from what is inside it. The mountain is not dead rock. It is *active*. The slow processes of mineral transformation, of deep water heated by pressure and rising through fractures, of geological respiration — they keep this shelf just warm enough, just wet enough, for life to persist at an altitude where life should not be possible.

And the life *knows this*. The lichen spreads toward the warm cracks. The moss towers lean toward the channels. The pale creatures follow the moisture gradients with perfect fidelity. Everything is oriented toward the maintenance of the system, the preservation of the water, the continuation of the heat. It is not conscious. It is not designed. It is *convergent* — a billion small behaviors, each one simple, adding up to something that looks like intention, like stewardship, like a civilization of organisms tending a garden they did not plant.

Except.

The cairn. The shelter. The bone blade.

*Someone did.*

---

You climb back down in the fading light — though the light does not fade so much as *dim*, the white sky going gray-white, then pearl, then a luminous dark that is not night but the absence of the sun behind the curve of the world. The sphere glows brighter in the darkness. The moss towers pulse with bioluminescence — soft gold, the color of the lichen, the color of the creatures, a single wavelength repeated across every living thing on the shelf, as though they all share a single chemistry, a single source.

The iron rings are cold. The rock is damp. The drone continues.

You reach the base and look up. The shelf is invisible now, lost in the dark and the thin air and the cloud-sea below. But you know it is there. You can feel it — a warmth, a hum, a slow and patient presence clinging to the side of a mountain that should hold nothing, sustaining itself on seep and condensation and heat from the deep, watched over by whoever left the cairn, the shelter, the blade, and the sphere that shows you, in fragments, the world as it is and the world as it was and the world, perhaps, as it is becoming.

Something taps behind you. You turn. A creature, basalt-colored, eyeless, perched on the lowest ring of the climb. It regards you with whatever sense it uses instead of sight.

*Tik. Tik. Tik.*

An invitation, perhaps. Or a warning.

Or simply: *you were here. We noticed. We remember.*
