# The Hanging Boroughs of Sorvenn

## First Impressions

You arrive from below, which is the only way to arrive at Sorvenn. The town clings to a vertical basalt face so sheer it seems a wound rather than a cliff — the stone is dark, glassy in places, still carrying the ribbed texture of its cooling. The face is young; it was thrust up perhaps ten thousand years ago by a volcanic event that didn't quite complete itself, leaving a single enormous slab tilted at seventy degrees rather than a full cone. Nothing has had time to round its edges. Every ledge is crisp. Every fracture line is legible. Wind hasn't softened a single surface.

And across this face, spread over an area roughly two miles wide and half a mile tall, people have built.

## The Boroughs

Not a single town but a collection of them — six major settlements, each occupying its own ledge-system, connected by a web of rope bridges, ladders, and carved staircases so narrow they require sideways shuffling. The bridges are the real streets. They sway. They are made of braided hemp and cedar slats, and they have been replaced so many times in the same places that the anchor holes in the rock have become smooth cups worn by rope friction.

Each borough has a character that emerged from necessity rather than plan.

**Tannvik**, at the lowest elevation, is where water collects. Not dramatically — there are no waterfalls here, no springs. But the cliff face condenses moisture from the air persistently, a slow weeping that keeps the lowest ledges perpetually damp. Stone glistens. Moss grows in vertical stripes. Tannvik's buildings are raised on stilts even here, where the ground is rock, because the damp creeps upward through everything. They work hides and process the slime-mold that grows in the perpetual wet — a food source, a medicine, a glue.

**Hael**, the largest borough, occupies a broad natural shelf midway up the face. It is the market, the meeting place, the center of gravity. Buildings here are stacked stone-and-timber, three and four stories, with flat roofs that serve as the floor of the street above, because on a cliff face every horizontal surface is a street. Hael smells of hearth smoke and rendered fat and the sharp mineral tang of the wet stone.

**Corrith**, near the top, is cold and exposed. Its people keep goats on the narrow ledges above the town — sure-footed animals that graze on the lichen. The goats are abundant, almost too many. They move across the cliff face in loose herds, visible from below as slow white shapes migrating across dark rock. Their bells provide much of Sorvenn's ambient sound: a constant, scattered chiming with long silences between.

## Water

This is a dry place that isn't dry. The air holds moisture. Rain comes perhaps twice a week — not storms, just a grey settling-in of the sky that lasts an hour or two and leaves everything slick. Between rains, the rock sweeps. Condensation beads on the basalt in the mornings, running downward in thin sheets that the people channel into gutters carved directly into the stone. Every home has a collection basin. Water is not scarce but it is *particular* — it must be gathered, it must be channeled, and it behaves on a vertical surface differently than it does on flat ground, always trying to take the fastest route down. The gutters of upper boroughs feed the basins of lower ones, and this creates an economy of obligation between the settlements that has nothing to do with money and everything to do with the direction gravity insists upon.

At the very base of the cliff, where the weepage finally arrives, there is a flat apron of ground — the only truly horizontal surface for miles. Here the water pools in shallow, irregular basins that evaporate almost as fast as they fill. A few hardy reeds. A smell of minerals. No one lives here; the ground floor, paradoxically, is the least desirable place. Too much damp. Too far from the bridges.

## The Vegetation

What grows here grows vertically. The cliff face is striped with plant life in horizontal bands that correspond to moisture and elevation. Near the bottom: slime-molds in vivid oranges and pale greens, moss thick enough to grab, a few ferns that have found cracks deep enough to hold soil. Mid-face: a scrubby juniper analog, woody and low, gripping the rock with roots that look like fingers gripping a ledge. Its branches grow horizontally outward from the cliff, fighting gravity, creating small dense shelters under which the bridge-anchors are hidden. Higher up: only lichen, pale and crustose, feeding the goats.

The juniper-analog is important. It provides wood, which is scarce. Every branch is accounted for. Cutting one requires a public conversation. The wood is hard, slow-grained, and smells like turpentine and honey.

## Light and Weather

Light is honest here. The cliff faces east-southeast, so mornings are bright and the whole town is visible from below — every bridge, every balcony, every smoke-thread rising from Hael's chimneys lit gold against the dark stone. By noon the sun has moved past and the face falls into its own shadow, and this is when Sorvenn feels most like itself: cool, blue-grey, intimate. The temperature at elevation is genuinely cool even in what passes for summer here. Fingers go numb on the bridges. Breath is visible in the mornings.

There is no fog, exactly, but the moisture in the air creates a persistent softness at distance. Looking across the valley from the upper boroughs, you can see the opposite wall of the gorge, but it is a watercolor version of itself — edges blurred, colors muted. The valley below is mostly invisible, just a suggestion of green and a sound of wind.

## The People

They are not numerous. Perhaps eight hundred across all six boroughs. They build with stone, wood, hide, rope, and a crude plaster made from the slime-mold and ash. Tools are bone, hardwood, and a dark volcanic glass from the base of the cliff — sharper than obsidian, the people claim, though this may be pride. Metal exists in small quantities, brought by traders who climb up from the lowlands with iron implements that are worth more than their weight in goats. The people of Sorvenn are good ropemakers, good at reading stone for fractures, and surprisingly good singers — their voices carry across the bridges in the still air, and singing is how they communicate between boroughs when the wind isn't too strong.

## The Strange Thing

Sorvenn has a quality that visitors notice but residents do not: it is *too stable*. Not politically — there are arguments, alliances, old grudges between Tannvik and Hael about water rights. But the place itself does not change. The cliff face has looked the same within living memory and within the memory of the oldest stories. The erosion rate is nearly zero; the basalt is that hard, that young, that resistant. The moisture patterns are identical year to year. The juniper bands hold their positions. The goats graze the same lichen stripes. Even the bridges, which are constantly being replaced, are replaced in the same places, with the same designs, as if the town has found its shape and is holding it.

There is no word for this in the local language because the concept is unnecessary — why would a thing change? But a geologist passing through would be unsettled. The place looks like a snapshot. Like something that was *set* rather than something that grew. The moisture comes. The lichen grows. The goats eat. The water runs down. The bridges are rebuilt. And nothing — nothing at all — accumulates. No soil deepens. No crack widens. No ledge crumbles.

The people sing across the bridges in the evening. The sound carries down the cliff face and dissipates into the damp air of the gorge. The goats settle on their ledges. The slime-mold glistens. The smoke rises straight up in the still air, because there is never wind at dusk, and it rises and spreads at the level of Corrith's goats and becomes invisible.

Sorvenn holds.
