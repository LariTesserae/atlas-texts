# The Altar Fields of Vorn

---

You stand on a shelf of rock that should not exist at this height.

The air is thin enough to make each breath a conscious act — not painful, not gasping, but *noticed*. Every inhalation is a transaction. The cold is not the cold of snow or ice, but of sheer altitude: a dry, penetrating chill that seems to originate inside the bones rather than outside them. Your fingertips pale. Your thoughts slow by some imperceptible fraction. The temperature has been this way for longer than memory, longer than the structures below, longer than the erosion patterns that have settled into their current forms like a riverbed that has forgotten it was ever anything else.

The elevation is staggering. Below you — far, far below — there is nothing visible. No valleys, no foothills, no descending slopes that a body could walk down. The shelf where you stand projects from the face of a mountain that has no visible summit above and no visible base below. It is as though a blade has cut through the world at this altitude and discarded everything beneath. The rock extends upward perhaps two hundred meters to a lip of darker stone, and above that: cloud, or vapor, or something that behaves like cloud but does not move. It has not moved. You have been watching it. It has not moved.

The shelf is wide. Perhaps three kilometers across, roughly crescent-shaped, open to the void on its southern edge where the rock simply stops and there is sky. The stone is dark grey, fine-grained, layered in horizontal striations that speak of deep time and patient compression. In places it has cracked along its bedding planes, producing shallow ledges and declivities where — and this is the first thing that unsettles you — water has gathered.

Not much. Not streams, not pools, not anything that *flows*. But in the hollows and cracks, moisture has condensed or seeped or simply *appeared* in quantities just sufficient to wet the stone. The surfaces glisten faintly in the dim light. Touch one and your finger comes away damp, cold, with a faint mineral residue that tastes of copper and old iron when you reflexively test it. The water does not seem to come from anywhere. There are no springs, no snowmelt channels, no drainage from above. It is simply *present* in the low places, in the right amount to keep things perpetually moist, perpetually dark-wet, perpetually on the edge of growing something.

And something does grow.

Not much. The vegetation here is the absolute minimum that can be called life at all. In the wettest crevices, dark patches of something between moss and fungus cling to the rock — black-green, thin as paint, spreading in fractal patterns that follow the moisture with mathematical precision. No stems. No leaves. No flowers. Just a film of living matter that is technically photosynthetic, technically autotrophic, but that has stripped plant-ness down to its barest expression. In a few places, slightly larger growths emerge: stubby, segmented things like corals, like fingers, pale as cartilage, rising two or three centimeters from the dampest cracks. They do not move. They do not appear to grow while you watch. They are simply *there*, in their established positions, as permanent-looking as the stone around them.

Between these pockets of life: emptiness. The shelf is overwhelmingly rock, open space, air. The density of *anything* — matter, structure, presence — is vanishingly low. You can stand in the center of the crescent and see the whole of it, nearly treeless and bare, a dark plain under a dim sky, with nothing between you and the edges but flat wet stone and distance. The space feels *consumed* by its own openness. Your voice, when you test it, does not echo. It falls into the distance and is absorbed by something you cannot identify — the moisture in the air, perhaps, or the texture of the stone, or a property of the atmosphere at this altitude that does not forgive sound its usual liberties.

Except — there is sound. There is a great deal of sound.

It comes from below.

From the void beneath the shelf, from the invisible depths below the elevation where you stand, there is a constant low roaring. Not wind. Wind would vary, gust, pause. This does not vary. It is a steady, powerful, omnipresent tone — somewhere between a river and a factory, between geological and mechanical — that rises from beneath the stone and fills the air at a volume that should be unbearable but somehow sits just below the threshold of pain. It is the sound the world makes at this altitude, in this configuration. It has always made this sound. The erosion patterns confirm it: the stone is worn in ways consistent with long exposure to vibration, to a shaking so constant and so evenly distributed that it has become a *condition* rather than an event. Small pebbles in the wet hollows tremble faintly. The moisture on the surface shows standing wave patterns. The air itself has a texture you can almost feel against your skin, a low-frequency pressure that your body registers as unease before your mind has time to name it.

This sound is why you feel the place is *loud*. Not in the sense of sharp or sudden noise, but in the sense that the ambient sonic pressure is enormous — a wall of bass tone that you must speak through, think through, exist through. It defines the acoustic space completely. And yet the place is silent of anything else. No birds. No insects. No wind in leaves, because there are no leaves. The roaring from below is the *only* sound, and it is total.

---

You notice the structures.

Scattered across the shelf at wide intervals — two hundred meters, three hundred meters apart — are objects. Small. Low to the stone. You almost mistook them for natural rock formations before you recognized the intentionality in their shapes.

They are made of the same dark stone as the shelf itself, or something so close in color and texture that the distinction is meaningless. Each structure is roughly the size of a human torso, roughly the shape of a chest or an altar — a rectangular solid with a flat top and a hollow interior, open on one side. They are built without mortar, without tool marks, without any sign of how they were assembled. The stones fit together with a precision that suggests either impossible craftsmanship or that the stones *grew* into these configurations, the way crystals grow, the way erosion wears but also, in its own logic, *builds*.

There are perhaps nine of them. You count, lose count, count again. Nine. They are placed without any obvious pattern — not in a line, not in a circle, not aligned to compass points that you can determine, because the light here has no clear source. The sky is uniformly dim, a pale grey-white that provides illumination without shadow, without direction, without any indication of a sun's position. The light is simply *ambient*, as though the atmosphere itself glows faintly at a level just sufficient to see by. It is not daylight. It is not overcast. It is the light that exists when the concept of a light source has been removed from the equation and only its residue remains.

Each hollow structure contains something.

Approach the nearest one. The roaring from below intensifies slightly as you near it — or seems to, or you imagine it does, because the sound is so total that localizing it is impossible. The structure's interior is wet, as everything here is wet, and in the moisture, growing against the inner walls with the same fractal precision as the shelf's mosses, is a dense colony of the black-green film. But thicker here. Deeper. Layered in three dimensions, forming shapes that are almost — *almost* — recognizable. Almost like organs. Almost like a brain. Almost like something that was once a living thing and has been reduced by this cold, sparse, dim place to its most persistent component: wetness, and the simplest possible life that wetness can support.

The structures feel old. Not ancient in the way that stone is ancient — that is a given here — but old in the way that a habit is old. Someone or something made these. Not recently. The erosion has softened their edges, filled their bases with the same fine sediment that accumulates in the wet hollows, begun the long process of reclaiming them into the geology. But the erosion has also *stopped*, or reached its own equilibrium. The structures exist in a state of settled decay, balanced between dissolution and persistence, neither being built nor being destroyed but simply *continuing*. They have been here for a time that the landscape does not measure. They will be here for a time that the landscape does not measure. The dynamic of this place is stasis — not the stasis of death, but the stasis of a system that has found its level and has no gradient left to flow along.

---

The animals.

You see one eventually — or it sees you, or you become aware of each other simultaneously, which feels different from either party spotting the other.

It is near the southern edge, the open edge, the place where the shelf ends and the sky begins. It stands on the very lip of the stone, motionless, facing outward into the void.

It is difficult to describe because it is difficult to look at directly. Not because it is bright or terrible, but because your eyes keep trying to resolve it into a known form and failing. The best your perception can do is: *quadruped*. *Pale*. *Roughly the size of a large dog*. It has the proportions of something that walks on four legs and holds its head up. Its skin — if it is skin — is the color of the wet stone, the color of the dim sky, the color of nothing in particular. It is watching something in the distance below, or something in the distance above, or nothing.

You do not approach it. Something about the stillness of the scene — the animal, the edge, the void, the constant roaring, the unchanging light — communicates that motion here is significant in a way it is not elsewhere. That to move would be to introduce a *change* into a place that has none, and that the place would resist this not with force but with a kind of ontological friction, a grinding of incompatible states.

The animal does not move.

You do not move.

The roaring continues. The moisture glistens. The light stays exactly as dim as it was.

---

After some time — and time here is difficult to track, because nothing changes to mark its passage — you become aware of the wrongness.

Not danger. Not threat. *Wrongness*.

It is in the way the distance behaves. The shelf is roughly three kilometers across. You have not crossed it. But the far edge seems no closer than when you arrived, and no farther, and the *quality* of the distance between you and it does not match the measured span. It is as though the shelf is exactly as large as your perception of it at any given moment — that there is no fixed spatial extent, only a maintained *impression* of one.

It is in the way the structures are nine in number. You counted them multiple times and received the same answer each time. But the locations shift between counts. Not obviously. Not dramatically. The nearest one has always been the nearest one, but *which* one is the nearest has not always been the same.

It is in the way the roaring from below is *directional*. You hear it as coming from beneath you. But the shelf is a finite thickness of stone projecting from an infinite face of mountain, and below the stone there should be more stone, more mountain, more geology extending downward. The roaring comes from *below the stone*, yes, but when you kneel and press your ear to the wet surface — against the trembling, against the mineral dampness — what you hear is not the interior of a mountain. What you hear is a space. An *enormous* space. A volume of air so vast that the roaring is simply the sound of it existing, the atmospheric pressure of some unspeakable void expressing itself as vibration.

Below the shelf, there is not mountain. There is *distance*.

The shelf floats. Or hovers. Or is suspended over something that is not geological, not atmospheric, not anything that the word "below" should apply to. The elevation is 2.9 out of 3 not because you are high up on a mountain, but because you are high up on *something*, above *something*, and the something above and the something below are both wrong in the same way — they extend in directions that exist but should not.

---

The moss in the nearest altar-structure has grown while you were not looking. You are sure of this. The fractal pattern has extended by some measurable fraction, reaching a finger of black-green film closer to the open side of the structure, as though reaching toward you, or toward the edge, or toward the void. This should not be possible. Nothing grows here at a perceptible rate. Everything is in equilibrium, in stasis, in the long settled state of a system with no gradients.

But the moss has grown.

And the animal at the edge is closer than it was. Not by much. By some fraction of the impossible distance between you and it. It has not taken steps. There is no gait, no locomotion, no mechanism by which it has reduced the separation. It is simply *nearer*, in the way that the structures were *nine*, in the way that the shelf is *three kilometers across* — by the landscape adjusting its internal mathematics to accommodate a new value, a new arrangement, a new state that is almost identical to the old state but not quite.

Something is changing here. Something has always been changing here. The stasis is not real; it is the *appearance* of stasis maintained by a system so large and so patient that its changes occur below the threshold of human perception, below the threshold of human *time*. The erosion is established. The forms are weathered. The balance is real. But the balance is between forces so vast and so slow that what you witness in any single span of awareness is only the *surface* of the equilibrium — the topmost layer of a process that extends downward into scales of duration and transformation that the mind cannot hold.

---

You stand on the shelf. The roaring fills you. The moisture beads on your skin. The dim light renders everything in the same tonal range — grey, dark grey, black-green, pale. The animal is at the edge, or nearer the edge, or at the edge again. The structures hold their wet interiors. The moss grows its slow, patient, impossible growth.

The elevation is extreme. The water is present. The cold is constant. The place is empty and open and scattered with the small remains of intention. The sound is total. The light is dim. The life is sparse.

And underneath it all — under the stone, under the equilibrium, under the weathered and settled and balanced surface of a world that has found its level — something enormous and invisible and *not quite right* continues to roar, and continues to be the foundation of everything that exists here, and continues to remind you, with every vibration that passes through the soles of your feet and into your bones, that the ground beneath you is not ground at all, but a *lid*, and that what it covers does not obey the rules that the landscape above it so carefully maintains.

The animal turns its head. Not toward you. Toward the interior of the shelf, as though it has heard — felt — detected a change in the pattern. A structure has moved. Or a structure has *not moved* but is now a different structure, or the same structure in a slightly different state, or a structure that has always been in this configuration and your previous perception of it was the aberration.

The landscape settles. The landscape *continues to settle*, as it has been settling for longer than the concept of settling has applied to it.

You are damp. You are cold. You are at an extreme elevation above an impossible depth, standing on stone that should not be able to hold you, in a place that is not quite a place, listening to a sound that is not quite a sound, watching an animal that is not quite an animal, in a light that has no source, on a shelf that has no mountain beneath it, in a world that is almost, almost, *almost* stable.

The moss grows.

The roaring continues.

The light does not change.
