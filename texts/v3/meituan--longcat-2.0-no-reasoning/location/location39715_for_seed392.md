# The Lattice Garden of Kethra-Voss

## I. Approaching

You come from the north, where the ground begins to speak.

Not metaphorically. The ground *speaks* — a low, almost subsonic murmur that you feel in your sternum before you hear it with your ears, a tone like a finger dragged along the rim of an enormous glass vessel. It is the sound of water moving through porous stone far below, except the stone is not entirely stone, and the water is not entirely water, and the distinction between the two has become a matter of local theology rather than physics.

The air is thick. Not humid in the way of rainforests — there is a wetness to it, but it is architectural. The moisture hangs in layered sheets, each layer at a slightly different temperature, and they do not mix. You can feel them as you walk: a band of warm damp at your ankles, a cooler stratum at your waist, a hot one pressing against your face. Occasionally a layer will *fold*, and you will feel a brief, startling cold against your knees where there should be none. The locals call these folds *the breath of the Underking*, but they are more like the dreaming spasms of some enormous sleep.

The sky is the color of hammered tin — bright, almost white, with no visible sun. The light comes from everywhere and nowhere. It does not cast shadows in any single direction. Instead, every object casts several shadows, each pointing a few degrees off from the others, as if the light source were not a point but a distributed smear across the entire vault of the sky. Your own shadow is a dark blue-black that seems to pool around your feet like something spilled, then trails behind you at a slightly wrong angle. It doesn't lag. It doesn't lead. It is just *offset*, as though you and your shadow occupy nearly the same space but not quite.

The landscape before you is vast and open, but not empty. This is important. Openness here is not the openness of desert or ocean. It is the openness of a cathedral — a sense of enormous volume populated by discrete, carefully spaced objects that give the space its rhythm. The distance between things is part of the design.

## II. The Grid

You are standing at the edge of the Lattice.

It extends in all directions as far as you can see: a network of low walls, channels, platforms, and basins made from a material that looks like fired clay but isn't, that looks like poured stone but isn't, that looks like it was grown but wasn't. The local people call it *voss*, which is the same word they use for the city itself, the same word they use for the language they speak, the same word they use for the concept of *order*. You will learn not to ask which they mean. Context will not help. They will mean all of them simultaneously, and they will not find this confusing.

The voss is pale — cream, bone, the color of old paper — and it is everywhere. The ground is voss. The walls, rarely higher than your waist, are voss. The bridges that arch over the channels are voss. The basins, some the size of your hand and some the size of buildings, are voss. It was not built in the way we understand building. There is no mortar, no joints, no seams. Each structure appears to have been formed in a single continuous pour or growth, and yet the surface is textured with fine, regular patterns — hexagons mostly, sometimes spirals, sometimes branching fractal trees — that suggest either decoration or function or both. The texture is not carved. It is *integral*, as though the material chose this pattern for itself, the way bone chooses its trabecular architecture.

And the material is young. This is the strange thing. The voss is sharp. The edges of the hexagonal plates have not rounded. The corners of the basins have not worn. The narrow bridges over the channels still have knife-sharp railings that would cut your hand if you ran your fingers along them. Everything looks newly made, freshly emerged, as though the entire Lattice rose from the ground yesterday. But it is old. The locals are old. The channels have been flowing for generations. The sharpness is not a sign of youth. It is a *state*. The voss does not erode. It does not weather. It does not change.

This is because it *cannot* change. Or rather: change is forbidden here, and the prohibition is not social or legal. It is physical. A law of the world, written into whatever fundamental grammar governs this place. You will come to understand this slowly.

## III. The Water

Water is the lifeblood of the Lattice, and it is everywhere, and it is wrong.

The channels between the walls are filled with it — not flowing, but *present*, a sheet of clear liquid so still it looks like glass until you see the things moving beneath the surface. The water in the channels is shallow, perhaps ankle-deep, and perfectly transparent. You can see the voss bottom through it, can see the hexagons rendered in miniature beneath the surface, can see — sometimes — the shapes that move there. They are not fish. They are not insects. They are *patterns*: brief, luminous disturbances in the water that move with intention, that pause, that turn, that dissolve and reconstitute somewhere else. The locals call them *shed spirits* and do not eat them, speak to them, or acknowledge them with any ritual. They simply accept them as part of the water, the way you might accept the presence of dissolved minerals.

The basins are more interesting. Some hold water that behaves normally. Some hold water that is *warm* — not hot, not steaming, just warm, as though it were heated from within by some slow metabolism. Some hold water that is viscous, that moves like honey when you disturb it, that takes a long time to settle back into stillness. A few hold water that appears to be *deep* — far deeper than the basin's dimensions should allow. You peer into one and see, impossibly, a vertical shaft descending into blue-black darkness, and you understand that the basin is a window into something vast and submerged, and you step back, and you do not peer into another one.

The water weeps. This is the most disorienting thing. The voss walls themselves exude moisture — not in drops, but in a fine, continuous film that coats every surface. The walls are always damp. Run your hand along one and your palm comes away slick and cold. The moisture doesn't pool or run down. It simply *is there*, a perpetual skin of water over every surface, as though the entire Lattice is sweating gently. This is the source of the layered humidity in the air. The walls breathe moisture into the atmosphere constantly, and the atmosphere holds it, and the water does not cycle. It does not fall as rain. It does not evaporate into clouds. It simply accumulates, layer upon layer, stratum upon stratum, in the air above the Lattice, like geological deposits laid down in geological time except the time is not geological and the strata are vertical.

There are fountains. Not mechanical fountains — nothing here is mechanical — but *places* where the water rises from the voss itself, pushing up through the surface in slow, rhythmic pulses that look like breathing. Each fountain produces a column of water perhaps a foot high, perfectly cylindrical, perfectly steady, that holds its shape for a moment before collapsing and being replaced by another identical column, identical height, identical rhythm. The columns never splash. They never vary. They are as precise and invariant as the ticking of a clock, except the clock is the size of a cathedral and made of water and has been ticking for as long as anyone can remember.

The locals collect this water in shallow bowls made of a different material — something dark, almost black, with a matte finish that absorbs light rather than reflecting it. They place the bowls at the base of the fountains and let the columns fall into them, and the water that is collected this way is used for things the locals will not explain. The bowls themselves are old. They are the only things in the Lattice that show wear.

## IV. The Vegetation

The plants grow in the Lattice like thoughts in a structured mind — organized, intentional, occupying specific positions within the network.

They do not grow from soil. There is no soil. They grow from the voss itself, pushing up through cracks and joints that the material does not seem to have until the plant needs them. The largest are trees — or tree-analogues — that rise from circular basins set at the intersections of the channel network. Their trunks are pale and smooth, like the voss, but they are not voss. They are wood, or something like wood: fibrous, warm to the touch, faintly fragrant of something between cinnamon and ozone. Their bark peels in long, curling strips that the locals collect and weave into cloth, or fold into containers, or chew for reasons they describe as *keeping the mouth awake*.

The leaves are the color of deep jade — not the bright green of temperate forests, but a dark, saturated green that borders on black, with a waxy surface that repels the perpetual moisture. When the layered air folds and a band of cold passes through, the leaves do not shiver. They do not move at all. No wind blows here. The air is still except for its own internal stratification, and the plants are still, and the only motion is the motion of growth, which is too slow to perceive except over days, except that in certain basins, where the water is warm and deep, the plants grow *fast* — visibly fast, an inch an hour, their leaves unfurling like slow-motion explosions of green, and you can sit and watch them and measure the passage of time by their expansion.

Vines thread along the voss walls, following the hexagonal patterns with an exactness that suggests either intelligence or a very deep understanding of geometry. They bear flowers — small, six-petaled, the color of old brass — that open and close on a cycle that does not correspond to day or night (there is no day or night here; the light is constant) but to some other rhythm that the locals track with the worn black bowls. The flowers smell like hot metal and honey.

Moss grows in the channels, coating the bottoms in a thin, emerald carpet that glows faintly — not bioluminescence, exactly, but a kind of *response* to the bright overhead light, as though the moss were amplifying what it received rather than generating its own. The glow is green-white, cold-looking despite the warmth of the air, and it turns the channels into lines of pale fire that trace the geometry of the Lattice like circuitry on a vast board.

Fungus appears in the deep basins — the ones with the impossibly deep water — rising from the submerged shafts in towering, branching structures that break the surface and spread into canopies of their own. They are white. Perfectly white. The white of fresh bone, of new paper, of something that has never been exposed to color. They do not decay. They do not change. They simply exist, year after year, white and still, and the locals leave offerings at their bases — small carved pieces of the dark material, no larger than a thumbnail, placed in precise arrangements that the locals adjust daily.

## V. The Inhabitants

The people of Kethra-Voss are sparse. Not few in number — there are many of them — but *sparse* in distribution. They occupy the Lattice at wide intervals, each dwelling or workshop or gathering-space separated from the next by long stretches of empty channel and silent wall. You will walk for an hour between one settlement and the next, and the Lattice will seem abandoned, and then you will round a low wall and find a dozen people sitting in a circle around a fountain, speaking in low voices, and they will look up at you without surprise.

They are tall. Narrow. Their skin has the same pale, warm tone as the voss, and their hair is dark and fine and moves in the still air as though underwater. They wear garments made from the peeled bark of the tree-analogues — long, draped, layered — in shades of cream and brown and the faintest green. Their feet are bare. They have been barefoot for so long that their soles are hard and pale and smooth, like the voss itself, and they walk the sharp edges of the bridges and walls without pain or injury.

They make things. This is their primary activity, their occupation, their identity. They are fabricators, crafters, builders in the most literal sense, and yet the things they make are strange. They work with tools of bone and stone and the dark, light-absorbing material that the bowls are made of — not metal, not exactly, though some of the tools have a faint metallic sheen. They carve. They grind. They polish. They fit pieces together with a precision that suggests either great skill or great patience or both.

What they make: containers. Vessels. Hollow objects of every size and shape, from thimble-sized cups to enormous, building-sized enclosures that the locals enter and exit through small doors. The containers are always sealed — always closed, always complete, with no openings except the small doors, and even those are fitted with plugs made of the same dark material. The containers are placed throughout the Lattice, at the intersections of channels, beside fountains, at the bases of the white fungal towers. They sit there, sealed and silent, and no one opens them.

When you ask what is inside, the locals look at you with an expression that is not quite puzzlement but is adjacent to it. They say: *the kept*. They say: *the held*. They say: *the not-yet*. They do not elaborate. They do not need to. You will come to understand, or you will not.

The fauna is abundant but unobtrusive. Small, six-legged creatures — not insects, not reptiles, something with chitin and warm blood — move through the channels in purposeful herds, grazing on the glowing moss. They are the color of the voss, and their bodies are segmented and angular, and they move in geometric patterns that mirror the hexagonal texture of the walls. They make no sound. Their feet — all six of them — touch the voss with a soft, regular tapping that should be audible but isn't, that seems to be absorbed by the material before it can become sound.

Birds — or bird-analogues — perch on the white fungal towers. They are large, white, silent, with long, trailing tail feathers that hang down like streamers into the water of the deep basins. They do not fly. They have wings, fully formed and functional, but they do not use them. They sit. They watch. They are as still as the towers they perch on, and their eyes are the only things that move, tracking you as you pass with a slow, mechanical precision that is more unsettling than any predator's stare.

There are larger animals in the deep basins — things that move in the blue-black depths, shapes that are mostly shadow, glimpsed in fragments: a curve of something pale, a flash of something luminous, a suggestion of scale or fin or limb that resolves into nothing when you look directly at them. The locals do not fish. They do not swim. They do not acknowledge the deep water at all, except to place their carved offerings at its edge.

## VI. The Rules

Here is what you learn, slowly, from observation and from the locals' oblique explanations:

The Lattice does not change. This is not a metaphor. It is not a cultural value. It is a *constraint*, as fundamental as gravity, as non-negotiable as the speed of light. Nothing in Kethra-Voss decays. Nothing erodes. Nothing ages. The voss is as sharp today as it was on the day it was formed — and it was formed all at once, in a single event that the locals refer to as *the Settling*, which they date not in years but in *before and after*. Before the Settling, there was something else. After the Settling, there was the Lattice. The something else is not discussed.

The sharpness of the edges is the visible evidence of this rule. In any other place, stone would wear. Clay would soften. Surfaces would round and blur under the patient assault of time and friction and weather. Here, the assault does not work. The hexagons on the voss walls are as crisp as the day they formed. The knife-edge railings on the bridges have not dulled by a fraction of a millimeter. The white fungal towers have not yellowed, have not darkened, have not developed the patina of age that even the most pristine organisms acquire over time.

This is because time, here, is *structured*. It does not flow. It *holds*. The locals experience duration — they age, they grow, they change — but the Lattice does not. The Lattice is frozen in the moment of its creation, locked into a permanent present tense, and the things that exist within it are caught in the same freeze unless they are *kept* — unless they are placed inside the sealed containers, where, the locals imply, time moves differently, where change is possible, where things can become what they are meant to be before they are released back into the eternal stillness of the surface.

The water is part of this. The water does not cycle. It does not evaporate, does not condense, does not fall as rain. It simply *is* — in the channels, in the basins, in the weeping walls, in the air — a closed system in perfect equilibrium, neither gaining nor losing, neither warming nor cooling, held in place by the same constraint that holds the voss. The fountains pulse, but the water they pulse is not new. It is the same water, endlessly recirculated, endlessly identical, rising and falling in columns that are always the same height, always the same width, always the same temperature, always the same.

The layered air is part of this too. The strata of humidity do not mix because mixing would be *change*, and change is forbidden. Each layer holds its position, holds its temperature, holds its density, and the folds that occasionally occur are not instabilities but *accommodations* — brief, localized violations of the rule that are immediately corrected, the layers snapping back into position with a sound like a whispered word.

The light is part of this. The bright, sourceless, shadow-splitting light is constant. It does not dim. It does not brighten. It does not shift in color or angle. It has been this way since the Settling, and it will be this way until the end, and the end will not come because endings are change and change is forbidden.

And yet.

*And yet.*

The plants grow. The people age. The small six-legged creatures are born and die. The brass-colored flowers open and close. The water in the warm basins is warmer than the water in the cool basins, which means there is a gradient, which means there is *difference*, which means there is the possibility of change, which means the rule is not absolute but *negotiated* — held in tension against the living, breathing, growing things that inhabit the Lattice and need change the way we need oxygen.

This tension is the engine of Kethra-Voss. This is what the sealed containers are for. This is what the locals are doing when they carve and grind and polish and fit pieces together. They are *building change*. They are constructing, piece by piece, in the only place where construction is possible — inside the containers, where time moves and things can transform — the changes that will eventually be released into the Lattice to nudge it, incrementally, carefully, toward whatever it is meant to become next.

The process is slow. The locals are patient. They have been working on this for longer than their history records, and they will continue working on it for longer than their future can imagine. They do not rush. They do not despair. They sit in their circles beside the fountains, and they speak in low voices, and they carve their small dark offerings, and they place them at the bases of the white towers, and they wait.

The Lattice waits with them.

The water weeps. The light shines. The air holds its layers. The voss holds its sharp edges. The flowers open and close. The shadows point in their wrong directions. The shed spirits move through the channels in their luminous, purposeless patterns. The white birds watch. The deep things move in the deep basins.

Everything is still.

Everything is working.

Nothing is finished.

## VII. A Note on the Quiet

It is quiet here. You noticed this already. Not silent — there are sounds, the subsonic hum of water in porous stone, the soft tap of six-legged feet that isn't quite absorbed, the whisper of air-folding, the low voices of the locals — but *quiet*, in the way that a library is quiet, in the way that a held breath is quiet. The sounds that exist are the sounds that are permitted. The others — the sounds of change, of breaking, of wearing, of erosion, of entropy — are absent. They are not muted. They are *unmade*. They are the sounds of things that cannot happen here, and so they do not exist, and the silence where they should be is the deepest silence you have ever heard, a silence so complete it has texture, has weight, has presence, and it presses against your eardrums like the layered air presses against your skin, and you realize that the quiet is not an absence but a *substance*, that it is the medium through which all the permitted sounds must travel, and that it is, in its own way, the most important thing in Kethra-Voss.

The most important thing in Kethra-Voss is the silence that holds everything in place.
