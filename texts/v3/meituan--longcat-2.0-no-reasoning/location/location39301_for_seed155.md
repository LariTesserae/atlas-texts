# The Mirehollow Liftworks

---

From a distance, it looks like a forest had a city, or a city had a forest — the distinction has long since ceased to matter. The Mirehollow Liftworks occupies a broad, ancient plateau, high enough that the air carries a persistent chill and the clouds sometimes descend to walk among the structures like slow, damp visitors. The plateau itself is old beyond reckoning, worn to a gentle undulation by erosion that has had geological ages to do its patient work. There are no dramatic peaks here, no sharp ridges. The land has settled into its final form, like a face that has finally stopped expressing anything and simply *is*.

And on this near-flat, ancient surface, the plants have taken everything.

---

## The Vegetation

This is not a place where plants grow *on* the land. The plants *are* the land, or at least the medium through which the land is experienced. Every surface is colonized. Every structure is scaffold. The dominant organism is a species — or perhaps a consortium, it's hard to say — called **girdervine**: a woody, slow-growing liana with a tensile strength that early industrial engineers found indistinguishable from wrought iron. Girdervine doesn't climb existing structures so much as it *becomes* them. It grows in straight lines when it finds a channel to follow, in lattice patterns when it encounters a frame, in thick load-bearing columns when it senses (through some mechanism no one has adequately explained) that weight needs to be held up.

The original builders of the Liftworks understood this. They didn't clear the plateau to build. They *planted* the plateau to build. They drove iron stakes into the ancient soil in the shapes they wanted, and the girdervine grew along those shapes, and within a generation the stakes had been absorbed — consumed, really, the iron metabolized into the vine's structure — and what remained was a hybrid: part plant, part remembered metal, all architecture.

The result is a city that breathes. Literally. The girdervine transpires on a massive scale, and the air in the Liftworks is thick with moisture — not raining, never quite raining, but *damp* in a way that gets into your clothes and stays there. Condensation runs down every surface. The buildings sweat. In the morning, if morning is the right word for the slow brightening that happens here under normal, unremarkable light, everything is beaded with water, and the whole city glistens like something just pulled from the sea.

Beneath the girdervine canopy, the understory is a riot of secondary growth: **moss-concrete**, a symbiotic mat of bryophytes and mineral-depositing bacteria that fills gaps between structures with a spongy, slightly yielding surface that is technically a floor. Ferns the size of small trees unfurl from the joints where vine-meets-vine. Fungi of every description — bracket, shelf, thread, and some that don't fit any category — emerge from surfaces in colors that range from the expected browns and whites to a deep, unsettling violet that seems to absorb light rather than reflect it.

And then there are the **pockets**. The plateau is not uniformly vegetated. There are clearings — not cleared, but *occurring*, places where for reasons unknown the girdervine won't grow. These pockets are scattered, sparse, and in them you find the bare ancient stone of the plateau itself: smooth, dark, worn to a polish by erosion that stopped millennia ago. The pockets are where water collects. Not streams, not rivers — just shallow, still pools that appear and disappear on a cycle no one has mapped, fed by condensation and the slow weeping of the vegetation above. The water is cold. It is always cold.

---

## The Built Environment

The Liftworks was, in its industrial prime, a place of manufacture and vertical transport. The name refers to the great lifts — mechanical systems designed to move goods and people up and down the plateau's edges, connecting the high plain to the lowlands below. Some of these lifts still function. Most don't. All of them have been absorbed.

The central district — if a place this organically grown can be said to have a district — is a cluster of structures that were once factories. Their original purposes are legible only in their shapes: long, low buildings that were assembly halls; tall, narrow towers that were chimneys or ventilation shafts; wide, open platforms that were loading docks. Now they are all girdervine. The vine has grown through and around and *as* the original materials, and the factories have become something like greenhouses in reverse — structures that were built to contain industry now contain only growth, only the slow patient business of living things doing what living things do.

The infrastructure is still visible, if you know how to look. Roads exist as compressed moss-concrete paths, slightly lower than the surrounding growth, following the original street grid with a fidelity that suggests the girdervine remembers the plan even if the planners are long gone. Conduits — once carrying water, steam, or electrical cable — now carry sap, or nothing, or something that is neither sap nor electricity but behaves like both. Pipes emerge from walls at odd angles, dripping. Junction boxes hang open, their copper contacts long since replaced by root structures that branch and rebranch in patterns that look, if you squint, like circuit diagrams.

There are residential structures too, or what were residential structures. They are smaller, more intimate, and the girdervine has treated them differently — growing thicker here, forming walls that are nearly solid, creating spaces that are more *room* than *building*. Some of these spaces are still occupied. Not by people, mostly. By the sound.

---

## The Sound

The Liftworks is loud. This is the thing that surprises people, if people still come here, which they do, occasionally, for reasons that range from the scholarly to the desperate.

The loudness is not mechanical, though there are mechanical components. The lifts that still function produce a deep, rhythmic groaning — metal on metal, or vine on metal, or vine on vine in a way that sounds exactly like metal on metal. This sound carries across the plateau, echoing off the ancient stone pockets, amplified by the dense vegetation in ways that make it seem to come from everywhere at once.

But the dominant sound is biological. The girdervine, as it grows, *creaks*. It is a woody plant under enormous structural tension — holding up buildings, bridging gaps, bearing loads that would test steel — and it expresses this tension acoustically. The sound is constant: a low, shifting chorus of groans, pops, and sustained notes that rise and fall like a string ensemble tuning forever. On damp days, when the vine is actively transpiring and the cells are full of water, the sound is louder, wetter, more resonant. The whole city hums.

And then there are the **drips**. Every surface is wet. Every wet surface drips. The drips fall onto moss-concrete, onto metal remnants, onto stone, onto other vegetation, and each surface produces a different note. The cumulative effect is a percussion section of staggering complexity — thousands of individual impacts per second, each one slightly different in pitch and timbre, combining into a wash of sound that is almost musical, almost random, and entirely overwhelming if you stand still long enough to really hear it.

The fauna contribute, though they are rare. There are birds — a few species adapted to the vine-forest, nesting in the upper canopy where the girdervine's leaves form a continuous roof. Their calls are sharp and clear, cutting through the vegetative drone like needles through cloth. There are insects, or insect-analogues, small and numerous but mostly silent. And there are, in the deeper pockets where the water collects, things that move beneath the surface and occasionally break it with a sound like a wet hand clapping.

---

## The Weirdness

Here is where the description becomes difficult, because the Liftworks does not entirely obey the rules that the rest of the world obeys, and it is not clear whether this is a property of the place or a property of the observer.

The girdervine is the primary agent of this wrongness. It grows in straight lines, as noted. It grows along channels, along frames, along *intentions*. The original builders drove stakes in the shapes they wanted, and the vine followed. But the builders are gone, and the vine is still following. It is following intentions that no longer have an author. It is building structures that no one designed. In the outer districts, far from the original core, the girdervine has begun to produce forms that have no industrial precedent: arches that lead nowhere, towers that taper to points too fine to support anything, lattices of such complexity that they seem to be three-dimensional projections of higher-dimensional objects. They are beautiful. They are useless. They are growing.

The electrical systems — the original industrial electrification — have not entirely died. The root-structures that replaced the wiring carry something. Not current, exactly. Not anymore. But there are lights in the Liftworks that turn on and off with no visible source, no switch, no pattern. They are the original fixtures: factory lamps, street lights, the small glass bulbs that once illuminated residential rooms. They glow with a light that is warm and yellow and entirely normal, except that it comes from nowhere and goes nowhere and sometimes illuminates rooms that have no doors.

The water in the pockets behaves strangely. It is always cold, always still, always clear. But its depth is inconsistent. A pocket that was ankle-deep yesterday may be knee-deep today, or it may be gone entirely, the bare stone exposed and dry for an hour before the water returns. Things are visible in the water — reflections, certainly, but reflections that don't always match the scene above. A person standing at the edge of a pocket may see, in the water, a version of the Liftworks that is slightly different: a building where there is none, a gap where there is a building, a sky that is the wrong color. The reflections are consistent with each other — they show a coherent place, a real place, just not *this* place. Not quite.

Time moves strangely in the Liftworks, or seems to. The light is normal — the sun rises and sets, the clouds pass, the seasons turn — but the *pace* of things is hard to judge. The girdervine grows slowly, visibly slowly, and yet structures that were half-formed last season are complete this season, and no one saw the intermediate stages. The lifts groan their deep rhythm, and if you count the intervals, they are regular, perfectly regular, but the count never matches the clock. You count sixty groans and check your watch and three minutes have passed, or thirty, or the watch has stopped.

And there is the matter of the **echoes**. The Liftworks is loud, as established. But some of the sounds have no source. The creak of girdervine where no girdervine is moving. The drip of water from a dry surface. The sound of a door closing in a building that has no doors. The sound of a machine starting up — a factory machine, a production line, the clank and hiss of industrial process — in a factory that has been silent for a century. These sounds are not frequent. They are not rare. They are *occasional*, in the same way that the rain is occasional: you learn to expect them without being able to predict them, and you stop looking for the source because the source is not there.

## The Ecology

The Liftworks is, in ecological terms, a monoculture with accessories. The girdervine is the substrate, the architecture, the primary producer, and the structural engineer. Everything else is secondary.

The moss-concrete understory supports a limited but specialized community: decomposers, mostly, breaking down the dead girdervine material that falls when old sections finally succumb to age or weight. The fungi are the most visible component of this community, and the most diverse. The violet fungi, in particular, seem to play a role in the vine's nutrient cycling, their mycelial networks intertwining with the girdervine's root system in a partnership that blurs the line between organism and ecosystem.

The birds are the most visible fauna. There are perhaps three species, all small, all adapted to life in the vine-canopy. They feed on the girdervine's fruit — small, hard, iron-rich nodules that the vine produces at intervals — and on the insects that live in the moss-concrete. Their populations are stable but small. The Liftworks is not a place that supports abundance. It supports *sufficiency*.

The water pockets support the most mysterious ecology. The things that move beneath the surface are rarely seen clearly. They are small, dark, and fast. They may be amphibians. They may be something else. They do not appear in any taxonomic record. They are heard more often than seen — the wet clap of their emergence, the ripple of their submersion — and they seem to be most active when the reflections in the water are most different from the world above.

There are no large predators. There are no large herbivores. The Liftworks is a place of small things and slow things and quiet things, punctuated by the loud, constant, creaking chorus of the girdervine doing its endless work of holding everything up.

---

## The Dynamic

The Liftworks is active, but its activity is not the activity of a living city. It is the activity of a living *system*. The weather cycles through its patterns — damp, less damp, damp again, with occasional rain that is almost redundant given the ambient moisture. The girdervine grows, slowly, continuously, building its incomprehensible new structures at the edges. The lifts groan. The water pockets fill and empty. The lights turn on and off. The sounds echo from sources that aren't there.

And the place is *settling*. Not collapsing — the girdervine is too strong for that, too deeply rooted in the ancient stone — but settling in the way that all old things settle, finding its final configuration, its last shape. The erosion that wore the plateau to its current flatness is finished, has been finished for eons, but the Liftworks itself is still in the process of becoming what it will ultimately be. The industrial phase is over. The organic phase is mature. What comes next is not yet clear, but the girdervine is building toward it, following intentions that have no author, growing in straight lines toward a purpose that has no name.

The air is cool. The light is normal. The sound is loud. The water is somewhere, always, dripping from a surface near you, collecting in a pocket just out of sight, running down the inside of a wall that is also a vine that is also a building that is also, in some sense that resists articulation, a memory of a building.

The Liftworks endures. It is not comfortable. It is not welcoming. But it is *there*, in the way that very old things are there: completely, unarguably, and with a patience that makes human timescales seem like mayfly flickers.

It will be here when you arrive. It will be here when you leave. It will be slightly different each time, and exactly the same.
