# The Blooming

---

You are standing in a city that is alive. Not metaphorically. Not in the way architects sometimes say "the building breathes." This city is a living organism — or perhaps an organism that has become a city — and you are inside it, standing on what you might call a street, though the word is approximate.

The ground beneath you is warm. It yields slightly when you shift your weight, like standing on something that was once flesh and has since calcified but not entirely forgotten its old pliancy. The surface is a deep ochre, almost rust-colored, ribbed with long parallel ridges that arc away in every direction, following the geometry of something enormous that once curled or stretched here. You are, you realize, walking along the fossilized interior of a massive rib. The street follows the curve of it. The buildings — if that is the word — grow from the next rib over.

They rise around you in a dense thicket, these structures. Some are tall, perhaps twenty or thirty meters, shaped like elongated seed pods or the upright casings of某些 ancient grains — tall, tapered, slightly curved, their walls a pale chitinous material that has hardened to the consistency of dense bone. Others are lower, broader, squat bulbous forms clustered together like puffballs on a dead log, their surfaces rough with papery bark that peels in translucent sheets. They are packed close. The gaps between them are narrow, winding, more like the passages between roots in old forest floor than anything designed by an architect. And yet there are signs of intention here — not human intention, but *someone's*. Certain walls have been opened where they abut each other, creating shared archways. Some of the tall pod-structures have been connected by bridges of woven fiber at upper levels, the fiber thick as rope, pale and dry, but still flexible enough to sway slightly in the wind that moves through these passages.

The wind. There is wind here. You feel it on your face — warm, dry, carrying a smell like sun-heated hay mixed with something sharper, almost ammoniac. It moves through the streets in predictable patterns, channeled by the dense architecture, and you can hear it: a low continuous hushing, like breath drawn through a partially closed mouth. That is the dominant sound. Underneath it, occasional clicks and creaks — the structures settling, adjusting, growing. Because they are growing. You watch a wall nearby and see, in the time it takes to blink, the faintest ripple move across its surface. Not much. Not fast. But the place is not static. It is accreting. New material is being deposited on surfaces, slowly, cell by cell or ring by ring. The city is under construction. It has always been under construction. It may never be finished.

There is no water anywhere. You have not seen a drop. No puddles, no dampness, no moss, no algae, no rust on any metal — because there is no metal. Everything here is biological or bio-mineral. The chitin, the bone-like walls, the peeling bark, the woven bridges. And the ground itself, which you now look at more carefully and see is not stone but something denser and more organic — a compressed mat of ancient root material, perhaps, or the compacted floor of a structure that grew here long before the current buildings, its biology pressed into substrate by time and weight and the sheer pressure of everything that came after.

And everything did come after. The density of this place is extraordinary. Every surface is occupied. The walls of the pod-buildings are not bare — they are covered in a fine fuzz of pale filaments, like the mycelial mats you might find on a forest floor, except these are aerial, growing on vertical surfaces, forming intricate branching patterns that catch the light. Between the buildings, in the narrowest gaps, things grow in profusion: low, dense, cushion-forming organisms in shades of sage and dusty rose and pale gold, their surfaces beaded with tiny structures that might be reproductive bodies or might be sensory organs or might be something for which you have no name. They are packed so tightly that in places they form a continuous carpet, a living mulch that fills every gap, every crack, every shadow.

The light is good. It comes from above — a sky that is pale, almost white, with a sun that is large and diffuse, not a sharp point but a broad luminous presence that casts soft shadows. The light has a quality you associate with late morning, though you have no way to measure time here. It is warm light. It falls through the gaps between the tall structures in broad shafts, and in those shafts you can see the air is not empty — it is full of particles. Not dust, exactly. More like spores, or pollen, or tiny desiccated fragments of the filamentous growths on the walls. They drift slowly, catching the light, giving the air a faintly golden haze. The whole city is breathing this out. The whole city is, in some sense, one organism exhaling.

You move deeper. The streets — the passages — narrow and widen unpredictably. Sometimes you walk through a broad avenue where the rib-ground is wide and the buildings stand back, and the sky opens above you and the light is full and warm. Sometimes you duck into a low tunnel formed by the arching of two structures that have grown into each other, and the light dims to a greenish amber, filtered through the translucent papery bark of the walls, and the air is closer, warmer, and the smell intensifies — that hay-and-ammonia smell, but also something sweeter underneath, almost floral, like dried lavender.

And then you hear it. A sound that is not the wind. A soft, rhythmic tapping, like a fingernail on wood, but irregular, coming from somewhere above and to your left. You look up and see movement.

The fauna. You had almost forgotten the parameter — abundant — but now it announces itself. On the wall of a tall pod-structure, about four meters up, something is moving. It is roughly the size of a large cat, but its body plan is unfamiliar. It has too many legs — six, perhaps eight — arranged in two rows along a flattened, segmented body. Its surface is the same ochre as the ground, and it moves with a fluid, undulating gait, each leg placing itself with deliberate precision. It is tapping the wall with a specialized forelimb — a hard, chitinous probe that strikes the surface in a pattern. *Tap. Tap-tap. Tap.* It is listening, perhaps. Or communicating. Or feeding. You cannot tell.

It notices you. Its body shifts, and you see that what you took for decorative patterning on its dorsal surface is actually a cluster of small, dark, bead-like organs that orient toward you — eyes, or something analogous. It watches you for a moment. Then it continues on its way, flowing up the wall and over the curve of the pod-structure's upper surface, disappearing over the rim.

You see more of them now that you are looking. They are everywhere. Small ones, no bigger than your thumb, moving in quick darting lines along the ground, following the ridges of the rib-street. Larger ones, the size of dogs, moving through the cushion-plants in the gaps, their bodies partially concealed by the dense growth, only their movement betraying their presence — a ripple, a sway, a brief flash of chitinous surface. Something flies — no, not flies, *drifts* — overhead: a translucent, membranous structure the size of a dinner plate, trailing fine filaments beneath it, catching the warm updrafts that rise from the sun-heated walls. It is alive. It is an animal, or the local equivalent. It moves with slow, banking turns, and you see that the filaments beneath it are not passive — they reach down occasionally, touching the surfaces of buildings, the tops of cushion-plants, as if tasting or sampling.

The ecosystem is in motion. You can feel it now, the dynamic quality of the place. It is not a museum diorama. It is a living system with energy flowing through it. The cushion-plants are being grazed — you can see where something has been feeding, the surface cropped back to a paler underlayer. The filamentous wall-growths are being harvested by small multi-legged creatures that move along them methodically, clipping the filaments with mandible-like mouthparts and carrying the clippings away in bulging cheek-pouches. The large wall-tapper you saw earlier is, you now realize, a predator — or at least a consumer of other animals — because you see another one lower down, and it has caught something, its body curved around a smaller creature that is still twitching, and it is feeding with a slow, methodical patience.

There are sounds, now that you are listening for them. The wind is the bass note. Over it, the clicks and taps of the wall-dwellers. A faint, high-pitched hum that seems to come from the cushion-plants themselves — perhaps a vibration produced by their internal structures as they grow, or perhaps a sound made by the tiny organisms that live among them. Occasionally, a sharp crack — something breaking, something growing too fast for its own structure, a wall splitting, a branch of chitinous material snapping under its own weight as it extends too far from its support. And then a wet, fibrous sound as the broken piece is immediately colonized — you watch it happen — by the filamentous growths, which surge across the fresh surface in a visible wave, beginning the process of either repair or consumption.

The scale of this place is difficult to comprehend. You have been walking for what feels like an hour, and the city has not thinned. It has changed character — here the buildings are taller, there they are lower and more clustered, here the ground is smooth and there it is rough with exposed root-masses — but it has not ended. You look back and cannot see where you entered. You look forward and cannot see an edge. The passages curve and branch and sometimes open into broad clearings where the ground dips into a shallow basin and the buildings ring the rim like spectators, and in the basin the cushion-plants grow so thick and so tall — waist-high, chest-high — that they form a kind of meadow, and the animals that move through them are hidden entirely, and you see only the swaying of the surface, the constant gentle agitation of a dense living layer being traversed by unseen bodies.

And above, always, the pale sky and the diffuse sun, and the drifting membranous flyers, and the golden haze of spores or pollen or biological particulate that fills the air and catches the light and makes every shaft of sunlight into a visible column, so that the whole city is latticed with light, divided into bright and shadow, and the shadow is not dark but merely less bright, still full of that amber filtered glow, still warm, still alive.

You realize you are thirsty. There is no water here. There has never been water here, not in the way you understand it. The biology of this place runs on something else — some other solvent, some other chemistry, or perhaps on no solvent at all, on direct metabolic processes that do not require a liquid medium. The organisms here are not desiccated. They are not surviving despite the dryness. They are *thriving* because of it, or in it, or with it. The dryness is not an absence. It is a condition. It is the medium in which this world makes sense.

You sit down on the warm rib-ground, and the ground yields slightly beneath you, and the filaments on the nearest wall orient toward your body heat, and a small multi-legged creature pauses in its path across your boot and regards you with organs you cannot name, and the wind moves through the passage and carries the smell of hay and ammonia and dried lavender, and somewhere in the distance something large shifts and the ground trembles faintly, and the city grows, and the city breathes, and the city is.
