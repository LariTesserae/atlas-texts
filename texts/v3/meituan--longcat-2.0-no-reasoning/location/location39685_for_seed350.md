# The Drowning Works

---

You stand at the edge of something that does not end.

The water is not water. It is thick, copper-dark, and it moves with a slow muscular churn, as though it were the blood of something vast and incompletely born. It holds you at the waist — warm, almost body-temperature but a degree higher, so that the heat feels deliberate, an intentional intrusion. The surface everywhere is broken. Columns rise from it at irregular intervals, hundreds of them, thousands, in no grid you can discern. They are not stone. They are not metal. They are a composite that looks almost organic — ridged, segmented, streaked with iridescent mineral blooms that catch what little light exists and break it into wrong colors, colors your eyes don't fully register, that sit at the edge of perception like a word on the tip of your tongue.

Some of the columns are tall — five stories, ten, tapering to sharp points that have not yet been softened by weather. They are young features, these structures. The erosion hasn't begun. Every edge is crisp, every angle freshly made. You can see where the material was extruded or grown or vomited up from below: layered strata of something between coral and poured concrete, still slightly tacky to the touch, still off-gassing a mineral smell that stings the sinuses.

And every column is alive with activity. Not animal life — there is none. No fish, no birds, no insects. The fauna parameter is a zero, and the silence where life should be is its own kind of screaming. But the columns themselves *do* things. They throb. Sections of them rotate with low grinding sounds. Portholes irised open along their surfaces and light — electric blue, arc-flare white — spills out in brief violent flashes, illuminating the dense overgrowth that clings to every structure, every surface, every cubic meter of available space.

The vegetation is not plant life as you understand it. It is a pale, fibrous matting that grows in overlapping layers — over the columns, over each other, over the floating platforms that drift between the columns, over the submerged lower reaches of everything. It looks like mycelium, like kelp, like foam insulation. It is soft underfoot when you step on the few surfaces that breach the waterline, and it is *everywhere* — dense, saturated, layered so thick in places that the structures beneath become pure geometry lost under organic accumulation. The growth has a faint phosphorescence. In the dim light — and the light is very dim, a perpetual overcast murk filtering from a sky you cannot see, as though the atmosphere itself were a low ceiling — the vegetation glows a faint, sickly jade.

The built structures are scattered. Not absent — scattered. A platform here, bridging three columns, its railings wrapped in the pale growth. A boxy habitation module there, half-submerged, its door hanging open, interior dark. Partial walls of something like a factory floor, the roof long gone, the machinery inside still — still *running*. That is the thing. The machinery runs. Conveyor belts move through the water, carrying nothing, or carrying something dissolved. Tanks bubble. Electrical conduits crackle where they dip below the surface and the current disperses in fractal branchings of blue light through the thick water. Generators hum in housings that have begun to sink. A crane, tall, skeletal, its arm frozen mid-rotation, encrusted in vegetation and mineral deposits, still powered — you can see the joint motors cycling through a slow, meaningless rotation. Seventeen degrees clockwise. Stop. Seventeen degrees counterclockwise. Stop. It has been doing this since before you arrived. It will continue.

The tech level is industrial, and it is *abandoned* — but not deactivated. This is the wrongness. This is where the weirdness begins.

Nothing here was built by hands you would recognize. The proportions are almost right. The habitation module has a door you could walk through, but the handle is six inches too high and the threshold is angled three degrees off level in a way that makes your ankle ache. The machinery operates on principles that are *adjacent* to engineering. The conveyor belts carry material from tanks that are fed by pipes that emerge from the water itself, and the material — when you look closely, when you let your eyes unfocus — is not uniform. It shifts. A section of whatever-sludge moves through a processing vat and emerges *different* not because it was refined but because it *decided* to be. The factory is producing something, but the product changes based on no input you can identify.

The sound. The sound is the hardest part. It is *deafening*. Not a single noise — a totality of noise, a wall of it. The groan of the columns shifting in their foundations. The hiss of steam or gas or something venting from a thousand rupture points. The electrical crackle. The grinding of machinery. And underneath all of it, a sound like breathing. Like the whole landscape is a single organism performing respiration, and the machinery is its metabolism, and the vegetation is its skin, and the water is its blood, and you are a bacterium on its surface, too small to register.

The turbulence is constant. The water rises and falls in slow swells — not waves, *swells*, as though something massive is displacing it from below. The columns sway, almost imperceptibly, but you can feel it through your feet when you stand on a platform. The vegetation grows visibly. Not fast — but fast enough that you can see it. A fibrous tendril extends an inch while you watch. A mineral bloom on a column face *spreads*, crystalline fingers reaching outward, catching the dim light in prismatic flashes that last a second and then subside. The floating platforms drift, collide, separate. The submerged machinery sends up bubbles in patterns that are almost but not quite rhythmic.

And the reality breakdown — it is subtle at first and then it is not. The distance between things changes when you are not looking. You turn your head and a column that was fifty meters away is thirty meters away, or seventy, and it has *always* that distance, you simply know it, the way you know your own age. The water's thickness varies. In some places it is genuinely liquid and you can see five meters down into murky copper depth. In other places it has the consistency of warm gelatin and objects move through it slowly, heavily, as though the medium itself were thinking about whether to let them pass. Time is not unreliable exactly, but *effortful*. It takes work to maintain a sequence of events. Cause and effect are still present, but they have become *polite* — they will wait for each other, but they will not hurry.

At night — if night comes, and it does, a deepening rather than a darkening of the already-dim light — the machinery gets louder. The vegetation glows brighter. The breathing sound deepens. And the columns *sing*. Not musically. Each one resonates at a frequency just below hearing, and the harmonics between them create a standing wave of sound that you feel in your teeth, in your marrow, in the part of your brain that recognizes that something is very wrong and has always been wrong and will continue to be wrong long after you have been absorbed into the pale growth, added to the density, made part of the Works.

You are not the first to be here. You find a boot, half-dissolved, laced into the vegetation on a platform. A clipboard, its pages fused into a solid block of fiber. A tool — a wrench? — that has begun to merge with the column it was left against, the metal and the composite becoming each other at the boundary.

Nobody comes here to stay.

The Works do not care. The Works are busy. The Works are producing something in their thousand half-sunk tanks, growing something on their thousand ridged columns, building something with their scattered, senseless, *running* machinery. The water churns. The pale growth spreads. The dim light flickers and holds.

The drowning is not an event. It is a condition. It is the medium. It is the air and the ground and the sky all at once, and it is warm, and it is getting warmer, and the Works do not stop.
