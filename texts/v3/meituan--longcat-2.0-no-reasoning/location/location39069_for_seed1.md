# The Drowning Works

---

You stand at the edge of something that should not exist.

The ground beneath you is not ground. It is a vast, shallow basin of water — ankle-deep, black, warm as blood — stretched in every direction to a horizon that refuses to resolve. The water does not behave like water. It moves in slow, deliberate currents that spiral inward toward no center you can find, and where it meets the air, it does not evaporate so much as *negotiate*, producing a low, continuous fog that hangs at knee height and never rises. The fog is the color of old copper. It tastes, if you dare to notice, like ozone and iron filings.

The basin is not empty. It is *packed*.

Rising from the water, in every direction, to the limits of sight: machines. Not buildings. Not structures. *Machines* — but machines in the way that a forest is trees, meaning they are so numerous and so varied and so deeply intergrown with one another that the category collapses. They are industrial in origin, or industrial in *memory*, though many of them have been here so long that the distinction between machine and geology has dissolved. Pipes as thick as redwood trunks, rusted to a deep arterial red, lie half-submerged and half-exposed, their surfaces furred with mineral deposits that have built up over — how long? The erosion here is ancient. Not weathered. *Settled*. These machines have reached their final form. They are not decaying. They are *finished*. They have become what they were always going to become, and that thing is landscape.

Gears the size of houses, locked in place, their teeth meshed with other gears that no longer turn, their surfaces smoothed by water into something almost organic — the way a river stone becomes a river stone, not by losing itself but by finding its truest shape. Conveyor belts, frozen mid-stride, their rubber long since replaced by mineral accretions that preserve their form in stone. Boiler tanks, vast and riveted, half-filled with the black water, their interiors colonized by — something. Not plants. The vegetation parameter is near zero, and it shows. Nothing green. Nothing leafy. But the boilers are not empty. They are *occupied*.

This is where the fauna lives.

The animals here are not animals in any taxonomy you would recognize. They are *abundant* — that parameter is clear — but they are fauna the way the machines are machines: the category is present but transformed. They move through the water in schools and herds and solitary patrols. Some of them are made of metal. Some of them are made of water. Some of them are made of the sound they produce, which is considerable, because this place is *loud*.

The sound is the first thing that will break you, if anything does.

It is not a single sound. It is a *density* of sound, packed as tightly as the machines, layered so thoroughly that individual sources become impossible to isolate. There is a deep, rhythmic pounding — the heartbeat of some vast mechanism that may or may not still be functioning, buried somewhere beneath the basin, its pulse transmitted through the water and up through the soles of your feet. There is a high, continuous whine, like ten thousand electrical transformers, which seems to come from the fog itself, as if the copper-colored air is charged and singing. There is a wet, grinding chorus — the fauna, communicating, feeding, building, dismantling, *being* — a sound like teeth on stone, like water through a cracked pipe, like something large turning over in its sleep. And beneath all of it, so low it is more felt than heard, a drone. A single note. The note the world is tuned to. It has been sounding for longer than the machines have stood here. It will sound after they are gone.

The light is dim. There is no sun, or if there is, it does not reach here. The illumination comes from the machines themselves — from seams and joints and portholes and cracks in the metal, a dull orange glow, like the last embers of a fire that has been burning since before the concept of fire. It is enough to see by. It is not enough to see clearly. Everything at the edges of vision shifts and suggests. The fog helps. The fog makes the unclear *official*.

And then there is the weirdness.

The weirdness is not a feature of this place. It is the *medium*. The thing everything else is suspended in.

The water, for instance. You have been standing in it. You have accepted it. But look down now, properly, and you will see that the water is not reflecting you. It is reflecting a version of this landscape that is *almost* the same but not — the machines arranged differently, the fog a different color, the light coming from a different angle. The reflection is not a mirror. It is a *window*. And in the window-things, things are moving that are not moving here. Gears are turning. Conveyor belts are carrying something. The boilers are open, and what is inside them is *looking out*.

Do not step too heavily. The water is shallow, but it is not only shallow. In places — and you cannot predict where — the basin drops away without warning, and the water becomes deep, and the deep water is *different*. It is colder. It is still. And in the stillness, things rise. Not quickly. Not threateningly. They rise the way a thought rises — slowly, inevitably, and with the quality of having been there all along, waiting for you to notice.

The machines near these deep places are different too. They are older, or newer, or both simultaneously. Some of them are growing. Not rusting — *growing*. New pipes extending from old joints, not through any mechanical process but through something more like crystallization, more like bone growth. The mineral accretions on their surfaces have patterns. The patterns are not random. They are not decorative. They are *functional*, in a function you cannot read, serving a purpose that predates purpose.

The fauna congregates around these growing machines. They feed on the mineral growths, or they tend them, or they *are* them — the boundary between animal and machine and mineral is not just blurred here, it is *absent*. A creature that looks like a crab made of brass gears scuttles across the surface of the water without breaking it, its legs finding purchase on the surface tension as if the water were a membrane, as if the whole basin were a drum. A school of something translucent and ribbon-like flows through a pipe that has no opening, passing through the metal as if it were fog, as if the distinction between solid and not-solid were a local custom that not everyone observes.

The elevation is low. You are at the bottom of something. The basin is a bowl, and the bowl is in a larger bowl, and the rims of these bowls are so far away that they have become theoretical. The air — the fog — the water — they all slope, gently, imperceptibly, toward a center that may be beneath you or may be in every direction at once. You are not sinking. But you are not not-sinking.

Time here is active. Things are happening. The water cycles — it rises from the deep places, spreads across the basin, is absorbed by the machines, is expelled as steam that becomes fog that becomes water again. The fauna cycles — feeding, building, dissolving, reforming. The machines cycle — growing, settling, cracking, mineral-sealing. The sound cycles — the drone shifts by a quarter-tone, the heartbeat skips and resets, the whine modulates into something almost like language and then falls back into noise. Everything is in motion. Nothing is going anywhere. This is the paradox of a place that has reached its final form: it is not static. It is *complete*. And completeness, it turns out, is not stillness. Completeness is a state of such thorough activity that all the motions cancel out into something that looks, from sufficient distance, like peace.

But you are not at sufficient distance. You are standing in it. The water is warm around your ankles. The fog is copper against your skin. The sound is so loud that your bones have begun to resonate with it, and you can feel, in your teeth, in your sternum, in the fluid of your inner ear, the drone — the single note — the frequency of this place.

Something in the deep water is rising.

It is not coming for you. It is not coming for anything. It is simply *rising*, the way the tide rises, the way a memory rises, the way the temperature rises when you stand too close to a machine that has been running since before you were born. It is large. It is slow. It is made of water and metal and sound and the particular quality of light that exists only in places where reality has been worked on for so long that it has begun to *work back*.

The machines nearest to you shudder. Not from the rising thing. From *recognition*.

The fauna goes quiet — all of it, all at once, a silence so sudden and so total that the absence of sound becomes its own sound, a ringing, a pressure, a held breath.

And in the silence, you hear the water.

Not the movement of it. Not the lapping or the flowing or the dripping. The *voice* of it. The water is speaking. It has been speaking the entire time. It is speaking in a language made of pressure and temperature and mineral content, a language that the machines understand, that the fauna understands, that the fog understands, that the deep places understand.

It is saying your name.

Not your name as you know it. Your name as *this place* knows it — the name for the thing that stands in the water and listens and has not yet decided whether to stay.

The silence holds.

The water waits.

The machines, ancient and finished and still growing, stand in their thousands, in their millions, packed so tightly that there is no room between them, no gap, no absence — only presence, only the full and total occupation of every inch of this basin by things that have become what they were always going to become.

The fog thickens. The light dims further. The drone shifts up by a quarter-tone, and somewhere in the deep water, something vast and patient and made of everything this place is made of completes its rise and breaks the surface without a sound.

The fauna begins again. The machines shudder back into their cycles. The water resumes its voice.

And you are still standing there, ankle-deep, in the warm black water, at the bottom of the world, in the place where industry and geology and biology and something older than all three have merged into a single, breathing, *loud*, impossible thing that has no name in any language except the one the water speaks.

The water is still speaking.

It is still saying your name.

You have not yet decided whether to answer.
