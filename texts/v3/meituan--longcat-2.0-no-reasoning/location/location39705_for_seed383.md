Below the surface, where the rock has finally stopped dreaming and settled into something like its true shape, there lies a city so old it has forgotten it was ever built.

Not abandoned. Not ruined. Still occupied. Still argued over, repaired, vandalized, lit up every night by fist-sized phosphorescent stones wired to nothing anyone can trace. The streets have been worn flat by feet that existed before the species walking them now. The corners are smoothed to soft shoulders. The staircases have developed concavities. Everything is slightly damp.

Water is the only thing that remembers the surface. It finds its way in through fractures so deep their origins are probably tectonic, and when it arrives it arrives in a state that is somewhere between mist and slow weather. You don't get rain exactly. The air just gets heavier, the walls just start sweating, and there is a period—long or short, nobody's keeping time that way—where the whole undercity is humid and bright and sounds like it's leaking. Then it passes. The stone dries out, or doesn't, depending on which chamber you're in. Some rooms stay damp for what locals call "a long time" and other locals call "an eternity." There's no agreement. There never was.

The brightness is the first thing visitors notice. The city is drenched in it. Not electric light—nothing so modern or consistent—but the accumulated glow of countless small sources. Luminescent minerals embedded in the walls. Colonies of something that might be fungus or might be slime mold or might be neither, cultures kept alive on slurry in wide shallow basins that the city's inhabitants tend like gardens, though nobody would call them gardens because there are no plants, no recognizable biology, nothing green. The light they produce is pale blue and white and sometimes faintly yellow, and it is everywhere, and it makes the whole city feel like it's underwater even when the air is dry.

The city is mostly stone. The stone is old. Geologically old, older than the city that sits in it, older than the caverns that house it, which were themselves carved by some unimaginable erosion that happened so long ago it's hard to tell whether it was water or heat or pressure or the slow hunger of the planet itself. The erosion has stopped. The place is stable. It has found its final shape, or close to it, and now it sits in that shape like a creature that has stopped trying to grow.

But it is also turbulent. That's the thing that's hard to explain to people who haven't been there. The stone is calm. The people are not.

The city is alive with activity. Not human activity—or not only human, though the people here are humanoid enough that the word sticks. They move through the streets in dense irregular clusters, trading things, carrying things, arguing about things, building things, tearing things apart. The sounds are low and constant: voices, hammers, the scrape of tools on stone, the distant percussion of something being demolished in a district you can't see. It is never loud exactly. The acoustics are strange. The stone swallows sound in some places and amplifies it in others, so you get pockets of eerie quiet and pockets of roaring conversation separated by a single corner. Silence lives here, but it shares the city with noise and neither one wins.

The animals—and there are many, so many—are something else. Not domesticated. Not wild exactly. They coexist with the people in a way that seems neither hostile nor friendly but ancient, like the relationship between two things that have simply been around each other too long to bother with formal arrangements. Some are small and scuttle in the walls. Some are large enough to ride, though nobody rides them; they just move through the streets at their own pace and people move around them. Some fly, or seem to fly, or have some other way of being airborne that the physics of this place permits but the physics of the surface might not. They eat the luminous slime. They eat the mineral crusts on the walls. They eat things that grow in the damp pockets where water has collected and left behind deposits that are probably not organic but are also not quite rock. The people eat those things too. Nobody has ever seen a plant here. The whole food chain—if it is a food chain—is built on something other than photosynthesis. Something older. Something that doesn't need the sun, which is good, because there isn't one.

The buildings are dense but not tall. They cluster along the main thoroughfares and thin out as the passages narrow, but even in the thin-out areas there is construction, always construction, because the city is being remade constantly even as it holds its ancient shape. People add rooms. People knock out walls. People stack new stone on old stone in ways that are not quite engineering and not quite art. The tech is simple: hand tools, levers, ropes, fire. Metal exists but it's rare and precious and mostly used for blades and fittings, not structures. The structures are stone and bone and something like ceramic that they make from a local clay, fired in kilns built into the rock itself, using fuel that burns blue and smells like hot minerals.

The weirdness is hard to pin down because it's distributed. It's not one big impossible thing; it's a thousand small ones that accumulate. Water that runs uphill in certain passages—just a few inches, just enough to notice, and only when nobody's watching directly, or so people claim. Shadows that lag behind their objects by a half-second. Animals that appear in mirrors but not in rooms. Tools that are found where no tool should be, exactly where they're needed, with no explanation. None of it is dramatic. None of it is threatening. It's the texture of a place that has been inhabited for so long that reality has worn thin in spots, and something else—something older, something geological and mineral and patient—has started to show through.

The people don't talk about it much. They have more immediate concerns. The slime gardens need tending. The walls need mending. The damp season is coming again, and the lower districts will flood as they always do, and someone needs to move the luminous stones to higher ground or the city will go dark for a week, and a dark city is a city where the animals get bold, and the bold animals knock things over, and the things they knock over are often load-bearing.

So they work. They work constantly. The city hums with it—a low, mineral, almost subsonic hum that you feel in your teeth more than hear with your ears. The sound of a place that is ancient and alive and damp and bright and full of animals and full of people and full of something else, something that might be the stone itself, something that might be the slow patient intelligence of a world that has been underground for longer than anyone can remember and has decided, for now, to keep going.
