# The Weeping Arcade

A low, sprawling factory settlement that has been here so long it has stopped pretending to be anything other than what it is.

---

## The Ground You Walk On

The floor is a kind of ironite — not quite stone, not quite metal — that has developed a permanent skin of moisture across every surface. The dampness never pools, never runs, never evaporates in any way you can detect. It simply *is*. Each surface holds a uniform film of water about as thick as a breath on glass, and when your foot comes down, the film parts exactly where pressure demands, then reforms behind you like a slow blink. The ironite underneath is dark, the color of old engine oil, and where it has fractured — along seams you can trace for a hundred meters in either direction — the fractures are full of standing water the color of weak tea.

The moisture makes everything faintly iridescent. Not oily-rainbow iridescence, but something cooler, like the sheen on the inside of a shell. Light across the floor doesn't so much reflect as *diffuse sideways*, so that every surface seems to glow faintly from within.

## The Structure

This is a settlement. Unmistakably a settlement. But it has been here so long that the distinction between *built* and *grown* and *worn into shape* has collapsed entirely.

The walls rise irregularly — here a clean concrete pillar pocked with rust blooms, there a wall of stacked ironite blocks so precisely fitted that no mortar was ever used, there a facade of bolted steel plates where the bolts themselves have become part of the architecture, overbuilt, ornamental, bearing nothing. The walls are low by city standards, mostly two to four meters, occasionally six. Nothing rises above a single industrial story. The settlement spreads horizontally, more like a village compressed into a factory district compressed into a single enclosed space.

The corridors between structures are the width of cart-paths. Not streets. Not roads. Cart-paths. The ironite floor is worn into shallow grooves here, parallel pairs about a meter apart, scored by something that rolled and rolled for so long that the grooves have become permanent features, as much a part of the plan as the walls.

## What Was Made, What Remains

The buildings are full of machines, but the machines are not running in any way you'd recognize. They are in their final state — every gear at rest, every piston mid-stroke or fully extended or fully compressed, every belt hanging slack or taut in whatever position it last held. And they *stay there*. Nothing moves. Nothing has moved here in a span of time you'd struggle to guess — long enough that the damp has etched oxidation patterns into every moving part, freezing the machines in their arrested gestures the way amber preserves the position of a limb.

Some of the machines are enormous. A press the size of a house, its die plate suspended a meter above its anvil, the gap between them full of that same standing tea-water. A row of lathes, their chucks still gripping workpieces that have fused to the jaws. A forge whose firebrick lining has gone white and smooth from heat and time, and whose gas lines still connect to manifolds that still connect to a distribution system that still connects, presumably, to a source of gas somewhere — though you find no tanks, no mains, no explanation. The infrastructure is complete. It simply isn't *doing* anything.

This is not a ruin. Ruins are places where function has been forgotten and structure is slowly failing. This is the opposite. The structure is *intact*. Every bolt is tight. Every wall is plumb. Every conduit reaches its junction box. The machines are in their exact final positions, preserved by the damp and the stillness, and the whole place has an air of *completion* rather than abandonment. It feels like it did what it was built to do, and has now been set down gently and left exactly as it was at the moment of finishing.

## The Absence

There is no plant life of any kind. No moss on the walls (the damp should permit it, but it's not there). No lichen. No vines threading through the broken ironite. No fungus in the corners. Nothing green, nothing organic at all except —

But we'll get to that.

The air smells of mineral damp and hot metal that is no longer hot. There is a faint ozone tang, the ghost of electrical work, though you find no active current anywhere. The temperature is cool but not cold — the cool of a basement, of underground space, of a place that the sun never directly reaches.

## The Fauna

Here is where the place becomes what it is.

They are everywhere. The settlement is *saturated* with them.

They are not animals in any taxonomic sense. They are not insects, not reptiles, not anything with a body plan you could sketch and label. They are *life*, unmistakably — they move, they respond, they gather and scatter — but they are life in a form that seems to have been assembled from spare parts of the industrial vocabulary.

Imagine a creature about the size of your hand, with a body like a segment of braided copper cable, articulated at intervals like a millipede but with more joints, more freedom. Its legs are thin steel rods that tap the ironite floor in rapid, precise patterns — *tik-a-tik-a-tik-a-tik* — as it moves. It has no head that you can identify. At one end, a cluster of what look like small ball bearings are arranged in a hemisphere, and they rotate independently of each other, tracking movement, tracking sound, tracking *you*. It uses these as its sensory apparatus. When you move, every ball-bearing eye in the cluster adjusts.

They move along the corridors in slow, dense processions — dozens at a time, hundreds if you count the ones in the buildings, in the machines, in the fractures in the floor where the tea-water collects. They do not avoid you. They flow around your legs like a stream parting around a stone, maintaining exactly the same speed, the same spacing, the same direction. Their leg-tapping creates a constant percussion — not chaotic, not rhythmic, but *dense*, a hiss of metallic contact that fills the air the way rain fills the air in a forest.

Others are different. There are larger ones — the size of a dog — that move through the buildings on what appear to be adapted conveyor rollers, their bodies flat and wide, sliding along the floors with a faint mechanical hum. There are small ones, barely visible, that cling to the walls and the undersides of pipes in clusters, and when you look at a cluster closely you realize they are *wired together* — fine copper filaments connect them in a mesh, and the whole cluster pulses with faint bioluminescence, a blue-white glow that cycles through patterns too slow and too complex to read as signal.

There are ones that live in the standing water. Flat, coppery, they skate across the tea-colored surface without breaking tension, moving in geometric patterns — perfect hexagons, perfect triangles — and when two of them approach each other, they exchange a small electrical spark, a flash you can see but not hear, and adjust course.

The sound they make, collectively, is extraordinary. The leg-tapping is the bass note — a constant, dry, rattling rain of metal on metal. Above that, the hum of the roller-creatures. Above *that*, a high, thin singing from the wall-clusters, a sound like a fluorescent tube about to fail, wavering at the edge of hearing. And threaded through everything, a deeper pulse — felt more than heard — that seems to come from the largest creatures, the ones you glimpse only at the edges of rooms, moving through doorways too large to be doorways into spaces too large to see into.

The place is *loud*. Not painfully, not aggressively. But there is no quiet corner, no silent room. The life fills every space with its sound, and the sound has a density to it, a *texture*, like standing inside a bell that is always almost ringing.

## The Rules of the Place

Nothing moves that does not belong. The machines stay exactly as they are. The water stays exactly where it is. The creatures move, but they move in patterns that have the quality of *established process* — not random, not exploratory, but settled, like channels worn into stone by water that has flowed the same way for so long that the channel *is* the thing, not the water.

The damp does not increase. The damp does not decrease. If you watch a surface for an hour, the film of water remains exactly as thick as it was when you arrived. If you press your hand to the floor and lift it, the film reforms in exactly the same way it did the first time.

The creatures do not eat. They do not sleep. They do not die, as far as you can tell. They move and pulse and tap and hum and spark, and the quality of their activity does not vary. The density of creatures in a given space is always the same. The sound level is always the same. The light —

## The Light

The light is normal in character but distributed strangely. It seems to come from everywhere and nowhere. Not from fixtures (there are fixtures — glass globes mounted on walls, conduits running to junction boxes — but they are dark, finished, part of the architecture the way the rust is part of the pillar). The light has a quality of *ambient fill*, slightly warm, slightly diffuse, as if the air itself has a low luminosity. It casts soft shadows. It is enough to see by comfortably. It does not change.

## The Strangeness

The strangeness is not in any single thing. It is in the *combination*. A settlement that is fully built, fully equipped, fully intact, and fully still. A world of metal and mineral and damp that is *teeming* with life that is made of metal and mineral and damp. A place that has reached equilibrium — not the equilibrium of death, but the equilibrium of a system that has found its final state and now simply *is*, without change, without decay, without growth, without end.

The creatures are not using the machines. The machines are not producing anything. The settlement is not a settlement in the sense of a place where people live and work. It is a settlement in the sense of a place where *something* has settled — has come to rest, has found its configuration, and now holds.

You stand in a corridor between two buildings. The ironite floor gleams with its permanent film of water. A procession of cable-bodied creatures flows around your ankles, their ball-bearing eyes tracking you with mechanical precision, their legs tapping out a rhythm that is not quite random and not quite regular. From inside the building to your left, the hum of a roller-creature. From the wall to your right, the thin singing of a cluster. From somewhere deeper in the settlement, the subsonic pulse of something large.

The air smells of mineral damp and old electricity.

Nothing changes.

Nothing has ever changed.

This is what it is.
