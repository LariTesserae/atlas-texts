# The Drowned Terrace

There is no sky here. Or rather, the sky is water — a ceiling of slow-moving ocean held at a distance above the city by nothing you can name, a membrane of pressure and ancient agreement between weight and weight. The city breathes beneath it.

---

You stand on a broad, shallow shelf of stone that tilts almost imperceptibly toward a central depression. The stone is old — not geologically old, but *inhabited*-old, the way a floor becomes old under generations of feet. It has been worn into channels, basins, hollows that collect warm seawater in pools of varying depth. Some are ankle-deep. Some are chest-deep. Some drop away into the dark and you cannot see the bottom, only feel the temperature shift as colder water rises from below.

The water is everywhere. It is the medium. It fills the air in a permanent, warm mist that is not quite rain and not quite fog — droplets so fine they hang in suspension, refracting what little light exists into a diffuse amber glow that seems to come from the water itself, or from the stone, or from the organisms living in both. Visibility extends perhaps forty meters before the haze thickens into a soft wall of gold-grey. You can hear things beyond it. You cannot see them.

---

The city — if it is a city — is built from the same stone as the shelf, or perhaps it *is* the shelf, carved and shaped rather than constructed. Structures rise in low, rounded forms: domes, half-pillars, walls that curve and branch like coral but with an intentionality that coral does not possess. They are dense. They crowd against each other, share walls, lean, interlock. Passages between them are narrow — sometimes wide enough for one body, sometimes requiring you to turn sideways, sometimes opening suddenly into small plazas where the ceiling of ocean above is closer and the light is fractionally brighter.

There are no straight lines. Everything has been softened by the water over time — edges rounded, corners dissolved, surfaces pitted and re-pitted until they have a texture like old bone. The erosion is not destructive here. It is *collaborative*. The water and the stone have reached an understanding. The stone gives itself slowly, and the water carries the dissolved mineral away to deposit it somewhere else, building new forms in other parts of the city. The city is simultaneously being unmade and remade. It has been doing this for so long that the process *is* the architecture.

---

The warmth is notable. The water that pools and flows through the city is several degrees above what you'd expect at this depth — there are vents somewhere below, fissures in the deep rock where geothermal heat rises. You can find them if you follow the temperature gradients: pockets where the water becomes almost hot, where it shimmers with convection, where certain organisms cluster in thick mats of color. The warmth rises through the stone and through the water and through the mist, so that the entire environment holds a steady, enveloping heat. It is not uncomfortable. It is the warmth of a body.

---

Life is abundant but not lush. The vegetation parameter is low, and it shows — there are no forests of kelp, no meadows of sea grass. Instead, what grows here is *microbial*: films, crusts, slimes, mats. They coat every surface in thin layers of rust-red, pale gold, deep violet, black-green. They are the primary producers, the base of everything, and they are everywhere, so that the city has a living skin. In places where the geothermal vents are close, the mats thicken into pillowy masses the size of bodies, soft and warm and faintly luminescent — the only real light source in this place, a dim bioluminescence that pulses slowly, asynchronously, so that the city seems to breathe with faint light.

And then there are the animals.

They are small, mostly. Worms that thread through the stone's pores. Crustaceans that pick their way across the microbial mats with delicate, deliberate legs. Things like sea slugs that leave trails of iridescence on the walls. Small, pale fish that school in the deeper channels, their scales catching the bioluminescent glow and throwing it back in brief silver flashes. There are larger things too — you hear them in the passages, the scrape and click of something moving through a tunnel too narrow for you, the distant thud of something heavy shifting position in a deep pool. You don't see them clearly. They are shy, or they are nocturnal in a place where night and day have no meaning, or they simply exist at the edges of your perception, aware of you in ways you are not yet aware of them.

The density of life is high but the *variety* is not. This is a specialized ecosystem, a closed loop. The microbial mats feed the grazers. The grazers feed the small predators. The small predators feed the large ones. Everything feeds the mats when it dies. The water circulates nutrients. The heat drives the circulation. It is a machine made of biology, and it has been running for a long time.

---

The quiet is the thing you notice most after the warmth and the dark and the strangeness. The water muffles everything. Sound travels differently here — it comes from unexpected directions, bends around the curved walls, pools in the plazas and dissipates in the narrow passages. You hear your own breathing, loud and close. You hear the faint, continuous hiss of the mist, which might be the sound of millions of tiny droplets colliding. You hear, very distantly, a rhythmic tapping — biological, probably, some organism with a hard shell and a repetitive behavior. You hear the water moving through channels, a low murmur that rises and falls like conversation in another room.

And sometimes you hear nothing at all, and the silence is so complete and so warm and so dark that you lose the boundary between your body and the water and the stone, and for a moment you are not a visitor. You are part of the erosion. You are part of the mat. You are a dissolved mineral being carried somewhere else to become something new.

---

The weirdness is subtle. It is not the weirdness of alien geometry or impossible physics. It is the weirdness of *familiar things in wrong relationships*. The city is built but there are no builders — or the builders are the erosion itself, the water, the slow accretion of mineral on mineral. The light is biological but it behaves almost like architecture, pooling in certain plazas, absent from certain passages, as if someone designed it. The animals are recognizable — worms, crustaceans, fish — but their behaviors are slightly off, slightly coordinated, as if the ecosystem itself has a rhythm that individual organisms follow without knowing why.

There is a sense of *intention without an intelligence behind it*. The place feels designed. It is not. It has simply been running long enough that optimization looks like purpose.

---

You move through it slowly. The water resists you gently. The stone is slick underfoot but not treacherous — it has been walked on, if not by feet then by the bodies of things that move, and it has adapted. The passages open and close. The light pulses. The warmth holds you.

Somewhere below, the vents breathe their heat into the deep water. Somewhere above, the ocean ceiling shifts in slow currents you cannot feel. The city dissolves and accretes. The mats grow and are grazed and grow again. The fish school and scatter and school.

It has always been like this. It will continue to be like this. You are the anomaly here — the brief, dry, loud thing passing through a world that has no need of you and will not remember you, but which holds you, for now, in its warm and patient dark.
