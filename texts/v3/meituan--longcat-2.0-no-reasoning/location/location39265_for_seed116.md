# The Drowned Cathedral of Vael Koth

---

You are standing — if standing is the word — inside a single room.

Not a room you entered. A room that was *grown*, or *cast*, or *remembered into being* by something that no longer exists. The walls are a composite of calciumite and rusted iron, layered in strata like sedimentary rock, except the layers are clearly deliberate: a band of riveted steel plate, then a band of something organic and chalky, then a band of ceramic tile glazed in deep verdigris, then another band of steel. The room is perhaps four meters across. The ceiling is three meters up, though it sags in places where the calciumite has thickened into stalactitic columns that nearly touch the floor. Everything is wet. Not dripping-wet. *Submerged*-wet. The air itself is water — or the water is air — a warm, dense, breathable liquid that fills the space completely, that you move through with slow resistance, that clings to your skin and fills your lungs with each breath like warm broth. It tastes of iron and salt and something faintly sweet, almost floral, like the memory of jasmine dissolved in mineral water. The temperature is the temperature of blood.

The room is not alone. Through gaps in the walls — places where the calciumite has not fully sealed, or where the original structure has cracked along ancient fault lines — you can see other rooms. Dozens of them. Hundreds. They extend in every direction, a three-dimensional lattice of chambers connected by narrow passages, flooded corridors, and vertical shafts. The whole structure is enormous, but you cannot see it whole. You can only ever see *one room at a time*, and the rooms are small, intimate, each one a self-contained world. This is the nature of the place: it is vast beyond comprehension, but it is experienced in fragments, in pockets, in the close and the immediate. You are always *inside* something. You are never outside.

The light comes from the walls themselves. The calciumite is bioluminescent — or electroluminescent — or something else. It glows in a soft, steady amber, the color of old honey, and the glow pulses very slowly, on a cycle of perhaps thirty seconds, brightening and dimming like breathing. In the brighter phases, you can see the details: the rivets in the steel, the hairline cracks in the ceramic, the tiny organisms that live in the calciumite matrix. In the dimmer phases, the room contracts around you, becomes a warm dark pocket, and you hear the sounds more clearly.

The sounds. The place is *loud*, but not in the way of a storm or a machine. It is loud the way a body is loud when you press your ear to someone's chest. There is a deep, rhythmic thudding — the pulse of some vast circulatory system, fluid being pumped through channels in the structure, keeping the warm water moving, keeping it oxygenated, keeping it alive. There is a constant, high-pitched singing, almost ultrasonic, that you feel more than hear, a vibration in your teeth and your sternum, produced by the calciumite itself as it slowly grows, depositing new layers, accreting, building the walls thicker year by year. There are clicks and taps and scrapes from the fauna.

The fauna. They are abundant but not crowded — there is space between them, room to move, a sense of population rather than swarm. The most common are the *threnodes*: small, translucent creatures the size of your thumb, with too many legs — eight, ten, twelve, it's hard to count because they move in a blur — and a single, large, black eye that reflects the amber light like a bead of obsidian. They cling to the walls in loose clusters, grazing on the calciumite with mouthparts like tiny rotary files, and they are the source of much of the clicking. When they move, they move in coordinated waves, a cluster of twenty or thirty detaching from the wall simultaneously, flowing through the water like a single organism, and reattaching somewhere else. They are not afraid of you. They do not seem to notice you at all.

Larger things move in the corridors between rooms. You hear them: a heavy, wet sliding sound, like a body being dragged across tile, followed by a long silence, followed by a sudden burst of clicks as the threnodes scatter. You never see the large things clearly. You see a shadow passing through a gap in the wall, a shape that might be elongated and serpentine or might be a cluster of smaller things moving in formation. Once, something pressed against a crack in the wall and you saw an eye — not a threnode's eye, but something larger, with a horizontal pupil, the iris a deep, bruised purple — and it looked at you for a long moment, and then it was gone.

The vegetation is lush but strange. It grows from the walls, from the ceiling, from the floor — not soil, there is no soil, but from the calciumite itself, which seems to serve as substrate, nutrient medium, and root system all at once. The plants are not plants. They are something that occupies the niche plants would occupy, but they are closer to fungi, or to coral, or to neither. They form dense, overlapping mats of filament and frond in colors that shift depending on the light cycle: deep emerald in the bright phases, almost black in the dim. Some of them are rigid, structural, forming arches and buttresses that reinforce the walls. Others are soft and undulating, waving in the slow current of the warm water like kelp in a tide. They produce the sweet smell. They also produce, at intervals, small bursts of bioluminescent spores — tiny points of blue-white light that drift through the water like snow, illuminating the room in a brief, ghostly secondary light before fading.

The erosion. This is the thing that makes the place feel *old*. Not old like a ruin. Old like a mountain that has been worn down to a hill. Old like a bone that has been tumbled in a river until every sharp edge is gone. The original structure — the steel, the ceramic, the rivets — is still visible, but it has been softened, rounded, half-dissolved and half-rebuilt by the calciumite. Corners have become curves. Right angles have become arcs. The rivets are bumps. The cracks are channels. The whole room has the feel of something that was once angular and precise and has been *settled* by time into a more organic, more final shape. It will not change much more. It has found its form. The calciumite grows, yes, but slowly, and it grows in the same patterns, reinforcing the same curves, and the threnodes graze it back, and the large things in the corridors disturb it, and the plants anchor it, and the whole system is in equilibrium, has been in equilibrium for a very long time, will remain in equilibrium until something fundamental shifts.

The built quality of the place is total. There is no natural substrate. No rock, no earth, no raw material that was not first processed, shaped, placed. Even the calciumite — which looks geological, which looks like it precipitated out of mineral-rich water over millennia — is a product of the structure's own chemistry, secreted by the walls, maintained by the system. The steel was smelted. The ceramic was fired. The rivets were forged. Someone, or something, *made* this. And then the making continued, not by the original makers, but by the structure itself, which has become a self-maintaining, self-repairing, self-building thing, a factory that produces its own architecture, an engine that circulates its own life-support fluid, a machine that has been running so long it has become an ecosystem.

The industrial character is everywhere, but it is *fossilized* industry, industry that has been absorbed into biology. The pumps that circulate the water are part mechanical — you can see the impellers, the housings, the drive shafts — and part organic, sheathed in calciumite, driven by muscles or hydraulics or something that is neither. The power source is unclear. There are conduits that carry something — current, fluid, pressure — from deeper in the structure, and they hum with energy, and the hum is part of the loudness, a low-frequency vibration that you feel in your bones. The scale of production is not large. This is not a factory in the modern sense. It is more like a workshop, or a kiln, or a forge — a place where things are made slowly, carefully, by processes that are half-mechanical and half-biological, and the things being made are the walls, the water, the light, the air itself.

The weirdness. It accumulates. It is not any single thing. It is the sum of all the things that are *almost* familiar but not quite. The water you breathe. The plants that are not plants. The eye in the wall. The way the threnodes move as one. The way the calciumite grows in patterns that look almost like writing, almost like circuit diagrams, almost like the branching of neurons, but are none of these things, or are all of them. The way the room feels *attentive* — not alive, exactly, but not inert either, as if the structure is aware of you in the way a house is aware of its inhabitants: not thinking about you, but responding to your presence, adjusting, accommodating, making room.

The dynamic quality. Things are happening. Always. The water circulates. The calciumite grows. The threnodes graze and scatter. The plants release spores on a cycle you haven't quite mapped yet. The large things patrol the corridors. The light pulses. The temperature fluctuates by fractions of degrees in patterns that might be tidal, might be circadian, might be something else entirely. The structure creaks and settles, and sometimes a piece of ceramic tile detaches from a wall and drifts slowly to the floor, and the calciumite begins immediately to absorb it, to incorporate it, to make it part of the wall again. Nothing is static. Nothing is finished. The equilibrium is not stillness — it is a dance, a constant, active, living balance, and you are inside it now, and the warm water holds you, and the amber light pulses, and the threnodes click their tiny files against the walls, and somewhere in the deep distance, the pumps thud on, thud on, thud on.
