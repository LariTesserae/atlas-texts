# The Root-Halls of Vael Keth

---

You come to it through a gap in a ridge of black stone — not carved, not quarried, but *split*, as though the mountain cracked open along a seam of something that wanted out. The fissure is narrow enough that your shoulders brush both walls, and the stone is cold, weeping. Water runs down the rock in thin, continuous sheets, not dripping but *flowing*, a skin of moisture that never quite becomes a stream. The air tastes of mineral and green.

Then the gap opens, and you are inside.

---

The space is perhaps two hundred meters across — a bowl in the high country, ringed on three sides by those same black, freshly-broken cliffs. They are young mountains, geologically speaking. You can see where the rock sheared: angular faces, no rounding, no soil on the upper reaches, just raw fracture planes with water seeping from every joint and bedding line. The stone is dark grey, almost blue where it's wet, and it carries a faint smell of iron and cold. Lichen has begun its slow colonization in pale green and rust-colored patches near the water lines, but the cliffs are mostly bare. They look like they were broken last winter. They were broken last century. In mountain terms, this is a fresh wound.

The fourth side of the bowl slopes downward and away, and through the gap you can see other peaks, other ridges, all of them sharp and new and wet. The sky is low. Not storming — just *low*, a ceiling of pale grey cloud that presses down close enough that the highest cliff-tops disappear into it. The light is diffuse, directionless, the color of old pewter. It never gets truly dark here, and it never gets bright. It is always the hour just before dawn, even at midday.

---

But the cliffs are not what holds your attention.

The bowl is *full*.

Not full of water, though water is everywhere — full of *growth*. The floor of the bowl is a single continuous organism, or perhaps a colony so old and so interwoven that the distinction has ceased to matter. It is plant matter, but not like any forest you have walked through. There is no ground here. There is no soil you can see. There is only *structure*: a dense, interlocking architecture of living tissue that rises, falls, arches, and buttresses itself into something that resembles a city if a city were grown instead of built.

The primary material is a woody vine — or perhaps a root, or perhaps a stem; it is thick as a forearm near the base, tapering to finger-width tendrils at its growing tips, and it is the deep red-brown of old blood. It grows in coils and lattices, weaving itself into walls, floors, arches, and vaulted ceilings. Walking surfaces exist — broad, flat platforms of compressed and interlocked vine, springy underfoot, always slightly damp. They are connected by ramps and spiraling staircases of the same material, all of it alive, all of it still growing. The edges of every surface fray into fine rootlets that reach downward, probing, seeking purchase, seeking moisture.

Between the structural vines, everything else has found a place. Mosses in a dozen shades of green and one shade of near-black fill every gap and joint, thick as upholstery, soft as felt. Ferns erupt from the walls of this living architecture — not the delicate ferns of temperate forests, but coarse, leathery things with fronds as long as your arm, their undersides heavy with moisture that beads and drips in a constant, quiet percussion. Fungi occupy the lower levels and the undersides of platforms: shelf fungi the color of bone, cup fungi filled with clear water, and long, pale, root-like rhizomorphs that hang in curtains from the ceilings of vine-vaulted chambers, swaying faintly in air currents you cannot feel.

And everywhere — *everywhere* — there is water. It seeps from the cliff faces and runs down the vine-structures in thin rivulets. It collects in the cup fungi, in the moss, in the curled hearts of new fern fronds. It beads on every surface. The air is saturated; your skin is wet within minutes of entering, your clothing heavy with absorbed moisture. The cloud ceiling presses the humidity down, traps it, holds it in this bowl like water in a cup. There is no evaporation here, or rather, the evaporation happens at the same rate as the seepage, and the balance is perfect and ancient and unchanging.

---

The scale of the structure is intimate but complex. Individual chambers — and they *are* chambers, enclosed spaces with walls and ceilings and sometimes doors (arches, really, draped with hanging rootlets like beaded curtains) — are perhaps five to ten meters across. You could live in one. Someone could. The platforms are wide enough for a person to lie down. The ceilings are high enough to stand. There are spaces that feel like rooms, spaces that feel like hallways, spaces that feel like plazas where several platforms meet and the architecture opens upward toward the grey sky. The total extent is bounded by the bowl itself — you can walk from one end to the other in perhaps ten minutes — but within that space, the density is extraordinary. Every cubic meter is occupied. Vine over moss over fern over fungus over vine again, layer upon layer, a three-dimensional maze of living material so packed that you cannot see more than a few meters in any direction before the green and brown and grey closes in.

It is quiet. The word does not do it justice. There is sound — the drip and patter of water, the faint creak of vine against vine as the structure shifts in imperceptible growth, the occasional soft *tap* of a water droplet falling from a height onto a broad leaf — but these sounds are so constant, so evenly distributed, that they blur into a kind of silence. A silence with texture. A silence that is not the absence of sound but the presence of so much quiet sound that the ear stops distinguishing individual events. You hear it as a single, soft, green hush.

---

There are animals. Not many. A pale, eyeless salamander — long-bodied, slow-moving — lives in the water that collects in the cup fungi, and you see them occasionally, motionless, their gills like tiny red feathers along their necks. Something small and quick moves through the upper canopy of the structure at night — you hear it, a faint rustling, a soft thud as it leaps from one platform to another — but you never see it clearly. There are insects, or insect-analogues: small, dark, hard-shelled things that live in the moss and emerge when you disturb it, scattering like drops of water in reverse. And there are birds, sometimes, passing overhead through the gap in the ridge — high, fast, calling to each other in sharp, cold voices — but they do not land here. Something about the place discourages them. Perhaps the density. Perhaps the wet. Perhaps something else.

---

The something else.

There is a quality to the Root-Halls that is difficult to name and impossible to ignore. The structure is *organized*. Not randomly grown — organized. The platforms are level. The arches are true. The ramps connect in ways that suggest intention, or at least function, as though the whole thing were designed for habitation by something that needed to move through three dimensions, that needed dry(ish) surfaces above the water-line, that needed enclosed spaces and open spaces in a particular sequence. The vine-lattices repeat in patterns. The chamber-sizes cluster around a consistent dimension. The hanging rootlet-curtains are spaced at regular intervals.

No one built this. There are no tools here, no cut marks, no evidence of any technology whatsoever. The vine grew this way. The moss filled these gaps. The ferns found these niches. And yet the result is *architectural* in a way that feels deliberate, as though the ecosystem itself were a builder, as though the accumulated intelligence of ten thousand years of growth had converged on a form that serves a purpose you cannot identify.

You find, in the deepest chamber — the one closest to the base of the largest cliff, where the water seeps most heavily and the light is dimmest — a space that is different from the others. The floor is smooth. Not worn-smooth by traffic, but *grown*-smooth, the vine-tissue compressed and polished to a surface like dark wood. The walls curve. The ceiling is a perfect dome. And in the center of the floor, there is a depression, a shallow bowl perhaps a meter across, filled with clear, still water that has no visible source.

The water in the bowl does not drip. It does not ripple. It is perfectly, unnaturally still, and it is slightly warmer than everything else in the Root-Halls — not warm, exactly, but *less cold*, as though something beneath it is generating a faint, persistent heat. The air above it does not condense. The moss around its edges grows in a precise circle.

You do not touch it. You are not sure why. You simply don't.

---

The place is alive in the way that a city is alive — not with the life of individual organisms, but with the emergent life of a system. Things are happening constantly, at a pace just below the threshold of perception. A vine-tip extends by a millimeter. A fern uncurls. A fungus releases a cloud of spores you cannot see but can smell — a faint, sweet, underground scent that rises from the lower levels like breath. Water moves through the system in channels too small to trace, drawn upward by capillary action, pulled downward by gravity, cycling through the living architecture in a closed loop that has no beginning and no end.

The Root-Halls are growing. Slowly. In a year, a chamber may be a hand's breadth larger. In a decade, a new arch may have formed. In a century, the structure may have extended to fill the bowl entirely, sealing it into a single, continuous, living mass beneath the low grey sky. The cliffs will continue to weep. The water will continue to flow. The moss will continue to thicken. And the architecture — the strange, deliberate, undesigned architecture — will continue to build itself, room by room, chamber by chamber, in the cold and the wet and the green, green silence.

---

You leave the way you came, through the gap in the ridge, and the mountain closes behind you like a door that was never a door. The air outside is drier, sharper, colder. The light is the same — always that pewter dimness, always that hour-before-dawn — but it feels different without the moisture, without the green, without the hush.

Behind you, the Root-Halls continue their slow, wet, patient work.
