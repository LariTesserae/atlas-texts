# The Hollow Choir

---

You are standing inside something vast and dead that has not yet stopped singing.

The darkness is not the absence of light. It is the presence of something else — a medium, thick and faintly viscous, that fills the air the way water fills a sea. You can feel it pressing against your skin, your eyes, the inside of your lungs. It does not obstruct movement so much as it *records* every movement. Each step leaves a slow trail of disturbance behind you, a wake of rearranged darkness that takes a long time to settle, spiraling outward in complex patterns that never quite repeat. In the near-distance, you see these wakes everywhere — the luminous residue of motion, uncountable overlapping signatures of things that have moved through this space over what might be years, or might be centuries, the oldest trails barely luminous, almost fully decayed into the background like memories fading in still water.

You are inside a structure, though it took a long time to understand that. The space is roughly circular in plan, perhaps ninety meters across, though the walls are difficult to perceive directly. They exist at the edges of your awareness — curved surfaces rising and arching overhead into a ceiling that might be fifteen meters high or might be a hundred. Distance is unreliable here. The darkness is not uniform; it pools and stratifies, thicker near the floor, thinner near the ceiling, and the boundaries between these layers are faintly visible as a kind of slow shimmer, like heat haze but operating at a glacial pace. Through these layers, you can sometimes see the walls: not stone, not metal, not any material with a name you know. They have the matte, light-absorbing quality of deep cave surfaces but are impossibly smooth, curving with a precision that suggests manufacture at a scale below visibility. They are wet. Not dripping — the moisture does not fall — but *holding* water, surfaces sheened with a film so thin it barely qualifies as liquid, a molecular-level adhesion that gives everything the look of having just been submerged and only recently emerged. The walls weep slowly, water beading and migrating downward in paths that have worn grooves over incomprehensible time, grooves so shallow and so numerous that the wall surfaces have acquired a fine texture, almost fabric-like, almost biological.

The floor is the same material, the same impossible smoothness, but broken in places. Not cracked — broken, as if something immense once pressed down from above or pushed up from below and the floor *accommodated* by folding rather than shattering. There are ridges and hollows, gentle undulations, places where the floor has warped into shapes that suggest ripples in a pond that froze mid-motion. In the hollows, water has gathered — not pools, exactly, but the same thin adhesive layer, slightly thicker, slightly more liquid, reflecting nothing because there is nothing to reflect. Your footsteps in these areas produce a sound: not a splash, but a low, resonant tone, as if you are tapping the surface of a drum. Each hollow produces a different note. They are tuned. They have always been tuned.

This is why the place is loud.

---

The sound is not coming from any single source. It is the sound of the structure itself, a continuous low resonance that you feel in your chest and teeth before you hear it with your ears. It is made of many tones layered together, a chord that shifts so slowly it seems static, frequencies that beat against each other in patterns that take minutes to cycle. The water contributes — the slow migration of moisture down the walls produces a sound like breathing, like the respiration of something enormous, inhalations and exhalations that last twenty or thirty seconds each. The air — if it is air — moves, barely, a circulation so gentle it is perceptible only by the way it carries sound, bending it, making the tones drift across the space like objects floating in a slow current. And there are other sounds: high, thin, intermittent, coming from the ceiling or from somewhere beyond it. These are the fauna.

You cannot see the fauna. Not at first. They exist in the upper reaches of the space, near or above the ceiling, and the darkness there is thick enough to be nearly opaque. But you can hear them — a complex, layered soundscape of clicks, scrapes, pulses, and long sustained tones that are almost musical. They are not communicating with each other in any way you recognize. They are communicating with the structure. Each click is a probe, a sonar pulse that maps the space and returns modified, carrying information. Each tone is a sustained interaction with one of the resonances — the animals, if they are animals, are *singing with the room*, finding the frequencies that excite specific responses in the walls and floor and riding them, modulating them, extracting something. Energy, perhaps. Information, perhaps. Something else entirely.

There are many of them. The density of sound suggests dozens, maybe hundreds, occupying the upper volume of the space while you stand in the lower. Occasionally one descends far enough to become visible — or rather, it becomes *sensible*, not through light but through its effect on the darkness. It displaces the thick medium, and you see a shape in the negative: a region where the darkness is less dense, roughly cylindrical, perhaps two meters long, tapered at both ends, moving with a sinuous lateral motion like a sideways swim. It has no features you can discern. It is a void-shape, a silhouette of darkness within darkness, distinguishable only by its movement and by the bright wake it leaves behind, a trail of disturbed medium that glows faintly with — not light exactly, but *clarity*, a momentary thinning of the obscuring thickness through which you can see the walls and floor with sudden, startling precision before the darkness fills in again and everything returns to its usual barely-there visibility.

These creatures are the reason the floor is broken. Not directly — they are too light, too fragile-looking for that — but over time, through some process you don't fully understand, their collective activity has stressed the structure. They gather in large numbers at specific points on the ceiling, and their combined sonic output excites resonances that the structure was not designed to sustain, and over very long periods — decades, centuries — the material fatigues. It doesn't crack. It *creeps*, deforming under sustained oscillation the way a metal paperclip bends when flexed repeatedly. The ridges in the floor are the result of this fatigue, slow buckling, patient deformation.

The structure was not designed for this. Nothing here was designed for this.

---

Vegetation grows in the hollows.

Not plants, exactly. Something that occupies the ecological niche of plants, performing a function analogous to photosynthesis but without light. It grows in the thin films of water on the floor and walls, spreading in patterns that follow the water's migration paths, dense networks of fine filaments that are individually invisible but collectively form patches of visible texture — a fuzz, a mat, a living stain that is dark green or dark purple or so dark a red it reads as black. It is lush in the sense that it covers every available surface, every place where water gathers or flows, a continuous living skin broken only by the ridges and the hollows. Where it grows thickest, in the deepest hollows where the water film is most substantial, it forms raised structures — soft, irregular, cushion-like growths that rise a few centimeters above the floor surface, vaguely geometric, almost architectural, as if the organism is trying to build something and keeps almost succeeding. They have the texture of wet felt. They are warm to the touch — warmer than the air, warmer than the walls, generating their own heat through whatever metabolism substitutes for photosynthesis in this lightless world.

This warmth creates microclimates. Over each cushion of vegetation, the air is fractionally warmer, and the darkness is fractionally thinner, and the sound is fractionally different — the resonance of the chamber shifts subtly above these warm spots, the frequency dropping by a fraction of a hertz, and the fauna notice, and orient toward them, and their probing clicks become more frequent, more focused. There is a relationship here that you are only beginning to see: the fauna feed on something the vegetation produces, or the vegetation responds to something the fauna emits, or both, in a co-evolutionary loop that has been running for a very long time in this enclosed space, this cathedral of darkness and water and sound.

---

At the center of the space, there is an artifact.

It is the only thing you would call a building. It rises from the floor — or is embedded in the floor, or grew from the floor — a structure roughly three meters tall, cylindrical, about a meter in diameter, made of the same wall material but shaped with a deliberate precision that contrasts sharply with the natural curvature of everything around it. It has the look of something made, not something formed. Its surface is covered in the same fine grooves as the walls, the same water-migration texture, which means it has been here as long as the walls — as long as anything. It is not damaged. The floor around it has buckled and crept, but the artifact stands exactly where it was placed, its base bonded so thoroughly with the floor material that there is no seam.

It is open at the top. Looking into it, you see a cavity filled with water — not the thin film that covers everything else, but a substantial depth of liquid, still and dark and perfectly clear in a way that is wrong, because there is no light to see through it, and yet you can see that it is clear, your perception bypassing the usual mechanisms. The water in the artifact is warmer than the water on the walls, warmer than the vegetation, and it emits a tone — a single, pure, sustained note, the fundamental frequency of the chamber, the frequency that all the other sounds orbit around. The artifact is the instrument. The chamber is the resonating body. The fauna are the players. And the vegetation, and the water, and the slow deformation of the floor, and the migration of moisture down the walls — all of it is part of a single system, a single process, a single *thing* that has been running for longer than you have frameworks to describe.

The artifact is technology. Not crude, not primitive, not the first attempt. It is mature technology, the product of a civilization that understood something fundamental about the relationship between vibration and matter, between resonance and structure, between sound and substance, and built this place as an expression of that understanding. Or as a tool. Or as an experiment that escaped its parameters. Or as a memorial. You cannot tell. The information is not available in the form you can read.

But it is still running.

---

The water on the walls is migrating. It has always been migrating. It will always be migrating, or at least on a timescale that makes no practical difference. The grooves it has carved are shallow but deepening, micrometers per century, and in a million years the walls will have the texture of eroded stone, and in ten million they will be nearly featureless again, the grooves having deepened to the point where they merge and cancel, the surface returning to smoothness, and the cycle beginning again. The floor is buckling in the same slow rhythm, rising and falling in waves that propagate at the speed of aging bone. The vegetation is growing and dying and growing, each generation of filaments slightly different from the last, evolving in the dark toward something that will not be recognizable as vegetation. The fauna are changing too, their calls shifting over generations, their relationship with the resonance evolving, becoming more complex, more precise, more *intentional*, though whether that intention belongs to them or to the system as a whole is a question without an answer.

This is a high place. Not in the sense of altitude — you are deep, buried, far below any surface — but in the sense of *elevation*, of being raised above a baseline, suspended in a state that is not the lowest energy configuration. The structure is holding itself up against entropy, against gravity, against the slow heat-death of all things, through the continuous input of energy from the resonance. The sound keeps it standing. The vibration prevents the collapse. As long as the artifact sings, as long as the fauna play, as long as the water flows and the vegetation grows and the air circulates and the darkness holds, the structure persists. It is a self-maintaining system, a dynamic equilibrium, a living thing that is not alive.

It is almost empty. The space is vast relative to the objects in it — the single artifact, the scattered cushions of vegetation, the invisible fauna in the ceiling. You could stand in the center and see almost nothing, hear almost everything, and feel the place pressing against you with a presence that has nothing to do with matter and everything to do with *system*, with the sense that you are inside something that is aware of you in a way that does not require consciousness. The darkness records your movements. The resonance shifts to accommodate your mass. The fauna descend to investigate, their clicking intensifying as they map you, their void-shapes spiraling around you in slow orbits, leaving wakes of clarity that make you briefly, luminously visible to yourself — your own body rendered in negative space, a silhouette in the dark, a guest in the hollow choir.

---

The place is warm. The place is wet. The place is old beyond your capacity to age it, and it is still changing, and it will outlast you, and it is, in its slow and voiceless way, *singing*.
