# The Stillworks

---

You are standing on a plain of dark stone that extends in every direction beyond the reach of sight. Not flat — never flat — but *settled*, the way a riverbed is settled after ten thousand years of finding its lowest path. The terrain rolls in long, shallow undulations, like the surface of something that was once liquid and chose, with great patience, to stop. The stone is basalt, mostly, but shot through with veins of something paler — quartz, perhaps, or a mineral with no name in any human tongue — that catch what little light there is and hold it, faintly, like memory.

The light itself is the first thing you notice, or rather the first thing you *fail* to notice, because there is so little of it. The sky is not dark. It is not overcast. It is simply *dim*, as though the sun — if there is a sun — is behind a veil of something translucent and permanent. The illumination has the quality of a room lit by a single candle in a cathedral: you can see, but you cannot see *far*. The horizon is a suggestion. The sky and the stone meet in a line that your eyes keep losing, as though the world is slowly forgetting where it ends.

And it is vast. Not merely large. *Vast* in the way that changes the meaning of distance. The plain does not feel like a place you could cross. It feels like a condition you could inhabit. Weather moves across it at its own discretion — you can see, far to the north or what you believe is north, a slow front of darker dimness crawling across the stone, carrying with it a faint shimmer of moisture. It will not reach you for hours. Perhaps days. Time here moves at the pace of geology.

---

The moisture comes not as rain, exactly, but as a *settling*. The air is cold — not bitterly, not sharply, but with a deep, patient cold that seems to emanate from the stone itself, as though the ground is slowly giving back heat it borrowed long ago and has no intention of borrowing again. In the hollows between the undulations, water gathers in thin sheets, barely deep enough to cover the stone, and freezes at its edges into delicate, fractured plates that catch the dim light and throw it back in pale, angular glints. The water does not flow. It *sits*. It evaporates so slowly that the process is invisible, and what remains is a mineral residue — a faint white crust, like a memory of the water's presence — that builds up over years, over centuries, in layers so thin they can only be detected by touch.

In some places, the moisture finds a crack in the stone and seeps downward, and where it emerges again — lower, in some shallow depression you might not notice unless you were looking — it carries with it a faint warmth, the ghost of geothermal heat from somewhere far below. These are the *seeps*, and they are the only places where anything grows.

---

The vegetation is sparse in the way that a desert is sparse: not absent, but *concentrated*, as though life has had to make decisions about where to spend itself. Around the seeps, in halos no wider than a few meters, there are growths — not plants, exactly, though they perform the function of plants. They are low, dark, fibrous things, more like lichen or fungal mats than anything with stems or leaves, and they spread across the stone in patterns that follow the moisture with absolute fidelity. Their color is a deep, bruised purple-black, and they have a texture like felt, or like the skin of something that has been underwater for a very long time. They do not move. They do not appear to grow while you watch them. But they are alive — you can tell by the way the moisture beads on their surface, held in a thin film by some property of their structure, and by the faint, almost imperceptible warmth they generate, just enough to keep the ice from forming directly over them.

Between the seeps, the stone is bare. Not clean — *bare*. There is a difference. The bare stone is covered in a fine, dark dust, the product of erosion so long-established that it has become the landscape's primary activity. The wind, when there is wind, moves this dust in slow, ground-hugging waves, and it settles into every crack, every depression, every surface, with the patience of sediment becoming rock becoming dust becoming sediment again. The dust is so fine that it does not crunch underfoot. It *compresses*, slightly, and then slowly rebounds, so that your footprints fill themselves in over the course of an hour, as though the plain is gently erasing your presence.

---

And then there are the animals.

They are everywhere. Not in the way that birds are everywhere in a forest, or insects in a meadow — not as a background hum of small lives. They are everywhere in the way that *weather* is everywhere: as a condition of the place, as something you move through rather than something you observe. They are the fauna, and they are overwhelming.

They are large. Most of them are large. The plain supports a biomass that seems impossible given the scarcity of vegetation, and the reason for this — the reason that will take you a long time to understand, if you ever do — is that the animals here do not eat the vegetation. They eat *each other*, in a food web so compressed and so efficient that it has become almost a single organism, a circulating system of flesh and hunger that moves across the plain in slow, tidal patterns.

The ones you see first are the *walkers*. They are quadrupedal, roughly the size of cattle, with long, narrow bodies and legs that seem too thin for their weight, though they carry it without apparent difficulty. Their skin is the same dark color as the stone, and it has a texture like the dust — matte, absorbent, almost velvety — so that when they stand still against the plain, they disappear. You do not see them until they move. They move slowly, with a gait that is more like *drifting* than walking, and they travel in loose aggregations of twenty or thirty, spaced at regular intervals, covering ground with the methodical patience of grazing animals, though they are not grazing. They are *patrolling*. They are looking for the smaller things.

The smaller things are the *burrowers*, and they are under your feet right now, though you cannot feel them. They are pale, segmented, many-legged creatures the size of your forearm, and they move through the dust and the upper layers of fractured stone in vast, coordinated networks, feeding on the mineral crusts left by the evaporated water and on the dead tissue of the lichen-mat growths. They are the base of the food web — the only things here that eat something other than animals — and there are so many of them that the ground, in places, has a faint, continuous *texture* of movement, a subtle wrongness underfoot, like standing on the skin of something breathing.

The walkers eat the burrowers. They find them by some sense you cannot identify — not smell, not vibration, not sight — and they dig for them with their forelimbs, which are broad and flat and shaped like shovels, and they eat them in quick, efficient motions, without pause, without apparent satisfaction. A walker can consume its own weight in burrowers in a day, and it must, because the cold demands energy, and the energy must come from somewhere.

And then there are the *others*. The things that eat the walkers. You have not seen them yet. You will. They are larger, and they are fewer, and they move even more slowly than the walkers, and they are so well camouflaged against the stone that you could be standing next to one and not know it until it moved, and when it moves, it moves with a speed that is shocking, a sudden, violent acceleration that seems to violate some law of physics, and then it is still again, and there is a walker that is no longer a walker, and the plain continues.

---

The silence is the last thing, and it is the thing that holds everything else in place.

There is no wind, or almost none. There is no birdsong, no insect hum, no rustle of leaves, because there are no leaves. The animals do not vocalize — or if they do, they do it at frequencies below or above the range of your hearing. The only sound is the sound of your own body: your breath, your footsteps, the faint creak of your clothing, the pulse in your ears. And beneath that, so faint that you might be imagining it, there is a sound that is not a sound — a *pressure*, a subsonic vibration that seems to come from the stone itself, as though the plain is resonating at some frequency too low to hear but not too low to feel, a hum that you perceive not with your ears but with your chest, your bones, the fluid in your inner ear.

It is the sound of the machinery.

---

Because there is machinery here. Not built — nothing here is built, in the sense that humans understand building. But there are *structures*, and they are industrial in scale and industrial in function, and they are made of the same stone as the plain, and they have been here so long that they have become indistinguishable from the landscape, and they are *working*.

You see them first as irregularities in the terrain — long, low ridges that run in straight lines across the plain, too regular to be natural, too weathered to be recent. They are conduits. Channels. Pipes, if you want to call them that, though they are not metal and they are not hollow in the way that pipes are hollow. They are channels cut into the stone, or grown into the stone, or *deposited* as the stone formed — it is impossible to tell, and the distinction may not matter — and they carry something. Water, perhaps. Or heat. Or information. Or all three, in a medium that does not map neatly onto any category you know.

The channels converge, periodically, into *nodes* — broad, circular depressions in the plain, fifty or a hundred meters across, where the stone has been shaped into concentric rings, like the growth rings of a tree, or the interference pattern of a wave. The rings are not decorative. They are functional. They are doing something to the water that seeps into them, or to the heat that rises from below, or to the animals that wander through them — and the animals do wander through them, the walkers and the burrowers and the others, and they do not avoid them and they do not seek them, and whatever the nodes do, it is done to the animals as a matter of course, as routinely and as invisibly as gravity.

The nodes are warm. Not hot — warm. Noticeably warmer than the surrounding stone, and the warmth is steady, and it does not vary, and it has not varied for a very long time. The lichen-mat growths cluster around them in dense, luxuriant halos, darker and thicker than the growths around the seeps, and the burrowers are more numerous here, and the walkers patrol the perimeters with a regularity that suggests not hunger but *duty*, or something that functions like duty in a system that has no consciousness but has *purpose*.

---

You begin to understand, slowly, that the plain is not a landscape. It is a *process*. The erosion, the moisture, the cold, the animals, the channels, the nodes — they are not separate things that happen to coexist. They are components of a single system, a machine made of geology and biology and time, that has been running for so long that it has reached a state of equilibrium so perfect it looks like stillness. The animals are not inhabitants of this place. They are *parts* of it. The walkers are transport. The burrowers are extraction. The others are processing. The lichen-mats are filtration. The seeps are input. The nodes are — what? Computation? Regulation? You don't have the framework to know.

And the system is not conscious. It is not alive, in any sense that you would recognize. It is simply *working*, the way a river works, the way a glacier works, the way the carbon cycle works — except that it is more concentrated, more efficient, more *intentional* in its design, though the design has no designer, or had one so long ago that the distinction between designed and evolved has ceased to mean anything.

---

The light does not change. The cold does not change. The plain extends. The animals move through their patterns. The nodes hum at their subsonic frequency. The dust settles and resettles. The moisture seeps and evaporates and seeps again.

You are standing on the Stillworks, and it has been still for longer than you can imagine, and it will be still for longer than you can imagine, and your presence here is a brief, warm, noisy interruption in a silence that is not empty but *full* — full of process, full of function, full of a patience so vast it has become indistinguishable from permanence.

The walker nearest to you has stopped. It is facing you. It has no eyes that you can see, but it is facing you, and it is doing something — processing, perhaps, or registering, or simply *noting* — and then it turns, slowly, and drifts away across the stone, and the plain closes behind it like water, and you are alone again, or as alone as you ever are here, which is not very alone at all.
