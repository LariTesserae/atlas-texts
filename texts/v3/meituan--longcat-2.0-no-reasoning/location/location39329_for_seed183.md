# The Weeping Spires of Keth-Avani

---

You are standing on a narrow shelf of rock that should not exist.

The air is bright — almost aggressively so — though there is no sun. The light comes from everywhere and nowhere, a diffuse radiance that seems to emanate from the stone itself, or from the water, or from the thin atmosphere that presses against your skin like a damp cloth. It is the brightness of a world that has forgotten how to be dark.

Below you, above you, around you: spires. Hundreds of them. Thousands. They rise from depths you cannot see and ascend into heights that blur and shimmer, as though the upper reaches are only partially committed to being real. The spires are tall and impossibly slender — some no thicker than your arm at the base, others wide as a house — and they are made of a pale, chalky mineral that weeps. Constantly. Water beads on every surface, runs in thin sheets down every face, drips from every edge and protrusion, and the sound of it is enormous. Not the gentle patter of rain but a dense, layered, continuous roar — like standing inside a waterfall that has been shattered into ten million individual streams and reassembled into architecture. The sound fills your chest. It is the loudest quiet place you have ever been.

The water is not rain. It is not groundwater in any sense you recognize. It *condenses* out of the air onto the stone, as though the spires are perpetually colder than the atmosphere around them, and the atmosphere is perpetually saturated. The air itself is thick with moisture — you can feel it settling on your lips, your eyelids, the backs of your hands. Every few seconds a droplet forms on your own skin, as though you are becoming part of the weeping. The water runs down the spires, collects in channels and grooves that have been carved over what must be an immense span of time, and falls. It falls between the spires in silver threads and broken curtains, and where it lands — on lower shelves, on the surfaces of other spires, on the narrow bridges and platforms that connect them — it immediately begins to weep again. The same water, cycling. The same water, perhaps, that has been cycling here since before anything thought to count the time.

The elevation is extreme. You understand this not from any single vantage but from the way your body responds — the slight thinness of the air, the way sound carries differently, the vertiginous sense that the ground is a concept that applies to other places. The spire-field extends upward and downward farther than you can see. You are somewhere in its middle reaches, on a shelf perhaps twenty meters wide, and the nearest neighboring spire is close enough to touch. Its surface is wet and slightly warm — not from any heat source but from the friction of water moving continuously across it, or perhaps from some slow mineral reaction you don't understand. The stone is not quite stone. It has a faint translucency, like alabaster, and in certain angles of light you can see shapes inside it — not fossils, not inclusions, but *patterns*. Geometries that repeat at different scales. Hexagons nested within hexagons. Spirals that tighten toward a center you cannot reach.

There is no soil. No moss. No lichen. No algae on the wet surfaces, no biofilm, no slime. The mineral world here is absolute — and yet it is not dead. The spires *grow*. You can see it if you look carefully at the tips of the nearest ones: a faint accretion, a mineral deposition so slow that it would be imperceptible over a human lifetime but that has, over whatever unimaginable timescale governs this place, built structures that rival mountains in height. The water carries dissolved minerals — you can feel the faint grittiness of it on your fingers — and deposits them, and the spires extend themselves by fractions. They are not alive. They are something else. They are *process* made visible.

And here is where the world begins to come apart.

The geometries inside the stone are not static. They *shift*. Not quickly — you won't catch it in a single glance — but if you mark a pattern and look away and look back, it has changed. The hexagons have become pentagons. The spiral has reversed its direction. And the spire itself — you are almost certain — is in a slightly different position than it was a moment ago. Not fallen. Not eroded. *Relocated*. As though the entire field of spires is a slow, vast rearrangement, a puzzle solving itself over millennia, and you are standing on a piece that has just slid one increment to the left.

The bridges between the spires confirm this. They are made of the same pale mineral, and they are old — deeply weathered, water-worn, some of them broken and hanging like severed limbs from their anchor points — but they do not connect where they used to. A bridge that clearly once linked your shelf to a spire thirty meters away now ends in empty air, its far terminus pointing at nothing. The spire it once connected to is gone, or has moved, or has *become* a different spire. And the broken stubs of the bridge are already being mineral-sealed, smoothed over, incorporated into the shelf as though the bridge is being *un-happened*.

There are structures. Small ones. You notice the first only because your foot nearly catches on it — a low wall, barely knee-height, made of stacked and fused mineral blocks. It is old. It follows the curve of the shelf's edge, and it might once have been a windbreak, or a retaining wall, or a boundary marker. But there is no wind to break, nothing to retain, and no one to mark a boundary for. The wall is incomplete. Sections of it have been absorbed into the shelf, the mineral fusion making it impossible to tell where construction ends and natural accretion begins. Other structures appear as you look more carefully: a ring of stones, a shallow basin carved into the rock, a series of notches cut into a spire's surface at roughly human height. The notches are evenly spaced. They look like they were made for hands.

Someone was here. Something was here. The tools were simple — you can see the scrape marks, the pecked surfaces, the crude but deliberate geometry of hand-worked stone. No metal. No fire-hardened points. Just stone on stone, patience, and a desire to leave a mark. The marks are fading. The world is reclaiming them, mineral by mineral, and in another span of whatever-time this place keeps, they will be gone entirely.

The sound shifts. Not in volume — it is always loud — but in *character*. There is a deep, subsonic pulse that you feel more than hear, a rhythm that seems to come from the spires themselves, or from the water moving through them, or from the slow rearrangement of the entire field. It is not regular. It is not mechanical. It is more like breathing — if the thing doing the breathing were the size of a mountain range and had a lung capacity measured in centuries. Each pulse changes the quality of the weeping. For a few seconds the water runs faster, the droplets are larger, the roar deepens. Then it eases. Then it builds again.

And in those moments of easing, you hear something else. A high, thin tone, almost electronic, that seems to come from the tips of the spires far above you. It might be wind — but there is no wind. It might be the mineral accretion itself, some piezoelectric effect of crystal growth under immense pressure. It might be the sound of geometry changing. It is the most unsettling sound you have ever heard, not because it is loud or discordant but because it is *structured*. It has intervals. It has phrases. It is not music, but it is not *not* music.

The ecology of this place is: water, mineral, light, and process. Nothing eats. Nothing decays. Nothing reproduces in any way you would recognize. And yet the spires compete — you can see it in their spacing, in the way smaller spires cluster around larger ones, in the way some spires have *absorbed* others, their bases swollen with the incorporated mass of a neighbor that grew too close. The water is shared and contested. The light — whatever it is — falls unevenly, and the spires that catch more of it grow faster, accrete more aggressively, cast longer shadows that slow their competitors. It is an ecology without life. A economy without minds. And it has been running for so long that it has developed something that looks, from certain angles, like intention.

You should leave. You know this the way you know the elevation is extreme — not from any single observation but from the accumulated weight of a thousand small wrongnesses. The water on your skin is not quite water. The light is not quite light. The stone beneath your feet is warm in a way that has nothing to do with temperature. And the spires are rearranging themselves around you, slowly, patiently, the way a hand closes around something it intends to hold.

The shelf you are standing on is not as wide as it was when you arrived.

The nearest bridge has moved another increment to the left.

The geometry inside the stone has become something you don't have a name for.

And the weeping continues, and continues, and continues, and the sound of it is the sound of a world that is not quite a world, in a place that is not quite a place, at a time that is not quite now.
