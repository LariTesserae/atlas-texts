# The Drownplain

There is no horizon here. Or rather, there are too many — they stack and dissolve and re-form like wet paper held up to a lamp. You stand on ground that is not quite ground: a mat of interlocked root systems, pale and fibrous, spongy underfoot, saturated to the point of weeping. Every step releases a small gasp of water that has no place to go because everything is already holding as much as it can. The root-mat is perhaps a foot thick, maybe two, and beneath it is not soil but a slow, dark percolation — water moving through compressed organic matter in no particular direction, a subterranean circulation without rivers, without channels, just a generalized seething dampness that makes the whole landscape feel like the skin of something alive and breathing.

The vegetation does not grow *from* the land so much as it *is* the land. There are no clear boundaries between one plant and the next. Vines as thick as your forearm braid together into columns that rise fifteen, twenty feet before splaying into canopies so interwoven that the light — what little there is — comes through in a diffuse, green-black murk. Epiphytes colonize everything: mosses in cascading curtains, bromeliads the size of buckets cupping stagnant water, orchids with petals so dark they appear to absorb light rather than reflect it. Ferns unscroll in fractal profusion at every level, from ground to canopy, their fiddleheads still coiled and dripping. The air tastes of chlorophyll and rot in equal measure, a thick sweetness that coats the back of your throat.

And it is dark. Not night-dark — there is no night here, no day, no cycle you can track. The darkness is structural, architectural. The canopy is so dense, so layered, so *committed* to its own expansion that light simply cannot penetrate in any meaningful quantity. What reaches the understory is a dim, directionless luminescence, the color of old jade, of pond water seen from below. Bioluminescence supplements it in patches: fungal networks threading through the root-mat glow a faint, sickly blue-white. Certain flowers pulse with a dim amber. The eyes of things — and there are many, many eyes — catch what little light exists and throw it back in brief, unsettling flashes of reflection.

The sound is the first thing that breaks you. It is not loud in the way a storm is loud, or a waterfall, or a city. It is loud in the way that *everything is happening at once and none of it is stopping*. Insects — and there are insects here in numbers that defy the word "swarm" — produce a continuous, layered drone that shifts in pitch and density like a living thing. Frogs, or things that function as frogs, call in overlapping cycles: some in rapid staccato bursts, some in long, descending moans that seem to come from the ground itself. Things move through the canopy above you with a constant rustling, cracking, and regrowth — branches snapping under weight, new growth already filling the gap before the broken piece hits the water below. The root-mat creaks and groans as it adjusts to your weight, and beneath it, the percolating water produces a sound like slow, thick breathing. Somewhere nearby, something large is moving through the undergrowth with a deliberateness that makes every other sound seem to pause, just for a moment, before resuming at greater intensity.

The fauna is not merely present — it is *the point*. This landscape exists because of them, for them, in response to them. Insects are the foundation: beetles with carapaces like polished obsidian, each the size of your hand, moving in columns along the root-mat, processing detritus with mechanical efficiency. Moths with wingspans wider than your arms drift through the understory in slow, spiraling flight, their wing-eyes glowing faintly in the dark. Spiders have built webs between the vine-columns at every height — some as small as coins, some stretching ten feet across, their silk beaded with condensation that catches the fungal light like strings of tiny pearls. The webs are so numerous that moving through the landscape requires constant negotiation with them, a slow, careful picking-through that the spiders tolerate with what feels like indifference.

Larger things inhabit the mid-canopy. Lizards, or lizard-analogues, with skin that shifts color not for camouflage but for communication — rapid pulses of iridescent pattern that ripple across their bodies in sequences too fast to fully parse. They move along the vine-columns in groups of six or eight, their toe-pads making a soft, rhythmic clicking on the woody surface. Amphibians cluster in the bromeliad pools: some frog-like, some salamander-like, some neither — soft-bodied, translucent things whose internal organs are visible as dark shapes beneath skin like wet parchment. They breed constantly. The pools are thick with eggs, with tadpoles, with the half-formed.

And there are predators. You can feel them before you see them — a particular quality of attention, a focused stillness in a landscape that is otherwise all motion. Something moves through the canopy above you with a weight that makes the whole structure sway. Something else watches from a hollow in a vine-column, its eyes reflecting the fungal glow in two steady points of green-gold. The predators here are not ambush hunters in the traditional sense; the landscape is too dense, too three-dimensional, too *active* for stillness to be an effective strategy. Instead they move through the chaos, reading it, finding the paths through the noise. You can hear them sometimes — a low, resonant call that cuts through the insect drone like a blade, answered by another call from somewhere distant, and another, a network of voices mapping territory in real time.

The water is everywhere and nowhere. It does not pool in the way you expect — the root-mat is too absorbent, too alive. Instead it exists as a generalized saturation, a condition of the air and the ground and the plants. Condensation forms on every surface and drips constantly, a rain that falls from the canopy rather than from the sky. Occasionally, a bromeliad overflows and sends a cascade down through the understory, a brief, localized waterfall that the insects scatter from and then return to. The root-mat is threaded with small channels where water moves more freely — not streams, exactly, but *preferences*, paths of least resistance that shift as the mat grows and compresses. If you press your ear to the mat, you can hear the water moving beneath, a complex, braided sound like many voices whispering.

The erosion parameter — young, fresh, sharp — manifests here not in geological features but in the *newness of the growth itself*. This landscape is not old-growth. It is not settled. The vine-columns are still rising, still competing, still finding their positions. New shoots erupt from the root-mat daily, pale and urgent, already reaching for the canopy. The canopy itself is in constant negotiation — plants overgrow each other, collapse under their own weight, are replaced. The whole system has the feeling of a city being built at impossible speed, scaffolding everywhere, nothing finished, everything in process. There are no ancient trees here, no deep-rooted permanence. The oldest structures are perhaps years old, not decades. The landscape is young in the way that a riot is young — fresh, volatile, still deciding what it is.

The weirdness is subtle but persistent. It lives in the details. The way the bioluminescent fungi seem to brighten slightly when you look directly at them, as if responding to attention. The way the amphibian calls sometimes synchronize across large distances, hundreds of voices falling into unison for a moment before dissolving back into chaos. The way the root-mat occasionally *pulses* — a slow, peristaltic wave that moves through it from somewhere you can't identify, as if something very large is moving beneath, or as if the mat itself is a single organism performing a function you don't understand. The way the condensation on certain leaves forms patterns that are almost, but not quite, geometric. Nothing here is overtly impossible. Everything is just slightly *more* than it should be — more responsive, more coordinated, more aware.

The turbulence is the governing principle. This is not a landscape at equilibrium. It is a landscape in a state of perpetual, frenzied becoming. Growth and decay happen simultaneously and at the same locations — a vine-column flowers and rots and is colonized by fungi and is fed upon by beetles and is replaced by a new vine all within the space of days. The canopy opens and closes. The water rises and falls. Species appear and disappear from your field of vision not because they are migrating but because the landscape itself is churning, rearranging, trying new configurations at a pace that makes observation feel like trying to read a book in a windstorm. There is an energy here that feels almost frantic, as if the whole system is racing toward something — or away from something — and has not yet decided which.

You move through it slowly. There is no other way. The density of life — the physical density of organisms per cubic foot — means that every motion is a negotiation. You push through a curtain of moss and it releases a cloud of tiny springtails that catch the fungal light like sparks. You step over a root and disturb a column of beetles that reassemble behind you with a sound like static. A moth drifts across your field of vision, close enough that you feel the air from its wings, and its wing-eyes leave afterimages in the dark. Something calls from the canopy — a long, descending note that seems to pull at something behind your sternum — and is answered by a chorus of smaller voices from the root-mat below.

There is no path. There is no destination. There is only the landscape, in all its wet, dark, deafening, teeming immediacy, and you are inside it, and it does not care about you, and it is the most alive place you have ever been.
