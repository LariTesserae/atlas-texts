**The Weeping Gallery**

You descend a narrow stair cut into living rock, and the air changes immediately — warmer, heavier, tasting of iron and wet clay. The walls glisten. Not with standing water, but with a perpetual film of condensation that beads and runs in slow, irregular rivulets down every surface. The stone drinks it and sweats it back. Somewhere above, rain is falling intermittently — you can hear it as a distant, arrhythmic drumming on the surface, and every few minutes a new pulse of moisture finds its way down through cracks and fissures, darkening the walls in fresh streaks before the warmth reclaims it.

The Gallery itself is a single long chamber, perhaps forty paces from end to end, and no wider than a large room. The ceiling is low — you can touch it if you raise your arm — and it is not natural. It is constructed: a dense lattice of wooden beams, old and black with moisture, interwoven with woven-reed matting and sealed with some kind of pitch that has gone tacky and dark over what must be a very long time. The beams are packed so tightly together that the ceiling reads as a single surface, a dark woven sky. Between the beams, in the gaps, things grow.

That is the first thing that strikes you: the density. Every surface is occupied. The walls are lined with shelves — hundreds of them, carved directly into the rock or built from stacked flat stones mortared with clay — and on every shelf there is something living or something made, and often both at once. Small ceramic pots, no larger than a cup, each holding a single plant: succulents with thick pale leaves, threadlike ferns, mosses in improbable greens. The pots are hand-thrown, crude, beautiful. Many have cracked and been repaired with cord and resin. Some have cracked and not been repaired, and the plant has grown through the crack, root and ceramic fused into a single object.

The floor is a constructed thing too — flat stones set in a bed of packed earth, with channels cut between them to carry water. And water does move here, constantly. Thin streams run in the channels, fed by the weeping walls, and they converge at the center of the gallery into a shallow basin — perhaps two feet across — that overflows slowly at one end and feeds a channel that disappears into darkness. The sound of water is everywhere: dripping, trickling, the occasional gurgle when a channel clears a blockage. It is loud. Not deafening, but insistent, a constant aqueous chatter that fills every silence before silence can form.

And there are animals. This is the second thing that strikes you, and it strikes you all at once, because they are *everywhere*. The gallery is dense with small life. Lizards — dozens of them, perhaps hundreds — cling to the walls and ceiling in a kind of living tessellation. They are small, no longer than your finger, in shades of grey and green and rust, and they are so still that at first you mistake them for part of the decoration. But they move. They shift position with a slow, deliberate patience, and when one moves, others adjust around it, and the whole pattern on the wall reorganizes like a slow kaleidoscope. They are hunting the insects that swarm in clouds near the ceiling — tiny midges and gnats that rise from the moss beds in visible columns when the air shifts. The lizards eat them. The insects feed on the moss and on something in the water. The water feeds the moss. The moss holds the walls together.

There are other animals too. In the water channels, small pale things — some kind of crayfish or shrimp, translucent, barely an inch long — move in dense clusters, their bodies packed so tightly in places that the water appears to be a single crawling thing. They filter something from the flow. On the shelves, in the pots, tiny snails with spiral shells the color of old bone move across the leaves of the plants with a glacial patience. And in the corners, in the dark spaces behind the stone shelves, there are nests — woven from plant fiber and hair and shed lizard skin — belonging to something small and mammalian that you never quite see, only hear: a quick chittering, a rustle, a small sharp sound like a cough.

The light comes from below. There are no windows, no openings to the surface. Instead, the light is provided by hundreds of small clay lamps — simple dishes filled with some kind of oil, with wicks made of twisted plant fiber — set into niches in the walls and on every shelf. They burn with a steady, warm, slightly smoky flame, and because there are so many of them, the gallery is well-lit, almost bright, but the light is *layered*: it comes from every direction at once, and it throws a dense web of shadows that overlap and cancel each other out until the shadows themselves become a kind of texture, a darkness that is not absence but presence. The flames flicker when the air moves — and the air does move, in slow currents driven by the temperature difference between the warm lower gallery and the cooler rock above — and when they flicker, the lizards on the walls shift, and the shadows reorganize, and the whole room seems to breathe.

The warmth is notable. It is not hot, but it is warm — warmer than the surface, warmer than it should be — and it has a quality of *enclosure*, of heat held in by the packed earth and stone and woven ceiling. The source is unclear. There may be a thermal vent somewhere in the rock below. Or it may simply be the lamps, hundreds of them, burning continuously, their combined heat trapped in this small dense space. The warmth is what keeps the water cycling, what keeps the moss growing, what keeps the insects breeding, what keeps the lizards active. It is the engine of the place.

And the place is old. This is the third thing, and it is the thing that underlies everything else. The gallery has been here a very long time. The beams in the ceiling are not just old but *replaced* — you can see where newer wood has been woven into the lattice alongside wood that has gone to charcoal-dark, and some of the newer wood is itself old, and some of the old wood has been replaced twice. The shelves have been rebuilt. The channels have been recut. The pots have been refired, reglued, re-rooted. The basin at the center has been worn into a smooth bowl by the water, and then lined with clay, and then worn again, and lined again, and the layers of clay are visible in cross-section like geological strata. The lizards have been here so long that they are *part of the architecture* — their shed skins are incorporated into the nest-material, their droppings have mineralized into a pale crust on certain stones, their bodies fill every vertical surface so completely that the walls seem to be made of them.

Someone maintains this place. You find evidence of maintenance everywhere: a stack of new clay lamps, unfired, drying on a shelf. A coil of fresh wick-fiber. A clay jar of oil with a fitted stone lid. A small toolkit — a bone needle, a flat stone for smoothing clay, a cord of twisted fiber, a sharpened piece of shell for cutting plant material — laid out neatly on a flat rock as if someone set it down moments ago and will return. But there is no one here. The gallery maintains itself, or is maintained by the small mammal you cannot see, or is maintained by the slow patient work of the lizards and the water and the moss, which are not alive in the way you understand aliveness but which *act* — which build and repair and adjust and persist.

The air smells of warm clay and green growth and animal musk and lamp oil and wet stone. It is not unpleasant. It is the smell of a place that has been *inhabited* for so long that the distinction between the inhabitants and the place itself has dissolved. The lizards are the walls. The moss is the ceiling. The water is the floor. The lamps are the light. The gallery is not a room that contains life. It is a life that has taken the shape of a room.

At the far end, the channel disappears into a low opening in the rock — too low to stand in, barely high enough to crawl. The water flows into it. The darkness beyond is absolute. From inside, you hear a new sound: a deeper resonance, a hollow echo, as if the gallery continues below in some larger space, or as if the water is falling, somewhere far down, into a pool that has no bottom.

The lizards on the wall nearest the opening turn their heads toward it, all at once, in unison, and are still.
