# The Drowned Workshop

---

You descend.

Not through air — through water. Always water. There is no surface above you, or if there is, it is so far up that the concept of "up" has become theological. The water is warm — not hot, not cool, but the precise temperature of a hand that has been holding a tool for hours. It presses against you from all sides with the intimacy of something that has been waiting.

You are inside a structure. You have been inside it for some time now, though you only just realized.

The walls are stone — or something that has become stone. They are carved, but not recently. The chisel marks have been softened by millennia of warm current into something almost organic, like the ridges inside a shell. The passages are narrow. You must turn sideways to move through some of them. The ceilings are low. Everything is scaled to a body not quite your own — slightly smaller, slightly more compact, as though the builders were dense people who did not waste space.

There are many of them here still.

---

They do not move quickly. They have never moved quickly. They are small — the size of your forearm, perhaps, or a closed fist — and they are everywhere. They cling to the walls, to the ceilings, to each other. Their bodies are smooth and pale, almost translucent in the faint bioluminescence that seeps from patches of mineral deposit on the stone. They have too many limbs. Not an alarming number — six, perhaps, or eight — but enough that their movement through the passages looks like a slow, continuous unfurling. They do not swim. They *climb*, always, along surfaces, using every wall and ceiling with equal indifference to gravity. The water is so dense with them that passing through a corridor means moving through a living curtain, and they part around you with the slow patience of something that has never known urgency.

They are the builders. They are still building.

---

The tools are simple. Bone, mostly — their own shed bones, or the bones of something else that lived here before. Chips of volcanic glass, knapped to edges that would not impress a human flint-knapper but that, over sufficient time, have carved these hundreds of meters of passage through stone that was already ancient when they found it. You can see the work in progress: a chamber being widened, the floor lowered by perhaps a millimeter per century. A cluster of them works a single wall, each one making a single scrape, then passing the tool to the next, then moving aside. The rhythm is so slow that watching it feels like watching geology.

They are not carving anything decorative. They are not carving anything *representational*. They are simply *making more room*. More passage. More surface. The structure grows outward and inward simultaneously — new tunnels branching from old ones, old tunnels being widened, ceilings being lowered and floors being raised until the passages become slots, and then the slots become something else, something with a purpose you cannot yet read.

---

The light is almost nothing. The bioluminescent patches are sparse and dim — a faint blue-green glow that does not illuminate so much as *suggest*. You see by the memory of shapes. The walls are there because your hands say they are there. The creatures are there because you feel the displacement of water as they move, a slow pulse against your skin like a heartbeat in another room. The darkness is not threatening. It is simply the default state of a place that has been underwater longer than light has been a concept.

---

The silence is extraordinary.

Water should not be silent. Water should lap and rush and transmit the clicks and groans of a living world. But here the sound is *absent* — not muffled, not quiet, but genuinely, structurally absent, as though the warm water itself has been taught to carry no vibration. The creatures make no sound. Their tools make no sound against the stone. When you touch the wall, you feel the texture but hear nothing. It is as if the place exists in a medium that has forgotten how to transmit waves. The only sound is your own body — the creak of your joints, the rush of your blood, the small click of your tongue against your teeth — and these sounds feel obscene in their loudness, like shouting in a cathedral.

---

The structure is old. Not old like a ruin — old like a mountain that has been worn to a hill. The original shape of whatever this was has been almost entirely consumed by the slow, patient work of its inhabitants. There are no sharp edges left anywhere. Every surface has been touched and retouched so many times that the stone has taken on a waxy, almost soapy quality, smooth beyond what water alone could achieve. In places, the walls have been worked so thin that you can see the faint glow of bioluminescence on the other side, and you realize that the passages are layered — that there are corridors behind corridors behind corridors, each one populated, each one being slowly reshaped.

The density of the place is not just the creatures. It is the *work*. Every surface bears the marks of intent. There are no natural walls left. No raw stone. Everything has been touched, scraped, polished, shaped. The mineral deposits that provide the faint light have been *cultivated* — you can see where they have been scraped back when they grew too thick, where they have been encouraged to spread across a ceiling in a pattern that might be functional or might be the closest thing these creatures have to art. The distinction, here, may not exist.

---

You find a chamber that is larger than the others. Not large — nothing here is large — but large relative to the passages, perhaps the size of a modest room. The ceiling is domed. The floor is flat and smooth. And in the center, there is a column.

The column is the only thing in the structure that appears to have been made in a single effort rather than accumulated over ages. It is carved from a single piece of stone — or grown, or deposited, or *become* — and it rises from the floor to the ceiling with a straightness that feels deliberate in a place where nothing else is straight. Its surface is covered in fine, dense markings. Not writing. Not decoration. *Records*. Each mark is a single scrape, made by a single tool, held by a single creature, at a single moment. There are millions of them. They cover every surface of the column in layers so dense that the original stone is invisible. The oldest marks have been almost entirely obscured by newer ones, and the newest marks are being added now — you can see three of the creatures clinging to the column, each working a small section with the focused patience of scribes.

What are they recording? You cannot tell. The marks do not vary in any way you can detect. Each one is identical to the last. Perhaps they are recording *time itself*. Perhaps each mark is simply: *we were here. we are still here. the work continues.*

---

The warmth of the water is not from any volcanic source. There is no vent, no fissure, no geological heat. The warmth comes from the creatures themselves — from the sheer density of their bodies, the accumulated metabolic heat of millions of small lives pressed together in stone passages, generation after generation, for longer than the stone above them has been stone. They have made the water warm by *being in it*. The warmth is a byproduct of persistence.

---

You realize, slowly, that you are not supposed to be here. Not because the place is hostile — it is not. The creatures acknowledge you the way they acknowledge a current: they move around you and continue their work. But the passages are too small for you. The air — if there is air, if you are breathing, if you have not been breathing water this whole time and simply failed to notice — is too warm, too thick, too *occupied*. You are a large, loud, fast thing in a place that has optimized itself, over geological time, for small, quiet, slow things.

You turn to leave. The creatures part around you. Their tools scrape the walls. The sound does not carry.

Behind you, the column grows another mark.
