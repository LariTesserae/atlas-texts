# The Grotto of Still Warmth

---

You descend three steps carved into stone so old the edges have gone soft, and the air changes.

It is warm here — not the warmth of sun, which never reaches this far, but the warmth of something held. The stone itself is warm to the touch, as though it has been sitting in a bath for a very long time and has not yet thought to cool. The temperature does not vary. You could press your palm against the wall for an hour and find it the same degree of warm when you pulled away as when you arrived. This is not a place that changes its mind.

The ceiling is low. You can reach up and brush it with your fingertips if you stand on the balls of your feet, and you would find it slick — not with slime, not with anything biological, but with a continuous, patient condensation. Water beads on every overhead surface and does not fall. The droplets are fat and still, clinging to the rock like they have made a decision. Occasionally one releases, but not in any rhythm you can predict. It simply lets go, and the sound it makes when it strikes the floor is the loudest thing in the room: a single, clean *tick* against stone, and then silence again, and then nothing for a long time.

The floor is a shallow basin, perhaps twenty paces across at its widest, and it holds water — not a pool exactly, but a film. A sheet of water so thin you might mistake it for a polish on the stone if you saw it from the right angle. It covers everything. Your feet are in it immediately, ankle-deep in some places, barely wetting your soles in others, and it is warm too, the same temperature as the walls, as though the entire chamber is a single held breath. The water does not flow. It has no current, no inlet you can find, no outlet. It simply *is*, at this depth, in this place, and has been.

The walls are the color of old bone where the water does not reach, and the color of dark tea where it does. They are limestone, or something like limestone — calcium-rich, dissolved and redissolved over spans of time that make the concept of years irrelevant. The surface is not smooth. It is *worked*. Long fingers of mineral deposit have built up in ridges and whorls, like the inside of a nautilus shell scaled down to the size of a room, or like the ripples you see in sandstone at low tide, except these are permanent, three-dimensional, and they cover every surface in dense, overlapping patterns. They are packed so tightly that there is no flat place to set your hand. Every square inch of wall is occupied by these formations — flowstone, rimstone, shelfstone, names you may or may not know — and they have grown into each other over so long a time that they have become a single continuous texture, a mineral tapestry with no gaps.

In places the formations have built up into shapes that are almost architectural. A low wall of rimstone, a few inches high, curves across the floor like the foundation of a house that was never finished. Near the back of the chamber, a series of mineral shelves step upward in a rough approximation of a staircase, each one holding its own thin sheet of warm water, each one level, each one still. They look deliberate. They are not. They are the result of water doing the same thing in the same place for longer than you can hold in your mind.

There is a single object in the chamber that is not stone or water.

It sits on one of the lower shelves, near the center of the room, and it is a bowl. It is made of clay — fired clay, reddish-brown, thick-walled, roughly shaped. The hands that made it were not skilled, or not patient, or both. The rim is uneven. One side is slightly higher than the other. It holds water, of course — everything here holds water — and the water inside it is the same temperature as the water outside it, and the same depth, as though the bowl is not a container but simply another surface the water has chosen to rest on.

There is no mark on the bowl that tells you who made it or when. There is no other artifact. No tool, no mark on the wall, no arrangement of stones. Just the bowl, sitting on the shelf, holding its water, warm and still.

The light is dim. It comes from behind you — from the passage you descended through, from whatever gray or overcast world exists above — and it reaches this far and no farther. The chamber is lit the way a room is lit when the curtains are drawn: not dark, but *reduced*. Shapes are visible. Colors are not, or barely. The mineral walls catch what light there is and hold it, faintly, the way wet stone does, so the room seems to glow from within at a level just above darkness. The water on the floor reflects nothing. It is too still, too thin, too much the same color as the stone beneath it. It is invisible until you step in it.

The sound, when there is sound, is water. Not flowing water — there is no flow. The sound is the sound of water *being*. The faint, almost subliminal hum of moisture against stone. The occasional release of a droplet from the ceiling. Once, perhaps, you hear something that might be a bubble rising through the thin sheet on the floor — a tiny, wet, almost musical note, like a finger pulled gently from the rim of a glass — but you are not sure you heard it at all. Mostly there is silence. The kind of silence that has weight. The kind that presses gently against your eardrums and makes you aware of your own breathing, which seems, by comparison, loud and careless.

There is almost nothing alive here.

You see, near the passage entrance, a single pale creature — something between a spider and a shrimp, translucent, no larger than your thumbnail — moving slowly across the wet stone. It is the color of the stone. You almost do not see it. It moves with the unhurried patience of something that has never known urgency, and when it reaches the water it does not stop or turn. It walks onto the surface. The surface holds it. It crosses the thin sheet of water as though the water were a solid, its legs dimpling the surface tension but not breaking it, and it disappears into a crevice in the far wall, and you do not see it again.

That is all. One creature. Perhaps there are others in the cracks and passages beyond, in the dark wet places you cannot see. Perhaps not. The chamber does not feel empty, exactly — it feels *complete*, as though everything that belongs here is already present, and nothing else is needed.

The air is thick. Not humid in the way of a greenhouse or a summer afternoon, but *dense* — saturated, fully loaded, holding all the water it can hold and a little more, so that the boundary between air and water is not a line but a gradient, a slow dissolve. You breathe it and it is warm and it tastes of stone. Not unpleasant. Mineral. Clean in the way that things are clean when nothing organic has ever touched them.

You stand in the warm shallow water in the dim room and you feel, without being able to name why, that you are inside something. Not a building — there is no building. Not a body — there is no body. But something that *contains*. Something that has walls and a floor and a ceiling and a temperature and a quality of light and a depth of water and a silence, and all of these things are in a state of equilibrium so perfect and so long-maintained that they have become a single condition, a single state of being, and you have stepped into it the way you might step into a held note, or a pause between words, or the space inside a bowl.

The bowl is still on its shelf. The water is still in the bowl. The water is still on the floor. The walls are still warm. The droplet on the ceiling has not yet decided to fall.

Nothing here is waiting for anything.

Nothing here is going to change.
