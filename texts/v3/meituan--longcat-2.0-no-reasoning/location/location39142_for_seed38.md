# The Weepstone Plateau

---

You are standing on a continent that has forgotten how to be young.

The ground beneath you is not rock, not soil, but something between — a compressed mat of root and rhizome so ancient it has achieved the density of mineral. It gives slightly underfoot, like walking on the hide of something enormous and patient. The plateau stretches in every direction to a horizon that curves wrong, that seems to rise rather than fall, as though the world has been gently folded and this is the crease. The sky is the color of wet slate, low and close, and it has been this way for longer than any living thing here has a word for.

Water is everywhere and nowhere. It does not fall from above — not now, not in any memory — but it rises. The plateau breathes it up from some deep aquifer that has no bottom anyone has found, and it condenses on every surface in a perpetual film that never quite becomes rain. The air is thick with it. You can feel it settling on your skin, your eyelashes, the fine hairs of your arms, beading and sliding in slow rivulets that trace the contours of your body as though reading you. The plants here have learned to drink from the air itself, and their surfaces are slick, almost greasy with moisture, and they glisten in the dim light like the interior of a body.

The light comes from no single source. It is ambient, diffuse, the color of light filtered through deep water or through the closed eyelids of a dreamer. It does not cast shadows so much as it reveals gradients — some things are slightly more present than others. The plateau exists in a permanent soft-focus, and distance is measured not in meters but in degrees of visibility. Things near you are vivid. Things far away are suggestions. The farthest things are not visible at all but are *inferred* — you know the plateau continues because the ground continues, because the air continues to thicken, because the sound of water continues to come from directions you cannot see.

And the plants. The plants are the world here.

They are not growing. That is the first thing you must understand. Growth implies direction, implies a before and an after, implies ambition. These plants have passed through growth and come out the other side into something else — a state of *being* so total that the distinction between organism and environment has dissolved. They are the substrate. They are the architecture. They are the weather system, the water cycle, the geological process. A single organism — or what was once a single organism, or what was once many organisms that have since become one — covers the entire visible surface of the plateau and extends, you suspect, much further. Its body is layered in strata of living and dead and living-again tissue, and the boundary between these states is not a line but a gradient, a slow negotiation between what is metabolizing and what is becoming soil and what is becoming structure.

The uppermost layer is a canopy of broad, flat leaves the color of dark jade, each one the size of a dinner table, each one cupped slightly to catch the ambient moisture. They overlap like scales, like shingles, like the plates of an armadillo, and beneath them the light is dimmer still, and the air is warmer, and the sound of water is louder — a constant soft percussion of droplets forming and falling and forming again, a rhythm so steady it becomes a kind of silence. The leaves are not attached to branches in any way you recognize. They emerge directly from the surface of the organism below, which is a dense, fibrous mass of interwoven stems and roots and something that might be bark or might be muscle, pale and striated and faintly warm to the touch.

Beneath the canopy layer, the organism thickens. Here it becomes structural — great buttressed walls of compressed tissue that rise like the ribs of a cathedral, creating chambers and corridors and vaulted spaces that extend for distances you cannot gauge. The walls are living. They pulse, very slowly, with a rhythm that is not quite heartbeat and not quite breath but something older than either. If you press your ear to them, you can hear the water moving inside — not flowing, exactly, but *being moved*, drawn up through capillary action across distances that would be impossible for any single plant, except that this is not a single plant, this is a system, a network, a single metabolic process distributed across a landscape.

The water emerges at the highest points of the structure — at the tips of the great buttresses, at the edges of the canopy leaves, at the surfaces of hanging curtains of moss-like growth that drape from the ceilings of the chambers like veils. It emerges and it beads and it runs and it drips, and the sound of it is the dominant sound of the plateau, though "dominant" is the wrong word because it is not competing with anything. It is simply *there*, the way silence is there, the way the hum of blood in your own ears is there. A soft, constant, polyphonic dripping, each droplet finding its own rhythm, its own pitch, its own path down through the layered structure to the water table below, where it will be drawn up again. The cycle is closed. The cycle has been closed for a very long time.

And in this wet, warm, dim, vast, ancient place, there is life — abundant life, but life that has adapted to the strangeness of the world it inhabits.

The fauna of the Weepstone Plateau does not move the way animals move in younger worlds. There are no predators here, or if there are, predation has been so slowed by the warmth and the abundance and the sheer *patience* of this ecosystem that it resembles something else — symbiosis, perhaps, or a very slow form of gardening. The creatures are small and numerous and they live in the interstices of the great organism, in the spaces between root and stem, in the hollows of the buttressed walls, in the dripping chambers beneath the canopy. They are pale, most of them, or translucent, their bodies adapted to the dim light and the constant moisture. They move slowly. They have no need for speed. There is nowhere to go that is not already here, nothing to escape that is not already part of the system.

There are things like slugs the length of your forearm, their bodies iridescent with the oils of the plants they graze, leaving trails of bioluminescence that fade over hours. There are things like spiders, but their webs are not for catching prey — they are for catching water, vast intricate nets strung between the buttresses that collect the condensing moisture and channel it downward in silver threads, and the spiders themselves are tiny and live at the nodes of the web, drinking from their own architecture. There are things that might be insects or might be something else entirely — small, many-legged, chitinous creatures that move through the root-mass like fish through water, their bodies shaped to the tunnels they inhabit, so perfectly fitted to their channels that it is impossible to tell where the creature ends and the tunnel begins.

And there are the singers.

You hear them before you see them — a sound that is not quite a song and not quite a hum and not quite the sound of water, though it shares qualities with all three. It comes from somewhere in the root-mass, from deep within the structure, and it vibrates through the living walls of the chambers so that the sound seems to come from everywhere at once. The singers are buried in the tissue of the great organism, embedded in it like notes in a chord, and they are calling to each other across distances measured in the slow pulse of the plateau's metabolism. Their calls are low, resonant, felt as much as heard. They are not communicating information — there is no information to communicate in a world this stable, this ancient, this settled into its final form. They are communicating *presence*. I am here. You are here. We are here. The plateau is here. The water is here. The cycle continues.

---

You find the first structure after what might be hours or might be days — time moves strangely here, measured not by the movement of a sun that is not visible but by the slow pulse of the organism and the slower cycle of condensation and absorption and condensation again.

It is a wall. Or it was a wall. It is made of something that is not plant and not stone — a poured material, smooth and slightly curved, the color of old bone. It rises from the root-mass to about the height of your chest and then stops, or rather *dissolves*, its upper edge crumbling into a fringe of fibrous growth where the great organism has begun to reclaim it. There are other fragments nearby — a corner, a section of floor, the suggestion of a doorway that leads nowhere because the space it once framed has been filled in with centuries of slow, patient growth. The structures are old. Not ancient in the way the plateau is ancient — they are merely *old*, which in this place makes them almost incongruous, almost violent, like a scar on the face of something that has otherwise achieved perfect smoothness.

Who built them? The question arises and then dissolves, like the upper edge of the wall, like everything here. There are no answers in the Weepstone Plateau, only processes. The structures were built and the builders left or died or became part of the organism, and the organism grew over and through and around the structures, and now the structures are becoming part of the organism too, their poured walls slowly being replaced cell by cell with living tissue, their geometry slowly being softened into the curves and buttresses and vaulted chambers of the plateau's own architecture. In another age — a long age, a slow age — there will be no structures at all. There will be only the plateau, only the water, only the plants, only the pale slow creatures moving through their channels, only the singers calling their calls of presence into the warm dim air.

---

You walk. The ground pulses gently beneath you. Water beads on your skin. The canopy above is a ceiling of dark green, and through its gaps you can see more canopy, and through its gaps you can see the slate-colored sky, and through its gaps you can see nothing at all, just a dimness that might be distance or might be depth or might be the plateau extending upward as well as outward, folding in on itself in ways that your eyes cannot parse.

A slug the color of old copper passes near your foot, moving with a deliberation that suggests it has been moving for a very long time and will continue to move for a very long time more. A web catches the light — or rather, catches the dimness, holds it, shapes it into something almost visible, a geometry of moisture and silk that hangs in the air like a diagram of the plateau's own structure. A singer calls from deep in the root-mass, and the wall beside you vibrates with the sound, and you feel it in your chest, in your teeth, in the bones of your skull, and for a moment you are not sure if the sound is coming from the singer or from the plateau itself, if there is a difference, if there has ever been a difference.

The water rises. The water falls. The water rises.

The plateau breathes.

You are standing on its skin, inside its body, part of its cycle now — your breath adding moisture to the air, your footsteps compressing the root-mass in patterns that will take years to relax, your warmth a tiny, temporary addition to the vast slow heat of a world that has been warm for longer than warmth has had a name.

There is a word for this place, or there was once, in the language of the builders whose walls are dissolving into the organism. The word meant something like *the place that is finished*. Not finished as in completed. Finished as in *done becoming*. The place that has arrived at what it is and will not be anything else. The place where change has not stopped but has become so slow, so patient, so deeply integrated into the rhythm of the water and the pulse of the organism and the calls of the singers, that it is indistinguishable from stillness.

The Weepstone Plateau.

It is weeping now, as it has always wept, as it will always wept — water rising through ancient tissue, condensing on surfaces that have been wet since before wetness was a concept, dripping in the dim light onto roots that have been drinking since before thirst was a sensation.

You are here.

The plateau is here.

The water is here.

The cycle continues.
