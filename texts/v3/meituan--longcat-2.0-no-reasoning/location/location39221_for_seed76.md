# The Lithoderm Highlands

---

You are standing on a world that has never known rain.

The ground beneath you is not rock, not soil, but something between — a vast, cracked expanse of pale mineral crust, the color of bleached bone and old parchment, stretching in every direction to a horizon that seems too far away for the size of the planet should allow. The crust is thick, ancient, and deeply weathered: it has been broken into enormous plates by forces that have long since settled into equilibrium, and between the plates run fissures of varying width — some narrow enough to step across, others gaping open into blue-black depths where the air shimmers with a faint, sour warmth. The surface of the crust is not smooth. It is dimpled, ridged, and pocked with the evidence of long erosion — not by water, never by water, but by wind and thermal cycling and the slow, patient chemistry of a dry atmosphere working on mineral surfaces over spans of time that make geology look hasty. Every surface is matte. Nothing glints. The world has a quality of having been *finished* — worked over so thoroughly that all its roughness has been softened into a kind of granular, chalky refinement.

And yet it is alive.

The vegetation here does not grow from the ground. It grows *on* it — and in it — and sometimes *as* it. The dominant form is what you might call a lithophyte, though that word implies a passivity that these organisms do not possess. They are thick, sprawling, low-slung bodies of tissue that have the texture of felted leather and the color of deep viridian, viridian so dark it is almost black in the folds and creases where the tissue bunches against itself. They spread across the mineral plates in irregular patches — never continuous, always broken, always leaving expanses of bare crust between them — and they are *lush* in a way that seems impossible given the absence of water. Their surfaces are dense with fine, hair-like filaments that move, slowly, constantly, in patterns that are not quite like the movement of leaves in wind. They are harvesting something from the air. Moisture, perhaps, in trace amounts — or something else entirely, some chemical energy that substitutes for the aqueous metabolism that life on wetter worlds takes for granted. The filaments catch the light and hold it, giving each patch a faint, luminous halo, as though the plants are generating their own soft radiance in addition to the abundant light that falls from above.

Between the patches of vegetation, the bare crust is not barren. It is colonized by thinner, more cryptic life — films and crusts of organisms that paint the mineral surface in subtle variations of ochre, rust, and a deep, bruised violet. These are not plants in any recognizable sense. They are something older, or something parallel — chemotrophic, perhaps, feeding on the slow exhalations of the planet's interior, drawing energy from the thermal gradient between the cool surface and the warmer depths. They form patterns: concentric rings around the edges of the fissures, radiating lines along the cracks, dense clusters in the dimpled depressions where the crust has worn thinnest. The patterns are not random. They are the visible expression of chemical gradients, of competition and cooperation played out in mineral pigments across timescales of centuries.

The elevation is high — not in the sense of mountains, though there are ridges and escarpments where the crustal plates have tilted and buckled against each other, but in the sense of *exposure*. You are on a world that feels open to the sky in a way that lowlands never do. The atmosphere is thin but present, and it carries light with a clarity that makes everything seem simultaneously sharp and distant. The sky is not blue. It is a deep, luminous amber — the color of late afternoon stretched across the entire dome of the heavens — and it is *bright*, overwhelmingly bright, a brightness that does not come from a single point source but from the whole sky itself, as though the atmosphere is faintly, uniformly luminous. There is a sun, or something that serves as one: a region of the sky that is brighter than the rest, a diffuse radiance that shifts slowly across the amber field, but it is not a disk, not a point. It is more like a brightening, a thickening of the light, as though the sky itself is thicker in one place and thinner in another. The shadows it casts are soft but present — long, blue-tinged shadows that stretch across the pale crust and give the landscape a depth and dimensionality that the flat light of the amber sky alone would not provide.

The temperature is cool. Not cold — there is no frost, no ice, no brittleness in the air — but cool in a way that you feel on any exposed surface of your skin, a steady, gentle extraction of heat that never becomes urgent. The air moves, and when it moves it carries this coolness with it, and you understand that the coolness is not a property of the air itself but of the world — the vast thermal mass of the mineral crust, radiating its coolness upward, maintaining a surface temperature that has been stable for longer than any living thing has existed here. The air smells of nothing, or almost nothing — a faint mineral sharpness, like the smell of dust in an empty room, and beneath it something organic and green, the faintest exhalation of the lithophyte patches, a smell like crushed herbs but drier, more resinous.

The scale of this place is difficult to hold in your mind. The crustal plates are hundreds of kilometers across. The fissures between them run for distances that you cannot see the end of. The ridges and escarpments rise and fall in waves that have the rhythm of mountain ranges but the texture of something worn smooth by ages of patient erosion. And the sky — the sky is not a sky over a landscape. It is a sky over a *world*, a world large enough to have its own weather, and the weather is happening. You can see it: vast, slow movements of the amber air, high bands of cloud or haze that are darker than the surrounding sky, drifting in patterns that speak of circulation cells and pressure gradients and the slow, planetary breathing of an atmosphere in motion. There are no storms — the air is too dry for that, too stable — but there are *events*: regions where the amber light thickens and darkens, where the air becomes briefly opaque with fine mineral dust lifted from the crust by thermal currents, where the visibility drops to a few hundred meters and the world becomes a soft, diffuse glow of amber light with no features, no edges, no ground. These events pass. The dust settles. The clarity returns. The landscape re-emerges, unchanged, patient, waiting.

The density of life is low. The lithophyte patches are scattered — you can see the nearest one, a dark, felted mass perhaps two hundred meters away, its filament-hairs catching the light in a slow, shimmering wave — but between you and it there is nothing but bare crust, bare mineral, bare world. The next patch beyond that is a dark smudge on the horizon. The one after that is invisible. The life here is present but sparse, distributed across the vastness of the crust in a pattern that speaks of limitation — not of water, which does not exist here, but of *something else*, some resource or condition that is scarce enough to keep the patches separated, to prevent them from merging into continuous cover. They compete for space, these patches, but the competition is slow, geological, a matter of millimeters per year, and the outcome is a landscape of scattered green-black islands in a pale mineral sea.

There are animals. Rare, but present. You have not seen one yet, but you have seen the evidence: a track in the thin mineral dust that has settled in a dimpled depression, a track with too many legs and a gait that suggests something low and wide and deliberate. A small, dark shape on a distant ridge that might be a lithophyte patch or might be something else, something that does not move when the wind moves, because it is not moved by wind. A sound — the only sound you have heard since arriving, a sound so faint you are not sure you heard it at all: a low, resonant vibration that seemed to come from the ground itself, from the crust, from the depths below, a sound like a bell struck once and left to ring for a very long time. It might have been geological. It might have been biological. In this place, the distinction is not always clear.

The silence is the dominant acoustic feature of this world. The air is too thin to carry sound well, and there is little to make sound — no water, no rustling leaves (the lithophyte filaments move too slowly to produce audible vibration), no wind strong enough to whistle or moan. What sound exists is subtle: the faint, almost subliminal hum of the lithophyte patches, a vibration produced by the movement of their filaments that is felt more than heard. The occasional tick and creak of the mineral crust as it responds to thermal changes — the sun-bright region of the sky moving across the amber dome, heating one area and cooling another, and the crust expanding and contracting in response with sounds like the settling of an old building. And the deep, rare, resonant tone from below — the sound of the planet itself, or of something living in it, or of the boundary between the two.

The weirdness of this place is not dramatic. It is not a world of impossible geometries or alien physics. It is a world that is *almost* familiar — almost Earth-like, almost comprehensible — but wrong in ways that accumulate as you pay attention. The plants that grow without water. The sky that glows from everywhere and nowhere. The animals that might be geological. The silence that is not empty but *full* — full of the slow, subliminal hum of life and the deeper, slower hum of the world itself, a hum that you feel in your bones before you hear it with your crusty, water-world ears. The light is too bright and too soft at the same time. The air is too thin and too present. The ground is too solid and too alive. Everything is in balance — the erosion has reached its equilibrium, the life has reached its equilibrium, the thermal and chemical and atmospheric cycles have reached their equilibrium — and the equilibrium is *strange*, is *other*, is a stable state that no wet world would ever find.

You stand on the pale crust. The amber sky glows above you. The nearest lithophyte patch shimmers its slow, filamentous shimmer. The fissure to your left drops away into blue-black depth. The ridge to your right rises against the luminous sky, its crest sharp and weathered and old. Somewhere, far away, something makes a sound like a bell, or like stone, or like a living thing that has forgotten it is alive.

The world breathes. You breathe with it. The air is thin and cool and tastes of minerals and green resin and nothing else.

You are very high up. You are very far from anything you have ever known. The light is very bright, and the silence is very deep, and the world is very, very old, and it is not finished yet.
