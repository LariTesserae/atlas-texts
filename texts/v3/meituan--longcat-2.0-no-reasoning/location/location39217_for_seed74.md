# The Breathery

You step down a narrow stone stair — the last remnants of some older access — and emerge onto a platform suspended between two immense root structures. The air is cool and damp against your skin, carrying the mineral smell of wet stone and something sweeter underneath, like overripe figs left in a cellar. Condensation beads on every surface. You can hear it: a slow, irregular pattering as droplets fall from leaf to leaf to root to platform, never quite reaching the ground because there is no ground, only more foliage below, and below that, more still.

The Breathery is a cathedral-sized hollow that exists inside a single continuous living mass — not a tree, exactly, though it began as one, centuries ago, before it learned to incorporate everything it touched. The walls are a dense mat of interwoven roots and secondary trunks, some as thick as a person's torso, others thin as fingers, all of them slowly moving. You can see the movement if you watch long enough: a root thickening over the course of minutes, a tendril curling around a support beam that was clearly installed by human hands and is now being gently, patiently swallowed. The wood has a waxy, dark-green tone, almost black in the hollows, and the surface is perpetually slick with moisture — not rain, though it does rain here sometimes, but transpiration. The mass breathes. That's where the name comes from. During the light hours, the system pushes moisture out through millions of tiny stomata in the bark analog, and the humidity spikes to something almost unbearable. Then, as the light fades, it draws inward, and you can hear the sound change: a faint, collective creaking, like a ship in a gentle swell, as millions of cells adjust their turgor. The whole structure contracts by a centimeter or two. You can feel it under your feet if you're standing still.

The space is organized. That's the unsettling part.

Platforms have been constructed at various levels throughout the hollow — not built so much as negotiated. Metal brackets are bolted directly into living wood, and the wood has grown around them, sometimes accommodating, sometimes resisting. You can see the evidence of failed compromises: a bracket snapped clean in half by a root that thickened beneath it over years, a walkway tilted fifteen degrees because the tree shifted its center of mass. But most of the infrastructure has found its equilibrium. There are walkways of corrugated steel grating, rusted to a deep umber, bolted into the root-walls with expansion joints that allow for slow movement. There are stairs. There are railings. At the lowest accessible level, perhaps thirty meters down from where you stand, there is a workshop — you can hear it before you see it: the rhythmic clank of a mechanical press, the whine of a small generator running on biodiesel, the hiss of steam venting through a pipe that runs up along a root and disappears into the canopy above.

The workshop processes the mass. This is what the settlement does. Slow tendrils and root tips are harvested — carefully, selectively, always leaving enough that the tree doesn't react defensively — and brought down to the workshop to be rendered into fiber, into resin, into a dense amber fluid that has properties no synthetic chemist has fully replicated. The harvesting is governed by rules posted on laminated signs at each access point: *Do not cut primary structural roots. Do not harvest within 2 meters of a node cluster. Report any signs of reactive thickening.* The signs are laminated because of the damp, and even so the edges are curling and spotting with mold.

The people are sparse — you might see three or four during a long visit. They move with the unhurried competence of people who have learned the rhythms of a place that is alive and reactive. A woman in a waxed canvas coat crosses a walkway below you, carrying a bundle of pale root fiber under one arm. She doesn't look up. A man sits on a lower platform with a device that looks like a stethoscope connected to a gauge, pressed against a root, reading something. A child — there's always one child, there's always one child in these places — is crouched at the edge of a platform watching a beetle navigate a crack in the grating with the total absorption that children bring to small things.

The fauna, beyond the human, is limited but present. The beetles are everywhere — a species that has adapted to the constant humidity, small and dark, with an unusual tolerance for the mildly acidic moisture that drips from the canopy. They eat the algae that grows on the root surfaces. Occasionally you'll see a small gecko-analog, pale and wide-footed, hunting the beetles along the walkway edges. Something lives in the upper canopy that you never quite see — a rustling, a brief shadow against the light filter from above — but no one has identified it, and no one seems to be looking very hard. The tree, it seems, has its own ecology up there, and the settlement has an unwritten agreement not to investigate too closely. The roots deeper in the mass, below the lowest platform, are warm to the touch, and sometimes they pulse — not with a heartbeat, exactly, but with a slow peristaltic rhythm, as if something were being moved through internal channels. The engineers have theories. No one has tested them.

The light is normal but strange. It filters down from above through layers of leaves — the tree's own canopy extends far beyond the hollow, and shafts of light descend at angles that shift as the leaves move. The effect is like being underwater in a kelp forest, if the kelp forest were enclosed in living wood and filled with the smell of wet stone and biodiesel exhaust. In the workshop, electric lights — harsh, fluorescent, industrial — clash with the organic dimness, and the transition between the two registers is jarring, like stepping from one world into another within the space of a few stairs.

There are rules. Everyone knows them, though not all of them are written. Don't touch the roots with bare hands during the breathing-in phase — the surface temperature drops sharply and the bark contracts, and more than one person has lost a fingertip to a root that closed around their hand before they realized what was happening. Don't make loud sounds near the node clusters — the tree responds to vibration, and reactive growth can come fast, a wall of new wood sprouting overnight and blocking a walkway or collapsing a platform. Don't go below the lowest platform. No one enforces this rule with anything stronger than custom, but it holds, because below the lowest platform the mass is less predictable, older, and the roots there have been growing without human guidance for a long time, and their pattern is not one that accommodates human bodies.

The place is settling. Not failing — settling, like sediment, like a house on old foundations. The platforms sag imperceptibly year by year. The walkways tilt a little more. The tree grows and the metal adjusts and the people maintain and the workshops process and the beetles eat the algae and the moisture falls and falls and falls, never hitting the ground, only finding another leaf, another root, another surface to bead on and slowly roll down, an endless slow circulation. The sound is mostly quiet: the drip, the creak, the distant clank of the press, the click of beetle feet on steel. And underneath it all, if you press your ear to the wall — if you're brave enough — a sound like breathing. Not metaphorical. Actual. The mass inhales and exhales on a cycle that takes roughly forty minutes, and if you time it right, you can feel the platform rise beneath you, almost imperceptibly, and then settle back, and then rise again, slow and patient, like the chest of something vast and alive and only half-aware that you're here.
