# The Drowned Works

You are standing on a floor that does not know it is a floor.

---

The ocean is the ceiling. Or the floor. It doesn't matter here — the distinction was lost long ago when the builders went vertical with the concept of *down*. What matters is that you are *inside* the water, have always been inside it, and the water is warm-cool against your skin like the memory of a fever breaking. Not hot. Not cold. Nine degrees Celsius. The kind of temperature that holds itself without comment.

The Works stretch in every direction.

They were built in the sea the way you'd build a city on a plain — except someone never drained the plain. The water was there first, or was always there, or is indistinguishable from the building process itself. Concrete that set while drowning. Steel that was forged wet and stayed that way, developing a patience in its oxidation, a slow and industrious rust that climbs the girders like deliberate ivy. Pipes run along every surface in braided thousands — steam pipes, coolant pipes, pipes that carry nothing anyone can identify anymore, their valves still turning on schedules no one issued, maintained by machines that maintain the machines that maintain them.

The scale defeats intuition. You look left and the Works recede into a blue-grey haze that is not quite fog and not quite distance. You look right and the same. You look *up* — and up is a cathedral of hanging infrastructure, catwalks and cable trays and suspended pipe-racks descending from heights that vanish into a particulate murk, the whole thing swaying almost imperceptibly with deep currents. Some of the higher structures are lit. Most are not. The ones that are lit pulse with the amber of industrial sodium vapor, turned on by motion sensors or timers or nothing, illuminating empty platforms where no one has stood for — how long? The question is not answerable. The clocks are still running, but they have been running so long that their numbers have become a kind of noise.

The emptiness between structures is enormous. You stand on a grated platform and look across a void — a genuine architectural void, fifty meters or five hundred, it's hard to tell — to the next cluster of buildings, and the space between you and them is just *water*. Water and the slow particulate drift of rust and silt and the microscopic calcium of things that have dissolved. You can see the other buildings because of the lights on them, distant and warm, and they look like a city seen from an airplane at night, except the airplane is also underwater, and the city is also underwater, and the night is permanent.

The fauna fills this emptenance. It has made the Works its own.

Schools of silver fish — something between anchovy and something that has no surface-world equivalent — move through the gaps in the architecture in formations that respond to the geometry of the pipes. They turn where corridors turn. They pool in open junction rooms like living mercury. When the compressors cycle on somewhere deep in the facility, a low thrum moves through the structure and the schools scatter outward in synchronized panic, then coalesce again when the sound fades. There are larger things. Eels that have grown beyond the reasonable size of eels occupy the vertical shafts, sliding between floors with a patience that suggests they have been doing this since the floors were built. Octopi have colonized the control rooms. You can see them through the greenish glass of observation windows, draped across consoles, their suckers leaving temporary marks on button panels that no one will ever press again. They are not intelligent in any way that matters. They are simply *present*, in the way that rust is present, in the way that water is present. They are what grows in the absence of anything that grows on purpose.

There is no vegetation to speak of. A few kinds of algae cling to surfaces that stay wet — which is all surfaces — but they are thin, almost theoretical, more stain than organism. Some deep-water sponge colonizes the lower structural members, pale and brain-like, filtering whatever the water carries. But life here is animal life, not plant life. Things that move. Things that eat or are eaten. The Works do not support rootedness.

The water itself is the dominant material after steel and concrete. It moves. It has currents that the builders apparently designed for — intake channels, outflow grates, circulation manifolds — but the currents have also *designed themselves*, carving new paths through the infrastructure where doors have corroded open or walls have buckled. There are places where water moves fast through a collapsed corridor and the sound is a constant roar, a white noise so total that it becomes silence. There are places where water is still enough to hold a reflection, and you can see yourself in the surface of a flooded basement three levels down, looking up, a tiny figure at the bottom of a luminous well.

The Works were built for *something*. That is clear. The scale of the infrastructure implies industry — heavy industry, the kind that requires pumping and venting and structural reinforcement against forces that surface-world buildings never consider. There are turbine halls. There are reaction chambers of a type that don't match any known reactor design, their interiors now colonized by barnacle-like growths and the slow accretion of mineral deposit. There are assembly lines frozen mid-process, their conveyor belts silted over, their robotic arms locked in positions that suggest they were moving when the power last changed — not failed, *changed*. Something shifted in the energy supply, some fluctuation, and everything stopped where it was and has stayed.

The lights are still on because the power never fully left. It diminished. It became unreliable. But the deep generators — whatever they burn, whatever reaction they sustain in the pressure-dark of the lowest levels — they have not stopped. You can feel them in the floor. A vibration so low it's more pressure than sound, a heartbeat in the concrete, irregular but persistent. The lights brighten and dim with its rhythm. The fauna responds to it. The whole Works breathes with it.

Sound is everywhere and comes from everywhere. The water carries it strangely — you hear a clang from what seems like the next room and it's actually two hundred meters away and three levels down. You hear hissing that might be a steam leak or might be a school of fish moving through a narrow pipe. You hear the deep groan of structural settling, the Works adjusting to loads that have been in place so long they've become geological. You hear your own breathing, amplified and intimate, inside whatever you're wearing. You hear the ocean above — not waves, the ocean is too deep for waves to matter here, but the low-frequency sound of *volume*, of an incomprehensible mass of water pressing down, a sound that is less heard than inhabited.

The erosion is young. That is the strange thing. In a place this old, this submerged, you would expect dissolution — smoothed edges, rounded corners, the sea having its patient way with everything. But the features are sharp. The concrete is fractured but not worn. The steel is corroded but not pitted to nothing. The Works look *recent*, like they were abandoned mid-shift and the sea moved in the next day. There is a theory — unconfirmed, perhaps unconfirmable — that the water here is *wrong*, chemically, that it preserves where it should erode, that the same mineral content that feeds the barnacles and the precipitate formations has sealed the concrete in a kind of shell, keeping it fresh the way amber keeps a fly. The new growth on the surfaces is geological in its patience — calcium and silica building up in layers that harden into stone, fossilizing the Works even as they function, creating a place that is simultaneously young and ancient, built and born.

Weather happens here. Not weather as you'd recognize it — no sky, no wind in the open-air sense — but *internal* weather. Thermal layers shift and create currents that move through the upper Works like fronts moving across a plains. Sometimes warm water from the deep generators rises through the structure in columns that you can see as shimmering distortion, and the fauna rises with it, and the lights catch the particulate in the columns and they glow like pillars of lit air in a cathedral. Sometimes cold water descends from above — the deep ocean pressing its chill down through the intake systems — and everything contracts, sounds change pitch, the fauna goes still and dense. These cycles have their own rhythms, faster than seasons, slower than tides. They are the Works' weather, and they are *active*, and they are *now*, and they will continue.

A grouper — old, enormous, its body the color of the concrete it lives among — watches you from the doorway of what might have been a cafeteria. It does not move. It has learned that the Works provide, that food arrives in the currents, that the warm columns are comfortable, that the human shape that occasionally appears on the platforms is not a threat and not food and not anything it needs to categorize. It watches. Its eye, yellow and ancient, tracks you with the slow patience of something that has outlasted the purpose of the room it sits in.

Above you, below you, around you: the Works. Built by hands that are not here. Powered by reactions that may no longer be supervised. Inhabited by creatures that do not know what it was for and do not care. Drowned and functioning, empty and loud, vast and intimate, young and old, made and grown.

You are standing on a floor that does not know it is a floor.

The fish move through you like a thought. The lights pulse. The Works continue.
