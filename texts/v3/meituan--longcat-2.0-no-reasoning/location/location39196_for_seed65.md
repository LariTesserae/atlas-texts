# The Glass Steppe of Kael

You stand on a knife of rock so narrow you can feel the wind against your ribs on either side, and the sky is enormous above you, white-bright and pitiless, a luminous dome that presses down with the weight of altitude. The air is thin. Every breath reaches only halfway into your lungs, as if the atmosphere itself has been stretched too far and thinned to a suggestion. You are impossibly high — not merely on a mountain, but in the architecture of something that was never meant for standing on, a world of verticality that has been left to settle into its own logic for longer than anything living can remember.

The stone underfoot is pale and splintered, a limestone so old it has begun to behave like a different substance entirely. In places it shelves into translucent plates — actual translucence, thin as fingernails — and you can see the faint light passing through them, a ghostly greenish glow. The locals, if you could find any here, might call it bone-glass, or they might not call it anything at all because they would have no reason to be here. This is a landscape that does not welcome. It simply *is*, with the calm indifference of something that has achieved equilibrium and has no intention of moving from it.

**The terrain** extends in a series of these ridges — angular, almost geometric, as if the mountain range were a set of stacked blades driven into the earth at sharp inclinations. Between the ridges are not valleys but *gaps*: enormous open distances where the rock simply ends and the next formation begins half a league away, separated by nothing, by air, by the vertiginous drop of empty space. The scale is difficult to judge because there are almost no reference points — no trees, no structures, no human marks of any kind. The ridges repeat in a rhythm that is not quite regular, not quite random, and your eye keeps trying to find a pattern in them the way it tries to find shapes in clouds. The mind refuses the emptiness. It insists on meaning where there is only stone and distance and light.

And the light — the light is *relentless*. At this elevation the atmosphere has been whittled down to a thin membrane, and the sun passes through it unfiltered. It is not harsh the way desert sun is harsh, hot and punishing. It is simply *total*. Everything is lit. There are almost no shadows because the surfaces are so pale and the rock faces reflect and scatter the illumination in every direction until the whole landscape glows faintly, as if it were lit from within. The brightness has a quality of permanence that feels slightly wrong, slightly more than what a single star should produce. Squinting here is reflexive but ultimately futile — the light comes from everywhere, not just above.

**The sound** is the thing that will undo you.

The wind does not blow across the Glass Steppe. It *screams*. It has been channeled and compressed by the ridgelines into a continuous, deafening howl that is less like weather and less like any natural phenomenon and more like the sound a structure makes when it is under enormous mechanical stress — a sustained, resonant, bone-deep roar that never stops, never modulates, never rests. You cannot hear your own footsteps. You cannot hear your own breathing. The sound is so total that after a few minutes you stop registering it as noise and start registering it as a condition of existence, the same way you register gravity. It is simply the state of things here. People who have come to the Steppe and survived long enough to speak of it say the sound is what breaks you first — not the altitude, not the cold, not the emptiness, but the *sound*, which fills your skull and vibrates in your teeth and eventually becomes the only sound you have ever heard, replacing memory of gentler things.

There is water here, but it does not behave like water should. The dampness manifests as a film — a thin, persistent condensation that coats every surface, not from rain but from the interaction between the cold stone and the thin moisture that exists even at this altitude. In the gaps between the ridges, where the air swirls in eddies driven by the screaming wind, this moisture collects into strange formations: long, hanging threads of water suspended in the open air, not falling but *holding*, trembling in the current like the strings of some enormous instrument. They do not freeze. They do not fall. They simply hang, and the wind moves through them and produces, at certain angles, a harmonic beneath the scream — a deep, subsonic pulse that you feel in your sternum before you ever hear it. This is the only variation in the soundscape. The only moment where the landscape offers anything other than assault.

**The ecology** is sparse in the way that extremophile ecologies are sparse — not absent, but concentrated, specialized, fierce. What grows here grows close to the stone: pale lichens in vast sheets, some of them decades thick, their surfaces crackled and mineral-encrusted, more geology than biology. Between them, in the cracks where condensation pools, there are small, colorless plants — nothing you would call a flower, nothing with a stem or leaf in any recognizable sense, but rather cushion-shaped masses of cellular tissue, grey-green, that photosynthesize with an efficiency that seems greedy given how little biomass they produce. They are not struggling. They have found their place. They do not need to be more.

But the fauna — the fauna is *unexpected*. In a place this empty, this loud, this hostile, you do not expect abundance, and yet it is here, in forms that seem to defy the environment rather than adapt to it. The sky is full of them. Birds — or things that occupy the niche birds would occupy — ride the screaming thermals along the ridgelines in formations that are too tight, too coordinated, turning in unison with a precision that looks choreographed. They are small, pale, almost translucent in the brutal light, and they make no sound. Against the roar of the wind, their silence is conspicuous. They eat the lichens, or each other, or something else — their ecology is opaque. They simply exist in vast numbers, wheeling and folding against the white sky like living flakes of the stone itself.

On the rock surfaces, in the micro-habitats where condensation is thickest, there are small arthropods — or things like arthropods — that move with a slow, deliberate pace that seems incongruous with their abundance. They are everywhere once you look closely, thousands per square pace, pale-bodied, segmented, their movements creating a faint collective shimmer across the stone like a living patina. They do not flee from your shadow. They do not acknowledge your presence at all. They have been here, in this form, for longer than the ridges have held their current shape, and they will be here when the next erosion cycle grinds the stone to powder and the wind carries it away.

**The strangeness** — and there is strangeness here, a low hum of it beneath the louder strangeness of the place — is in the way things persist. Nothing here is new. The lichens are not growing visibly. The stone is not visibly eroding. The birds do not appear to age. The water threads do not lengthen or shorten. The dynamic is zero. The landscape has arrived at a state and it is *staying there*, not through stasis exactly — things move, things live, the wind blows — but through a kind of deep equilibrium where every process is balanced against every other process so perfectly that the net result is a place that appears, at every scale, to be the same from moment to moment, from year to year, from century to century. It is not frozen. It is *settled*. Like a person who has finally stopped fidgeting. Like a machine that has found its resonant frequency and hums there, perfectly, indefinitely.

The erosion has been happening for so long that the land has reached its final shape — these ridges, these gaps, these angles — and will hold them until the sun expands or the plates shift. There is a completeness to the geology here that is almost architectural, as if the mountain range were not formed by accident but by *conclusion*, by the long working-out of a problem whose answer is this: pale stone, open space, bright light, screaming wind, and the small, tenacious things that have learned to live in the answer.

**There is nothing built here.** No cairns, no walls, no carved markers, no ruins. No one has ever had a reason to stay long enough to build, and no one has ever had the materials to build with — the stone splinters too readily, the wind would take anything standing, and the altitude would make the work fatal within hours. The closest thing to a human mark are the water threads themselves, which some distant observer might mistake for something strung deliberately, some vast and silent instrument tuned to a frequency below hearing. But they are natural — or they are as natural as anything can be in a place where the boundary between the made and the grown has blurred into irrelevance over geological time.

You will not stay here. No one stays here. But if you were to descend in memory, years later, in some warm and sheltered place where the air is thick and the sounds are small and the light is gentle, you would find that the Glass Steppe had left something in you — a ringing, a faint subsonic pulse beneath the threshold of hearing, the harmonic of wind through water in empty space, the sound of a place that has found its voice and will not stop speaking.
