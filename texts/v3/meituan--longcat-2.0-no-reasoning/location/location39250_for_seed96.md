# The Loom of Unfinished Rain

---

You stand on a platform of pale stone — not quarried, not carved, but *grown* — and the first thing you notice is that everything is wet. Not raining. Not flooded. Just *wet*, the way a held breath is wet, the way the inside of a mouth is wet. The air carries water the way thick fabric carries dye: saturated, total, inseparable from the medium itself. Your skin registers it before your eyes do — a cool film, a second atmosphere pressed against you, and when you exhale you can see your breath not as vapor but as a brief *darkening*, a shadow that the moisture in the air briefly holds before releasing.

The stone beneath your feet is young. That is the word that comes, and it is the right one. The surface has the unfinished quality of something that has only recently decided to be solid — edges that haven't yet learned to be edges, planes that still remember being liquid. It is a calciumite, or something like calciumite: a mineral that precipitates from the supersaturated air itself, accreting on every surface the way frost forms on a window, except here the frost never melts, only builds. The ground is layered in thin, overlapping plates, each one a record of a single day's deposition, and they are sharp — genuinely sharp, the way a fresh flake of mica is sharp — so that walking requires attention. The plates crack and shift under your weight with a sound like someone turning pages in a very old, very dry book, except the pages are wet, and the sound is more of a *click-sigh*, a small mechanical grief.

And everything is built.

This is the thing that takes time to understand, because at first it looks like geology. It looks like a landscape. The terraces, the channels, the ridgework, the arches — they have the appearance of natural formation, the way a termite mound has the appearance of a hill. But there is no natural substrate. You can tell because the calciumite grows on *other calciumite*, layer on layer, and if you find an edge — a broken plate, a fresh fracture — you can see that the material is uniform all the way through. No bedrock. No soil. No foundation of older stone beneath the newer stone. The entire world, as far as you can determine, is made of itself. It is a structure that has built itself out of the water in the air, and it has been doing so for a long time, and it is not finished.

The structures — you can call them structures now, because that is what they are — rise in dense profusion. Not buildings, exactly. More like the internal architecture of a body: vaults and buttresses and load-bearing curves that exist not to enclose space but to *support more surface area*, because more surface area means more deposition, means more growth, means more structure. They crowd against each other. The density is extraordinary. You cannot see through the landscape so much as *into* it, a few body-lengths, before the calciumite forms close ranks and the view becomes a wall of pale, wet, sharply-layered stone. Arches lean against arches. Terraces stack on terraces. Channels run between them — water channels, always, because water is the blood of this place — and the channels are full, not with flowing water but with *standing* water, water that sits in each channel like a patient animal, and the water is so still that each channel becomes a mirror, and the mirrors reflect the bright sky, and the sky is very bright.

The light comes from above — a sun, or something that functions as a sun — and it is relentless. Not hot. The temperature stays cool, stays in that narrow band between comfort and chill, the temperature of a root cellar, of deep shade on a summer afternoon. But the light is *bright*, and the moisture in the air does not dim it so much as *distribute* it, so that there are no shadows, or rather that shadows are soft and everywhere, a general dimming rather than a sharp exclusion. The calciumite plates catch the light and hold it, and because the plates are layered and slightly translucent, the light penetrates a few millimeters into each surface and then scatters, so that the entire landscape has a faint internal glow, as if the stone itself is faintly luminous, as if it is remembering the light even in the places the light does not directly reach.

The water weeps. That is the only word. From every horizontal surface, from every slight overhang, from every channel lip and terrace edge, water condenses and gathers and then releases — not in drops, exactly, but in a slow, continuous *shedding*, a film of water that moves across the stone surfaces and finds the channels and joins the standing water there. The weeping is silent. Or nearly silent. If you hold your breath you can hear it: a sound below sound, a texture rather than a tone, the way velvet has a sound when you run your hand across it. The place is mostly quiet. The water moves slowly. The stone, when it shifts, shifts on timescales you can feel only if you stand very still for a very long time — a faint *creep*, a settling, the sound of weight finding its most efficient path through a structure that is still, after all this time, deciding what shape it wants to be.

There is no life. You understand this gradually, the way you understand that a room is empty — not all at once, but as a series of small confirmations. No green. No movement that isn't mechanical. No smell of growth or decay. The calciumite is mineral. The water is mineral-rich but sterile. The air is wet and cool and empty of spores, of pollen, of any organic signature. And yet the place is not dead, because it was never alive. It is *active*. It is growing. The deposition continues. The structures accrete. The channels deepen by fractions. The arches thicken at their bases. It is a world that is making itself, slowly, patiently, out of nothing but water and light and the chemistry of dissolved minerals, and it has been doing this for longer than you can meaningfully conceive, and it will continue after you are gone.

The strangeness — and it is strange, deeply strange, in a way that settles into your bones — is the *intentionality* of it. Not conscious intention. Nothing here thinks. But the structures have the quality of *design*. The channels all slope toward the same direction — you check, you verify, you follow three separate channels through the dense calciumite maze and they all slope the same way, gently, almost imperceptibly, toward a destination you cannot see. The arches are load-bearing in ways that suggest an understanding of force, of compression, of the way weight moves through a structure. The terraces are spaced at intervals that optimize — you are certain of this, though you cannot say how — the surface area available for deposition. It is as if the process of growth itself has learned, has iterated, has found efficient forms and repeated them, the way evolution finds efficient forms, except there is no reproduction here, no selection, no death. Only accretion. Only the slow, wet, bright, patient accumulation of structure upon structure upon structure, each one slightly better than the last at being what it is.

You follow the channels. They lead you deeper into the Loom — you have started calling it that, privately, because the interlocking structures remind you of threads on a warp, because the place feels *woven* even though it is mineral, even though nothing here was ever made by hands. The calciumite grows denser. The channels multiply. The standing water in them is so still, so perfectly reflective, that walking among them is like walking through a double world — the pale stone above, the pale stone below, and you suspended between them in a band of cool bright wet air, and the only thing that tells you which direction is up is the faint pull of gravity and the faint sound of weeping from the surfaces above you.

You find a place where the structures open — not a clearing, exactly, but a *chamber*, a space where the calciumite has grown in a ring, leaving the center open, and the center is a pool. The pool is perfectly circular. The water in it is perfectly still. The light falls into it and does not scatter — it *enters*, it goes down, it illuminates the pool from within, and you can see the bottom, which is more calciumite, more plates, more layers, and the layers are deep, and they are old, and they are the history of this place written in mineral, and you cannot read it, but you can see that it is *there*, that it means something, that the record is complete and patient and waiting for someone who will never come.

The air is very wet. The light is very bright. The stone is very quiet. The water moves, when it moves, so slowly that you cannot see it moving — you can only see, if you look away and then look back, that it has moved, that the level in a channel is slightly lower, that a new plate of calciumite has begun to form on a surface that was bare a moment ago. The world is making itself. The world has always been making itself. You are standing inside a process, inside a slow bright wet act of creation that has no creator and no audience and no purpose beyond the continuation of itself, and it is beautiful, and it is strange, and it is utterly, profoundly alone.
