# The Rootworks

## A Place That Was Once a Mountain

---

You stand — if "stand" is the word — on a surface that is not ground. It is a floor of interlocked root structures, each one as thick as a cathedral column, braided and fused into a continuous platform that extends in every direction until the darkness swallows it. The roots are not wood. They are something that grew like wood, hardened like wood, but under your feet they have the warmth and faint give of living tissue. They are hot. Everything here is hot. The air sits at the temperature of a fever — not unbearable, but insistent, a heat that comes from below and from within, as though the entire structure is metabolizing.

There is no sky. There is no horizon. There is only the architecture of growth, and the dark.

---

## What Grew Here

This place was, once, a mountain range. You can still find the bones of it — here and there, a ridge of exposed stone juts through the root-mass like a knuckle through skin, and these stones are old beyond any geology you'd recognize. They have been worn to near-smoothness, not by wind or water — there has never been water here, not in any form that would be called water — but by the slow, patient abrasion of root against mineral over spans of time that make "ancient" feel like an understatement. The stones are glassy. They have been polished to a dull sheen by ten billion passes of fibrous growth, and they are warm to the touch, and they hum.

The roots have consumed the mountains. Not destroyed — *consumed*. Incorporated. The mineral substrate is still here, but it has been threaded through, replaced, converted. What you walk on is a composite: part cellulose-analogue, part silicate, part something that has no name in any chemistry you'd know. The roots grew down into the stone and the stone became root and the distinction collapsed. This is the oldest stable state. This is what happens when growth has had enough time to finish.

And yet it is not finished. It is *turbulent*.

---

## The Living Dark

The light here is almost nothing. A faint bioluminescence rises from certain root-junctions — a deep amber, the color of heated iron just before it goes dark — and it pulses slowly, arrhythmically, as though the structure itself has a heartbeat that can't quite settle on a rhythm. Between these dim glows, the darkness is total. You cannot see more than a few body-lengths in any direction. The root-architecture rises and falls around you in forms that the faint light only partially reveals: arches, buttresses, walls, ceilings, all made of the same braided root-mass, all growing, all *moving*.

Not quickly. But moving.

You can feel it if you stand still. The surface beneath you shifts — a slow peristalsis, a contraction and expansion, as though the entire structure is breathing. And in the distance, you hear it: a deep, subsonic groan, the sound of root-mass rearranging itself, of new growth forcing through old growth, of the architecture *building itself* in real time. The sound is mostly below hearing. You feel it in your sternum, in your teeth.

This is the turbulence. The place is not stable. It is in a state of perpetual, violent construction — but the construction is so slow, and so vast, and so *plant-like* that it resembles stillness unless you know how to look. New root-columns thrust upward from the floor and harden in the space of hours. Arches form and collapse. Walls thicken. Ceiling-vines descend and fuse into new floors above you, and the space you're standing in changes shape while you stand in it. The architecture is being continuously demolished and rebuilt by the same organism, or the same colony, or the same *process* — it's not clear there's a meaningful distinction.

---

## The Factories

And here is the strange part. The *built* part. The *industrial* part.

The roots are not just growing. They are *manufacturing*.

In certain chambers — and "chambers" is the right word, because they are enclosed, deliberate, shaped — the root-mass has formed structures that are unmistakably mechanical. Gears made of hardened root-fiber, turning slowly, driven by the contraction of living tissue. Conveyors of braided vine, carrying nodules of mineral from one place to another. Vats where root-secreted enzymes break down stone into component minerals and reassemble them into — something. New root-structures, yes, but also things that are not root. Things that are *made*.

There are engines here. You can hear them: a rhythmic thudding, like a piston stroke, but organic, wet. They are powered by the heat — the deep heat that rises from below, the metabolic heat of the root-mass itself, concentrated and channeled through root-ducts that function as pipes, as boilers, as pressure vessels. The engines drive the gears. The gears drive the conveyors. The conveyors feed the vats. The vats produce new growth-medium. The new growth-medium is laid down and the cycle continues.

No one built this. No one designed this. The roots grew into these shapes because these shapes *work*, because over the incomprehensible span of time this structure has existed, the configurations that produced more growth survived and the configurations that didn't were consumed and recycled. It is evolution. It is industry. It is the same thing.

The factories are not separate from the organism. They *are* the organism. The root-mass is simultaneously the mine, the refinery, the factory, the product, and the worker. It is a fully constructed environment that constructs itself, endlessly, in the dark.

---

## The Ecology of One

There are no animals. There have never been animals. The concept of animal life — of something that moves independently, that consumes other living things, that exists as a separate organism — has no referent here. There is only the root-mass. One thing. One vast, continuous, self-constructing, self-consuming, self-repairing thing that fills every cubic meter of this space from the deepest foundation to the highest vault.

And yet it is not uniform. It is *packed* with differentiation. Every surface is alive and every surface is doing something different. Here, a wall of root-fiber is photosynthesizing — except there is no light, so it is chemosynthesizing, drawing energy from the mineral substrate, from the heat, from the slow exothermic reactions of its own decomposition and recomposition. There, a cluster of root-tendrils is sensing — reaching into the air, tasting it, measuring temperature and gas composition and vibration, feeding information back into the mass through chemical signals that travel at the speed of diffusion. Elsewhere, a thicket of root-columns is *storing* — packed with dense nodules of energy-rich compound, a larder for the time when the deep heat fluctuates, when the mineral substrate runs thin, when the structure needs to rebuild.

The density is total. There is no empty space. Every gap between root-columns is filled with finer root-mesh, and every gap in the mesh is filled with root-secreted resin, and every gap in the resin is filled with — nothing. Air. The only thing here that is not alive. The air is hot and still and smells of heated mineral and something organic, something like the smell of a greenhouse but deeper, older, more complex. It smells like a place that has been alive for a very long time.

---

## The Sound of Almost Nothing

It is mostly quiet. The subsonic groans. The distant thud of organic engines. The faint creak of root-fiber under tension, like the rigging of a ship, but slower, deeper. Occasionally, a sharp crack — a root-column splitting as new growth forces its way through, or a mineral deposit fracturing under the pressure of root-expansion. The crack echoes through the architecture and fades into the dark.

No birds. No insects. No wind. The silence between sounds is profound and warm and close, like being inside something's body.

---

## The Rules

This is a place where growth is the only process. Not growth as metaphor — literal, physical, material growth. The root-mass grows, and everything else is a consequence. The heat comes from growth. The architecture comes from growth. The industry comes from growth. The erosion — the ancient, patient, total erosion of every mineral surface — comes from growth. Even the darkness is a consequence of growth: the root-mass has grown so thick, so dense, so *packed*, that no light from outside could penetrate it, and the only light it generates for itself is the faintest minimum, just enough for the chemosynthetic processes to calibrate, to know where the surface is, to know where to grow next.

The place is not hostile. It is not friendly. It is not anything that has a relationship to you. It is a process that has been running for longer than your species has existed, and it will continue running after your species is gone, and it does not know you are here. If you stand on the root-floor, the root-floor will slowly grow around your feet. Not quickly. But inevitably. The structure does not distinguish between foreign matter and substrate. You will be incorporated. Your minerals will be extracted and reprocessed and built into new root-columns, and the architecture will continue, and the engines will continue, and the dark will continue, and the heat will continue, and the growth will continue.

This is the oldest stable state. This is what the end of erosion looks like: not flatness, but *saturation*. Every surface occupied. Every gap filled. Every process internalized. A world that has grown so thoroughly into itself that there is no outside left.

---

## What You See, If You Look

In the faint amber light, the root-columns rise around you like the pillars of a forest that has forgotten it was ever a forest. They are not straight. They curve and branch and fuse and split, and their surfaces are covered in a fine root-fuzz that moves — actually moves — reaching and retracting in slow waves, like the cilia of some vast organism. The floor between the columns is a mesh of finer roots, and through the mesh you can see, far below, the dim glow of deeper structures, more engines, more vats, more growth, extending down into the heat.

Above, the ceiling — if it is a ceiling — is a vault of interlocked root-arches, and from the arches hang tendrils, some of them thick as your body, some of them fine as hair, all of them slowly swaying in convection currents generated by the heat below. The tendrils are tipped with nodules that glow faintly, and the nodules pulse, and the pulsing is not synchronized, and the effect is of a sky full of dim, arrhythmic stars.

It is beautiful. It is deeply, fundamentally strange. It is a place where the distinction between the grown and the built has collapsed entirely, where industry is just another metabolism, where the architecture is the organism and the organism is the architecture and both are engaged in the endless, turbulent, self-consuming work of continuing to exist.

The root-floor shifts beneath you. Something groans in the dark. A new column thrusts upward three body-lengths away, cracking through the old mesh, hardening as you watch, already beginning to branch.

The place is making itself. It has always been making itself. It will never stop.
