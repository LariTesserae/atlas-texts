# The Condensation Cathedral

You are inside a machine that is weeping.

Not metaphorically. The walls — if you can call them walls — are sweating. Every surface is beaded with water so thickly that the boundary between solid and liquid has become a negotiation. Droplets form, merge, slide, and are replaced before they can fall. The air is a held breath, saturated, and your own exhalations join the general dampness without distinction. You taste metal and ozone and something older, like the inside of a stone that has never seen sun.

The space is small. You can touch both sides if you extend your arms, though you don't, because the surfaces are not still. They are *active*. The walls are dense with machinery — pipes, valves, coils, plates, meshes, all layered so thickly that no substrate is visible beneath. Brass and black iron and copper green with patina and something white and ceramic and something that might be bone but isn't. The components are packed so tightly that they press against each other, and where they press, water is squeezed out. The whole structure is a press. The whole structure is a wound that won't close.

And it is *loud*.

Not the loud of a single source — not an engine, not a roar — but the loud of ten thousand small mechanisms all operating in a space too small for them. Valves chattering. Pistons the size of your finger cycling so fast they blur. Gears meshing with gears meshing with gears, each one adding its own note to a chord that has no melody, only density. The sound is not directional. It comes from the walls themselves, from the floor, from the condensation on your skin. It is the sound of industry compressed to the scale of a room, and it is *deafening* in the way that a held note becomes deafening — not through volume but through relentlessness, through the absence of any gap in which to rest your hearing.

The light is dim. It comes from somewhere — bioluminescent tubes, perhaps, or chemical reactions in the pipes themselves, a faint blue-green phosphorescence that makes everything look like it is seen through deep water. Shadows don't fall so much as pool, gathering in the crevices between components, and the water on every surface catches and scatters the light so that the whole space seems to glow faintly from within, like a lantern made of rust.

And the fauna.

They are *everywhere*.

Not animals, exactly. Not insects, though they share the quality of insects — the skittering, the multiplicity, the sense that each one is both individual and interchangeable with ten thousand others. They are small mechanisms, or mechanical organisms, or something that has never had to choose between those categories. They move through the machinery like blood cells through capillaries, following channels in the pipes, nesting in the joints of gears, emerging from vents and disappearing into grates. Some are the size of your thumbnail. Some are smaller — barely visible, a glint of movement in the condensation. They are made of the same materials as the walls: brass, iron, copper, ceramic. They click and whir as they move. They are *part of the machine* and they are *alive in it*, and the distinction does not seem to matter here.

There are so many of them that the walls appear to breathe. The surface of the machinery is in constant subtle motion — not the motion of the mechanisms themselves, but the motion of the things that live among them, a crawling, shifting, restless skin of tiny bodies. When you look closely at any single component, you see that it is *inhabited*. A valve is a city. A pipe is a highway. A gear tooth is a cliff face scaled with climbers.

The temperature is cold. Not the cold of ice — the cold of deep stone, of underground places, of metal that has never been warm. The water on the walls is not quite condensation in the ordinary sense. It is *exuded*. The machine is producing it, or the machine is a membrane through which water is being drawn from somewhere else — from the rock below, from the air above, from a reservoir you cannot see. The cold makes the water cling. It doesn't run so much as *persist*, a film, a sheen, a second skin over everything.

The strangeness of the place is not in any single element but in their combination. A room-sized machine, fully built, no natural substrate visible, and yet it is *wet* — wet in a way that suggests geology, suggests a cave, suggests the weeping face of a cliff after rain. The erosion parameter is near zero, meaning nothing here is wearing down, nothing is being worn away, and yet the water suggests erosion, suggests time, suggests the slow work of dissolution. The contradiction is the point. This is a place that has been built to *simulate* a process that it is simultaneously preventing. The machine is making water the way a body makes tears — not because it is breaking down, but because that is what it does. It is its function. Its only function.

And it has been doing it for a long time.

The dynamic parameter is near zero. Nothing changes. The valves chatter at the same rate they have always chattered. The fauna move in the same patterns. The water beads and slides and is replaced. The light holds at the same dim phosphorescence. There is no gradient here — no warm side and cool side, no entrance and exit, no before and after. The place is in equilibrium with itself, a closed loop, a perpetual motion that is not perpetual motion because it is not trying to do work. It is simply *maintaining*. It is a system that has found its state and will not leave it.

You are inside it. You are the only thing here that does not belong to it, and the water is already beginning to bead on your skin, and the tiny mechanisms are already beginning to investigate the new surface you represent, and the sound is already filling the spaces inside your skull where silence used to live.

The machine weeps. The machine has always been weeping. The machine will always be weeping.

You are in the weeping now.
