# The Gorge of Loosened Teeth

There is a place where the ground has only recently decided to stop being flat.

You come to it from the east, where the terrain is ordinary—grey, damp, indifferent—and then the earth simply drops away into a gorge so fresh it hasn't finished forming. The walls are the color of wet concrete, layered in bands that read like sediment but aren't sediment, not exactly. They are *recent*. The rock here is young in the way that a wound is young: raw-edged, not yet scabbed, still weeping. Thin streams of water run down every surface in parallel lines, thousands of them, and where they meet they braid into rivulets that lose their grip and fall. The whole gorge wall is in the slow act of dripping downward, grain by grain, as if the entire cliff face is settling into a decision about what shape it will eventually take.

The water doesn't come from above. There is no river feeding this place. The moisture simply *is here*—it condenses out of the cool, heavy air onto every surface, pools in hollows that haven't had time to deepen into basins, runs in film-thin sheets that catch the dim light and hold it, briefly, before releasing it back into the grey. The air tastes of mineral. It tastes of the inside of a stone that has been cracked open for the first time.

The gorge is not wide—perhaps two hundred meters across at its broadest—but it is deep, and it extends north and south farther than you can see, curving gently like a scar that hasn't decided whether to heal or widen. The floor is a chaos of freshly broken rock: angular slabs, sharp-edged rubble, boulders that have only recently fallen and still carry the pale unweathered faces of their fracture points. Nothing has rounded them yet. Nothing has worn them. They sit in the shallow water that covers the gorge floor in a sheet perhaps ankle-deep, and the water moves over them with a sound that is—

Loud. The sound is the first thing you notice, before the sight, before the smell. It is not a roar. It is not a rush. It is the sound of *countless small events happening simultaneously*: water finding its way over and around and through ten thousand sharp obstacles, each one producing its own tiny note, and all of them combining into a dense, layered, almost choral sound that fills the gorge from wall to wall. It is the sound of a place that is still *making itself*, and the making is audible. There are higher notes where water threads through narrow gaps between rocks, and lower notes where it pools and releases in slow, heavy drops from the cliff edges, and between these a constant mid-range murmur that never resolves into silence. The gorge hums. The gorge chatters. The gorge, in certain spots where the walls narrow and the water is forced through constrictions, *sings*—a thin, reedy, almost vocal sound that rises and falls with the pressure of the flow.

The light is dim. The gorge runs roughly north-south, and the walls are high enough that direct sun reaches the floor only briefly, if at all, and what light there is has already been filtered through the moisture-heavy air into something diffuse and directionless. Shadows exist but are soft-edged, reluctant. The rock walls, wet, reflect what little light there is back in pale, bluish tones, so the gorge has a quality of being lit from within—not brightly, but persistently, as though the stone itself is faintly luminous. It is not. It is only wet, and the wetness catches light the way a dark room catches the glow from a hallway.

There are structures. Not many. They appear at irregular intervals along the gorge walls—small, low, made of the same stone as the cliff face, so that at first you mistake them for natural formations. But they are not. They are *placed*. Some are simple: a flat slab propped against the wall at an angle, creating a shallow overhang. Some are more deliberate: rectangular openings, perhaps a meter tall, cut into the rock face at heights that suggest they were meant to be reached by standing on something that is no longer there. They are empty. They are old in a way that the gorge itself is not—they have been here longer than the fresh fractures, longer than the sharp edges. Whatever made them predates the current state of this place, and the gorge has been forming around them, or through them, or in spite of them. In one, a thin stream of water emerges from the back wall and falls into a carved channel that directs it to the gorge floor. The channel is precise. The water follows it exactly.

There is almost no life. This is the thing that makes the place feel *strange*—not the structures, not the sound, not the wetness, but the absence. A gorge this wet, this mineral-rich, this full of water and sheltered from wind, should be thick with moss, with algae, with the first colonizers of bare rock. It is not. The walls are bare. The rocks on the floor are bare. The water runs over surfaces that are clean in a way that feels *maintained*, as though something is preventing the first films of biological growth from taking hold. You look for lichen and find none. You look for the green smear of algae in the standing pools and find only water, clear and cold, over grey stone.

But not entirely empty. In the deepest part of the gorge, where the walls narrow to perhaps thirty meters and the sound becomes a single sustained tone, there are *presences*. They are not animals. They are not plants. They are not fungi. They are—

They are patterns in the water. Where the flow is forced through the narrowest constrictions, the water moves in ways that are not quite hydraulically explicable. It forms standing shapes—not waves, exactly, but *persistent configurations* that hold their form for seconds at a time before dissolving and reforming. They are dense with internal structure: nested curves, recursive folds, geometries that suggest organization without purpose. They do not move toward anything. They do not move away from anything. They simply *are*, in the water, in the narrow places, and when you watch them for a long time you begin to feel that they are watching back—not with eyes, not with intention, but with the same quality of *attention* that a held breath has. The water is doing something. The water has been doing something for a long time.

There are, rarely, animals. Once, near the southern end of the gorge, you see something move in the water—a dark shape, low-profile, moving against the current with a slow, deliberate motion. It is gone before you can identify it. It leaves no ripple. The water closes over its passage as though it had never been there. You do not see it again. But you feel, afterward, that the sound of the gorge has changed slightly—that one of the many small voices in the chorus has shifted its note, almost imperceptibly, in response.

The air is cool. Not cold—cool in the way that deep stone is cool, a temperature that has nothing to do with season or weather and everything to do with *depth*. The gorge is low in elevation, but it feels lower than that. It feels like a place that has sunk below the level of the ordinary world, not in altitude but in some other dimension—a place where the ambient temperature of existence is a few degrees less than it should be. Your breath does not mist. The air is too wet for that; the moisture is already saturated, already condensed, already running down the walls in its ten thousand small streams. But you feel the coolness in your hands, in your face, in the parts of you that are exposed to the air. It is the coolness of a place that has not been warm in a very long time.

The density of the place is its most overwhelming quality. Not density of matter—though the gorge is full, packed with rock and water and sound and the strange presences in the narrows—but density of *event*. Everything is happening at once, at every scale. The water is moving. The rock is settling. The structures are slowly being absorbed into the cliff face. The patterns in the water are forming and dissolving. The sound is a continuous, layered, almost unbearable richness of small noises that never resolve into a single thing. The gorge is not a place you look at. It is a place you *stand inside*, and it presses against you from all sides—not physically, but informationally, sensorially, with a weight of happening that makes the ordinary world feel thin and slow by comparison.

And yet it is slow. The gorge changes, but it changes across durations that are longer than a human visit, longer than a human life. The water has been running for—how long? The structures suggest years, decades, perhaps longer. The rock is young but not *new*. The patterns in the water have been forming and dissolving in their recursive geometries for as long as the water has been flowing, which may be as long as the gorge has existed, which may be—

You don't know. The gorge does not tell you its age. It only tells you that it is *still becoming*. The walls are still weeping. The floor is still settling. The water is still finding new paths over the sharp, unweathered rock. The place is not finished. The place may never be finished. It is a landscape in the act of *arriving*, and it has been arriving for a long time, and the sound it makes—that dense, layered, choral, almost-living sound—is the sound of arrival without destination, of a place that is always almost what it will be, and never quite.
