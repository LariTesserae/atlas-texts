# The Looms Below

---

You descend.

The entrance is not dramatic — a spiral stair cut into living rootstock, the walls weeping continuously, a thin film of water sheeting down the root-bark and pooling in the carved grooves that serve as handrails. The air thickens immediately. Not with heat exactly, though it is warm — warm the way a compost pile is warm, the way a sealed room full of breathing things becomes warm — but with *saturation*. Every cubic centimeter of air is doing work. Carrying moisture. Carrying scent. Carrying the green-sweet almost-rot smell of total biological activity, the smell of a place that is metabolizing so intensely that the air itself has become a broth.

The sound reaches you before the space does. It is not loud in the way of machinery, though machinery is part of it. It is loud the way a rainforest is loud — a dense, layered, omnipresent *filling* of the auditory space. The drip and rush of water through a thousand channels. The creak and groan of growing things under tension — because the plants here are not passive, they are structural, they are holding up ceilings and spanning distances, and living tissue under load makes sounds, slow pops and fiber-stretches and the deep almost-subsonic creak of a root-arch bearing weight it was never meant to bear but has been bearing for so long that it has grown into the burden. And beneath and through all of this, the industrial sound: the rhythmic chug of water-powered pistons somewhere deeper, the whir of belt-drives, the clank and hiss of a small factory's worth of equipment that has been running, in some cases, for generations without stopping, because stopping would mean cooling and cooling would mean contraction and contraction would mean cracks in the water-seals and the whole thing would flood.

Because it is always about the water.

---

The stair ends. You step out onto a walkway — a horizontal root, thick as a bridge, planed flat on top and railed with woven tendrils — and the Looms Below open around you.

It is not large. That is the first thing to understand. The space is roughly the size of a cathedral, or perhaps a generous village square covered over. Maybe two hundred meters across at its widest, and the ceiling — which is not a ceiling but a dense mat of interlocking root-canopies from the forest above, thickened and deliberately cultivated into a living roof — hangs anywhere from eight to twenty meters above the floor, depending on where the root-swells bulge downward. It is a bounded space. You can see, if you look, where the walls are: not walls exactly, but the edges of the excavation, where the living city stops and the surrounding soil begins, held back by root-walls so dense and interwoven that they have become something like wood, something like stone, something that has not decided what it is because it is still growing.

The light is dim. It comes from below, mostly — bioluminescent panels cultivated along the walkways and walls, sheets of fungal tissue bred for brightness, casting a blue-green glow that makes everything look like it is underwater. Which, in a sense, it is. The air is so saturated that condensation runs constantly down every surface, and the fungal light refracts through the moisture on the air itself, giving the whole space a hazy, submerged quality, as though you are standing in very shallow, very warm sea. Some light comes from above too — thin shafts where the root-roof has gaps, letting down columns of warmer, amber light from the surface, and in these columns you can see the moisture turn to visible mist, the particulates of spores and pollen and root-dust drifting slow as snow.

The water is everywhere and in everything. It seeps from the walls in slow vertical trails. It drips from the root-roof in a constant irregular percussion — not rain, but the memory of rain, the forest above wringing itself slowly into the space below. It runs in deliberate channels carved along the floor, which is not flat but gently dished, so everything drains toward the central cistern, a dark pool maybe ten meters across that sits at the lowest point and glimmers with its own deep bioluminescence. The cistern is the heart of the place. From it, channels radiate outward, and along these channels the settlement is organized.

---

Because it is a settlement. Twenty, perhaps thirty thousand people live here. Maybe more — census is difficult when the architecture is alive and rooms grow and shrink over seasons.

The buildings are plants. This is the simplest way to say it and also the least adequate. They are plants the way a house is wood — technically true but missing the point. The point is that no one *built* them, not in the way we mean built. They were *grown*. Guided. Trained. Persuaded. The original founders — and no one quite agrees when that was, three generations back? Five? — discovered that certain deep-rooted species in the forest above could be coaxed downward, their root systems redirected, their growth patterns shaped by careful application of water and pressure and the mineral-rich effluent from the workshops. Over time, the roots thickened. Interwove. Hollowed in deliberate places where the inner tissue died and was cleared out, leaving rooms. The walls are living root — two, three meters thick in places, still carrying nutrients from the forest above, still growing, slowly, so slowly that you would not notice except that doorways get a little narrower each decade and ceilings creep a little lower and every generation the maintenance crews have to trim and redirect and negotiate with the architecture.

The rooms are small. That is what the density means here — not that the space is crowded, though it is, but that there is no wasted volume. Every gap between buildings has been filled with a corridor or a stair or a workshop or a garden, which is to say another plant, because all gardens here are load-bearing. Mushroom shelves grow from the walls at intervals, producing food and filtering water and providing the dim phosphorescent light, and they are structural — remove them and the wall weakens. The distinction between infrastructure and organism does not exist. The gutters are root-chutes. The plumbing is hollow-stemmed vine. The electrical conduits — because there is electricity here, water-powered turbines in the deep channels — are wrapped in living bark that insulates and protects and slowly, slowly tries to grow around them, so the electricians spend half their time cutting back the encroachment.

---

The workshops are where the industrial sound comes from, and they are the loudest thing in the Looms Below. Not because the machines are especially large — they aren't, the space doesn't permit it — but because sound bounces and lingers in the dense humid air and there is nothing soft enough to absorb it. Every surface is either living tissue (which creaks) or water (which rings) or the fungal panels (which hum faintly with their own biological processes). The workshops themselves are caverns in the root-mass, rooms where the walls have been deliberately hardened — a technique involving mineral-saturated water and a particular species of root that deposits something like lignite in its outer layers, creating a dark, dense, almost stone-like surface. In these rooms, the equipment runs.

There is a water-powered loom — the place's namesake, or one of them — that has been operating for so long that the root-floor around it has worn into grooves from the vibration. It weaves fiber from the root-walls themselves, a coarse strong textile that is the settlement's primary material for everything from clothing to curtains to the bags they use for water filtration. There is a small foundry, barely more than a crucible and a bellows, where they smelt the metals that wash down from the surface — the forest above is old and was once, stories say, the site of something else, something that left iron and copper in the soil, and the roots draw it up and it concentrates in the root-tips, which are harvested and processed. The heat from the foundry makes its neighborhood the warmest place in the settlement, and so that is where the drying rooms are, where the fungal panels are cultivated, where people go when they are sick, because warmth and humidity and the antifungal compounds released by certain root-species are the closest thing the settlement has to medicine.

The turbines are deeper, below even the main floor, in channels where the water flow is strongest. You can feel them as much as hear them — a deep vibration in the walkways, a thrumming in the root-railings, a pulse that is almost biological, almost like a heartbeat, and that is the sound that underlies everything, the sound of the place's metabolism made mechanical.

---

The walkways are the streets, and they are crowded, and they are beautiful in the way that things are beautiful when every element serves three purposes and nothing exists for aesthetics alone. The walkways are roots — always roots, everything is roots — and they branch and merge and ascend and descend in a three-dimensional web that connects every part of the settlement. They are wide enough for two people to pass, usually, and they are surfaced with a cultivated moss that provides grip when wet, which is always, and that is itself a food source for the tiny cultivated mites — not fauna, not really, the mites are more like mobile fungi, the one exception to the no-animal-life rule, though whether they count is debated — that clean the moss and are in turn harvested and pressed into a paste that is a primary protein source. Everything eats everything. The mites eat the moss. The people eat the mites. The waste feeds the moss. The water carries the nutrients. The roots carry the water. The people tend the roots. The circle is so tight it is almost a knot.

And the people are there — you see them as your eyes adjust to the dim fungal light. They move along the walkways with the particular gait of people who have lived their whole lives on slightly-yielding surfaces, a kind of unconscious ankle-flex that accommodates the living give of root-underfoot. They are clothed in the coarse root-fiber textile, and their skin has the particular quality of people who live in constant humidity — soft, well-hydrated, but also marked by the fungal spores that are inescapable here, faint patterns of colonization on their hands and forearms that they cultivate deliberately, certain species that provide protection against the more aggressive molds. They are, in a sense, gardening themselves. Participating in the ecosystem. The distinction between person and place is thinner here than it is on the surface.

---

It is slow here. That is the last thing, and perhaps the most important. Everything moves at the speed of growth. The changes in the settlement happen on the scale of seasons, of years, of generations. A new room takes a season to grow. A new walkway takes a year. A new building — a truly new structure, a new wing, a new branch of the settlement — takes a decade or more of careful persuasion, of redirecting root growth, of negotiating with the living architecture. The machinery runs constantly because stopping it would be catastrophic, not because it cannot be restarted but because the rhythms it sets — the vibration, the heat, the water flow — are woven into the biological rhythms of the place, and disrupting them would be like disrupting a heartbeat. The settlement is a steady-state system, a dynamic equilibrium between growth and decay, between the forest above pulling water and nutrients down and the settlement below processing and returning them, between the slow creep of root-growth and the patient maintenance of the crews who trim and guide and redirect.

Nothing is fast. The water drips at the rate of dripping. The roots grow at the rate of roots. The people move at the rate of people who know they have time, who have always had time, who live in a place that has been the same for as long as anyone remembers and will be the same, with variations, for as long as anyone can imagine.

The air is thick and warm and loud with the sound of water and growth and industry and the deep slow pulse of a living place that has found its balance and is holding it, patient and saturated and dense with life, in the wet dark below the forest floor.

---

You stand on the walkway. Water runs over your feet — warm, mineral-rich, faintly sweet. The fungal light plays on the condensation on your skin. Somewhere below, the turbines hum. Somewhere above, the forest drinks. And here, in between, the Looms Below continue their slow, loud, saturated work.
