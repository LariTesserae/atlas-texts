# The Drowned Nursery

You are standing inside a hollow.

Not a cave — a *hollow*, a pocket of air held in the throat of something vast and living. The space is perhaps forty feet across, roughly spherical, and every surface is root. Roots as thick as your forearm, pale and slick, woven so tightly they form walls, a ceiling, a floor that gives slightly under your weight like a drumhead. The roots are not dead wood. They pulse. Slowly — one subtle expansion every eleven seconds or so — a peristaltic rhythm that belongs to no single organism but to the whole structure, the way a murmuration belongs to no single starling.

It is cold. Not the clean cold of altitude or winter sky, but a *wet* cold, the kind that finds the gaps in clothing and settles into joints. The air is heavy with moisture. Condensation beads on every root-surface and drips in an irregular percussion — not rain, but the exhalation of the walls themselves. Water runs in thin sheets down the curved interior, collecting in channels that have been deliberately, if not consciously, carved into the floor-roots. The water is dark. It carries tannin, humic acid, the dissolved memory of everything these roots have consumed. It smells like a cellar and like a wound.

The light is almost absent. What exists comes from two sources: a faint bioluminescence in the root-tips themselves, a blue-green so dim it only registers when you've been still for several minutes and your eyes have abandoned the effort of seeking more, and a single shaft of grey daylight entering through a breach in the ceiling-roots about twenty feet up — a place where something tore through from the outside, long ago, and the roots healed around the wound but never fully closed it. That shaft illuminates a column of drifting particles: spores, mites, fragments of fungal hyphae, a slow blizzard of biological material suspended in the cold wet air.

The fauna is *everywhere*, and this is the thing that makes the space unbearable and sacred in equal measure.

The roots are not bare. They are coated — furred, almost — with a living carpet of organisms so dense that the root-surface itself is only intermittently visible beneath them. Beetles, primarily. Small, dark, with carapaces that gleam wetly in the bioluminescence. They move in slow, purposeful columns along the root-ridges, following chemical trails you cannot detect. There are so many that their collective movement produces a sound: not a rustle, not a click, but a *texture*, a continuous low whisper like someone breathing through cloth. Among the beetles: mites, pale and almost translucent, moving faster, erratic. Springtails that launch themselves in tiny arcs from root to root. Fungal bodies — not mushrooms but bracket-like growths the color of old bone — that have rooted into the root-bark and are slowly, slowly digesting it, and the root is slowly, slowly growing around them, and this negotiation has been ongoing for longer than you can conceive.

And there are the worms. Thick, pale, segmented things that live in the water-channels, half-submerged, their bodies breaking the dark surface in slow undulations. They are the size of your finger and the length of your forearm, and they are *numerous*, and when you shift your weight and the floor-roots flex, they all move at once, a synchronized ripple that travels the length of every channel.

The space is not silent, but it is quiet in the way that a body is quiet — the sounds are internal, biological, close. Drip. Whisper. The faint creak of root-fiber under tension. The almost-subsonic thrum of the pulse. Your own breathing, which seems obscenely loud.

This hollow is not natural. Or rather: it is natural the way a termite mound is natural, the way a beaver dam is natural. It was *built*, but not by hands. The root-architecture is too deliberate for accident — the channels, the drainage, the way the ceiling curves to shed condensation toward the walls rather than letting it fall on the floor. Something grew this. Something with intention but without consciousness, or with a consciousness so alien that intention is the wrong word. A fungal network, perhaps, directing root-growth through chemical signaling over centuries. Or the roots themselves, self-organizing, solving the problem of water and cold and structural load the way a river solves the problem of gravity — not by thinking, but by *being*.

You are not the first animal to find this place. In one corner, half-hidden by a curtain of hanging root-fibers, there is a nest. Not a bird's nest — something larger, something mammalian, constructed from shredded root-bark and beetle-carapaces and a dark fibrous material that might be fur. It is empty now. It has been empty for some time. But the space around it is *clean* in a way the rest of the hollow is not — the beetles avoid it, the mites do not cross its threshold, and the root-surface beneath it is bare and pale, as though something about the nest's occupant was anathema to the living carpet, or as though the occupant maintained its space with a fastidiousness that outlasted its presence.

The cold is getting into you. The moisture is on your skin, in your hair, finding the seams of whatever you're wearing. The bioluminescence is slightly brighter now, or your eyes are slightly more adapted, and you can see that the root-walls are not uniform — there are knots, bulges, places where the root-mass has grown around and incorporated foreign objects. A stone. A bone. Something metallic, green with corrosion, too far gone to identify. The hollow is collecting things. Growing around them. Making them part of itself.

Outside — and you can feel this through the root-walls, through the pulse, through the way the water-channels all slope toward a single low point where the dark water seeps away into something larger — outside, the world is vast and wet and cold and alive, a low landscape of marsh and fen and standing water under a sky that has not been clear in weeks, and this hollow is a single cell in its body, a pocket of held breath, a place where the overwhelming life of the world has concentrated itself into a space small enough to stand inside.

The beetles continue their columns. The worms undulate. The roots pulse. The water drips.

You are inside something that is not finished with you yet.
