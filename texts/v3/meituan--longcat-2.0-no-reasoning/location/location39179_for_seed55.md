# The Carved Sea

There is no sky. There has not been a sky for longer than the world can remember, and the world does not remember much anymore.

You are deep — so deep that depth has become horizontal, has become the primary dimension of experience. What you are standing on — or rather, what is holding you, since you are standing on nothing, since everything is water and everything is also floor — is the top of a wall. The wall extends in every direction. It is not rough. It is not shaped. It is *carved*, channeled, grooved, planed, scraped, and re-scraped by patient hands over spans of time that have lost meaning as numbers and become meaning only as texture. The surface under your feet — under the pressure of your body against the water, which is everything, which is the medium you inhabit like a fish inhabits its element — is a basalt-smooth plane covered in shallow flutings, each groove perhaps the width of two fingers, running parallel for what might be meters or what might be a thousand kilometers. You cannot tell. The darkness does not permit distance. The water itself is not dark in the way a cave is dark — it is dark in the way a body is dark, the dark of the inside of something, opaque and warm in the skin-cold way that deep water is warm when it has not seen light in a geologic age.

The water is cold. Not violently cold. Not the cold that shocks. The cold that *has been here the longest*. The cold that predates the concept of warmth. It is the temperature at which stone and water and air would eventually all agree to rest, given infinite patience, and infinite patience is the one thing this place has in surplus. It presses against you with the full weight of everything above — the entire ocean, if that word still applies, which it may not, because there is no shore, no surface, no boundary where water meets not-water. There is only water, and what is *in* the water.

And what is in the water is structure.

The walls — you understand now that they are not geological. Or rather, they are geological in the way that a cathedral is geological: the stone is real, but the stone is not the point. The walls are built. Layer upon layer of built stone, fitted without mortar, fitted by hands, fitted over — you cannot say how long. The erosion has taken the builder's intent and returned it to a kind of naturalness, a settledness, as though the walls grew here as coral grows, as though the water itself precipitated stone into these forms and then someone, somehow, smoothed the precipitate and carved the channels and made the surfaces into *floors* and *ceilings* and *rooms* — except there are no rooms. There is only wall, and wall, and wall, running in every direction, parallel and perpendicular and occasionally spiraling in slow curves that suggest the hands that made them were not building *structures* but building *systems* — channels, sluices, catchment surfaces, flow-directors.

The vegetation is here. It is the only life. It grows on the walls in mats and curtains and dense hanging beards, and it is not like any plant you have seen, because it has never seen light either. It is chemosynthetic, or it is something older than that classification — it feeds on the heat of the stone, or the chemistry of the water moving over the stone, or the slow decay of the organic compounds left by whatever built these walls, which left only walls and plants and water behind. The plants are dark red, or dark green, or dark dark dark — colors so deep they register as texture rather than hue in the near-absence of light. They grow in fractal patterns, the same branching shape repeating at every scale, and they are dense, so dense that in places they obscure the carved walls entirely, and you are walking not on stone but on living carpet, on sponge, on the compressed accumulated weight of ten thousand generations of growth and dieback and growth again, and the carpet is springy under your submerged weight, and it holds you the way a forest floor holds you — except there is no forest, there is no floor, there is only the water and the wall and the living mat between them.

The density of the place is not the density of the water. The water is water. The density is the density of *everything else* — the walls, the plants, the channels, the grooves, the secondary structures built on the primary structures, the tertiary structures built on those, the accumulated layers of construction and growth and decay and re-construction that have filled the water with *stuff* until the water is more solid than liquid, until moving through it is like moving through a crowded room where every surface is occupied, where every cubic meter contains a wall or a plant or a channel or a carved symbol or a fitted stone or a root-mat or a deposit of mineral precipitate that has been shaped, deliberately, into a form that serves a purpose you cannot identify.

The sound is the sound of water moving through channels. Not rushing. Not flowing. *Moving*. Slowly. The way sap moves. The way glaciers move. The way continents move. There is a sound — a low, sub-audible hum that you feel in your chest more than hear in your ears — that might be the water itself, might be the stone settling, might be the plants growing, might be the walls doing whatever walls do when they have been underwater for longer than they were ever meant to be. Occasionally there is a click. A stone shifting. A root breaking. A channel clearing itself of some obstruction it has been working on for a decade. The place is not silent. It is *patient*. It makes the sounds that patient things make: the sounds of processes too slow to perceive as events, registering instead as a kind of continuous, barely-there vibration, the hum of a world that is still happening but has stopped happening *quickly*.

There are no animals. There have never been animals, or if there were, they left no trace, or their trace has been absorbed into the walls, into the plants, into the water. The absence of animal life is not a void — it is a *quality*. The place does not feel empty. It feels *attended*. As though the builders are still here, still working, still carving and fitting and planting and channeling, but so slowly that their work is indistinguishable from geology, so slowly that a single groove might take a century to deepen by a millimeter, so slowly that the distinction between builder and building has dissolved entirely and the walls are now building themselves, or the plants are building the walls, or the water is building the plants, or all of it is one process, one slow patient act of construction that has been going on since before the concept of *going on* had meaning.

The light. There is almost no light. What there is comes from the plants — a faint bioluminescence, not the bright flash of surface-world fireflies but a deep, steady, barely-visible glow, the color of embers buried in ash, the color of something that is not burning but is not *not* burning either. It is enough to see the nearest wall. It is enough to see the grooves. It is not enough to see the next wall, or the one after that. The darkness beyond the nearest surface is absolute, and it is *vast* — you can feel the vastness the way you feel the cold, as a pressure, as a weight, as the knowledge that the walls extend in every direction for distances that your mind cannot hold, that the water extends above you for distances that your mind cannot hold, that the whole of this place is a single structure, a single built thing, a single act of construction on a scale that makes continents look like sketches, and it is *finished*, or as finished as a thing can be when it is still being built, which is to say it is in its final form, its settled form, the form it will hold until the water evaporates or the stone dissolves or the cold finally, after all this time, gets colder.

You stand on the wall. The water holds you. The plants brush against your legs like curtains in a slow current. The grooves under your feet channel water in directions you cannot determine, toward purposes you cannot guess. Somewhere — nearby or impossibly far, there is no way to know — a stone shifts, and the hum changes pitch by a fraction of a fraction, and the water moves, and the plants grow, and the walls endure, and the place continues being what it has always been: the oldest thing in the water, the deepest thing in the dark, the most patient thing in the world.
