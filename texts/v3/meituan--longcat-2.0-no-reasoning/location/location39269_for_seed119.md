# The Dripworks

You are standing in a place that should not be able to hold water, yet does.

The floor beneath you is a lattice — hand-laid, ancient, made of something between fired clay and bone. Each tile is hexagonal, roughly the span of two cupped palms, and they interlock with a precision that suggests generations of the same hands returning to the same work. The gaps between them are deliberate, maybe a finger's width, and from every gap, water is rising. Not pouring. *Rising.* It beads up through the seams in fat, trembling globules that cling to the edges of the tiles before sliding down into the dark spaces below. The sound is the first thing that truly registers: a constant, overlapping *plink-plink-plink* from ten thousand points, like a room full of clocks all ticking at slightly different speeds, layered over a deeper gurgling that comes from somewhere far beneath the floor. It is loud. It is the loudest quiet place you have ever been.

The air is thick with it. Not rain — there is no sky here, or if there is, it is so far above that the concept has become theoretical. The ceiling, if it can be called that, is a vaulted construction of interlocking wooden beams, dark with moisture, disappearing into a haze of condensation that hangs at head height like a low fog. Droplets form on every surface and fall in slow, lazy arcs, so that you are always being touched by small, cold impacts — on your shoulders, the back of your neck, your upturned palms. The light comes from somewhere in this haze: a diffuse, directionless amber glow, as if the moisture itself is faintly luminous, or as if there are lamps burning behind screens of wet cloth somewhere in the murk. It is enough to see by. Barely.

The space is not large. You can sense the walls — or rather, the *sides* — maybe forty paces in any direction, though the fog makes distances uncertain. The walls are built of stacked stone, mortared with something pale and chalky that has been stained in long vertical streaks by centuries of water running down them. The stones are irregular, chosen and fitted by hand, and in places they have been carved with shallow channels — gutters, essentially — that direct water in deliberate paths down to the floor lattice. Someone designed this. Someone maintained it. The channels are clean. They have been recently cleared of moss and mineral buildup, and the water in them runs in thin, quick ribbons that catch the amber light.

And then there are the animals.

They are everywhere, and they are *wrong*, but wrong in a way that feels intentional, as if wrongness is simply what lives here. The first ones you notice are the size of large rats, but they have too many legs — six, eight, ten — and their bodies are smooth and pale, almost translucent, so that you can see the shadow of their organs moving beneath the skin. They skitter across the lattice floor without breaking stride, their many feet finding the dry ridges between the wet gaps with an ease that suggests they have never known any other terrain. They are not afraid of you. They flow around your feet like water around stones, and they make a sound — a dry, papery rustling, like old leaves being shuffled — that adds another layer to the acoustic density of the place.

Higher up, clinging to the wooden beams, are things that might be bats if bats were the size of your thumb and had shells instead of wings. They hang in clusters of twenty or thirty, their carapaces clicking softly against each other as they shift and resettle. When one drops from the beam, it does not fall — it *glides*, on wings that are more like pleated fans, and it lands on the wall, or on a stone channel, or once, briefly and coldly, on the back of your hand before launching again. They are hunting the condensation. They skim the air at chest height, mouths open, drinking the fog.

In the water that pools in the channels along the walls, there are eels. Or eel-things. They are black and thick as a wrist, and they move with a muscular, purposeful undulation, traveling the channels like roads. They have small, bright eyes — too many eyes, arranged in a line along each side of the head — and when they surface, they break the water with a soft, wet sound that is almost a whisper. They are watching you. They are all watching you.

The temperature is cool — not cold, but the kind of cool that settles into your joints and makes you aware of your own body. Your breath does not quite fog, but it wants to. The air tastes of mineral and wood and something faintly organic, like the smell of a root cellar, or a cave where animals have denned for a long time.

There is evidence of habitation, but not recent habitation — or rather, not *human* habitation, or not habitation in any way you fully understand. Along one wall, set into the stone, are niches — small, arched recesses, each about the size of a loaf of bread. Some of them hold objects: a coil of fiber rope, darkened with age. A stack of flat stones, each one drilled with a single hole. A bundle of dried plant material — reeds, perhaps, or something like reeds — tied with a cord made of twisted sinew. In one niche, there is a tool: a short handle of polished wood fitted with a blade made of something that is not metal — flint, or obsidian, or a dark glass — and the edge is still sharp. It has been used recently. There are pale residues on the blade that might be scale, or skin, or something from the eel-things in the channels.

The water is the engine of this place. Everything turns on it. The lattice floor is not merely a floor — it is a *system*. The water rising through the gaps feeds the channels in the walls, which feed the pools, which feed the eel-things, which feed the shell-bats, which feed the many-legged skitterers, which — and here the chain becomes unclear — feed something else, something you have not yet seen but can sense in the way the smaller creatures orient themselves, the way they pause and turn their heads (or head-analogues) toward a particular section of wall where the stone is darker, wetter, and the channel there descends into a hole that is just large enough for a body to pass through.

The hole is breathing. You can feel it — a slow, rhythmic pulse of warm, moist air that pushes out and pulls back, out and pulls back, like the respiration of something very large and very old that is sleeping, or waiting, just below the floor.

The place is in balance. That is the overwhelming impression. Every element is in relation to every other element. The water feeds the channels, the channels feed the creatures, the creatures maintain the channels (you can see the skitterers clearing debris from the gutters with their many legs, a behavior so consistent it must be instinctive, or trained, or *designed*), and the system sustains itself with a closed-loop elegance that no single mind could have planned but that many minds, or many generations, could have grown into.

It is strange. It is deeply, fundamentally strange. But it is not chaotic. It is not broken. It is a place that has been *tended*, and the tending has a logic, and the logic has a purpose, and the purpose is — 

The warm air from the hole pulses again, stronger this time, and the eel-things in the channels all turn in unison toward it, and the shell-bats click and resettle on their beams, and the skitterers pause in their work and lift their translucent heads, and the water in the lattice floor rises slightly — just slightly — so that the gaps between the tiles fill to the brim and hold there, trembling, as if the entire floor is holding its breath.

The purpose is *down there*.
