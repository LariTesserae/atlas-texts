# The Glowing Hollows

Beneath a low ceiling of pale, sweating stone, the air is thick and warm — not hot, but close, like breath held in a closed mouth. The light is extraordinary. It comes from everywhere and nowhere: a diffuse, sourceless radiance that seems to seep from the walls themselves, from the mineral veins threading the rock, from the shallow pools that gather in every depression. It is bright enough to read by, bright enough to make the water shimmer, but it casts almost no shadows. The world is flattened by it, made strange and luminous, as though the darkness has been pressed thin and stretched across everything like a membrane.

The ceiling is low — perhaps four meters at its highest, lower in most places, forcing a tall person to duck. It is not a cave in the dramatic sense. There are no grand chambers, no stalactite cathedrals. Instead, the space is a warren of small rooms connected by short passages, each room no larger than a modest dwelling, each one distinct. The walls are smooth in some places, rough in others, but everywhere they are old. The stone has the settled look of something that finished changing a long time ago — no fresh fractures, no active drips building new formations. The erosion here is complete, or nearly so. The water has done its work and moved on, or settled into a quiet equilibrium, and the rock has accepted its final shape.

And yet the water is still here, in its way. It beads on the ceiling in fat, slow droplets that never quite fall. It films the walls in patches, making them slick and dark. It pools in the floors — shallow, still, perfectly clear — and in these pools the light concentrates, becoming almost liquid itself, a bright syrup that seems denser than the air above it. The humidity is high enough that your skin never quite dries. Your clothes stay damp. Your hair curls. After a few minutes you stop noticing, the way you stop noticing your own breathing.

The fauna is the first thing that truly unsettles.

They are small. They are everywhere. They are not quite like anything.

In the pools, things move — translucent, many-legged, no longer than a finger joint. They are not insects. They are not crustaceans. They have too many segments, or too few, or their segments shift as they move, as though their bodies are still deciding on a final form. They are pale, almost white, and they glow faintly — not bioluminescence exactly, but something adjacent, as though they are absorbing the ambient light and re-emitting it at a slightly different frequency, a shade warmer, more golden. When they cluster, the water around them brightens. When they scatter, the pool dims. The light here is partly alive.

On the walls, other things cling. Flat, dry, leaf-shaped organisms that might be lichen or might be something else entirely. They are the color of old parchment, and they are warm to the touch — warmer than the air, warmer than the stone. They pulse, very slowly, a rhythm so gradual you feel it more than see it, a faint expansion and contraction like the breathing of something vast and patient. Where they grow thickest, the wall texture changes, becomes softer, almost organic, and the boundary between stone and living thing blurs.

In the air, occasionally, something drifts — a filament, a thread, a tiny ribbon of tissue that floats on the warm convection currents rising from the pools. They have no visible means of propulsion. They simply move, slowly, purposefully, from room to room, and when they touch a wall they adhere and begin, over hours or days, to grow into the flat leaf-things. The lifecycle is visible if you stay long enough. Everything here operates on a timescale just slow enough to be maddening.

The silence is not absolute. There is a sound — a very low, almost sub-audible hum that you feel in your chest more than hear with your ears. It might be the water. It might be the stone. It might be the organisms themselves, the aggregate pulse of millions of tiny living things breathing in unison. Occasionally there is a soft *tick* — a droplet finally releasing from the ceiling, striking a pool, sending out ripples that catch the light and throw brief, bright rings across the walls. These sounds are rare enough to be startling. Each one resets your sense of time.

The strangeness accumulates slowly. It is not any single thing. It is the combination — the light without source, the warmth without fire, the life without obvious ecology, the water without rain. Nothing here eats anything else, as far as you can tell. The many-legged pool-things do not hunt. The wall-things do not decay. The drifting filaments do not seem to compete for space. There is no predation, no rot, no visible death. The organisms simply *are*, persisting in their slow, luminous cycles, and the question of what sustains them — what energy feeds this warm, bright, damp little world — has no obvious answer.

The stone itself may be part of it. The mineral veins that thread the walls glow brightest, and the organisms cluster near them most densely. Perhaps the rock is slowly releasing something — heat, radiation, some chemical energy — and the entire ecosystem is built on this geological exhalation, this quiet breath of the deep earth. The pools may be condensers, gathering moisture and dissolved minerals from the air, concentrating them, feeding them back to the wall-things, which in turn process them into something the pool-things can use. A closed loop. A self-sustaining glow.

You are the only large thing here. The only thing that moves quickly, that breathes hard, that casts a shadow — faint, but present, the only real shadow in this place. The organisms do not react to you. They do not flee or approach. They simply continue their slow business, indifferent, as though you are just another feature of the hollows, a temporary warm spot, a brief disturbance in the humidity.

The passages between rooms slope gently downward. The air gets warmer as you descend. The light gets brighter. The pools get larger. The organisms get more numerous, more varied, more *confident* — their colors shift from pale white to amber to a deep, rich gold that is almost orange. In the deepest room you find, the largest pool is perhaps ten meters across, perfectly still, perfectly clear, and so bright that looking into it is like looking into a pane of yellow glass. The many-legged things swarm in it in their thousands, and the light they generate is enough to read by, enough to warm your face, enough to make the whole room feel like a lantern.

At the far edge of this pool, half-submerged, there is a shape in the wall. It might be a natural formation — a fold in the stone, a mineral deposit. But it is regular. It is symmetrical. It has the rough proportions of a face, or a hand, or something that was once a face or a hand before the stone grew over it, slowly, over centuries, the way the wall-things grow, the way everything here grows — patiently, incrementally, in the warm and the wet and the light.

You do not touch it. The hollows are not hostile, but they are not yours. They were here before you arrived and they will be here after you leave, glowing in their slow, strange way, breathing their warm breath, tending their quiet, luminous life in the dark beneath the world.
