# The Glimmering Below

---

You are standing in a place that should not exist, and yet it insists on itself with the quiet confidence of something that has been here longer than the concept of "should."

The ceiling — if it can be called that — is a membrane. Not rock, not soil, but something between: a translucent, faintly pulsing tissue that filters light from somewhere above into a diffuse, almost surgical brightness. The light has no obvious source. It simply *is*, the way humidity simply is, saturating every surface equally, casting no shadows because shadows require a direction and this light has forgotten what direction means. It is the brightness of a thought you can't quite finish.

Beneath your feet, the ground is coming into being. You can feel it — not the solidity of finished earth, but the tentative commitment of material that has only recently decided to be solid at all. The surface is a lattice of mineral threads, hair-thin filaments of something between crystal and root, woven into a mesh that holds your weight but yields slightly, like stepping on a sleeping animal's flank. Between the threads, gaps open into darkness — not deep darkness, just the suggestion that below this layer there is more layering, more tentative structure, more geology still arguing with itself about what it wants to become. In places the mesh has thickened into plates, pale and smooth, like the skin of a healing wound. In other places it has not yet decided, and your foot sinks through into a brief, unsettling nothing before finding the next tentative shelf.

Water appears without system. A bead forms on an overhang of mineral-thread and swells to the size of a thumbprint before releasing — not falling, exactly, but *departing*, drifting sideways in a slow, deliberate arc to land on a surface three feet away, where it is absorbed instantly, as if the ground is thirsty in a way that has nothing to do with thirst. Condensation beads on every horizontal surface in tiny, perfect spheres that never quite merge, each one holding a miniature, inverted image of the bright membrane above. Occasionally a thread of water will trace a path down a filament, following it like a road, pooling in a knot of crossing threads, and then — without overflowing — simply stopping, as if it has reached its destination and is now content to be still.

The air is cool in the way that coolness exists before temperature was invented — not cold, not warm, but the specific coolness of a place that has never been warm and doesn't know it's missing anything. It carries moisture without being wet. Your skin registers it as a presence, a slight tightening, the way a room feels when someone has just left it.

And then there are the animals.

They are everywhere, and they are wrong, and they are beautiful.

The first thing you notice is movement at the edge of vision — a skittering, a fluttering, a slow undulation — but when you turn to look, the thing you've seen has already become something else, or has moved in a direction that doesn't correspond to the space it occupied. Small creatures, mostly. Things with too many legs or the right number of legs arranged in geometries that don't quite resolve. A beetle the color of static, its carapace flickering between patterns — hexagons, then spirals, then something that might be a map of this very place, then hexagons again. It walks along a mineral thread with the careful precision of a tightrope artist, and where its feet touch the thread, the thread briefly brightens, as if the contact is a transaction.

There are flyers, too — though "flying" is generous. They move through the air the way fish move through water, with a liquid, continuous motion that doesn't require wings, though some of them have wings, or wing-like structures, or structures that become wings when observed directly and become something else — fins, perhaps, or hands — when seen peripherally. They are translucent, these flyers, and in the bright sourceless light they cast no shadows but instead cast *refractions* — small, mobile rainbows that slide across the mineral ground and the membrane ceiling like living stains.

On the ground, larger things move slowly. A creature like a pill bug the size of a fist, but segmented in a way that suggests it could be unrolled into a line and rolled back into a sphere and that these are not two different creatures but the same creature in two different moods. It grazes on something — a film of moisture, perhaps, or the mineral threads themselves, or something invisible that lives in the space between the threads. When it feeds, it makes no sound, but the light around it dims slightly, as if it is consuming illumination along with whatever else it eats.

There are predators here, or something like predators. A shape that moves through the mesh itself, beneath the surface, visible only as a distortion — a darkening of the threads above it, a slight depression in the ground, a wake of disturbed moisture. It is large relative to the other fauna, perhaps the size of a cat, but its shape is difficult to hold in memory. You see it for a moment — elongated, many-limbed, purposeful — and then it passes beneath a thicker plate of mineral and is gone, and you are not entirely sure you saw it at all, only that the smaller creatures scattered in a pattern that suggested something had moved among them.

The ecology is active in ways that feel accelerated, compressed, as if the entire food web is operating on a faster clock than the one you're used to. Things eat and are eaten in cycles you can almost track — the grazers feeding on the film, the flyers feeding on the grazers, the sub-mesh predator feeding on the flyers — but the transitions are too quick, too fluid, as if predation here is less an event and more a continuous process, a gradient of consumption that never quite resolves into the discrete act of one thing killing another.

And scattered through all of this — among the threads, between the plates, half-submerged in the pools of standing water — are structures. Small ones. A pillar of mineral, no taller than your knee, that rises from the mesh with the deliberate symmetry of something built rather than grown. It is smooth where everything else is textured, and its surface is covered in fine lines that might be writing or might be the same kind of pattern that flickers across the beetle's carapace. Nearby, a low wall — three stones, or three mineral growths, arranged in a line with the precision of intention. Beyond that, what might be a doorway: two uprights and a lintel, framing nothing, leading nowhere, or leading somewhere that is not accessible from this angle of reality.

These structures are old, or they are new, or they are both. They have the weathered quality of things that have existed for a long time, but they also have the unfinished quality of things that are still being decided. Some of them are partially absorbed back into the mesh, mineral threads growing over and through them, as if the place is slowly reclaiming its artifacts. Others seem to be emerging *from* the mesh, as if the ground is extruding them, building them from below in a process too slow to watch but too persistent to ignore.

The silence is not empty. It is the silence of a place that is full of quiet sounds too small to hear individually — the tick of mineral threads adjusting, the almost-inaudible pop of a water bead releasing, the sub-audible hum of the membrane ceiling, the soft percussion of too-many legs on crystalline ground. Together they form a texture, a sonic equivalent of the visual texture of the mesh: dense up close, smooth from a distance, and always, always slightly more than you can fully perceive.

The weirdness is not dramatic. It does not announce itself. It simply *is*, the way the light simply is, the way the water simply appears. It is in the way that distances seem to change when you're not looking directly at them — a structure that was twenty feet away is now ten, or thirty, and neither measurement feels more correct than the other. It is in the way that the fauna sometimes move in ways that don't conserve momentum, starting and stopping without acceleration, changing direction without turning. It is in the way that the ground sometimes feels like it is breathing — not rising and falling, but *pulsing*, a subtle expansion and contraction that might be geological or might be biological or might be something for which no category exists.

You are in a pocket. A small place, contained, intimate — perhaps fifty feet across, perhaps less, perhaps more, the boundaries uncertain because the membrane ceiling curves down at the edges and becomes wall becomes floor in a geometry that doesn't distinguish between these things. You are below the surface of something, embedded within it, in a space that is still becoming a space, still negotiating with itself about what kind of place it wants to be.

And it is bright. Impossibly, uniformly, sourcelessly bright. The kind of brightness that makes you aware of your own eyes, of the act of seeing, of the strange miracle that photons striking a surface can become a world.

Something small and many-legged walks across your foot. You don't flinch. The place doesn't require flinching. It requires only attention, and the willingness to let it be exactly as it is: unfinished, alive, luminous, and quietly, persistently impossible.
