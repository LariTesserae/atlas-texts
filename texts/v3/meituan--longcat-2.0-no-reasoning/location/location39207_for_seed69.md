# The Drowning Plateaus of Vethara

---

You stand at the edge of a world that has been weeping for longer than memory.

The land here is not land in any settled sense. It is a vast, high plateau — or rather, a series of them, stacked and fractured across a continental expanse so large that the curvature of the horizon is a suggestion rather than a fact. The elevation is significant: the air is thinner than it should be for the warmth, and your lungs work slightly harder than expected. But the warmth is real, a thick, wet heat that clings to skin like a second membrane, and the water — the water is everywhere, not as rivers or lakes but as a condition of existence. The rock itself weeps. Every surface is damp. Condensation forms on stone faster than it can drip away, and the ground between the plateaus is a labyrinth of marsh and fen that stretches in every direction, broken only by the dark, angular rises of ancient basalt and limestone that have been here so long they seem less like geology and more like architecture.

The erosion is deep and patient. Millennia of water have carved the plateaus into forms that feel almost intentional — fluted columns, undercut arches, channels and gutters that direct the constant seepage into rivulets, then streams, then the slow, brown rivers that thread through the marshlands below. The rock faces are stained in bands of mineral color: iron-red, copper-green, sulfur-yellow, the black of manganese. In the darkness — and it is dark, profoundly dark, a light value of near-zero that makes the world feel like the inside of a closed mouth — these colors don't show as colors. They show as textures. You learn to read the rock by touch and by the sound your fingers make against it: the slick whisper of wet limestone, the gritty rasp of sandstone, the cold, almost metallic smoothness of water-polished basalt.

But it is not silent. It is not even close to silent.

The sound is immense. A constant, layered roar that resolves, if you listen long enough, into constituent parts: the hiss of condensation on every surface, a billion tiny impacts per second, like static made physical. The deeper groan of water moving through rock — not flowing but *percolating*, a subsonic rumble you feel in your sternum before you hear it. The marshlands produce their own symphony: the thick bubble of gas rising through anaerobic mud, the splash and rustle of things moving through shallow water, the high, almost electronic chirping of creatures you cannot see. And beneath all of it, threaded through everything, the industrial sound.

---

The machines are old. Not ancient — they are not ruins — but old in the way that a river is old: they have been here long enough to become part of the landscape, and the landscape has begun to digest them.

They are scattered across the plateaus in clusters and singletons, connected by cables that sag under their own weight and the weight of the water that coats them. Pumping stations, mostly. The water here is near the surface — sometimes at the surface — and it must be managed, directed, kept from overwhelming the places where the machines need to operate. Great iron pumps the size of buildings stand on the highest points of the plateaus, their pistons moving with a slow, hydraulic rhythm that you can feel through the ground. They are powered by geothermal taps — the high elevation sits atop volcanic activity deep below, and the steam that rises through fissures in the rock is captured, channeled, made to turn turbines that hum at a frequency just below hearing. You feel it as a vibration in your teeth.

There are refineries too, smaller, built into the eroded cavities of the limestone. They process the mineral-rich water — extracting copper, lithium, rare earths that have been concentrated by the endless cycle of dissolution and precipitation. The work is automated, has been for a long time. The machines maintain themselves with a slow, deliberate intelligence that is not quite mechanical and not quite biological. Gears are replaced by arms that might be robotic or might be something else. Pipes are repaired by growths that might be fungal or might be engineered. The boundary between the built and the grown has been blurred so thoroughly that it no longer exists.

The light — what little there is — comes from these installations. Not electric light, not exactly. Bioluminescent cultures grown in glass tanks along the refinery walls, fed by the mineral slurry, producing a dim, blue-green glow that barely reaches ten meters before the darkness swallows it. The effect is of floating islands of faint luminescence suspended in an ocean of black, each one a small world of its own, each one surrounded by the sound of water and the deeper sound of the machines.

---

The ecology is dense. Aggressively, almost oppressively dense.

The warmth and the water and the darkness have produced a biosphere that operates on principles that feel familiar in their components but alien in their arrangement. Photosynthesis, in the traditional sense, does not exist here — there is no light to photosynthesize. Instead, the base of the food chain is chemosynthetic: bacteria and archaea that feed on the mineral-rich water, on the sulfur compounds rising from below, on the chemical energy of the geothermal vents. They form mats — thick, rubbery, brightly textured mats that cover every surface not actively being eroded. In the faint bioluminescence, they appear as landscapes within the landscape: rolling hills of microbial life, forests of filamentous structures that wave slowly in the convection currents of warm air.

Above them, the fauna. Abundant, as the parameters demand, but not in the way of a sunlit forest or a coral reef. The animals of Vethara are adapted to darkness, to wetness, to the constant sound. They are small, mostly — the largest creatures are the size of a human torso — but they are *everywhere*. Arthropods with elongated limbs that move across the wet rock like living calligraphy. Mollusks that have abandoned their shells for flexible, muscular bodies that can squeeze into the narrowest fissures. Worms — vast, segmented, bioluminescent worms that move through the marsh water in braided columns, their collective glow creating the illusion of submerged lightning.

And the predators. There are predators. They are not large, but they are numerous and they are fast. Something like a centipede, but with too many legs and a body that seems to phase between solid and liquid as it moves through the water-saturated air. Something like a spider, but social, building communal webs across the gaps between plateaus — not to catch prey, but to catch *water*, to harvest the condensation that forms on the silk, to drink from their own architecture. Something that might be a mammal, or might be a very convincing imitation of one: warm-blooded, furred, with eyes that have not degenerated but *changed*, becoming broad, flat organs that detect infrared radiation, that see the heat-signature of living things against the cooler background of wet stone.

The weirdness is in the details. In the way the bioluminescent worms synchronize their glow in patterns that resemble language. In the way the microbial mats grow over the abandoned sections of the refineries and seem to *use* the metal structures as scaffolding, incorporating iron and copper into their metabolism until the machines become geological features, become *rock*, become part of the plateau itself. In the way the water sometimes moves uphill — not often, not dramatically, but enough that you notice, enough that you begin to question whether the rules of this place are the rules you brought with you.

---

The weather has its own weather. The scale is vast enough for that.

Warm air rises from the marshlands, carrying moisture, and meets the cooler air at the elevation of the plateaus. The result is a perpetual fog — not a mist, not a haze, but a dense, rolling fog that moves across the landscape like a slow-motion tide. It reduces visibility to meters. It makes the sound louder, because it reflects and refracts the noise, creates echoes that seem to come from impossible directions. Within the fog, the bioluminescent organisms activate — not in response to light, but in response to the increased humidity, the changed pressure — and the world becomes a constellation of dim, blue-green points, each one a living thing, each one surrounded by the dark.

Rain falls often. Not dramatically — there are no storms here, no lightning — but a constant, warm drizzle that is indistinguishable from the condensation, that makes the already-wet world fractionally wetter, that feeds the marshes and the microbial mats and the slow, patient erosion that has been shaping these plateaus since before the machines arrived.

---

There are structures. Scattered, as the parameters say. Small structures, faint marks, partial remains.

Not the machines — those are industrial, those are *present*. These are something else. Older, or newer, or simply *other*. Foundations of a material that is not stone and not metal, set into the rock in patterns that suggest habitation but not in any geometry you recognize. A wall, standing alone on a plateau edge, with no building attached, its surface covered in a script that might be writing or might be the trace of some biological process. A series of posts — wooden, impossibly preserved by the mineral-saturated water — arranged in a line that leads from the edge of a refinery into the marsh, and then beneath the water, and then is gone.

Someone was here. Someone built here. Someone left marks that the water and the dark and the slow growth of living things have not yet erased. But they are not here now, or if they are, they have become part of the density, part of the abundance, part of the strange and wet and loud and dark world that does not need them and does not remember them and continues, with its pumps and its worms and its weeping stone, in its own active, balanced, industrial, organic way.

---

The ground beneath your feet is warm. The water is rising — not flooding, just rising, the way it does here, the way it always does, the marsh reclaiming another centimeter of plateau, another machine slowly sinking into the mat of microbial life, another year of erosion fluting the rock into shapes that will outlast everything that has ever been built on this vast, high, wet, dark, living, mechanical, strange and ancient and ongoing place.
