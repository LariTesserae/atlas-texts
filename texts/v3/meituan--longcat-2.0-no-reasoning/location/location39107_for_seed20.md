# The Wound Baths of Cael-Voss

---

## The Water First — Because There Is No Avoiding It

You are standing in water. Not *on* it — in it. The water begins at your feet and does not end. It fills a basin of carved and broken stone, a vast irregular depression in the high rock, maybe two hundred meters across, walled on three sides by cliffs so young they look like they were made yesterday: no smoothing, no patience in their geology, just freshly-shattered faces of rust-red and bone-white mineral rising up in jagged verticals, still shedding flakes and pebbles. The fourth side of the basin opens — you can see it — toward a drop that goes further than sight can follow.

But the water. The water is the thing.

It is warm. Not hot — warm, like skin, like a mouth held open. A steady 38 degrees, maybe 40, the temperature of a body that is alive and slightly afraid. And it is *moving*. Not in waves. Not in currents. It is *organizing*. The surface — if you can call it a surface — is in a state of perpetual, deafening self-construction. Fountains erupt and collapse in half-second cycles. Columns of water rise a meter, two meters, then fold into themselves and become whirlpools that last three seconds, four, then invert into fountains again. Everywhere. Simultaneously. The entire basin is a machine of water building and unbuilding itself at a pace that should require engines but requires nothing — no tech, no mechanism, just water doing what water does when the rules are slightly wrong.

And the sound. **The sound.**

It is not loud. It is *deafening* — a different category entirely. It is the sound of every drop of water in the basin striking every other drop of water at every possible moment, layered, compressed, constructive-interferenced into a continuous roar that has texture. You feel it in your teeth. It is not unpleasant. It is *total*. It fills the space between your thoughts. You will not hear yourself speak. You will not hear your own breathing. The water has taken the air and replaced it with sound so dense it behaves like a solid. The roar has *pockets* — brief half-second silences that appear like bubbles of vacuum, then burst, and the sound rushes back in with a roar that seems to be *angry* about the interruption.

## The Geometry of the Place

The basin is small. Not *cozy* — *contained*. Cathedral-scale, as the prompt suggested, and the comparison holds: the proportions are sacred, deliberate-feeling, even though nothing here was designed by anything with intent. The walls rise 40, 50 meters on three sides. The open side is the drop. From the basin floor — which you cannot see, which is somewhere beneath the churning water — to the highest point of the cliffs is a vertical distance that feels *designed*, the way a pipe organ feels designed, the way a throat feels designed for swallowing.

The cliffs themselves are young. Aggressively, impossibly young. The stone has no history. No stratification you can read. No fossils. No lichen colonies that have been growing for centuries. Instead: raw faces of mineral, crumbling as you watch, the erosion happening in *real time* — pebbles loosening, sliding, dropping into the water with sounds swallowed instantly by the roar. The rock looks *wet* even above the waterline, as though the stone itself is sweating, as though the mineral is still deciding whether to be solid.

This is the youth of the place: nothing here has been *settled* into its final form. Everything is mid-process. The basin is still being carved. The cliffs are still falling apart. The water is still figuring out what shape to take.

And yet it has a coherence — the way a choir warming up has coherence before anyone starts singing the actual song. The place *knows what it is*. It is still becoming it.

## The Violation of Physics — Because Weirdness Is High and Honest

Here is where the description must stop pretending the world operates normally.

The water *breathes*. Not metaphorically. The entire basin rises and falls on a slow cycle — you can't feel it under your feet, but you can see it against the cliff faces. A tideline that climbs and retreats, climbs and retreats, over a period of roughly ninety seconds. But it is not a tide. There is no moon here. There is no ocean to pull from. The water is *exhaling* and *inhaling*, and the exhale produces those fountains, and the inhale produces the whirlpools, and the cycle is biological in rhythm but geological in scale.

There are places where the water is *vertical*. Not falling — standing. Walls of water, two or three meters tall, rising in curtain-shapes from the surface, holding their form for five or ten seconds. They are translucent. You can see through them — dimly, with distortion, like looking through old glass. They do not move laterally. They do not fall. They simply *stand*, and then they don't.

Sometimes a wall of water will curve. Will *reach* toward you. Will extend a limb of itself — there is no other word — across the surface, across the air, a bridge of liquid that defies the basin it came from, that seems to be testing whether it can leave. It never does. It reaches as far as it can, holds the stretch for a moment that feels like *consideration*, and withdraws.

The water has no algae. No plankton. No microorganisms that you could find with a microscope. It is chemically pure. H₂O. Nothing else. And yet it *moves like it has reasons*.

## What Grows — Almost Nothing, But It Matters

Vegetation is sparse. The scale is 0.7, which means: you will not miss it, but you will not find much.

There are things on the cliff faces that might be plants. They cling to the raw stone near the waterline, where the rock is still damp, where the spray from the perpetual eruption-cycle reaches. They are dark — not green. A deep red-black, almost maroon, the color of dried blood but with a sheen that is *wet*, always wet, as though their surfaces are coated in a film that cannot dry. They grow in shapes that suggest succulents — thick leaves, fat stems — but the geometry is wrong. The leaves spiral in patterns that don't match any phyllotaxis you know. Some of them appear to be *growing downward*, roots reaching up toward the sky, leaves buried in crevices. Some of them are moving — slowly, a rotation over minutes, a turning toward something you cannot identify.

There are no flowers. There are no seeds. There is no visible reproductive structure. The plants — if they are plants — appear to have simply *appeared*, as though the warm water and the young rock and the sound created them by accident, as a byproduct of being too alive to allow nothing else to live.

Below the waterline, nothing. No kelp. No coral. No biofilm. The basin floor — which you can't see — appears to be bare stone, clean as bone.

The sparseness of life here is not a *lack*. It is a *tension*. The place is warm, wet, mineral-rich, energetically chaotic — it should be *teeming*. The fact that it isn't is a louder statement than abundance would be.

---

## What Lives — Even Less

Fauna is rare. 0.7/3, matching vegetation — the place has a consistent, almost philosophical refusal to populate itself.

You have seen, so far, one thing: a creature that moved through the water near the eastern wall. It was pale, elongated, perhaps thirty centimeters long. It moved *against* the churn — against the fountains, against the whirlpool cycle — with a slow, deliberate undulation that suggested it was not struggling against the water but *arguing* with it. It had no visible eyes. It had a mouth, or something like a mouth — an opening at the leading end that opened and closed with the rhythm of the water's exhale, as though it was breathing the same air the water was releasing.

You did not see it clearly. The water is too active, too bright-with-sound, too chaotic. But you saw it move, and then you saw it not move, and it was gone, and you will not see it again. Or you will see another one. Or you will see something else entirely. The fauna here is not *absent* — it is *shy*. Or indifferent. Or so integrated with the water's motion that it is impossible to distinguish from the water itself.

There are no birds. The cliffs are too sheer, too unstable, too loud. Nothing nests here. Nothing perches. The sky — what you can see of it above the cliff walls — is empty.

## The Built Marks — Scattered, Faint, Possibly Imagined

There are structures. 1.1/3 — scattered, small, partial remains. They are embedded in the cliff walls on the western side, and they are *old* in a way that conflicts violently with the young geology around them.

Holes. Rectangular holes in the raw stone, too regular to be natural erosion, too irregular to be precisely engineered. They are perhaps a meter tall, half a meter wide, spaced at uneven intervals along the waterline. Some of them have ledges inside — shallow shelves carved into the back wall. Some of them have *marks* on the lintels — scratches, grooves, patterns that might be writing or might be the nervous habits of whatever made them.

No one built these recently. No one built them in a time that matches the stone's age. They are *older than the cliffs*. This is the central impossibility of the place: the basin is freshly formed, violently young, still carving itself — and yet it contains marks of habitation that belong to a different timeline entirely. The holes are not eroding. They are not crumbling. They are *patient*. They have been here longer than the rock that holds them, and they will be here after the rock is gone.

There is no other evidence of builders. No tools. No roads. No carved steps leading to the holes. The people — if they were people — either flew, or the landscape was different when they came, or they *were* the landscape, and the holes are the marks of something that lived inside the stone the way a parasite lives inside a host, and the stone is new, but the parasite is old, and the parasite made the holes before the stone existed.

This is not a comfortable thought. The water roars.

## The Light — Dim and Complicit

The sky is not dark. The sky is *uncertain*. The light source — sun, something else — is high and diffuse, as though filtered through a layer of cloud or water or distance that never clears. The light arrives at the basin floor already tired, already diminished, a pale amber that casts no shadows because it comes from everywhere and nowhere. It makes the water look thicker than it is. It makes the cliff faces look warm — warmer than the stone actually is, warm in a visual sense, a color-sense, the light lending the rock a blush of *almost-life*.

The water reflects the light poorly. It is too active, too broken up, to hold a mirror-image. Instead, it *scatters* the light in rapid flickers — strobe-like, subliminal, a constant sub-audible visual pulse that makes the basin feel like it is *blinking*. You do not notice this consciously. But if you stand still for long enough — five minutes, ten — you will begin to feel a nausea that is not physical but *perceptual*, a mismatch between what your eyes report and what your brain expects. The light is dim. The light is *wrong*. The light is part of the water's system, not separate from it.

At the waterline holes, the light behaves differently. It *pools*. It gathers in the rectangular openings with a density that the open air lacks, as though the holes are collecting it, saving it, using it for a purpose you cannot determine. Inside the holes, the amber light deepens to something closer to gold, and the shadows — yes, there *are* shadows inside the holes — are sharp and strange and cast by objects you cannot see from outside.

## The Dynamic State — This Is the True Subject

Everything described above — the water, the cliffs, the plants, the holes, the light — exists in a state of *turbulent becoming*. The dynamic parameter is 2.7/3. The place is not stable. It is not approaching stability. It is not *departing* from stability. It has simply never been stable, and the concept of stability does not apply.

The water cycle — exhale, fountain, inhale, whirlpool — is not regular. It is *rhythmic*, which is different. It has a pulse, but the pulse varies in amplitude and duration in a pattern that almost repeats, that teases you into predicting it, and then changes. You begin to anticipate the next fountain. You begin to map the whirlpools. You begin to think you understand the system. And then the system *shifts* — a new pattern emerges, or the old pattern returns with one element changed, or everything stops for a full second (silence — the most terrifying sound in the world) and then restarts with the water spinning in the opposite direction.

The cliffs are *growing*. Not quickly. Not visibly, in the way you can watch a fountain. But you can see the evidence if you look: a ledge that was ten centimeters wide is now eight. A crack that ran from floor to ceiling has *widened* since you arrived. The stone is falling apart at a rate that is slow by geological standards and *frightening* by human ones. The cliffs will not last. The basin will not last. The holes will last. The holes will outlast everything.

The plants are moving. The slow rotation you noticed — they are tracking something. Not the light. Not the water. Something else. Something *inside* the basin, or *beneath* it. They are turning toward a center of gravity that is not the center of the basin, that is *offset*, that may be moving, that may be the thing the pale creature was also tracking, the thing that is not the water but is *in* the water, the thing that makes the water breathe.

---

## The Sound — Again, Because It Cannot Be Left Alone

The sound is 3.0/3. Deafening. And it is not just volume.

The sound has *meaning*. Not language — not words, not syntax, not communication in any sense that a linguist would recognize. But the roar of the water contains *patterns* that your brain processes as information, as *message*, as something that is being *said* even though nothing is speaking. You cannot understand it. You cannot repeat it. But you know — standing in the warm water of Cael-Voss, surrounded by young cliffs and old holes and almost-nothing-life — you *know* that the place is *talking to itself*, that the water and the stone and the light and the faint biological traces are all part of a single utterance that has been going on for longer than the cliffs have existed and will continue after they are gone, and the utterance is not *about* anything, it is simply the sound of a place that has not yet decided what it is, trying itself out, testing configurations, building and unbuilding and building again, forever, in warm water, in amber light, in a basin that will not last, in a world that may not be a world, in a geometry that remembers builders who may never have been.

The sound is deafening.

You stand in it.

You are part of the utterance now.

---

## The Elevation — High, Final, and Meaningful

You are high. The basin sits at 1.6/3 elevation — not mountaintop, not cloud-level, but *high enough that the air is thin and the silence above is different from the silence below*. The sky above the cliffs is a pale gradient, darkening toward a zenith you can almost reach if you climbed the unstable walls. The world below — whatever is below, past the open side of the basin, past the drop — is invisible. Not obscured by cloud. Simply *gone*. The elevation is a wall in itself. You are here, in this basin, in this water, and the below is not accessible, and the above is not inviting, and the only place to be is *here*, in the warm, loud, churning now, while the cliffs fall and the water breathes and the holes wait for something that has already been and will be again.
