# The Mirengine

Below the skin of the world, the Mirengine runs — a built world of wet rooms and deliberate silence that isn't silent at all.

## Descent

You arrive through a culvert that wasn't built so much as *persuaded* — the walls are poured calciumite, like bone-white concrete but with a faint vascularity, tiny channels in the surface that wick moisture upward from the perpetual damp. It beads on the ceiling before it ever falls. The air thickens as you descend, warm as a held breath, 29°C and climbing. The culvert opens, and you stand on a gantry overlooking the first vault.

## The Vaults

The space is enormous but feels intimate, like standing inside a single carved tooth. Cathedral-scale, yes, but organized, subdivided — this is a city. The vault stretches maybe 80 meters across, its ceiling lost in darkness that your eyes can't negotiate because the light down here is not absent but *specific*: it comes in shafts and points from bioluminescent sconces that grow directly from the walls, colonies of engineered photobacteria housed in ceramic cups that someone, something, installed long ago. The light is amber-green, the color of deep water at dusk, and it falls in discrete cones, leaving vast pockets of true dark between them. You learn to navigate by these scattered illuminations, each one a small territory.

The walls are layered construction — this place was not built once but continuously. Original calciumite, then repairs in a darker stone that doesn't quite match, then panels of something metallic and warm to the touch, then the living accretion: mineral deposits, root-analogues from the photobacteria, and the *grouting*, that strange interstitial material that seals gaps between human-scale engineering and whatever the structure wants to become on its own. The grouting is pale and slightly translucent, like resin, and it's warm. It pulses. Not your imagination — the Mirengine breathes through its walls on a slow hydraulic rhythm, expanding and contracting by millimeters, and the grouting flexes to accommodate this. The whole settlement is a diaphragm.

## The Sound

And it is loud. Not cacophonous — *loud*, in the way a cathedral organ's pedal tone is loud. The Mirengine hums. The resonance comes from the water system: every surface runs with moisture, and the moisture is *channeled*. Thin streams course through grooves in the floor, converge into shallow canals that branch and rebranch through the vaults like a vascular network, and the water moves fast enough to sing. Not babbling — *singing*, a low continuous chord produced by the interaction of flow rate, channel width, and the resonant frequencies of the calciumite walls. The pitch varies from vault to vault. Some rooms drone at 40 Hz, felt more than heard. Others whine at the edge of audibility. You move through the city and the key changes. The locals — the few that remain — have told travelers that the Mirengine is tuning itself. That it has been tuning itself for longer than anyone has been here to listen.

Rain happens indoors. Not everywhere, not constantly — but the condensation is so heavy and the air so warm that thermal layers form, and where they meet, water falls in sheets from specific points on the ceiling. There are *rain rooms* you learn to walk around. The rest of the time, the damp is just present, a film on every surface, a weight in the air that makes your clothing heavy within an hour.

## The Living and the Rare

Fauna is sparse but not absent. There are *trackers* — flat, many-legged things the size of a handprint that move along the wall channels, grazing the photobacteria. They move in short, fast bursts and then freeze, and their bodies match the calciumite exactly when still, so you only see them in motion, a flicker of dark movement at the edge of a light cone, gone. There are fewer of them than there used to be. Someone — or something — keeps their numbers down.

In the deeper vaults, there are larger things. You'll hear them before you see them: a slow, wet displacement, like a body moving through standing water. The locals call them *druids* and won't elaborate. They are not hostile. They are not friendly. They are *there*, in the way a river is there.

Vegetation is limited to the photobacteria and a thin, tough lichen that grows along the canal edges where mineral content is highest. It's the color of dried blood and it crumbles at a touch. That's it. That's the green life of the Mirengine.

## The Technology

The built environment is the technology. The Mirengine is not a city that *has* infrastructure; the Mirengine *is* infrastructure. The calciumite walls are grown, not poured — seeded with a catalytic agent that encourages mineral deposition in specific structural forms. The water channels are not carved but *negotiated* — the flow is managed by a system of organic valves, muscle-like sphincters in the channel walls that open and close in response to pressure differentials, maintaining flow rates without any central control. The system is self-repairing. A crack in the calciumite heals in days. A blocked channel reroutes. The photobacteria cups are self-cleaning. The grouting re-grouts.

This is engineering that operates on principles adjacent to but not identical with what surface science would recognize. The catalytic seeding, the organic valves, the self-healing walls — they work. They work *well*. They have worked for centuries. But they don't map cleanly onto any materials science, any biology, any physics that the surface world has formalized. The Mirengine is a machine made of processes rather than parts. It is running, and it has been running, and it will keep running after the last person leaves. The question — the question that the locals won't answer directly — is whether the Mirengine was built *for* people, or whether people simply moved into something that was already functioning.

## The Weird

The unusual thing — the thing that sits just off-balance — is the way the city responds to occupation. Empty rooms seal. Their channels dry up, their grouting hardens, their photobacteria dim to nothing. They become indistinguishable from the raw stone. But occupied rooms — rooms where a body sleeps, where breath and heat and moisture are present — those rooms *accelerate*. The water flow increases. The photobacteria brighten. The walls exhale. The grouting softens and becomes more translucent, and through it you can see the faint vascular channels working, carrying fluid, carrying *something*, from the deep structure outward toward the occupied space. The Mirengine feeds its inhabitants. Or: the Mirengine is curious about its inhabitants. Or: the Mirengine is *cultivating* its inhabitants.

The locals don't discuss this. They live well enough. The air is warm and damp and breathable. The water, filtered through the channels, is clean. The light is sufficient. They grow food — small crops of something like mushrooms in the rain rooms, fed by the constant indoor weather. They are few, maybe forty in the first vault, fewer in the deeper ones. They are not unfriendly. They are *attentive*. They watch you with the same steady focus that the city itself seems to apply, and you cannot tell where one observation ends and the other begins.

## The Deeper Vaults

There are at least seven vaults below this one. The descent between them is a spiraling ramp, worn smooth by water and by the passage of things that are not human feet. The ramp is damp. The sound changes as you go — lower frequencies, fewer photobacteria, more darkness. By the fourth vault, the organic valves in the channels are larger, and you can hear them operating: a slow, wet *thump* at irregular intervals, like a heartbeat in a body the size of a building. By the sixth vault, the grouting on the walls is thick enough to obscure the calciumite entirely, and the walls are soft, and warm, and they pulse.

No one lives in the seventh vault. The locals say it is not empty.

The Mirengine hums. The water runs. The walls breathe. The light falls in its scattered cones, amber-green, insufficient, enough. Somewhere in the deep structure, something is tuning, and the chord is not yet resolved.
