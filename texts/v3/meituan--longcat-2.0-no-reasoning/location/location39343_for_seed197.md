# The Wailing Tendril-Knot

---

You are standing inside a cavity. Not a building's cavity—though it was one, once, or something close enough that the distinction has collapsed. The space is roughly the size of a small town's central square, or a large cathedral's nave, if cathedrals grew rather than were built. The ceiling is somewhere above you—not far, maybe thirty feet, maybe less, but you can't quite resolve it because the space is not empty in the way a room is empty. It is *absent*. Void. You look around and the nearest anything solid is twenty feet from you in every direction. The nearest anything at all is forty. And then the world begins.

What begins is vegetation, and the vegetation is not decoration.

---

The walls—let's call them walls, because the word is approximate but you need something—are tendrils. Not vines, not roots, not stems. Something that occupies the conceptual space where those words meet and cancels out each other's boundaries. They are thick as a man's torso, these tendrils, and they are *moving*, but so slowly that you watch one for ten minutes and see it has crept an inch toward the floor, or toward another tendril, or toward nothing in particular. They are the color of old wood that has been soaked and dried and soaked again for centuries—a brown so deep it reads as purple in certain light. Their surfaces are ridged with a texture like bark, like skin, like the corrugation of dried muscle. They do not grow from the ground. They grow from each other. Every tendril is a branch of some larger tendril that is itself a branch of a trunk that you have never seen, or that is too large to be called a trunk, or that is the concept of *structure itself* made organic.

And between the tendrils: nothing. The density of this place is almost zero. You stand in a cathedral-sized pocket of near-vacuum, and beyond you, in every direction, a sparse web of these enormous vegetative limbs stretches and twists and spirals across distances that your eye can't quite resolve. It is as if someone took a city—the *idea* of a city, the topology of one—and built its streets and buildings and bridges and aqueducts out of living tissue, and then *removed almost everything*, leaving only the connective framework. Roads of tendril. Overpasses of tendril. Archways that span voids so large the far wall is just a suggestion of brownish movement. There are tendrils overhead that cross and re-cross like the vaulting of a Gothic ceiling, but the gaps between them are so wide that the open sky—if there is sky—is visible in patches. And there are tendrils below you, forming a floor that is more like a net, and through the net you can see other tendrils below, and below those, darkness or distance or both.

You are standing on a tendril-floor. It holds you. It does not want to hold you, but it is strong enough that your presence is irrelevant to its structure.

---

The water is here but it is not falling. The air is damp in a way that feels *structural*, as though moisture is a building material the tendrils secrete. Droplet by droplet, the surfaces weep—slowly, almost politely—and the droplets do not fall so much as they *migrate*, sliding along the curves of the tendrils and merging with other droplets and growing fat and trembling and then, at some threshold you can't predict, releasing and drifting downward in no particular hurry. Some of them never reach the bottom. The air is thick enough with humidity that small pools form in the crooks where tendrils intersect, and these pools are cupped like hands and they are warm to the touch—not hot, not cool, just *warm*, a few degrees above the air, as if the water has been metabolized slightly before being released. The warmth has no obvious source. It is simply a property of this place that water is gently warm.

Rain happens, but intermittently and without clouds you can identify. The moisture just *accumulates* in the upper reaches of the tendril-structure until it reaches some critical density and then it falls, not as a storm but as a slow, dense misting that lasts an hour or two and then stops. During these episodes, the sound changes. We'll get to the sound.

---

Now: the sound. The sound is **deafening**. It is not loud in the way a waterfall is loud, or a highway, or a jet engine. It is loud in the way a *biome* is loud. Every living thing here is vocal, and there are living things *everywhere*, which contradicts the near-zero density but does not, because the tendrils are alive and the things that live on the tendrils are alive and the things that live on *those* things are alive, and the sounds they make overlap and stack and resonate through the hollow spaces until the air itself seems to vibrate with meaning.

Start with the base layer: a hum. Not mechanical—biological. The tendrils themselves produce a low, sub-audible thrumming that you feel in your sternum before you hear it in your ears. It is the sound of sap moving, or of cellular processes at scale, or of something for which no human analogy exists. It is a single note, or it seems to be a single note, but if you listen long enough you realize it has overtones—dozens of them, shifting, dissonant, resolving and then dissolving back into dissonance. This hum is constant. It is the bedrock of the soundscape. Without it, the place would be silent, and that would be worse.

Above the hum: the fauna. There are animals here. Many of them. They are not animals you would recognize, but they are *animals*—they move, they eat, they vocalize, they have the erratic energy of creatures with metabolisms and agendas. Some of them are small and live in the pools that collect at tendril-junctions. They are translucent, roughly the size of your thumb, and they sing. Not beautifully—in short, sharp, repetitive bursts that sound like a fingernail drawn across a comb's teeth. Each pool has dozens of these creatures, and each one is singing at a slightly different pitch, and the aggregate sound is like a miniature orchestra being played in a room full of echo. These are everywhere. There are pools every ten, twenty, thirty feet along the tendril-networks, and each pool is a concert.

Some of the fauna is larger. There are things that cling to the tendrils and move along them with the slow deliberation of sloths. They are covered in something between fur and moss, and they make a sound like breathing—a long, slow inhale that takes thirty seconds and a long, slow exhale that takes forty. Their breath has a rhythmic quality that is almost hypnotic, and because there are hundreds of them distributed across the visible structure, the breathing overlaps into a continuous wash of inhalations and exhalations that sounds like surf. Not ocean surf. The surf of some other substance, some other body, something wetter and denser and more *alive* than water.

And then there are the flyers. Not birds. Not insects. Something that is to those categories what the tendrils are to trees—occupying the same conceptual space but arriving there through a completely different path. They have wings, but the wings are too numerous, too irregular, too *plantlike* to belong to anything with a spine. They move through the hollow spaces in lazy spirals, and they produce a sound like wind chimes—not the gentle, decorative sound of wind chimes in a garden, but the sound of wind chimes in a hurricane, a cascading, overlapping, atonal chiming that never resolves into melody and never stops. There are enough of them that the chimes form a continuous tone, a shimmer, a *texture* of sound that fills every gap the other sounds leave open.

The total effect: a roar. A wall of sound. It is not painful—your ears adjust, or the sound adjusts to you, or something—but it is *total*. You cannot find silence. You cannot find a frequency that is not occupied. The hum, the singing, the breathing, the chiming, and underneath it all, the slow creak and groan of tendrils moving, growing, adjusting, settling. The sound of a living structure under living stress. The sound of an ecosystem that has been established so long it has *settled into its own acoustics* and now produces sound the way a geological formation produces weathering—not as an event but as a state of being.

---

The light is normal, which is strange, because there is no obvious source. The patches of open sky show a sky that is overcast, white, featureless, but the light that reaches the spaces between the tendrils is not dim. It is full, even, diffuse—the light of a bright overcast day, the kind of light that casts no shadows and reveals everything with flat, democratic clarity. But the tendrils themselves seem to *modify* the light that passes through the gaps. It is warm-toned by the time it reaches you. Amber. The color of afternoon, but without any sense of time-of-day, without any sense of *time at all*. The light has been here forever. It is a property of the place, not a condition of the sky.

And the tendrils cast shadows after all, but the shadows are not dark. They are a *different color*—a deep, warm blue, almost ultraviolet at the edges, and they move independently of the tendrils that cast them. This is where the weirdness begins.

---

The weirdness.

The place is *old*. Not ancient in the way mountains are ancient, with a sense of endurance and patience. Old in the way a wound is old when it has healed into something functional but no longer remembers being skin. The erosion here is not geological. It is *biological*. The tendrils have weathered themselves into their current shapes through millennia of growth and stress and decay and regrowth, and the shapes they have settled into are *almost architectural*. There are arches that look designed but weren't. There are corridors that look intentional but emerged from competing growth patterns. There are platforms that look like they were built for habitation but are simply places where three tendrils happened to converge and the resulting surface happened to be flat.

But some of them look *too much* like architecture for coincidence. There are repeating motifs—spirals that appear at regular intervals, branchings that follow geometric rules, junctions that form shapes almost but not quite like letters in an alphabet you almost but don't quite recognize. The place has been *organized*, or has organized itself, or has been organized by something that thought in organic rather than geometric terms. The result is a settlement—a *city*, almost—that is also an organism, or an organism that has accidentally produced a city, or a city that was built by something so alien that its buildings grew instead of being assembled.

There are *rooms*. Or what function as rooms—enclosed spaces where tendrils have grown close enough together to form walls, floors, ceilings, and where the interior is humid and warm and dark and filled with the concentrated sound of pool-creatures singing in echo. Some of these rooms have features: ledges that are almost shelves, depressions that are almost seats, openings that are almost doors. They are empty. Nothing has lived in them for a long time. But they were shaped for living. By what? By whom? The tendrils don't answer. They don't answer anything. They just grow.

---

And the reality breakdown. It is subtle, and it is everywhere.

The shadows that move wrong are one example. Another: distances are unreliable. A tendril that looks thirty feet away might be fifty, or twenty, or the distance might change when you look away and look back. The hollow spaces between structures *breathe*—not visibly, but you can feel it, a slow expansion and contraction of the available space, as if the entire settlement is a lung and you are inside it. Sometimes a tendril you were sure you passed five minutes ago is not there anymore, and in its place is a tendril you have never seen before, identical in every respect except *position*, and you cannot decide whether the tendril moved or the space moved or you moved or the concept of "here" is simply less stable than you assumed.

The pools sometimes show you things. Not reflections—the water is not still enough for reflections, and the surfaces are too irregular. But in the ripples, in the interference patterns of droplets merging and separating, there are *images* that last too long to be random and resolve too clearly to be imagination. You see shapes. Structures. Other versions of this place, or this place at other times, or places that are *adjacent* to this one in a direction that has no name. You see tendrils that are buildings that are tendrils. You see creatures that are growing. You see yourself, sometimes, from angles that don't correspond to any pool's surface, looking back at you with an expression you didn't know you were making.

The fauna is aware of this. The pool-creatures sing differently when the images appear—their pitches cluster, briefly, into something almost like harmony. The breathers on the tendrils hold their breath. The flyers stop moving and hang in the air, wings still, and the chime-sound cuts out entirely for three or four seconds—an eternity in this soundscape—and then resumes as if nothing happened. As if reality hiccupped and the ecosystem noticed and adjusted.

---

This is the Wailing Tendril-Knot. It is a living settlement, or a settled organism, or a wound that healed into a habitat. It is warm and damp and filled with sound and almost empty of matter and full of life. It has existed long enough to have *weathered into its own shape*—the erosion here is not wearing it down, it is wearing it *in*, like a river stone becoming smooth, like a bone becoming fossil, like a memory becoming myth. It is a place that has settled into itself so completely that the boundaries between its component parts—the tendrils, the creatures, the water, the sound, the light, the weirdness—have become permeable, uncertain, negotiable.

It is not hostile. It is not welcoming. It is *occupied*. By processes. By growth. By the accumulated consequence of millions of small biological decisions made over timescales that make geology look hasty. It is a place that has become what it is and is still becoming it, simultaneously, and the two states are indistinguishable.

The hum continues. The creatures sing. The tendrils creep. The shadows drift sideways. The water weeps warm from surfaces that are not quite alive and not quite not-alive. The sound is a wall and you are inside it and it is inside you and the distinction between inside and outside is one of those things that has become negotiable here, one of those boundaries that erosion—biological, slow, patient erosion—has worn smooth until it barely exists.

You are standing on a tendril. It is warm. It hums. It is growing, toward something, away from something, for reasons that are its own.

The place *is* the reason.
