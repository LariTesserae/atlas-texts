# The Immersion Vaults of Reth

You descend through a skin of rain that never reaches the ground — it simply *thickens* as you go down, transitioning from precipitation to something denser, something that still moves like weather but holds you like water. By the time you're fifty meters below the surface, you are inside it. Fully inside. The rain has become a medium. It does not wet you. It *contains* you.

Welcome to the Immersion Vaults.

---

The Vaults are a city built in water that hasn't yet decided what kind of water it is.

Each "building" is a pressurized bubble of structured fluid — not air, not liquid, but a thickened state that engineers here call *murr* — held in shape by lattice-fields that operate at a physics layer most of your senses can't parse. The murr is slightly cooler than blood temperature. You feel it on your skin the way you feel the inside of your own mouth: intimate, neutral, faintly alive. It is dense. Standing in it is like standing in a crowd where every person is paying attention to you. It presses against your body from all directions with a weight that is not quite pressure — more like *acknowledgment*. The medium knows you are there.

The Vaults themselves are small. Each one is a single enclosed space — a room, a chamber, a hollow — rarely larger than twenty meters across. They are embedded into and between one another like organs in a body, connected by narrow throats of moving current. You don't walk between them. You *transfer*: you step into the throat and the current carries you, tumbling you gently through the connection, spitting you out into the next chamber's murr like a cough of rain. The throats are loud. Everything here is loud.

---

**Sound.** The dominant feature of the Immersion Vaults. Each chamber has its own acoustic signature based on its shape and the frequency of its lattice-field, and because the murr is so dense, sound doesn't travel through it so much as *bloom*. A footstep in a spherical vault becomes a low, sustained chord that pulses for minutes. A whisper in a long tubular vault arrives at the far end not as an echo but as a *recomposition* — the same whisper in a different key, with notes added that weren't in the original voice. Some residents have learned to speak in a way that uses this. They compose sentences across distance, trusting the vault to complete their meaning. Other times the vault completes it wrong. Misunderstandings here are architectural.

Rain, or its echo, is constant overhead — a hiss-and-thrum that bleeds down through the upper lattice-fields. Even though you are submerged, the Vaults still hear the storm above. It's a reminder that this is a *post-flood* city. The world above drowned. The people below did not.

---

**Construction.** The vaults are young. This is visible in their edges, which are sharp — not weathered, not softened by the murr's flow, but crisp as broken glass. The lattice-fields that maintain their shape were established only recently, geologically speaking. Some chambers still have moments where their fields *hiccup* — a brief shudder, a flicker in the walls — and the shape of the room shifts by a degree or two. A corner becomes slightly more acute. A wall develops a new fold. The residents track these changes. They read them like weather. A vault that hiccuped three times in a week is considered unstable. One that hasn't hiccuped in a month is considered *settled*, which is the closest thing to safe.

The walls themselves are not solid. They are *dense* — impossibly dense — but they are made of the same murr as the interior, just lattice-organized at a different frequency. Up close, you can see the structure: geometric patterns repeating at scales your eyes can barely resolve, hexagonal at one magnification, fractal at another, then suddenly a pattern that doesn't have a name because the geometry it uses doesn't exist in your home mathematics. This is the beyond-modern tech: the lattice-fields are engineered using principles that are not quite physics and not quite something else. The closest analogy is that they *persuade* the water to hold still in a shape, and the water agrees because it finds the shape interesting.

There are residents — human, or close enough — who study lattice acoustics and lattice geometry as separate disciplines, and they argue constantly about whether the two are the same thing.

---

**Density.** The murr is saturated. There is no empty space in it. Not microscopically — *actually*. The fluid is packed with dissolved minerals, with lattice-residue from the field generators, with microscopic crystalline structures that form and dissolve on cycles you can feel as subtle changes in viscosity. The air-equivalent you breathe is not air at all but a dissolved oxygen compound carried in the murr, absorbed through the skin with an efficiency that makes lungs optional. Most residents have lungs anyway. They use them for singing.

Because everything is so dense, everything is also *close*. A vault full of people is not a room with individuals in it. It is a single dense system. You cannot stand next to someone without feeling their heat in the murr before you touch them. You cannot turn around without displacing fluid that immediately tells everyone in the room that you moved. Privacy here is a technology, not a given. The wealthy live in smaller vaults — tighter, more controlled — because in a small space the density works differently, and you can find pockets of relative stillness.

---

**Vegetation.** Sparse, but present in the cracks. Between vaults, in the throats where currents run fastest, there are growths — thin, pale, barely organic — that the residents call *thread*. It clings to the throat walls in filaments so fine they look like frost. It feeds on lattice-residue. It is not quite plant and not quite mineral, and it has no light to photosynthesize by, so it runs on something else. Geothermal, perhaps. Or it eats the sound. Some researchers believe the thread is what makes the throats so loud — that the sound isn't acoustic at all but *biological*, the thread vibrating in dense colonies like cilia in a throat, which is a metaphor that makes some people uncomfortable given the setting.

The thread does not grow inside vaults. The lattice-fields discourage it. But in the interstitial spaces — the cracks, the seams, the places between one vault and the next — it thrives, pale and dense and faintly luminous in a spectrum that the eye processes more as *pressure* than color.

---

**Fauna.** None. The thread is the closest thing, and it doesn't move with intent. There are no fish, no crawling things, no birds. The absence of animal life is one of the quiet griefs of the Vaults — a grief that doesn't need to be spoken because everyone carries it in the same way, a shared dullness behind the sternum. The world above had animals once. The flood took them. The murr sustains human life but has never produced anything more complex than thread. Biologists here — and there are many, obsessive, half-mad biologists — spend their lives trying to coax something alive from the crystalline residue in the murr. So far: nothing. The lattice-fields won't permit it. Or the murr won't. Or life, in this form of water, is simply a different category of thing than anyone has learned to recognize.

Some biologists believe the vaults themselves are alive. Others find this unhelpful.

---

**Light.** Normal, but sourced strangely. The vaults have no sun. Their light comes from the lattice-fields themselves, which emit a soft, even illumination at a frequency calibrated to approximate the light of a cloudy afternoon on the surface. It is warm-neutral. It casts no shadows — or rather, it casts *diffuse* shadows, soft gradients of slightly-less-light that give depth to objects without hard edges. This changes how people look. Faces in the vaults are softer than faces on the surface. Anger looks like thoughtfulness. Joy looks like calm. The residents have developed a vocabulary of micro-expressions so precise it reads as body language from a distance — a twitch of the temple, a tightening below the ear — because the face alone is not enough.

Some vaults deviate from the standard light. The deeper ones, the older ones, the ones where the lattice-fields have developed what might be called *personality*, emit light in frequencies that lean toward violet or amber. The violet vaults are considered beautiful but unsettling — in violet light, the thread-growths in the throats become visible as fine glowing networks, and the throats look like veins, and the whole system of vaults starts to look like a body, and people who spend too long in violet vaults begin to think of the city as an organism, which circles back to the biologists' argument.

---

**The city.** It is small. The entire inhabited system — every vault, every throat, every interstitial crack — could fit inside a surface-world neighborhood. Perhaps three hundred residents. They have governance, argument, commerce, art, boredom, grief. They have a market in Vault Thirteen where lattice-residue is traded like spice, its quality graded by color and density. They have a school in Vault Seven where children learn to read the hiccups. They have a church in Vault Two where they pray to the rain — not above, not below, but the *concept* of rain, the idea that water can fall and keep falling and become a world.

They have a problem.

The Vaults are growing. Not outward — they can't expand into the flood-pressure above — but *inward*. New chambers are forming spontaneously in the dense murr between existing vaults, lattice-fields self-organizing in the crystalline residue without any human intervention. These new vaults are smaller, stranger, sharper-edged. They hum at frequencies that make teeth ache. Some of them open — a wall simply *parting* like a wound — and release bursts of murr into adjacent chambers, floods of dense fluid that reshape rooms and displace residents and leave behind crystalline deposits that the thread finds delicious.

The new vaults are where the action is now. The dynamic energy of the city has shifted from the inhabited chambers to the forming ones, from the social to the geological. Residents stand at the edges of new openings and watch the murr organize itself, and they feel something that is not quite awe and not quite fear — something that might be *recognition*, as if the city is becoming what it always was, and the residents are just the intermediate form, the larval stage, the brief animal phase of something that was never going to stay human.

The thread grows thicker every season. The throats sing louder. The rain above never stops.

The Vaults are young, and they are becoming.
