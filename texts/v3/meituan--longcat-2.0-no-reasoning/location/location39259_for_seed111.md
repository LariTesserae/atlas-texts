# The Grief of Unfinished Stone

---

You are standing in a place that has not yet decided what it is.

The air is cold enough that your breath does not so much cloud as *arrive* — each exhalation a small, startled ghost that lingers too long, reluctant to dissolve. The moisture here is not rain. It is something more intimate than rain. It is the slow weeping of the rock itself, a condensation that beads on every surface like sweat on fevered skin, gathering in the hollows of things, running in threads so fine they seem to move against gravity, climbing, pausing, falling. The sound of it is everywhere and nowhere — not the percussion of droplets on leaves, but a continuous, almost subliminal *ticking*, as if the walls of this place are counting something in a language of water.

You are inside a crevasse. Or a throat. Or a wound.

The walls rise on either side of you — not cliffs, not quite, but *fissures* in something too large to comprehend from within. The stone is young. You can feel this in a way that has nothing to do with geology and everything to do with *texture*: the rock faces are sharp, almost glassy in places, fractured along lines that look less like natural cleavage and more like something was *pulled apart* before it could set. There are edges here that would draw blood from a careless touch. The surfaces are raw, unweathered, still carrying the memory of whatever force opened them — and that force was recent, in the way that a cut is recent when it still weeps. Lichen has not yet found purchase. Moss has not yet softened anything. The stone is naked and it knows it.

The crevasse is narrow — perhaps four body-widths across at its widest, narrowing in places to a squeeze, widening in others to small chambers no larger than a room where a person might sleep. The ceiling is lost in darkness. Not the darkness of night, which has depth and texture and the promise of stars, but a *material* darkness, a darkness that seems to have weight and presence, pressing down from above like a held breath. The light that exists here comes from below — or rather, from *within*. Certain seams in the rock emit a faint, cold luminescence, the color of deep bruises, of old violets left too long in water. It is enough to see by, barely, in the way that you can see the shape of a room by the light of a single dying ember. Everything is rendered in tones of grey-blue and shadow. Edges glow faintly. Depths absorb.

And the place is *full*.

Not full of objects. Full of *them*.

They are in the walls. They are in the water. They are in the air between you and the next bend of the crevasse, so thick sometimes that you must move through them the way you might move through a curtain of hanging threads. They are small — most of them no larger than your thumb, some as large as your fist — and they are *everywhere*, a density of living things that borders on the obscene, a saturation of biology in a place that should, by all rights, be barren.

They are not insects. They are not quite anything you have a word for.

Imagine something between a snail and a spider, if both were made of wet paper. Pale, almost translucent bodies that catch the faint light and glow from within, each one carrying its own dim lantern of bioluminescence — not bright, but *present*, so that the walls of the crevasse appear to be studded with hundreds of small, cold stars. They move slowly. Everything here moves slowly. They extend limbs — too many limbs, or perhaps limbs that split and re-split into finer and finer filaments, so that each creature is less a body than a *fractal*, a branching thing that touches the rock at a dozen points simultaneously, feeling its way along the sharp young stone with a patience that borders on the geological.

They eat the rock. Or something in the rock. Or the moisture on the rock. You watch one press its body — if it has a body, if it is not merely a process given form — against a glistening patch of wall, and the condensation there *diminishes*, drawn into the creature's surface, and the stone beneath darkens by a shade, and you understand that these things are *mining* this place, slowly, over spans of time that make the rock's own youth seem ancient.

The sound is the thing you cannot escape.

It is not loud in the way of waterfalls or thunder. It is loud in the way of a room full of people whispering — a dense, layered, *textured* sound that fills every frequency between a hum and a click. The creatures communicate, or perhaps merely *exist*, in a register that overlaps and interlocks: a continuous, shifting chorus of small sounds, wet sounds, the sound of soft bodies moving over sharp stone, the sound of water being drawn through membranes, the sound of filaments touching filaments in patterns that might be language or might be something older than language. It rises and falls in waves that have nothing to do with you. It is the sound of a place that is *occupied*, thoroughly and completely, by minds that do not acknowledge your presence because your presence is, to them, approximately as significant as a change in air pressure.

The water runs in a thin stream along the floor of the crevasse — not a river, not a brook, but a *thread* of water, no wider than your finger, moving with a slowness that suggests it is not flowing so much as *being pulled*, drawn by some gradient too subtle to perceive. It catches the bioluminescence of the creatures that cluster along its banks — and they cluster *thickly* there, packed so densely that their bodies overlap, a living skin of pale light moving over the wet stone — and the stream becomes a line of cold fire in the darkness, a glowing seam running through the floor of the wound.

The air smells of mineral and something else. Something organic but not decay — more like the smell of bread dough, or of a greenhouse, or of the inside of a living thing. It is the smell of *process*, of chemistry happening slowly, of water and stone and biology engaged in a conversation that has been going on since before the crevasse opened and will continue long after it closes.

Because it will close. You can feel this too. The walls are not stable. Not in the sense of imminent collapse — nothing here is imminent, nothing here is fast — but in the sense of *unfinished*. This place is a moment in a longer process. The fissure is still forming. The stone is still deciding whether to be one thing or two. In a thousand years — or ten thousand, or a hundred thousand, time moves differently when you are made of water and patience — the walls will close, or widen, or the water will find a new path, and this particular configuration of darkness and light and sound and living density will cease to exist, and the creatures will go elsewhere, or become something else, or simply *stop*, their purpose fulfilled in a way you cannot understand.

For now, though, it is this: a narrow place, high in the body of something vast, cold and damp and dark and *full*, so full, a density of small bright lives pressed against sharp young stone, drinking the moisture, eating the light, making their slow sounds in the dark, indifferent to you, indifferent to everything except the patient work of being alive in a place that has only just begun to exist.

You stand in the crevasse. The water ticks. The walls glow faintly with the bodies of things that are not quite animals. The darkness presses down. The sound rises around you like a tide of whispers.

The place does not care that you are here.

The place is busy becoming.
