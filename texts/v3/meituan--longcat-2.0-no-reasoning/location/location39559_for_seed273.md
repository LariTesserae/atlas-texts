The air tastes of iron and wet stone and something green underneath, like crushed stems. You are standing on a platform of riveted steel grating, and through the gaps you can see another platform below it, and another below that, descending into a haze of warm vapor that never quite becomes fog and never quite clears. The whole structure breathes — not metaphorically. The metal expands in the heat of the day with a low, almost subsonic ticking, and at night it contracts and the entire edifice settles with groans that travel through your feet before your ears catch them.

This is the Cascades. Not a natural formation. A built one.

Someone, or some civilization, or some process that outlasted its architects, constructed a vertical city into and around and through a cliff system that may once have been a river gorge, or a rift, or something else entirely. The elevation is extreme — you can feel it in the way sound behaves, the way your ears pop when you descend three or four levels, the way the sky above is a narrow bright strip between the uppermost tiers. The light up there is fierce and white, almost surgical, but by the time it filters down through seventeen or forty or a hundred layers of walkway and scaffold and hanging garden and pipe-run and cable-truss, it has been broken into something dappled and green-gold, refracted through condensation on brass fittings and the broad waxy leaves of plants that have colonized every horizontal surface.

The plants are not decorative. They are load-bearing.

That is the first thing that disorients you. The vegetation here is lush — aggressively, structurally lush — and it is doing work. Thick-rooted creepers have been trained over generations (or decades, or centuries; the timescale is unclear) along tension cables, their root masses braided into the cable weave, adding tensile strength, absorbing vibration. Broad-leafed things with stems like your forearm grow from planters that are themselves bolted to the framework, and their leaves are oriented — not by chance, not entirely — to catch the updrafts that rise from the lower levels, channeling airflow through the structure for cooling, for ventilation, for the dispersal of spores and seeds that are themselves part of the maintenance ecology. The plants filter the air. The plants manage moisture. The plants are, in a very real sense, the reason the metal hasn't rusted through.

Because there is water everywhere, but it is not flooding. It is managed. It drips. It condenses on the undersides of upper platforms and is caught in gutters — some metal, some carved wood, some living channels formed by the curved stems of plants trained into trough-shapes — and fed downward through a distribution system that is half plumbing and half root network. You hear it constantly: the drip, the trickle, the occasional rush when a cistern somewhere above tips its load into a main channel. The sound is loud not because any single source is loud but because there are hundreds of them, thousands, layered over each other, a percussive texture that becomes the baseline of silence here. When it stops — and it does stop, occasionally, in certain zones where the system has been shut down or has failed — the absence is eerie and total.

The fauna is abundant and mostly small. Insects first: beetles with carapaces that gleam like oiled mahogany, moths the color of rust and verdigris that rest on metal surfaces and are nearly invisible until they move. Lizards — or lizard-analogues, scaled things with too many toes — that live in the wall cavities where insulation has been packed between inner and outer cladding. Birds nest in the upper reaches, where the light is brightest, and their calls echo down through the structure, distorted by the metal and the moisture into something that sounds almost mechanical, almost like signal codes. There are larger things in the lower levels. You haven't seen them. You've heard them moving at night, a heavy deliberate shifting, like someone rearranging furniture in a room below yours. The people who live here — and people do live here, in the mid-levels, in compartments built into the interstices of the structure — do not talk about the lower fauna. They close certain hatches at dusk. They hang certain charms, or sensors, or living plant-forms that are said to bristle and hiss when something large passes.

The built environment is total. There is no ground. There is no natural substrate visible anywhere in your field of view. Everything is made: the platforms, the railings, the stairs, the walls, the ceilings, the bridges between sections, the ladders, the pulleys, the lifts (some powered, some hand-cranked, some operated by counterweight systems that use water in sealed tanks — fill the tank, the lift descends; drain it, the lift rises). The materials are layered by age and by function. The oldest construction is stone — actual stone, massive blocks, fitted without mortar in a style that suggests dry-stack but with a precision that makes the seams nearly invisible. This is the foundation, the core, the spine of the structure, and it is found at the deepest accessible levels, where the air is warmest and wettest and the light is a dim amber filtered through layers of bioluminescent growth that has been cultivated — or has cultivated itself — on the stone surfaces. Above the stone is iron, old iron, hand-forged, with the marks of hammers still visible in the surface texture. Above the iron is steel, industrial-era steel, rolled and riveted, and this is the bulk of what you see: a vast lattice of beams and plates and girders, painted in places with compounds that have mostly worn away, leaving streaks of ochre and white and a pale verdigreen where the anti-corrosion treatments have reacted with the plant secretions. Above the steel is newer work — aluminum, composites, materials you can't identify, lighter and thinner, added in recent decades or years to extend the upper reaches toward the strip of bright sky.

The technology is industrial but not advanced. There are engines — steam, mostly, and some internal combustion, and a few electrical generators powered by water turbines fed by the cascade system. The engines are loud. They live in dedicated chambers, walled off with thick stone and insulated with packed fiber, but you feel them as much as hear them: a rhythmic thudding that travels through the structure, a vibration in the handrails, a pulse in the soles of your feet. The factories — and there are factories, small ones, workshops really, but scaled for production — are where the metal is worked, where the cables are braised, where the plant-fiber is processed into rope and textile and composite panel. The sound of hammering is constant in certain districts, a bright ringing that cuts through the water-sound and the engine-thud and the bird-calls and creates a third layer, a metallic music that the residents seem to find comforting. Visitors find it overwhelming for the first three days, then stop noticing, then find its absence unsettling.

The density is high. Not in the sense of crowding — though there are crowded places, market-levels where the walkways widen and stalls are hung from the framework like fruit — but in the sense of *stuff*. Every surface is used. Every gap is filled. Cables run in bundles along every beam. Pipes — water, steam, waste, gas — are layered over the cables. Plant growth covers the pipes. Signage, in multiple scripts and some pictographic systems, is affixed to the plant growth. Lanterns hang from hooks driven into the plant growth. The air itself is dense with moisture and spores and the fine mist that rises from the lower levels where the water is warmest, heated by the engines and by geothermal vents that the stone foundation intercepts. You breathe deeply and you are breathing a living thing, a suspension of organic and mineral particulates that coats your lungs with a faint sweetness.

The erosion is established. This is not a new place. It has been here long enough that the metal has developed a patina that is specific to each level — the upper steel is pale and powdery with oxidation; the mid-levels are dark and glossy with the residue of plant oils and human touch; the lower iron is thick with a deep red-brown scale that flakes away in sheets the size of your hand, revealing brighter metal underneath that will, in time, scale again. The stone at the base is worn smooth in places where water has run over it for what must be centuries, and the wear-patterns have been incorporated into the design — channels carved by water have been deepened and formalized into drainage routes, and the smooth stone is left exposed in certain areas as a kind of aesthetic choice, a display of age and patience. The plants, too, show their age: the oldest creepers are woody and thick as a man's torso, their bark furrowed and mossy, and they have been growing so long that the metal they cling to has deformed around them, beams bent into gentle curves by the slow persistent pressure of living tissue.

The dynamic is slow. Things change here, but they change the way a glacier changes, or a forest, or a language. A new level is added every few years. A section of walkway is replaced when it finally fails, and the replacement is slightly different from the original — a different alloy, a different joint, a slightly wider gauge — and over decades these small differences accumulate into a visible evolution of style and method. The plant ecology shifts: a new species appears, or an old one retreats to a narrower range, and the residents notice and adjust their maintenance routines accordingly. The water system is in a constant state of slow modification, channels redirected, cisterns added or decommissioned, the whole hydrological network gradually optimizing itself in response to the changing weight and shape of the structure above it. Nothing is urgent. Nothing is fast. The people who live here think in seasons and in generations, and they are bemused by visitors who want things done by tomorrow.

The weirdness is low. This is not a surreal place. It is strange to outsiders because of its scale and its totality — the absence of ground, the verticality, the integration of living and built systems — but it operates on principles that are comprehensible. Gravity. Hydraulics. Tensile strength. Photosynthesis. The warmth rises, the water falls, the plants grow toward the light, the metal fatigues and is replaced. There are no paradoxes here, no spatial impossibilities, no phenomena that defy explanation. The only thing that might strike a visitor as unusual is the *completeness* of it — the sense that nothing has been left to chance, that every element serves multiple functions, that the boundary between the made and the grown has been not blurred but *erased*. A wall is also a garden. A gutter is also a vine. A structural beam is also a habitat. This is not magic. It is simply very, very good engineering, applied over a very long time, with a very patient understanding of how living systems and built systems can be made to serve each other.

The light, when you reach the upper levels, is startling. After the green dimness of the mid-reaches, the brightness hits you like a physical force — white, clean, unfiltered, pouring down into the top of the structure and illuminating everything it touches with a clarity that makes the lower levels feel like a dream. The upper platforms are open, unroofed, and the sky above is a deep blue that darkens to indigo at the zenith. The metal here is hot to the touch. The plants are different — smaller, tougher, with thick cuticles and silvered leaves that reflect the light. The sound changes too: the water-sound fades, the engine-thud is distant, and what you hear instead is wind, and birds, and the high thin hum of cables vibrating in the breeze. It is loud up here, but it is a different kind of loud — open, expansive, the loudness of space rather than enclosure.

You stand at the top and look down into the green depths of the Cascades, and you see the whole structure at once: the stone roots, the iron trunk, the steel branches, the living leaves, the water falling through it all like blood through a body, and you understand that this is not a city built in a place. This is a place that *became* a city, slowly, over time, the way a reef becomes an island, the way a tree becomes a forest, the way a process becomes a world.
