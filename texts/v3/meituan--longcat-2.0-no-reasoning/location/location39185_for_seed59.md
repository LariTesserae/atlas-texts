# The Drownings

You are standing in a place that should not be able to hold together but does.

The city is small — a hamlet, really, perhaps a few hundred meters across — but it is *stacked*. Not upward so much as *inward*. Every surface is occupied. Every surface is alive. The buildings are not built upon the land so much as they are *negotiated with it*. Roots of enormous pale trees — not quite mangrove, not quite anything with a name — have been trained, guided, or simply accepted as load-bearing structures. Walls are braided from living branch. Floors are layered cut-stone mortared with some dark resinous compound that still smells faintly of pine and still weeps sap in the cooler hours. Roofs are thatched with a broad-leafed fern that never seems to die, only thicken.

But the water. The water is the first thing, the last thing, the everything thing.

It is *everywhere*. Not flooding — *present*. The settlement sits at the crown of a broad, gentle rise, maybe a hundred meters above the valley floor, though the air is so saturated that distance is hard to judge. The ground between structures is perpetually damp, dark earth that compresses under your boot and slowly springs back. Water seeps from every wall-base in thin vertical lines, as though the stone itself is sweating. Small channels — some deliberate, some not — run between the buildings, carrying clear water that never seems to stop arriving. Where channels meet, there are little pools, waist-deep at most, covered in a film of duckweed so dense it looks like poured paint. The air is thick. Not tropical-thick. *Cool*-thick. The moisture doesn't warm you; it settles on your skin like a second, colder skin.

And it *condenses*. At this elevation, the air holds more moisture than it should, and it is always deciding what to do with it. Mornings — if mornings can be identified through the permanent grey-white overcast — bring a fog so dense that the buildings across the central clearing are only suggestions. By midday the fog lifts to a low ceiling, perhaps ten meters up, and the light comes through it diffuse and even and sourceless, like light remembered rather than light observed. The result is that nothing casts a hard shadow. The world has edges but no silhouettes. Everything is present and nothing is crisp.

The settlement has been here a long time. You can tell because the erosion has *finished*. The water has already worn every channel it intends to wear. The roots have already found every path through the stone they intend to find. There is no raw earth anywhere — no landslide scars, no fresh cuts, no disturbed ground. Even the woodpiles show the dark staining of long moisture. This place is *settled* in the geological sense. The forces that shape it have reached their conclusion and now simply *maintain*. It is not growing. It is not decaying. It is *persisting*.

The built structures follow this logic. They are modest — single-story, mostly, with some buildings that climb two or three levels where the root-architecture allows. The construction is stone and wood and thatch, but everything has been *adapted* by the water. Corners are rounded where seepage has worn them. Door thresholds are recessed below ground level, with small stone lips to redirect the flow. Windows are few and small and covered with some translucent membrane — oiled membrane of animal origin, it looks like — that admits light but not rain. Chimneys are fitted with angled caps that hiss with condensate. The whole place looks *wet-functional*, like a dock that became a village that became a home.

And now the fauna.

You don't notice it at first because it is so *integrated*. But then a wall moves. Or rather, a section of wall that you took for a panel of bark is revealed, as it peels away and launches itself into the air with a sound like tearing linen, to be a bat — or something bat-like, slightly larger, with a membrane that is more grey than black. And then the thatch above you shifts, and something drops onto your shoulder, and you flinch, and it is a frog the color of moss, and it looks at you with the calm authority of something that has never once been concerned about your presence.

They are *everywhere*. The frogs, primarily — dozens of species, it seems, from the size of a thumbnail to the size of a dinner plate. They occupy the pools, the channels, the walls, the roofs. Some are bright — startling vermilion, electric blue — and some are the exact color of wet bark and only give themselves away by breathing. There are insects in numbers that defy the cool temperature: dragonflies that never stop moving, hovering in place in the air like small tense punctuation marks, and beetles that click audibly as they navigate the channels, and mosquitoes — yes, *mosquitoes*, thick enough in the still air around the pools that they form visible columns, like smoke from invisible fires.

There are birds. You hear them before you see them — a sound like someone running a wet finger along the rim of a glass, repeated and varied, overlapping, *loud*. They are small and dense and mostly green, nesting in the thatch, in the root-masses, in crevices between the stones. The sound of the settlement, its baseline frequency, is the sound of birdsong and water and the clicking of beetles and the rustle of small bodies moving through thatch. It is not loud in the way a storm is loud. It is loud in the way a *market* is loud — many discrete sources, none of them dominant, all of them insistent.

The people. Yes, there are people. You see them now, or you see the *evidence* of them — a figure crossing a root-bridge between two structures with the practiced ease of someone who has never known dry footing, carrying a basket of something pale and dripping. They do not look up. The frogs do not move for them. The birds do not fall silent. The people are, like the water, like the roots, like the fog, simply *part of the system*. Their tools are simple — you see a woven net, a stone blade, a wooden frame strung with membrane for catching something from the water. No metal that you can identify. Everything they have made could have been made a thousand years ago and will probably be made the same way a thousand years from now.

A wind moves through, and the fog shifts, and for a moment you can see the full shape of the settlement: a rough circle of structures around a central clearing that is mostly pool, mostly duckweed, mostly the sound of frogs. Beyond the circle, the roots continue, down the long gentle slope into the valley where the water goes to do whatever water does when it leaves. The fog closes again. The world becomes room-sized again.

The frog on your shoulder has not moved. It is watching you. It is not afraid.

Nothing here is afraid. This is what happens when water and life and time reach their agreement. The world doesn't become gentle — it becomes *saturated*. Every niche filled. Every surface occupied. Every moment full of small urgent motion.

You are standing in it. It has not acknowledged you. It does not need to.
