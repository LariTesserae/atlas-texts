# The Hollow Growth

---

You arrive at dusk, though dusk here is indistinguishable from noon. The light does not come from above — there is no above, or rather, the above has grown over, a ceiling of interlocked cambium twenty meters up that admits only a brownish, directionless luminescence, like the light inside a seed. This light does not cast shadows. It presses equally against all surfaces, making the world look like a photograph of itself.

The space you stand in is vast but not open. Imagine a nave — the interior of some enormous organism, ribbed and vaulted — except the ribs are living trees that have been coaxed into architectural curvature over decades, maybe centuries. They arc overhead in precise geometries: catenary curves, parabolic vaults, flying buttresses made of wood that is still alive, still growing, still exchanging moisture with the air. The spacing between them is deliberate. This is *built*, emphatically, but the builder used living tissue as raw material and then simply waited. Between the ribs, the canopy has fused into a solid mat, and from its underside hang rootlets by the millions — pale, dangling, some as thin as thread, some as thick as your wrist — reaching down like the stalactites of a cave made of inverse stone.

The ground is a composite. You can feel it give slightly underfoot: compressed moss, rotting leaf-litter, layers of mycelial mat that have been walked on so many times they've developed a kind of leathery resilience. In places, the floor has been paved with something — but the paving is *grown*, not laid. Plates of fungal sclerotium, irregular and overlapping like scales, each one the size of a dinner plate, hardened into a surface that rings faintly hollow when you step on it. Between the plates, moisture beads up. The air is damp but not wet, the kind of dampness that lives in the spaces between things — in the gaps between bark plates, in the cupped centers of broad leaves, in the tiny pools that collect where rootlets knot together overhead. You can hear it if you stop moving: not dripping, but *breathing*. The moisture is migrating, condensing and re-condensing, pulled by temperature gradients so subtle they barely exist.

There are structures here. That word feels right even though every structure is also a living thing. Columns rise from the floor — the trunks of trees that have been shaped, not cut, their growth directed by frameworks of wire and ceramic and something that looks like braided sinew. The frameworks are still in place, half-swallowed by bark that has grown around them, over them, through them. Some frameworks hold lamps: not electric, not fire, but bioluminescent cultures housed in glass-and-wood fixtures that have themselves been grafted into the column, so the light source is *part* of the architecture, a glowing vein running through the bark like a slow pulse of pale blue. The light they produce is minimal. This place lives in a permanent near-darkness, and everything in it has adapted to that — including, presumably, the people.

*People.* You notice them slowly, the way you notice animals in tall grass. Movement at the edge of perception. A figure on a raised walkway that spirals around one of the great columns, three meters up. Another crossing a bridge between two ribs — the bridge is a living root, flattened and widened, with railings grown from smaller roots trained into parallel arcs. They are small at this distance. They move with the unhurried purposefulness of people who know their environment completely and have stopped being impressed by it.

---

The fauna reveals itself in layers.

First: the sound. The place is mostly quiet, as the parameters demand, but *mostly quiet* is not silent. There is a sub-auditory hum — the resonant frequency of the space itself, the way the vaulted canopy creates a chamber that amplifies certain wavelengths. You feel it in your sternum more than your ears. Underneath it: the high, thin ticking of beetles in the bark. The occasional *thock* of a falling seed pod hitting the sclerotium floor. A rustle — too large for an insect, too deliberate for wind — from somewhere in the root-hung ceiling. You look up and see movement: something pale and multi-legged traversing the root network with the ease of a spider on a web, except it is the size of a cat, and it is not a spider, and it does not move like anything you have a name for.

The second layer of fauna is the *integrated* animals. Birds — or bird-analogues — nest in the rootlets overhead, and they have been here so long that the rootlets have grown *around* their nests, incorporating them into the ceiling's structure. The nests are load-bearing. The birds understand this, or at least they have not done anything to contradict it. Smaller creatures live in the space between the bark and the swallowed frameworks: lizards, moths, things with too many legs that might be one or might be many. They are not pests. They are, you begin to understand, *components*. This ecosystem is not built *on* the plant architecture. The ecosystem *is* the architecture. The animals are as structural as the wood.

The third layer is stranger.

---

There are pools here — the *water*, sparse but present. They form in depressions where the sclerotium floor has cracked or been deliberately broken, and beneath it, water wells up from some deep source, warm against the cool air. The pools are small, no more than a meter across, and they are *occupied*. In one, you see what appears to be a flower floating on the surface — white petals, a yellow center — but when you step closer, the flower turns to face you, and you realize it is an animal. A radial animal, like a sea anemone, but terrestrial, floating in this warm pool like a lily. It has no eyes that you can identify, but it is watching you. Its petals — *arms? leaves?* — shift slightly, rearranging, and you have the sudden vertiginous sense that it is *reading* you. Not threatening. Just... taking in data.

Other pools contain other things: colonies of tiny translucent organisms that have arranged themselves into geometric patterns on the water's surface, patterns that change slowly, that might be communication or might be computation. A pool near one of the columns has a skin on it — a biofilm, thick and iridescent — and when a drop of condensation falls from above and strikes it, the biofilm *rings*, a clear tone that hangs in the air for three full seconds before the space's sub-auditory hum swallows it.

---

The *weirdness* is not an addition to this place. It is the substrate. You are beginning to understand that the rules here are not the rules you brought with you.

The trees that form the ribs of this vault are growing in a direction that does not correspond to any light source — because there is no light source to grow toward. They are growing *toward each other*, has been growing toward each other for a long time, and they have met, and they have fused, and now they are one continuous structure that has no beginning and no end, a single organism wearing the costume of a forest. The architecture is not a forest that was shaped by builders. It is a single organism that *chose* to build. Or was taught to. Or was always building and the people simply moved in and learned its grammar.

The water does not flow downhill. There is no downhill — the floor is level, or rather, the concept of level has been locally suspended. The water flows in small, looping circuits, pooling and evaporating and condensing and pooling again, driven by temperature gradients so fine they are almost philosophical. You follow one circuit with your eyes: condensation on the underside of the ceiling mat, a drop forming on a rootlet, falling, striking a leaf, sliding down the leaf's funnel-shape into a cupped depression, from there seeping through the moss floor to a pool, from the pool evaporating upward in the warmth of whatever biothermal process keeps it slightly heated, rising, and beginning again. The loop is closed. Nothing is lost. This is not a hydrological cycle. It is a *circuit*, and it has been running for a very long time, and it has achieved something like *patience*.

Time here is — you are not sure. The sclerotium plates on the floor have growth rings, and when you kneel to count them, you get different numbers depending on which angle you count from. Not because you are counting wrong, but because the rings are not concentric. They spiral. They branch. One plate, examined closely, has a growth ring that *crosses over itself*, which should not be possible, and yet it is right there, a line in the fungal tissue that passes through its own history like a finger through a soap bubble. You stop counting.

---

The people — you see them more clearly now, or they have allowed themselves to be seen — are dressed in layers of woven plant fiber, dark with moisture, and they move through the space with a familiarity that borders on the symbiotic. One of them is tending a column: scraping away a section of overgrown bark to expose the framework beneath, checking something — a junction, a connection point where the living and the built meet — and then allowing the bark to remain open, knowing it will grow back, knowing the check was temporary and the growth is permanent. Another is harvesting something from the ceiling: reaching up into the root-hang, detaching a cluster of small, luminous growths — fruit? eggs? something else? — and placing them in a woven container. A third is simply *sitting* on the root-bridge, legs dangling, doing nothing that you can identify as productive, and you realize with a start that this person might be *listening*. Listening to the hum. Listening to the space. Listening to the organism that is the space.

They do not seem surprised by you. They do not seem unsurprised, either. You are, for the moment, outside their category system.

---

The *industrial* element is here, but it has been digested. You find it in the frameworks: the wire, the ceramic, the braided sinew-like material that might be engineered tendon. You find it in the glass-and-wood lamp housings, which are clearly manufactured, clearly *made*, but which have been so thoroughly incorporated into the living structure that they have become indistinguishable from it. You find it in a structure deeper in — you walk toward it, following a corridor between two columns that narrows and then opens into a smaller chamber — where a machine sits. The machine is *growing*. It is a engine of some kind, or was: you can see the cylinder block, the housing, the remains of a drive shaft, but the machine has been colonized by the same fungal sclerotium that paves the floor, and the fungus has not killed the machine but has *continued* it, has extended its logic into biological tissue. Where the drive shaft ends, a root continues the rotation — slowly, so slowly, a full turn every several minutes — and what the root turns is unclear. A pump, perhaps. Something that moves water through one of the closed circuits. The machine is not broken. It has been *adopted*. It is part of the metabolism now.

---

You stand in the center of the nave — if that is what it is — and you feel the space around you as a single thing. Not a place. A *process*. The trees are building. The water is cycling. The animals are maintaining. The fungus is paving and connecting and, you suspect, *thinking* — not in any way you would recognize as thought, but in the way that a mycelial network processes information, routing nutrients and signals through its web, making decisions about growth and resource allocation that are distributed and slow and, over sufficient time, indistinguishable from intention. The people are participating. The machine is participating. The lightless, damp, cool, low, small, empty-but-full space is *active*, humming with processes that operate on timescales from the insect-fast to the geological-slow, and you are standing in the middle of all of it, breathing air that has been breathed and re-breathled a thousand times, air that tastes of green and mineral and the faint, sweet, unsettling scent of something that is alive in a way you don't have a word for.

A drop of condensation falls from the ceiling and strikes your forehead. It is warm.

You look up. The rootlets are closer than they were a moment ago. Or you are taller. Or the ceiling is lower.

The space is not static. The space is *growing*.

You are inside it.
