The air tastes of metal and wet stone.

You stand — or hover, or are conveyed, the distinction blurs here — at the edge of a platform that is itself only one layer of a city built vertically into a rift that should not exist at this altitude. The elevation is wrong. Everything about the elevation is wrong. The altimeter reads a number that makes no sense given the surrounding geography, and the atmospheric pressure is too thin for the heat, and the heat is too sustained for the thinness, but the instruments don't argue with each other long enough for you to catch them in the contradiction. The place simply *is.*

The city has been here for a long time and also has just been built.

Both statements feel equally true, depending on where you look. The structures nearest you are raw — fresh-cut basalt or something like basalt, pale grey with a glassy sheen, stacked in geometries that suggest planning but not patience. The joints between blocks are too precise. No mortar. No visible fasteners. The stone fits the stone the way teeth fit teeth in a closed jaw, and the seams are damp with condensation that beads and drips and never quite pools, running in threads down the vertical surfaces to be caught by channels that haven't been cut yet but are already smooth with use.

This is the erosion of a place still forming. Weather works on surfaces that are simultaneously new and ancient — moisture finds microscopic fissures in the rock and opens them slowly, patiently, the way it would over centuries, except the rock has only been here for days. Or the rock has been here for centuries and is only now choosing to have fissures. The distinction matters less the longer you stay. Gradients are weak here. Things change, but they change the way a thought changes — you don't notice until you look back.

The heat is the most immediate thing, after the air. It has weight. Not oppressive exactly — the thin atmosphere keeps it from being suffocating — but *present,* like standing near a vast engine that is idling. And there are vast engines here. The city is loud in its bones but quiet in its throat. The soundscape is mostly silence — or rather, a dense quiet, the kind of quiet that is made of many small sounds too low or too high to resolve into anything you can name. The industrial mechanisms that thread through the city's midsections vibrate at frequencies that your body registers as pressure rather than noise. You feel them in your sternum, in the roots of your teeth. Occasionally something clanks, distantly, and the sound dies immediately, swallowed by the stone.

The city is dense with presences.

Not people, exactly. Not always. There are figures moving along the elevated walkways and through the apertures in the stacked architecture, and some of them are clearly human — bipedal, clothed, purposeful — and some of them are something else that has chosen a bipedal shape for the moment. The population has the quality of a factory that is running at capacity: everything in motion, everything in its lane, but you can't tell if the motion serves production or if production serves the motion. Conveyance systems — rails, cables, open-platform lifts — move between levels continuously, carrying loads that are sometimes cargo and sometimes structural components and sometimes things that are both. Sparks fall from a welding operation three levels below. The sparks fall slowly. Not dramatically, not floating, just — *slowly,* as if the gravity here is negotiating with something.

Water threads through the city the way nerves thread through a body. Not rivers, not plumbing exactly, but a distributed dampness that connects everything. The air is humid enough that your skin never fully dries. Metal surfaces carry a permanent sheen. In the lower levels, where the heat concentrates and the air thickens, water runs openly in narrow channels carved into the floor — cold water, startlingly cold, fed from somewhere above, some reservoir or condensation system or rain-catch that operates on principles the engineers are still arguing about. The channels don't flood. The water moves with purpose, as if it's been routed, but the channels look natural, or look *emerging,* as if the water is slowly teaching the stone to hold it.

Vegetation is absent. Not dead-and-absent — *never-present* absent. There is no soil, no mulch, no creeping tendril of green, no lichen even. The stone doesn't support life as you understand it. Instead, in the cracks and damp seams, there are films. Iridescent, thin as smoke, that move when you're not looking directly at them and freeze when you are. They might be chemical — mineral blooms, oxidation patterns, some reaction between the stone's unknown composition and the ambient moisture. They might be alive in a way that the word *alive* hasn't been stretched to accommodate yet. Nobody seems concerned about them. A figure walks past a wall coated in the film and doesn't slow down, and the film doesn't react, and you realize that the boundary between infrastructure and organism may not exist here. The city might be growing. The city might be digesting.

The light is ordinary. That's almost the strangest thing. After the heat, the wrong altitude, the slow sparks, the presence-dense quiet, the light comes in from above at what appears to be a normal angle for whatever latitude and time of day this is supposed to be, and it illuminates the scene with a flat, even clarity that feels like a courtesy. No drama. No cathedral shafts. Just light, doing its job, making everything visible. The stone catches it and returns it grey-white. The damp channels return it silver. The distant upper levels of the city, where the architecture grows more complex and more crowded and more impossible, return it in fragments, broken by geometry.

Rare fauna. You see one, eventually — or something assumes your attention and you decide it counts. It is high up, near the rift's rim, where the city's topmost structures claw into rock that looks molten even though it isn't. The shape is long-bodied, many-limbed, and it moves across the vertical surface the way a spider moves, except it's too large for the word *spider* to be comfortable, and it's too deliberate for the word *creature* to be accurate. It might be a machine. It might be an animal. It might be a maintenance process that has achieved locomotion. It pauses, and you feel it notice you — not with eyes, with something else, some pressure in the air — and then it continues up and over the rim and is gone, and the air where it was is slightly warmer than the air around it.

The strangeness here is not theatrical. It doesn't announce itself. It accumulates. You notice it in small things: a stairway that descends seven levels and deposits you at the same level you started from. A window that looks out onto a section of the city you can see directly overhead from where you stand. A puddle of condensation that is shaped like a footprint and then, over the course of a minute you watch it, reshapes itself into a handprint, and then into neither.

The city has a name, probably. The people here, whatever portion of them are people, likely call it something. But the name hasn't reached you yet, and you suspect the name might be one of those things that arrives only after you've been here long enough to be a feature of the place rather than a visitor to it.

You stay. The damp air presses against your skin. The engines hum their sub-audible hum. The city grows, or the city was already this large, or both, and the water finds new seams in the stone, and the stone opens to receive it.
