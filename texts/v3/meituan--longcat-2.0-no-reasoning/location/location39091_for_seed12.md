# The Greenhouses of Aeth

---

You arrive at the edge and understand immediately that this place is **unfinished**.

Not ruined. Not decayed. Not abandoned. Unfinished — as though the world itself is a project that someone started long ago and then, gently, set down. The geology is raw. You can see where the mountains are still deciding their shapes: ridgelines that taper into suggestions, valleys that cut only partway through stone before losing interest and becoming shelves, becoming terraces, becoming wide flat shelves of pale mineral where nothing has yet been carved by wind or persistent rain because the wind here is still learning to be wind, and the rain — the rain is something else entirely.

---

## The Water That Is Not Rain

It falls, but not from clouds. There are no clouds, or if there are, they are indistinguishable from the air itself, which carries a constant, fine suspension of moisture that does not quite condense and does not quite evaporate. It exists in the state between. Your skin registers it as neither wet nor dry but as a kind of *pressed presence*, the way a held note presses against silence. Over hours — not minutes, hours — you begin to glisten. Your clothes grow heavy by imperceptible degrees. Everything in Aeth is subject to this slow saturation. The walls of the buildings bevel with moisture. Moss does not grow so much as *become*, creeping outward from joints and seams like a thought surfacing through a mind that is mostly asleep.

Standing water is everywhere and nowhere. The valley floor, if it can be called a floor, holds shallow pools that follow no drainage logic — they sit on flats, they gather in the bowls of partially-formed depressions, they thread through the lower terraces of the settlement in sheets no deeper than a finger's width. The pools are warm. Not hot — warm, the temperature of blood, the temperature of something recently alive. They do not cool at night because the night here is not meaningfully darker than the day, and the warmth does not come from the sun, which is a pale diffuse presence somewhere above the moisture-veil. The warmth comes from below. From whatever is still cooking in the raw geology, the slow new mineral processes that are the embryonic equivalent of volcanism — not eruption, not even pressure really, just a vast warm patient *activity* under everything.

Sometimes, at certain points in the valley, the standing water thickens. Becomes more than water. Becomes a column of liquid that rises straight up, slowly, like a plant growing in time-lapse — a spire of fluid reaching thirty, forty, fifty feet before it loses cohesion and sags back down, or doesn't sag but simply *stops*, and hangs there, a vertical pool, a standing wave that refuses to fall. These are called **the Pillars** by the people who live here, or rather, that is the closest translation. The local word refers more to "the places where the water has decided to be somewhere else." They do not last. They form over days, hold for hours or weeks, then subside. But while they stand they are the most remarkable features of the landscape — gleaming columns catching the dim light, distorting the view behind them, and if you walk close to one you feel the pull, a faint suction on your skin, as though the column is considering whether to include you in itself.

## The Settlement: the Stacked Greenhouses

The built environment is the most clearly *intentional* thing in Aeth, and yet it doesn't look built. It looks grown. Or it looks like something that was built so long ago and so slowly that the distinction between construction and growth has ceased to mean anything.

The structures are layered — stacked upward and outward in accretions that follow the emerging terraces of the valley walls. They are made of a pale, porous stone that the inhabitants cut from the still-soft geology with simple copper-bladed tools. The stone is worked while it is young, before it hardens into its final form, which it will do over decades. This means the builders of Aeth are engaged in a negotiation with their material: they must quarry and shape rock that is not yet fully committed to being rock, and the structures they build will themselves continue to change — slowly settling, slowly hardening, slowly becoming more solid and more brittle over time. Nothing is finished. The walls of a house built ten years ago are still curing. You can still press a thumb into the mortar.

The buildings are mostly greenhouses. This is what gives the place its name. The settlers grow things in enclosed, humid spaces — not for food, or not primarily, but because the growing of things is what they *do*. The greenhouses are tiered, stacked along the valley terraces like amphitheater seats, each one a long low structure of pale stone and translucent membrane — the membrane made from a processed secretion of a local organism, something between a mollusk and a fungus, that lives in the warm pools and can be harvested, stretched, and dried into a material that is not quite glass, not quite skin. It is cloudy. It flexes. It has a faint pulse if you hold it to your ear, though this may be imagination.

Inside the greenhouses: lushness. The word is inadequate. Vegetation here is not a carpet or a canopy but a *volume* — plants growing in three dimensions, in layers, in dense interwoven thickets that fill the interior space the way foam fills a box. The species are not familiar. There are things with leaves that are more stem than leaf, spiraling fractal fronds that grow toward the dim membrane-light. There are things with no leaves at all, just masses of root-tendril that hang from frames and hooks and each other, dangling into the warm mist that fills every greenhouse like held breath. There are things that are almost certainly not plants by any conventional definition — dark, slick, gently undulating masses that may be colonial organisms or may be something else, something the settlers cultivate without fully understanding, the way a medieval farmer might grow mushrooms without knowing what a fungus is.

The density of life inside the greenhouses is extraordinary. Every surface is occupied. Every volume is filled. Nothing is wasted. The air inside is thick, warm, and sweet in a way that borders on cloying — the sweetness of aggressive growth, of chlorophyll and sap and the faintly ammoniac smell of organic processes running hot. You breathe it and feel slightly dizzy, slightly too awake, as though the air itself is photosynthesizing and offering you the excess.

## The People (Rarely Seen)

There are people here. Not many. They move through the settlement slowly, on foot, along narrow paths between the greenhouse terraces. They carry tools — copper blades, woven baskets, hooks made from hardened wood. They wear clothing that has been so thoroughly saturated by the ambient moisture that it has become a kind of second skin, clinging and dark, growing its own fine patina of algae-green at the hems.

They do not speak loudly. They may not speak at all in any way you'd recognize. The sounds of the settlement are mostly the sounds of water and growth — the soft *tick* of droplets falling from membrane-ceilings, the creak of expanding plant-fiber, the wet whisper of tendrils moving against each other. When a human sound occurs — a low word, a footfall, the soft scrape of a copper blade on young stone — it is startling for its rarity, like a bird call in a library.

The people tend the greenhouses. They harvest membrane-secretion from the pools. They cut stone. They do not seem to sleep on any schedule you can identify. They seem to operate on a slower rhythm than you are accustomed to — the rhythm of the place itself, the slow patient dynamic of geology that is still unfinished and biology that is still deciding what it wants to be.

Fauna beyond the settlers and the pool-organisms is scarce. There are things in the water — you see movement beneath the surface of the pools, pale shapes that might be larvae or might be something more developed but still larval in spirit, still becoming. There are small segmented creatures that live in the greenhouse walls, burrowing through the soft stone. There is, occasionally, something that passes overhead through the moisture-veil — you never see it clearly, only the disturbance it makes in the mist, a brief thinning, a silhouette that might be wings or might be fins or might be something that has not yet decided which it prefers.

## The Strange

It is not one thing. It is the accumulation.

It is the Pillars, rising and standing and subsiding, and the way they seem to be more deliberate than natural but less deliberate than built.

It is the membrane, which has a pulse.

It is the warm pools, which have no source of heat you can identify, and which occasionally — very occasionally — produce a sound. Not a bubble. Not a boil. A tone. A single sustained note, low and resonant, that lasts for several minutes and then stops. No one reacts to this. It has always happened. It is part of the place's unfinished acoustics, the sound of something deep and large and still-forming.

It is the way the plants in the greenhouses sometimes grow in patterns that look like writing, and then grow past those patterns into something else.

It is the erosion that isn't happening — or rather, the erosion that hasn't started. In a world this wet, stone should be dissolving, soil should be moving, valleys should be deepening. But the water here is not erosive. It is *additive*. It deposits. It leaves behind a fine mineral residue on every surface it touches, so that over time things in Aeth become coated, layered, encrusted — not worn away but built up, the landscape accumulating rather than diminishing. This is what it means for erosion to be near zero and the geology to be near-zero in formation: the world is not being sculpted. It is being *coated*. Layer by slow layer. Like a pearl forming around a grain of sand, except the pearl is an entire valley and the grain of sand is whatever this place was before it decided to become itself.

## The Light and the Air

Dim, always dim. The moisture-veil diffuses everything. There are no hard shadows. Light arrives from all directions equally, a warm grey-gold glow that makes the pale stone of the greenhouses luminous and the water in the pools shine like pewter. At what might be midday, the light intensifies slightly — you notice it the way you notice a room getting quieter, a subtle shift rather than a change. At what might be night, it dims again, and the greenhouses become dark volumes from which the sounds of growth seem slightly louder, as though the plants make more noise when no one is watching.

The air tastes of mineral and sap. It is thick enough to chew. Moving through it requires a slight extra effort, not because it resists you but because it is *present* in a way that thinner atmospheres are not — you feel it against your face, in your hair, settling on your eyelashes. After some time in Aeth, you stop noticing it the way you stop noticing the weight of your own tongue. It becomes part of the sensory baseline. The ambient. The given.

---

## The Pace

Nothing is fast here. The Pillars rise over days. The stone hardens over decades. The membrane is harvested once per season, if the seasons can be said to exist — there is a cycle, but it is long and gentle, a slow pulse of slightly-warmer and slightly-less-warm, slightly-brighter and slightly-less-bright, that may take months to complete. The people move at the speed of their work, which is the speed of the place. The plants grow at a rate you can almost perceive — not the time-lapse surge of tropical vegetation but something slower, more deliberate, a visible *increment* if you watch a single tendril for an hour.

This is a place that is teaching itself to be a place. The geology is young. The biology is experimenting. The water is doing something that water doesn't usually do. The people are the most finished thing here, and even they seem provisional — as though they are a placeholder for whatever comes next, or whatever was here before and left, or whatever is still forming in the warm pools alongside the pale indeterminate shapes that have not yet decided what they are.

You stand on a terrace between two greenhouses and look out over the valley. The Pillars stand in the middle distance, three of them, motionless for now. The shallow pools catch the light. The moisture-veil shimmers. A shape moves beneath the water, pale and purposeful, going somewhere it has already been.

The air thickens by one more imperceptible degree.

The place continues becoming itself.
