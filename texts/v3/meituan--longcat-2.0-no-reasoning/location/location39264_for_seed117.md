# The Dripworks of Yann

It is never dry here. Not once, not anywhere you can stand.

The ground beneath your feet is a field of pale, open-jointed stone — something like travertine, though no name quite fits — laid down in slow tiers no taller than your knee, descending gently toward a center that holds no visible water and yet is never dry. Water arrives from everywhere except above. It beads from the stone itself, rising through capillary fracture networks you can hear when you press your ear to the ground: a faint, continuous clicking, like a million tiny typewriters. It condenses on every surface in the cool, still air — the temperature sits just above the point where your breath would show, so close to visible that the atmosphere seems to be *considering* becoming fog, perpetually, and never quite deciding. The air tastes of mineral and iron and something older than either, a flat, subterranean sweetness.

The tiers are not terraced by any hand. They have grown here over time scales that make the word "old" feel childish — each rind of deposit laid down by seep-water over epochs, each one catching the last slow ripples of a receding surface that has been receding for longer than most species have existed. The erosion is not violent. It is a conversation between water and stone that has reached equilibrium: the water cuts channels no deeper than a finger's width, and the stone accepts them, and neither side is winning. The surface of every tier is scalloped, pocked, miniature — like the inside of a seashell, if the shell were turned inside-out and made of chalk and bone-white calcium. If you crouch down, the texture becomes a landscape in itself: hollows and ridges, tiny hoodoos no taller than a coin, each one weeping a single slow bead of water from its tip at intervals you can almost count.

There is no plant life. None. Not lichen, not moss, not the faintest thread of root. The surfaces are clean in a way that clean rarely is in nature — *scrubbed*, rather, by water so mineral-heavy it leaves nothing for biology to hold onto. The pH, if you measured it, would be hostile in both directions depending on the minute and the millimeter. Instead of organisms, the stone has **occupants**.

They are small. Most no larger than your thumb. They resemble, depending on your disposition, either mollusks or gears — segmented, jointed things with hard exteriors and soft interiors that extend and retract with slow, mechanical purpose. They are alive in some sense. They move in some sense. But watching them, you are never quite sure if what you are witnessing is biology or something else — a slow mineral process that has learned the shape of a creature, or a creature that has committed so thoroughly to the material of this place that it has become indistinguishable from the rock. They are everywhere. The density is remarkable: turn over any rind of deposited stone and find a dozen of them underneath, packed together like seeds in a pomegranate, each one oriented toward the water source with a precision that feels either instinctive or calculated.

They feed, if that is the word, by filtering the seep-water through bodies that are themselves partially mineral — calcium and silica bound in matrices that blur the line between shell and stone. When they die, they do not decay. They fuse. The floor of the Dripworks is, in places, a composite of dead occupants layered so thickly that the original stone is no longer visible beneath them — a pavement of fused carapaces, pale and smooth, over which the living ones continue their slow procession.

The sound, overall, is near-silence. Not silence — there is always the clicking from below, and the faint *tik-tik-tik* of water beads finding their weight and releasing from stone, a sound so constant it becomes a texture rather than a noise. The occupants make a sound when they move: a soft, ceramic-on-ceramic whisper, like a fingertip drawn across a teacup. When many of them move at once, the whisper becomes a kind of susurrus, just at the edge of language. The place is mostly quiet in the way a library is mostly quiet — full of small sounds that never resolve into noise.

There are structures. Not many. Here and there, half-sunk into the calcium tiers, you find **machines** — or the remains of machines. Something industrial was brought here once, long ago, and the Dripworks has been digesting it. A pump housing, green with mineral accretion, its impeller still visible through a window of fused occupant-shells. A length of pipe, split open and repurposed as a channel for seep-water, now more stone than metal. A control panel, its buttons replaced by slow-growing nodules of calcite that press themselves inward at geological speed, as if the machine is still trying to operate, still trying to execute some program whose purpose has been forgotten by everything except the equipment itself. The tech is industrial-era — heavy, analog, built to last — and it has lasted, but not in any way its builders would recognize. The machines have become part of the water cycle. They are features now, like the hoodoos and the tiers, and the occupants treat them as such, building their fused pavements over and around them with the same indifference they show to natural stone.

The light is dim. There is no sun here — the Dripworks exist in a space that is enclosed but not small, a cavern or a constructed hall whose ceiling is lost in the mineral haze that hangs in the air at about the height of a tall tree. The light comes from the occupants themselves, faintly: a bioluminescence or a chemiluminescence or a piezoelectric glow, something that activates when they move, so that the floor of the Dripworks is a slow field of dim, pale points of light, each one winking on and off as its owner extends and retracts. From a distance — if there were a distance from which to view this place whole — it would look like a low, slow constellation, a galaxy of thumb-sized stars breathing in the dark.

The dynamic quality of the place is what unsettles, if anything does. It is not still. It is not alive in the way a forest is alive, but it is *active* — processes everywhere, all the time, none of them fast. Water arriving. Stone accreting. Occupants filtering, moving, dying, fusing. Machines mineralizing. The whole place is a slow engine running on water and calcium and time, and it has been running for so long that it has developed something that looks, from certain angles, like intention. The water flows where the occupants have built channels. The occupants build channels where the water flows. The machines, half-dissolved, still direct water through their corroded housings in patterns that serve no industrial purpose but serve the Dripworks perfectly. Everything is in motion. Nothing is hurried.

You stand in the Dripworks and you feel, more than anything, that you are standing inside a process. Not a place that a process is happening — a place that *is* a process, that has been happening for so long that the happening has become the thing itself. The water weeps. The stone grows. The occupants click and glow and fuse. The machines dream their slow mineral dreams of function.

It is cool. It is wet. It is quiet.

It is not empty.
