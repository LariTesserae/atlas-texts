# The Plateau of Uncounting

You stand at the edge of a world that has forgotten how to change.

The plateau stretches in every direction — not flat, but *flattened*, as if some enormous patience has been applied across geological ages to grind every sharpness into curve, every ridge into swell, every peak into a long and gentle exhalation of stone. The elevation is real. You feel it in the thinness of breath, in the way sound behaves strangely here, arriving from distances that don't correspond to what your eyes see. But the land itself has been erosion's final student. It has learned every lesson. The rock faces are worn to the smoothness of old bones. Boulders sit in the contours of their final resting positions, rounded so completely they look like eggs, like the idea of eggs, like the memory of something that once had a shape more specific than this.

And everywhere — *everywhere* — there are bones.

Not scattered. Not sparse. Layered. Accumulated. Dense with presence. The plateau is a shelf of calcium and old keratin and mineralized tissue, packed into the compressed sediments of a billion lives that lived and died and were buried and exhumed and buried again across spans of time that make the stone around them seem young. Femurs ribbed against ribs. Skulls nested inside pelvises. Vertebrae strung across the ground like rosaries, like seeds spilled from some impossible pod. The fauna parameter is overwhelming because the *remains* of fauna are overwhelming — this is a graveyard so old it has become geology, a cemetery that has forgotten it was ever a cemetery and is now simply the floor of the world.

But it is not only the dead.

Living things move through the bone-fields in numbers that resist counting. Small things, mostly. Pale crabs with shells the color of the limestone they crawl across — you cannot see them until they move, and then you see that the ground *itself* is moving, a slow churn of carapace and leg. Beetles. Millipedes. Things that are perhaps centipedes but with too many segments, a little too many, just enough that you stop trying to track the count. Birds — not flocks in the traditional sense, but a *density* of individual birds, each one occupying its own few square feet of bone-field, standing on one leg or pecking at marrow-dust or simply watching with an intensity that feels personal. They do not startle when you approach. They watch you the way a crowd watches a stranger who has not yet decided to speak.

The warmth is present but strange. It does not come from the sun, which hangs in a sky the color of old linen — present, luminous, but somehow diffused, as if the light has been aged the way everything else here has been aged, worn to a softness that illuminates without revealing. The warmth rises from the ground itself, from the slow chemical conversations happening in the bone-sediment, from the respiration of uncountable small organisms metabolizing the deep past. It smells faintly of ammonia and mineral water and something sweeter underneath — the sweetness of compost, of matter returning itself to usefulness.

Water appears in the way the dampness parameter suggests: not as rivers or lakes but as *occasional*. Seeps. Condensation that gathers in the concave surfaces of old skulls — each one holding a small, still mirror of sky. Damp patches where the bone-field gives way to bare stone and the stone glistens, not with flowing water but with the memory of it, a sheen that never quite dries. On some boulders, lichen holds moisture in its body like a cactus holds it in its flesh — a closed system, a self-contained wetness that exists in the middle of all this dryness. When it rains, which is rare and brief and feels almost accidental, the water has nowhere to go. It pools in the topography of the dead — in eye sockets, in the cups of vertebrae, in the long channels of ribcages — and sits there for days afterward, evaporating slowly, feeding the organisms that live in the standing water and the organisms that live in the wet bone beneath it.

The vegetation is sparse but not absent. It grows in the places where bone has become soil, which is to say in small pockets, in patches no larger than a hand, in the occasional crack where enough organic matter has accumulated to support a root. The plants are low and tough and old-looking even when they are young — gray-green things with thick leaves that store water, with stems that lignify almost immediately, as if they have learned the same lesson the stone learned: that survival here means becoming durable, means slowing down, means choosing persistence over growth. Some of them grow directly out of the bone-fields, their roots threading through marrow cavities, drawing calcium from the dead to build their own small stubborn bodies. They look like sagebrush that has been compressed, concentrated, distilled to its most essential argument for continuing to exist.

The built elements are scattered and ambiguous. Here, a wall — or the memory of a wall — three courses of stone laid with enough intention to suggest a structure, now half-reclaimed by the bone-sediment that has drifted against it like snow. There, a post, or a pillar, or a standing stone, worn to a nub, its purpose either forgotten or never known. No roads. No foundations. Just the faintest marks of someone who was here once, or several someones, or perhaps just the land itself arranging stone in a pattern that the human eye reads as intention because the human eye cannot bear a world without it.

The weirdness lives in the details. It is the wrongness of the birds' silence — two thousand birds on a plateau and the sound is a whisper. It is the way the crabs sometimes gather in patterns that look almost like writing, then disperse, then gather again in different patterns that look like the same writing from a different angle. It is the dampness that appears in places where no water source exists, that beads on the surface of a skull found in the driest part of the plateau, as if the skull is sweating. It is the sense that the bones are not randomly distributed — that there is a gradient to them, a density that increases as you move toward the center of the plateau, as if something drew all these lives inward, as if the plateau is a drain and everything that lived on it was slowly, slowly spiraling toward a point of collection that has been operating for longer than the bones can record.

The quiet is not empty. It is *full* — full of small sounds that almost cancel each other out. The click of crab-shell on bone. The subsonic rumble of the plateau settling, a sound you feel in your sternum before you hear it. The almost-silence of birds breathing. The wet sound of lichen expanding as it absorbs condensation. Each sound is small and the sum of them is a hush, a held breath, the acoustic equivalent of the landscape itself: dense with presence, quiet with age.

Time moves slowly here. The crabs do not hurry. The birds do not migrate — or if they do, they do it on a timescale that makes a human visit irrelevant. The bone-field accumulates at a rate measurable only in centuries. The dampness comes and goes in cycles that might be seasonal or might be something longer, something tied to the deep breathing of the plateau itself, to the slow thermal expansion and contraction of all this calcium and stone. Nothing is urgent. Nothing has been urgent for a very long time.

You are standing in a place that has reached its final form. The erosion has finished its work. The bones have found their arrangement. The living things have found their niches in the architecture of the dead. The water comes and goes like a visitor who is no longer expected but is still welcomed. The warmth persists. The light persists. The quiet persists.

It is not a place that is ending. It is a place that has already ended and is now simply *being* — a landscape that has passed through all its changes and arrived at this: a warm, high, quiet shelf of bone and stone and small persistent life, holding its dampness in skull-cups, watched by birds that do not blink, crabs that write and unwrite their patterns in the dust of the ancient dead, and the slow, slow, slow settling of everything into whatever comes next, which, given the patience of this place, may not come for a very long time.
