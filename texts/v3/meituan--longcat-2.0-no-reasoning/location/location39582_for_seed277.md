# The Rusting Plateaus of Vael Korum

---

You stand on a shelf of dark stone that extends in every direction beyond the reach of sight. The plateau is not flat — it is *settled*, worn into long undulations like the surface of a sea that froze mid-swell a hundred million years ago. The rock is iron-rich, a deep umber-black that oxidizes in patches to a flaking vermillion, so that the ground appears to be perpetually bleeding and healing, bleeding and healing, in a cycle too slow to watch but too persistent to ignore. This is old country. The erosion here is not dramatic — no fresh cliffs, no scree slopes still finding their angle. Everything has found its angle. The gullies are soft-shouldered. The drainage channels have carved their final paths and now simply *occupy* them, like rivers that have forgotten they were ever in a hurry.

The elevation is significant. You feel it in the thinness of breath, in the way sound dies quickly here — not absorbed, but simply *released* into a sky that is too large and too empty to carry it back. The air is warm, though. Not hot — warm in the way of a room that has been closed for hours, a stored warmth, a heat that has settled into the stone and radiates back at you from below. The temperature does not fluctuate much. There is a steadiness to it, as though the plateau itself is a thermal mass that has reached equilibrium with some deep, slow engine.

And there is an engine. You can feel it before you understand it — a subsonic hum, just below the threshold of hearing, more a pressure in the chest than a sound in the ear. It comes from below. It comes from *everywhere* below.

---

The water here is not a presence so much as a *tendency*. It does not pool. It does not run in any channel you can follow for more than a few hundred meters. Instead, it *appears* — condensation beading on the undersides of overhangs, seepage lines tracing vertical paths down exposed rock faces, thin films of moisture that turn the ironstone slick and dark. After rain — and it does rain, intermittently, without pattern you can discern — the entire plateau darkens by two shades, and the oxidation accelerates visibly, the red patches spreading like bruises, and the air fills with a smell that is unmistakably, primordially *metal*. Not blood. Something older than blood. The smell of the planet's interior making polite contact with its surface.

In the lowest points of the undulations, where the worn stone bowls inward, water collects in shallow depressions no larger than a dinner plate and no deeper than a knuckle. These are not puddles. They are *lenses* — perfectly still, perfectly clear, reflecting a sky that is almost always dark. The light here is minimal. The sun, if there is a sun, is either distant, dim, or occluded by a permanent high haze that diffuses whatever light reaches the surface into a uniform, directionless gray-gold. Shadows are suggestions. Depth is inferred. You navigate by texture more than by sight.

---

The vegetation is sparse but *specific*. It grows in the seepage lines — dark, filamentous organisms that are not quite moss and not quite fungus, a deep green so dark it reads as black unless you are close enough to see the way it catches the thin light. It grows in single-file colonies along the water traces, following the moisture downhill with a patience that makes the rock look impulsive by comparison. In the shallow lenses, there are films — biological, certainly, but so thin and so still that they appear to be a property of the water itself, an iridescence that shifts between oil-slick violet and a deep, arterial copper.

There are larger organisms. In the rare places where soil has accumulated — centuries of the filamentous growth dying and compressing into a substrate no thicker than a finger — there are structures that might be called plants. They are low, radial, symmetrical in ways that suggest neither urgency nor ambition. They do not reach upward. They *spread* outward, flat against the stone, their surfaces textured to trap moisture, their colors ranging from the same dark green-black to a surprising, almost luminous rust-orange that matches the oxidized stone so perfectly that you step on them before you see them.

---

The fauna is abundant, but abundance here does not mean *visible*. It means *present*. The plateau is alive with movement at scales you have to adjust to perceive. The first thing you notice, if you stand still long enough — and stillness is the only way to perceive this place — is that the surface is *crawling*. Not with insects, exactly. With organisms that occupy a space between insect and mineral. They are small — the largest no bigger than a thumbnail — and they move with a mechanical precision that makes the industrial hum from below feel less metaphorical and more *familial*. Their bodies are segmented, chitinous, dark. Many of them are the exact color of the ironstone. Some are the exact color of the rust. They feed on the filamentous growth, on the biological films, on each other. They are the dominant consumers here, and they are *everywhere*, but they are so well-matched to the substrate that the plateau appears empty until the moment it doesn't.

Larger animals exist. You see evidence before you see them — scrape marks on the stone, too regular to be erosion; shallow burrows in the thin soil, their entrances rimmed with the same rust-orange growth, as though the organisms are cultivating it. The animals themselves are elusive. They are fast, low-slung, warm-blooded enough to maintain activity in the thin air. They have too many legs — six, eight, sometimes more — and they move with a fluid, lateral gait that makes them look like they are *flowing* across the stone rather than running. Their eyes, when you catch a glimpse, are large and dark and reflective, adapted to the low light, and they do not reflect the gray-gold of the sky. They reflect something *redder*. Something from below.

---

Because below is where the strangeness lives.

The industrial hum is not metaphorical. The plateau is *hollow* — not in the way of caves, but in the way of a shell. Beneath the settled, weathered, ancient surface, there is machinery. Not human machinery. Not alien machinery. Something that occupies the conceptual space where those two ideas overlap and then diverge. It is vast. It is old. It is *still running*.

You find the access points by accident — places where the stone has worn through to reveal a surface beneath that is not stone. It is dark metal, pitted and corroded but *structured*, with geometries that suggest function without revealing it. Grids. Conduits. Channels that might carry fluid or energy or something for which there is no word. In some places, the metal is warm to the touch — warmer than the stone, warmer than the air — and if you press your ear against it (and the sound level here is low enough that you can), you hear movement inside. Not mechanical movement. Not exactly. Something that pulses. Something that *breathes* in a rhythm that is almost, but not quite, biological.

The machinery does not appear to be maintained. It appears to be *self-sustaining* — or perhaps it has simply been running so long that maintenance and operation have become the same thing. The corrosion on the exposed metal is not random. It follows patterns. It *grows*. The rust spreads in fractal branches that mirror, at a different scale, the drainage channels on the surface above. The oxidation is not decay. It is *metabolism*. The plateau is not a landscape with a machine beneath it. The plateau *is* the machine — or the machine is the plateau, and the distinction between geology and engineering has been erased by time.

---

The weather here is local and self-contained, as befits a vast elevated surface with its own thermal properties. The warm stone heats the thin air, which rises, drawing in moisture from — somewhere. The source of the water is unclear. It may be that the machinery below produces it as a byproduct, venting steam through fissures that have been widened over millennia into something like pores. The moisture condenses on the cooler upper surfaces of the stone, feeds the filamentous growth, feeds the films, feeds the small chitinous organisms, which feed the larger ones, which die and decompose and feed the growth again. The cycle is closed. The cycle is *ancient*. It has been running as long as the machinery, which is to say: long enough that no one remembers a time before it.

Rain falls without clouds you can see — the moisture simply *arrives*, condensing out of the warm, still air in a fine, directionless mist that darkens the stone and accelerates the rust and sends the small organisms into a frenzy of activity that lasts for exactly as long as the moisture persists. Then it stops. The stone dries. The organisms settle. The hum from below continues, unchanged.

---

The soundscape is dominated by that hum and by silence. The silence is not empty — it is *textured*, filled with the tiny, dry sounds of chitin on stone, the almost-inaudible crack of oxidation advancing, the whisper of the filamentous growth expanding by microns. When the larger animals move, they are quiet — their bodies are adapted to silence, their footpads soft, their movements deliberate. The only loud sound, and it is not loud so much as *deep*, is the occasional exhalation of a vent — a low, resonant tone that you feel in your sternum and that sends a visible shudder through the nearest water lenses, concentric rings expanding outward in perfect symmetry before the stillness reasserts itself.

---

The weirdness here is not spectacular. It is *structural*. It is the wrongness of a place where the boundary between the made and the grown has dissolved so completely that you cannot find it. The machinery below is not built on the stone — it is *of* the stone, or the stone is *of* the machinery, and the organisms on the surface have evolved — or been designed, or *emerged* — to live in the space between those two truths. The rust is both corrosion and nutrient. The hum is both engine and heartbeat. The water is both coolant and rain. The darkness is both absence of light and *presence* of something else — a quality of the air itself, a density, a weight, as though the atmosphere here is slightly more *substantial* than it should be, carrying more than just gas.

You are standing on something alive. You have been standing on it the entire time. It does not mind. It has been here longer than mind, longer than matter as you understand it. It is warm and dark and damp and vast and it is *running*, has always been running, will continue to run long after you have become part of the thin soil, food for the filamentous growth, a trace of moisture in the stone.

The plateau extends in every direction. The hum continues. The rust spreads. The small things crawl. The large things watch from the dark.

There is nowhere else to go. There has never been anywhere else to go.
