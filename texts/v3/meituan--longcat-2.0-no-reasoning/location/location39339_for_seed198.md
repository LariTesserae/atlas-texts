# The Weeping Terraces

Something has been built here that has been allowed to grow.

The elevation is significant — the air thins just enough to make each breath a conscious act, and the cool temperature presses against skin like a damp cloth. This is high country, but not barren. Not wind-scoured. Instead, the altitude serves as a catch-basin for weather systems that roll in from distant lowlands, and the place holds moisture the way a sponge holds water: not at capacity, not dry, but perpetually, uncomfortably saturated. The relative humidity never drops below seventy percent. Surfaces weep. Stone — though there is very little exposed stone — glistens.

Because everything here is built.

Not constructed in the way you might think. Not a city. Not a factory complex, though factories are present, woven through the landscape like organs in a body. The *total* built quality of this place means that the ground itself is engineered — layered substrates of compressed aggregate, ceramic tile, rammed earth stabilized with lime, living mycelium boards that slowly digest waste and exhale warm CO₂ into the already close atmosphere. In some districts, you can see the seams between construction generations: Roman-style concrete giving way to Victorian brick giving way to sintered lunar regolith giving way to something that looks like grown bone, honeycombed and pale. The oldest substrates have been here so long — *erosion is established, the place has found its form* — that they've weathered into a kind of functional geology. People walk on them and call them ground. They are.

And on this built ground, the vegetation has achieved a kind of victory.

Lush doesn't begin to cover it. The density parameter here is *three* — packed, saturated, no room between presences — and this applies as much to plant life as to everything else. Ferns erupt from every horizontal surface. Moss has colonized the upper faces of structural beams in thick, pendulous beards that sway in the thermal updrafts from factory exhaust. Vines — not the polite, decorative kind, but thick, muscular specimens with stems as wide as a forearm — have claimed the upper terraces, pulling down awnings, wrapping around crane housings, splitting gutters open with patient hydraulic force. The water that weeps from every surface feeds them. The warm exhaust from the industrial infrastructure feeds them. The light — dim, filtered through a permanent haze of condensation and factory vapor — is apparently enough, because the green here has a desperate, reaching quality, every leaf oriented toward whatever photons might be available.

The industrial element is not separate from the rest. It is the skeleton beneath the flesh. Engines hum and clank in the lower terraces, where the gradient steepens and the built environment reveals its original logic: this was a processing district once, a place where raw materials came in on the low roads and finished goods departed from the high ramps. The machines are still running. Some of them have been running so long that the original purpose is forgotten, and the output — stamped metal fittings, extruded polymer tubes, something that might be ceramic insulators — is simply fed back into the system as raw material elsewhere. The economy of the place has become circular, not by design but by sheer duration.

And the dynamism — the *turbulence* — is the thing that makes it breathe.

This is not a static landscape. It is a landscape in a permanent state of becoming. Things are being built and demolished simultaneously. A crew might be pouring a new foundation on the east terrace while, three meters away, an older wall is being dismantled by a swarm of small, purpose-built machines that look like beetles and move with coordinated, almost biological precision. Smoke rises from a smelting operation on the seventh terrace. Water cascades down a channel cut through the sixth. A scaffold goes up. A scaffold comes down. The sound of it — mostly quiet, but with a persistent low-frequency mechanical murmur underlying everything, like a digestive system — gives the place its voice.

The fauna is rare. A few birds — hardy, crow-like things with iridescent oil-slick plumage — nest in the upper reaches where the infrastructure thins and the vegetation takes over entirely. Insects are present but not abundant; something in the industrial effluent keeps their populations in check. You might see a rat. You will not see anything larger.

But the people — if you can call them that, the inhabitants, the ones who maintain and are maintained by this place — they are everywhere. They move through the terraces with the confidence of creatures in a habitat they know intimately. They wear damp clothing. They carry tools. They step over roots and duck under vine curtains and pause at the edge of a terrace to look out over the valley below, where the mist pools in the folds of the built landscape like water in a cupped hand, and the green rises in every direction, and the machines keep turning, and the place keeps weeping, and the light stays dim, and the world — this world, this one, fully made and fully alive and fully strange in its familiarity — goes on.
