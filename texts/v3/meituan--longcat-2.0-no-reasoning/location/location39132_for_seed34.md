# The Spine of Weeping Cities

---

There is a range that has no name in any living language. The old ones died along with the people who built them.

Stand at the base of the eastern face, where the foothills finally give up pretending to be land, and look up. The first thing that strikes you is the wrongness of the height — these mountains shouldn't be here. The rock is sedimentary, marine in origin, ancient seabed thrust upward by a collision so long ago that even the mountains have forgotten it happened. They are exhausted mountains. They have settled into themselves. Every ridge line is rounded, every cliff face softened by the long patience of water doing what water does, which is arrive and arrive and arrive and never leave.

The elevation is enough to make your breath come shallow but not enough to make you gasp. It is the altitude of perpetual headache, of mild nausea that you learn to carry. Above you, the peaks disappear into a haze that is not clouds exactly — more like the air itself has given up being transparent. Condensation. The whole range breathes moisture. Water is not falling from the sky. Water is *rising* from the rock.

Run your hand along any wall of exposed stone and it will come away wet. Not damp. Wet. The rock is saturated from within, fed by aquifers deep in the crust that have found their way to every fissure, every pore, every microscopic channel in the limestone and dolomite. The mountains are weeping. Slowly. Continuously. Each surface holds a sheen of moisture that catches the light and throws it back in ways that make the whole range look lacquered, varnished, like something finished and unfinished at the same time.

The cities cling to the eastern slopes. You can see them from a distance if you know where to look — not because they announce themselves but because they *don't*. They were built to disappear into the rock, and mostly they have succeeded. There are hundreds of them, perhaps thousands, depending on how you count a settlement. Some are carved directly into cliff faces, their doorways opening onto nothing but air and the long drop to the valley floor below. Others are built of stone quarried from the mountains themselves, so that the construction and the terrain are the same material, the same color, the same degree of saturation. From a distance the cities are stains on the rock face. Closer, they reveal themselves as architecture — stairways, terraces, covered walkways, buildings stacked upon buildings in a vertical sprawl that follows the contours of the cliffs with an intimacy that suggests either worship or surrender.

The density is staggering. Not in the horizontal sense — the cities are narrow, pressed against the rock like lichen — but vertically and structurally. Every available surface has been built upon. Walkways bridge gaps between buildings that were constructed centuries apart and have since grown into each other, their walls fused by mineral deposits left behind by the constant water. In places you can walk for miles without touching the ground, moving through a continuous built environment of stairs, corridors, bridges, rooms, and plazas that has accreted over centuries the way coral accretes, organically and without plan. The infrastructure is layered — newer construction on older construction on older construction, each generation building on top of or into or through the work of the last, until the original shape of any structure is impossible to determine.

There is no technology here. Not in the sense of devices, mechanisms, the intervention of tools between the human hand and the material world. What there is instead is an almost incomprehensible sophistication of stone-work. The builders understood rock the way a cellist understands their instrument — not through analysis but through long intimacy. They knew which stone would hold and which would crumble. They knew how water moved through the range and how to channel it. They knew which cliff faces would shed water and which would absorb it. The cities are hydraulic systems as much as they are settlements. Channels cut into the rock direct the weeping water into cisterns, into irrigation for the thin gardens that cling to terraces, into the public fountains that are the center of every neighborhood. The water is the city's blood. Without the channels, the cities would drown in their own medium.

The temperature is cool. It is always cool. The moisture in the air holds the temperature steady — it never gets hot, never gets cold. It stays at that narrow band of coolness that makes you aware of your own body, that keeps you slightly uncomfortable in a way that never quite becomes unbearable. You dress in layers. Everyone dresses in layers. The clothing is made from fiber plants that grow in the terraces — coarse, durable, slightly stiff with mineral content from the water. Colors are muted. Everything is the gray-white of wet stone, the gray-green of the sparse vegetation, the deep gray-brown of the soil that exists in pockets where enough organic matter has accumulated to call itself by that name.

Because the vegetation is sparse. The rock is too steep, too saturated, too mineral-rich for most things that grow. What does grow has adapted to the conditions with the grim determination of organisms that have no choice. Low shrubs with leathery leaves. Mosses that form thick mats on the north-facing surfaces, so dense they look like felt, so saturated they squelch underfoot. Lichen — endless lichen, in every shade of gray and green and yellow-orange, painting the rock faces in patterns that shift with the moisture content, darkening when wet, going pale and almost white when they dry (which is rare). Small ferns in the crevices. That is the ecology, more or less. A handful of plant species that have carved out an existence in the narrow margins between rock and water, and that is enough to hold the thin soil in place, to provide fiber and medicine and the occasional fruit, to give the landscape a texture that isn't purely mineral.

The light is normal. That is to say, there is a sun, and it rises and sets, and the days have the length you would expect at this latitude. But the light is *filtered*. The moisture in the air softens it, scatters it, takes away its edges. Shadows are indistinct. Distances are hard to judge. The whole range exists in a kind of luminous haze, bright enough to see by but never quite bright enough to see *clearly*. On the western face, which catches the afternoon light, the cities glow. The wet stone reflects the softened sunlight back at itself, and the cities become luminous, almost translucent, as if they are not built of stone at all but of light held in place by habit.

The fauna are rare. Not absent — you will see them if you are patient, if you are quiet, if you know where to look. Small birds that nest in the cliff faces, their mud-and-fiber constructions adhered to the rock under overhangs. Insects, mostly — beetles and stoneflies and the small pale creatures that live in the moss. Bats in the deeper caves. Nothing large. Nothing that needs much. The ecology runs on a thin margin, sustained by the moisture and the mild temperature and the slow accumulation of organic matter, but it does not run far. A disruption — a drought, a freeze, a disease — would collapse it quickly. The system is balanced the way a pencil is balanced on its tip: perfectly, and precariously.

The sound is quiet. This is perhaps the most striking quality of the place, and the one that takes the longest to notice, because it is not silence. Silence would be noticeable, would announce itself, would become oppressive. What the range offers instead is a continuous low sound that is below the threshold of attention — the sound of water moving through stone. It is not the sound of a stream or a waterfall. It is the sound of moisture seeping through limestone at the molecular level, the sound of droplets forming on cave ceilings, the sound of water finding its way through channels cut centuries ago, still flowing, still doing what it was directed to do. It is a sound more felt than heard, a vibration in the rock that enters your body through your feet and stays there, a hum at the edge of perception that you stop noticing until you leave and its absence becomes a ringing in your ears.

The dynamic quality is weather. The range makes its own weather — not dramatically, not the violent storms of higher, drier mountains, but a constant cycle of mist and clearing and mist again. Clouds form against the western face and move through the cities, reducing visibility to a few hundred meters, then dissipate, then form again. Rain falls often but lightly — not storms but the sky simply releasing the moisture it has been holding, the way a sponge releases water when it is squeezed. The whole range is a sponge. The whole range is being squeezed, constantly, by the pressure of the air, by the weight of the water in the aquifers, by the slow geological forces that are still, after all these millions of years, pushing the range upward even as the water pulls it down.

And the cities are changing. Slowly — so slowly that a lifetime is not enough to see it — but changing. The water is winning, has always been winning, will always be winning. New channels form in the rock, old channels are blocked by mineral deposits. Buildings shift. Terraces settle. Walls crack and are repaired with stone that is already saturated, that will crack in its own turn. The cities are not being destroyed. They are being *digested*, absorbed back into the rock they were carved from, returning to the geological cycle with the same patience with which they were extracted. In a thousand years, the cities will be ruins. In ten thousand years, they will be stains on the rock. In a hundred thousand years, there will be nothing to distinguish the places where humans built from the places where they didn't. Only the channels will remain, perhaps, deeper in the rock, water still following paths cut by hands that have been dust for ages.

But that is a long time. For now, the cities hold. Water drips from every surface. The moss is green. Somewhere in the built mass of the eastern face, a person is walking home along a stairway that descends through seven levels of construction, past a fountain that has been running for four centuries, through a corridor where the rock walls are so close they must turn sideways, out onto a bridge that spans a chasm of mist and dropping water and the sound that is not quite sound, into a doorway that opens onto a room lit by a shaft of filtered light falling through a ventilation shaft that also serves as a rain channel, and the room is warm enough, and dry enough, and the person is home.
