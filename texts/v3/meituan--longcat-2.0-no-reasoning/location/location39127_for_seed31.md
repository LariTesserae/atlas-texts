# The Weeping Terraces of Oth

From a distance, the settlement looks like it is dissolving.

The terraces climb a broad volcanic shoulder — not a peak, but the wide, sloping back of an ancient shield volcano that rises above a permanent haze line. The elevation is enough that the air has a thin, bright quality, but the warmth keeps it from feeling cold. It feels like standing in a greenhouse with the glass removed: humid, close, the temperature hovering at that point where your skin can't decide if it wants to sweat or simply accept the moisture as ambient.

The rock here is young. That is the first thing a geologist would notice, and the first thing everyone who lives here has internalized without knowing the word for it. The basalt columns are still sharp-edged, still showing the hexagonal fracturing of their cooling, not yet rounded by millennia of wind. Lava tubes have collapsed in places to reveal fresh, glassy interiors — surfaces that catch light like obsidian mirrors and throw it back in flat, cold colors that don't belong in this warm air. The terrain has the look of something recently poured and only half-set. Ridges are knife-edged. Gullies are straight-walled. Nothing has been softened.

And everything is wet.

Not raining. Not flooded. *Weeping.* The volcanic rock is porous at this elevation — a sponge of connected vesicles and fractured tubes — and somewhere below, a deep aquifer presses upward against the weight of the mountain's own stone. The water doesn't emerge in springs or streams. It simply appears on every surface. The basalt walls glisten. The ground between the terraces is dark and slick, not muddy exactly, but perpetually damp, as though the earth itself is perspiring. In the mornings — though morning is a loose concept here, the light being diffuse and steady — you can see the moisture beading on vertical rock faces, running in slow, irregular lines downward, collecting in carved channels that the inhabitants have widened into gutters.

The settlement occupies perhaps forty terraces cut into the slope, each one a flat platform of packed volcanic gravel and fitted stone, each one separated from the next by a retaining wall of stacked basalt blocks. The walls are the most impressive construction here — not because they are ornate, but because they are *precise*. The blocks are fitted without mortar, each one shaped by hand to sit flush against its neighbors, and the walls have stood for — well, for however long they've stood. Time feels negotiable here. The walls look almost new. The stone hasn't weathered. Lichen hasn't taken hold, or if it has, the constant moisture washes it away before it can establish. The retaining walls are dark and clean and sharp-angled, and water runs down their faces in thin, continuous sheets.

On the terraces themselves, the buildings are low and broad — single-story structures of the same basalt, with roofs of some material that isn't stone. Woven plant-fiber, perhaps, or pressed bark, or something else that grows in the lower elevations and is carried up. The roofs are steeply pitched to shed the constant damp, and they drip. Everything drips. The sound of the settlement, if you could call it a sound, is a soft, irregular pattering — not rain, but the redistribution of moisture from surface to surface, roof to terrace, terrace to gutter, gutter to the carved channels that run along the edges of each platform and spill over the retaining walls in thin waterfalls to the level below.

The vegetation is present but not dominant. It grows in the cracks — ferns mostly, and some kind of broad-leafed plant with waxy green surfaces that seem designed for this exact environment. Moss, but not the soft kind: something more structural, more like a mat, that clings to the north-facing walls and the undersides of overhangs. The plants are lush in their small territories, vivid green against the dark rock, but they don't sprawl. The settlement has been kept clear of them, or the constant foot traffic on the terraces prevents anything from taking deep root. The greenery exists in the margins — in the gaps between walls, in the drainage channels, on the outer edges of the platforms where the retaining walls meet the raw slope.

The terraces are spaced far apart as you move up the slope — not every level is inhabited. Some are empty platforms, their purpose unclear. Storage, perhaps, or gathering spaces, or simply the result of a settlement that grew in fits and starts and left gaps. The overall density is low. You can stand on one terrace and see the next occupied level a hundred steps above, and between them there is only the bare slope, the weeping rock, the thin channels of running water, and the empty platforms like missing teeth in a jaw.

The light is strange only in its steadiness. There are no sharp shadows. The moisture in the air diffuses everything — the sun, when it's above the haze, becomes a bright area of sky rather than a point source, and the whole landscape is lit evenly, from all directions, as though the atmosphere itself is luminous. Colors are muted but not gray. The basalt is a deep charcoal. The water is black where it pools and silver where it moves. The vegetation is a green so saturated it almost hums. The sky, when visible, is a pale, washed blue-white, like the inside of an oyster shell.

There are no animals. This is the detail that takes longest to notice and the one that, once noticed, changes everything.

No birds. No insects. No lizards on the warm stone. No fish in the channels. The water runs clear and empty. The ferns grow without being eaten. The silence is not the silence of a place where animals are hiding — it is the silence of a place where the concept of animal has no foothold. The only movement is water and growth. The only sound is water and wind. The wind itself is minimal — the air is heavy with moisture and moves slowly, if at all, carrying the faint mineral smell of wet volcanic rock and the greener, more organic smell of the ferns.

The people — and there are people, this is a settlement, this is *urban* by the standards of this world — move through the terraces with a kind of deliberate calm. They carry water in vessels even though water is everywhere, which suggests ritual or preference rather than necessity. They tend the plants in the margins. They maintain the walls, replacing blocks that have shifted, clearing channels that have clogged with mineral deposits. They sit on the edges of terraces with their feet above the drop and look out over the haze below, where the lower slopes disappear into white.

What they are looking at, or for, is unclear.

The settlement has the feeling of a place that has reached equilibrium and stopped. Not declining. Not growing. *Finished.* The terraces are all carved. The walls are all built. The channels all run. The water comes, and the water goes, and the people maintain what is there, and nothing new is added, and nothing old is removed. The dynamic is zero. The place is a system in perfect balance — input equals output, construction equals decay, the weeping of the rock equals the evaporation of the air. It is a place that has solved itself.

And from a distance, from below, from the haze line looking up, it looks like the mountain is crying, and the tears have learned to build houses.
