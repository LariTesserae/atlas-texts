# The Burrow-Market of Keth-Avani

You are standing in a space that should not exist.

The ceiling is low — perhaps three meters above your head — and it is alive. Not metaphorically. The rock itself is a single organism, a calcified fungal mat the color of old bone, and it breathes. You can see it: a slow, tidal expansion and contraction, the surface rippling with pores that open and close like the spiracles of some vast sleeping animal. The warmth comes from it. The air is thick, almost chewable, held at a steady temperature that makes your skin prickle with sweat almost immediately. There is no wind. There is no sky. There is only this.

The space is roughly circular, maybe forty meters across, and every centimeter of it is occupied.

**The structures** are built from what the ceiling sheds — plates of the calcified fungal shell, broken off during the breathing cycles and harvested by the inhabitants. They are stacked and mortared with a dark resin that smells like burnt sugar and ammonia. The buildings are low, hunched, pressed against each other like teeth in a jaw. They lean. They interlock. Doorways are oval and too small; you would have to crawl. Windows are gaps in the stacking, irregular, stuffed with woven fiber or left open to the constant dim bioluminescence that seeps from the ceiling tissue itself — a blue-grey glow, the color of a bruise in its third day, just enough to see by if you let your eyes adjust.

And you are not alone here. You are not even close to alone.

**The fauna** is the first thing that truly registers, because it is everywhere, because it is the reason this place exists, because it *is* this place as much as the architecture is.

They are arthropods. Not insects — something older, something with too many segments and not enough symmetry. The largest are the size of your forearm, armored in overlapping chitin plates the color of dried blood, with too many legs that move in waves, and mandibles that click in constant, arrhythmic conversation. They are the builders, the carriers, the ones who stack the plates and spread the resin. They move through the space in dense rivers along the walls, up the sides of structures, across the ceiling itself, hanging upside down in clusters so thick the bone-rock disappears beneath them. Their clicking is the dominant sound — not rhythmic, not musical, but *dense*, a constant percussive chatter that fills the air like rain on a tin roof, rising and falling in waves that seem to correspond to nothing you can identify.

Smaller things move between them. Things with translucent bodies. Things that glow faintly green. Things that are not individuals but chains — linked organisms, ten or twenty bodies long, flowing through gaps in the architecture like living rope, leaving trails of moisture that evaporate almost instantly in the warmth. They are the reason there is vegetation here at all.

**The vegetation** grows in the moisture. It is not plants as you would recognize them — no leaves, no stems, no roots in soil. It is a film, a crust, a fur. It covers every surface that the resin doesn't seal: the lower walls, the undersides of plate-stacks, the dark corners where the arthropods don't travel as densely. It is deep green, almost black in the dim light, and it is *lush* — thick, layered, competing with itself for surface area. In places it hangs in curtains, heavy with absorbed humidity from the breathing ceiling. In places it has been deliberately cultivated, trained across frames of chitin-bone into living walls that divide rooms, that filter air, that are harvested and eaten. You can smell it: a green, sharp, living smell, like crushed ferns, like the inside of a greenhouse, cutting through the ammonia and the burnt sugar and the deeper, muskier smell of the arthropod colonies.

The floor is uneven — not paved, not flattened, but *worn*. Worn by millions of feet over a time scale you cannot intuit. The stone here is not the ceiling-bone but something older, something that was already ancient when the fungal organism colonized it. It has been polished to a dull sheen in the traffic paths, and in the low-traffic areas it is covered in a thick accumulation of shed chitin, broken shell-plate, resin drippings, and the compressed residue of centuries of organic life. It is soft underfoot. It gives.

**The sound** is overwhelming if you stop and actually listen. The clicking is the bass note. Above it: a constant rustling, the sound of chitin on chitin, of bodies moving against bodies, of the chain-organisms sliding through their channels. A wet sound, somewhere — the vegetation being harvested, torn, chewed. A deeper thrum that you feel in your sternum more than hear, the resonance of the ceiling's breathing amplified by the enclosed space. And occasionally, sharply, a single loud *crack* — a plate falling, a structure settling, the sound of the architecture adjusting itself.

**The dynamic** of the place is what strikes you last, because it takes time to see it. Nothing here is static. The arthropods are constantly building, unbuilding, rebuilding — a structure that was a wall an hour ago is now a ramp, now a bridge between two taller stacks, now dismantled entirely and the plates carried away to somewhere else. The vegetation grows visibly if you watch it — not fast, but *present*, the film thickening, the curtains lengthening, the cultivated walls needing constant trimming. The ceiling breathes. The chain-organisms flow. The light shifts in intensity with the breathing cycle, brightening slightly on the inhale as the pores open and the bioluminescence intensifies, dimming on the exhale. The temperature fluctuates by a degree or two with the same rhythm. Everything is in motion. Everything is being maintained, adjusted, consumed, rebuilt.

This is not a ruin. This is not a nest. This is a *city* — dense, ancient in its patterns though perhaps not in its materials, organized by intelligence that is distributed across thousands of bodies, built by hands that are not hands, in a space that is itself alive, under a sky that is flesh, in a world where the boundary between architecture and organism, between builder and building, between the living and the constructed, has never existed.

You are very small here. The space is very small. And it is full — so full — of life that does not care whether you are watching.
