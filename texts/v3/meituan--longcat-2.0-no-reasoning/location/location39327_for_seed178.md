# The Drownings

You are standing in a place that should not be able to hold this much life.

The ground is wrong. Not wrong like broken — wrong like *new*. The rock beneath the water is sharp-edged, freshly fractured, as though the earth cracked open sometime last week and hasn't yet learned to be weathered. Basalt columns rise at odd angles, their hexagonal faces still crisp, still catching light in geometric facets. Some are warm to the touch. Some are cold enough to burn. The fractures between them are narrow and deep, and from these fractures, water rises — not flowing, not seeping, but *expressing*, as though the rock is a membrane and something vast and pressurized is on the other side.

The water is everywhere. It is ankle-deep in most places, knee-deep in others, and it is not still. It moves in contradictory directions simultaneously — pooling in one spot while spiraling outward from another, climbing the basalt columns in thin wet fingers before losing cohesion and falling back. The surface tension behaves incorrectly. Droplets hover a centimeter above the general waterline before snapping back down. In some places, the water has formed standing waves that hold their shape for minutes at a time, miniature architecture of liquid, before collapsing and reforming in a new configuration.

And it is cold. The air temperature hovers just above freezing, and the water is colder still — fed by something deep, something that has never seen light. Your breath comes out in thick plumes. Ice forms along the edges of things: the rims of pools, the bases of structures, the backs of leaves. But the ice doesn't last. It forms and melts and forms again in a cycle that takes minutes, and each iteration leaves the ice in a slightly different crystalline structure — sometimes clear, sometimes milky, sometimes shot through with veins of something that glows faintly blue.

---

The vegetation is *aggressive*.

Moss covers everything that holds still for more than an hour — not the soft, cushioning moss of temperate forests, but a dense, almost fur-like growth in colors that range from deep emerald to near-black. It grows visibly. You can watch it extend across a wet rock face, the leading edge a pale green that darkens as it matures, advancing at perhaps a centimeter per minute. Beneath the moss, lichens have already begun their slower colonization, and beneath *those*, fungal networks thread through the fresh fractures in the basalt, their mycelia so dense that the rock appears veined with white.

The larger plants are stranger. There are trees — or things that occupy the ecological niche of trees — but they are young, impossibly young for their size. Trunks as thick as your thigh rise ten meters before branching, their bark smooth and pale, almost like skin. They have no rings. When you cut one (and someone has, recently — the wound weeps a clear fluid that the moss immediately begins to colonize), the interior is uniform, homogeneous, as though the entire tree grew in a single sustained effort rather than in seasonal pulses. The leaves are broad and dark, shaped like elongated hearts, and they are *wet* — perpetually beaded with moisture that does not evaporate. When the wind moves through them (and the wind is constant, shifting, turbulent), the leaves strike each other and produce a sound like distant applause.

Between the trees, ferns have established themselves in densities that seem competitive, almost hostile. Their fronds interlock, overlap, smother. Nothing grows alone here. Every surface is shared by at least three species, and the boundaries between them are zones of intense biological negotiation — color shifts, texture changes, small territorial advances and retreats that play out over hours.

And then there are the structures.

---

They are not buildings. They are not ruins. They are *presences* — scattered across the landscape at irregular intervals, never more than a few hundred meters apart, never close enough to suggest a settlement. Each one is different.

The nearest is a tower, perhaps fifteen meters tall, made of a material that looks like glass but isn't. It is translucent, and through its walls you can see *movement* — slow, deliberate, purposeful movement of shapes that might be machinery or might be organisms or might be something for which no category exists. The tower has no visible entrance. Its surface is warm. The moss avoids it, growing right up to its base and then stopping in a clean line, as though repelled. Water runs down its exterior in sheets, and where the water touches the material, it briefly changes color — a flash of amber, a pulse of violet — before resuming its normal appearance.

Another structure, visible perhaps half a kilometer to the east, appears to be a bridge. It spans nothing. It rises from the water in a graceful arc and descends back into the water on the other side of a stretch of open ground that contains no obstacle, no gap, no river. It is made of the same not-glass material, and it hums. The hum is low, below the threshold of comfortable hearing, but you feel it in your sternum, in your teeth, in the fluid of your inner ear. It is the bass note of the landscape's constant, overwhelming sound.

Because the sound is *deafening*.

Not in the way of machinery or explosions — in the way of *everything happening at once*. The water is the primary voice: a constant, churning, multi-tonal roar of liquid in motion, water against rock, water against water, water against the strange structures that interrupt its flow. Layered over this is the wind in the canopy, the clicking and rustling of ten thousand leaves, the creak of young trees flexing. Layered over *that* is the sound of growth — because you can hear it, here, in this density, at this scale. The moss extending. The ferns unfurling. The fungal networks pushing through rock with a sound like very slow tearing. And beneath everything, the hum of the structures, and beneath *that*, something deeper — a vibration that seems to come from the water itself, from the pressurized source below the fractured basalt, a sound that is less heard than *experienced*, a frequency that makes your vision blur at the edges.

The fauna adds to this. Things move in the water — small, fast, numerous. You catch glimpses: a flash of silver, a dark shape rolling just below the surface, a ripple that moves against the current. In the canopy, there are sounds of movement that are not wind — the heavy displacement of branches, the rustle of something large passing through leaves. You don't see the animals clearly. They are abundant but elusive, present in their effects rather than their appearances. The water is thick with them. The moss is threaded with tiny organisms that scatter when you press your hand down. The air, when you hold still long enough, reveals itself to be full of small flying things — not insects, exactly, but something with wings and purpose, moving in coordinated swarms that suggest communication.

---

The light is failing. It was never good — the canopy is too dense, the atmosphere too thick with moisture and the fine mist that rises constantly from the water's surface. What light exists is diffuse, gray-green, filtered through layers of leaf and vapor and the faint luminescence of the moss. But it is getting darker. The structures glow faintly as the ambient light drops — not enough to see by, but enough to locate them, pale shapes in the deepening murk, like lanterns half-drowned.

And the landscape is *changing*. Not slowly. Not geologically. *Now*. The water level in the pool at your feet has risen two centimeters since you began reading this sentence. A crack has opened in the basalt three meters to your left — you can hear it, a sharp report like a branch breaking, followed by the hiss of new water finding the surface. A tree has fallen somewhere in the middle distance, and the gap it leaves in the canopy is already being colonized by ferns that grow toward the new light with visible urgency. The bridge-structure has shifted — it is still an arc, still spans nothing, but it has rotated fifteen degrees on its axis, and the hum it produces has changed pitch.

This is a landscape in its earliest, most volatile state. The rock is fresh. The water is finding its level. The life is finding its niches. The structures are doing whatever the structures do — growing, adjusting, humming, watching. Everything is in motion. Everything is wet. Everything is cold and loud and dense and alive.

You are standing in the first day of a world that has not yet decided what it will become.
