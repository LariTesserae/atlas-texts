# The Lithic City of Keharas

It sits at an impossible altitude. The plateau extends for some forty kilometers in every direction — a vast, dry tableland of fused stone that was once, perhaps, volcanic. Now it is something older than geology. The rock is not rock. It does not chip, does not weather, does not erode. Millions of years of wind have passed across this surface and left it untouched, and the surface has begun to look like nothing so much as a single frozen gesture — a wave arrested mid-fall, then made solid.

The city — if that is the word — occupies roughly the central third of the plateau.

---

There is no water here. Has never been water. The stone underfoot is pale, a chalky near-white that the dim ambient light renders as grey, and it is dry in a way that feels *structural*, as though moisture is not merely absent but conceptually excluded. When you press your palm flat against it, the surface is warm — not warm like sun-heated, warm like *internally maintained*, like the thermal signature of a sleeping engine. 1.5, steady, slightly above what comfort requires. The plateau hums at this temperature the way a cathedral hums with held breath.

The light is nearly absent. You do not know the source. There is no sun, no moon, no visible sky — only a ceiling of low, flat darkness some hundreds of meters above that emits a faint, sourceless pallor, the color of fog seen through concrete. It is enough to see by, barely. Edges resolve slowly. Distance becomes uncertain.

---

The city is old construction on older ground. The buildings — dense, layered, built in the way that mountains are built, through accumulation and compression — are made of the same stone as the plateau, but shaped. Squared. Stacked. Someone, or something, cut this material and erected walls. The walls are thick. The buildings press close together in the city's center, forming canyon-streets that run straight for long distances before terminating at right angles, as though planned on a grid that the builders followed without deviation. The streets are empty.

The architecture is *heavy industrial* — not in the sense of aesthetic, but in the sense of purpose. The structures have the proportions of factories: wide doors, reinforced walls, large internal volumes, smokestacks or chimney-analogues that rise above the roofline and discharge nothing. No smoke has issued from them in a long time. They are sealed. Some of the buildings still contain machinery — enormous, rust-colored, half-fused into the floors they stand on. Gears that have grown into the stone. Conveyor mechanisms that lead nowhere. Control panels with indicators that are dark. The machinery is not broken. It is *still*. It has been still for longer than the concept of "breaking" has applied to it.

There is electricity here. Or there was. Some corridors carry a current you can feel as a faint vibration in the teeth when you stand near certain walls. The power system is incomplete — circuits that once connected everything now exist in patches, islands of function in a sea of dormancy. A fluorescent tube in a third-floor room still flickers, irregularly, drawing from a source no one has located. The light it casts is blue-white and ugly, and it illuminates a room full of identical desks, each with a drawer that opens to reveal a shallow layer of white dust — powdered stone, the desk itself slowly shedding.

---

The city is sparse *within its own density*. Buildings stand with gaps between them — empty lots where structures were never built, or were built and then became un-built through some process that left no rubble. The ground in these lots is the same fused stone, uninterrupted, as though the lot has been empty since the plateau formed. The effect is of a grid that was partially inhabited, a checkerboard of construction and void, and the void is winning. Not actively. Nothing is active. But the void reads as *more settled* than the construction, more final, more at home on this surface.

Population is effectively zero.

---

Yet there is life, or the residue of life.

In the deeper corridors — the ones below street level, accessed by ramps that slope at precise angles — there are *tracks*. Not footprints. Something broader, smoother, dragged rather than stepped. They appear on the floor of a long basement that runs the length of a block, and they recur at irregular intervals, as though a heavy, flat-bottomed organism moved slowly through this space for a very long time. The tracks are filled with the same white dust. They are ancient. Whatever made them has not passed through in ages that make the city's abandonment look recent.

On the plateau's eastern edge, where the stone drops away into a darkness that may be a cliff or may be something else entirely, there is a *colony* of something. They are not animals. They are not plants. They are small, pale, sessile things that attach to the underside of overhangs, and they are the only organic-seeming presence in this world. They do not move. They do not appear to respire. They look like calcified cysts, or barnacles made of something softer than calcium. They are the color of old bone. They are warm to the touch — the same 1.5 as everything else — and they are *rare*, appearing in clusters of three to seven, separated by stretches of bare stone that might be kilometers wide. You cannot tell if they are alive. You cannot tell if they were ever alive. But they are here, and nothing else like them exists in this place, and that makes them fauna.

---

The sound is almost nothing. The wind exists — a constant, very slow movement of air that you feel on your skin but do not hear — and occasionally, from somewhere inside the city's body, a metallic *tick*, the sound of a structure contracting by a fraction of a degree, or expanding. These ticks are separated by intervals of many minutes. They are not regular. They are not alarming. They are the sound of a place that is held at equilibrium and occasionally adjusts.

No birds. No insects. No voices. The silence is not empty. It is *weighted*. It has the quality of a held note, a drone that has been sounding for so long it has become the absence of sound rather than its presence.

---

The weirdness here is in the *rules*.

Things do not decay. The white dust is not decay; it is something else — a slow exfoliation of surface, a stone that breathes out its own material at a rate too slow to observe. Paper does not yellow. Metal does not oxidize in any way you recognize. The rust-colored machinery is not rust; it is the color of the metal itself, or the color of the environment's effect on the metal, which is *cosmetic only*. Nothing corrodes. Nothing rots. Nothing weathers. This is a world that has reached its final state and is simply *remaining there*.

But things *do* move. Not quickly. Not visibly. If you mark the position of a chair in a room and leave and return — and you will not know how much time has passed, because there is no light-cycle to measure by — the chair may be a centimeter from where you left it. Or two centimeters. Or it may be in the same position with an insistence that feels *wrong*, as though it has been carefully replaced. Nothing in this city is fixed. Everything is held at equilibrium, and equilibrium, at this scale, involves a very slow rearrangement.

The buildings are settling into the plateau. Or the plateau is rising into the buildings. Over sufficient time, the distinction becomes meaningless. The city and the ground are approaching each other, molecule by molecule, toward a final state where there will be no boundary between them. The buildings will not have been destroyed. They will have been *absorbed*. They will become features of the plateau's surface the way a wave is a feature of the ocean — a shape that the underlying material briefly held, and then did not.

---

You are standing in the central avenue. The street runs north-south, perfectly straight, and in the near-darkness you can see it extend until it becomes uncertain — not until it ends, but until your eyes can no longer distinguish it from the surrounding grey. Buildings rise on either side, four and five stories, their facades featureless, their windows dark. One window, on your left, on the second floor, is broken. The shards lie on the ground below it in a pattern that is too even, too complete, as though the glass fell all at once and was not disturbed afterward. The shards are white. The glass was white. Everything here is white, or grey, or the warm color of the stone, which is the color of the light, which is the color of the sky-that-is-not-a-sky, which is the color of whatever this place is settling into being.

Your feet are warm on the stone. The stone is warm beneath your feet. The warmth goes down and down, into the body of the plateau, into the body of the city, into the slow, patient heat of a world that has finished becoming and is now simply *being*, in the dark, at altitude, in silence, at the end of all erosion, holding its shape the way a held breath holds a note — not forever, but for now, and now is very long, and nothing is changing, and everything is changing so slowly that the difference has ceased to matter.
