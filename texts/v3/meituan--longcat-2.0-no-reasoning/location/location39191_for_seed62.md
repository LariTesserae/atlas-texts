# The Slagmoor

The air tastes of iron and stone-dust and green things forcing their way through green things that came before. It is never truly cold, never warm — the temperature holds at that narrow band where your breath is visible but the water never freezes, where the moisture on your skin never dries. The rain comes in intervals so brief and scattered you might not call it rain at all: a dampening, a darkening of surfaces, then a slow silvering as the perpetual daylight reasserts itself.

And the light — the light is extraordinary. It doesn't come from a single source. The sky is a high, pale membrane, nearly white, and it fills everything from every direction the way water fills a bowl. Shadows are soft-edged and brief, sliding across surfaces as cloud-masses drift overhead, but they never deepen into true darkness. The landscape is always legible, always washed in even luminescence, like the inside of a frosted lamp.

You are standing on a terrace — or rather, on what *will be* a terrace. The ground beneath you is young. Not recently formed in any geological sense, but *newly emerged*: this whole region has been uncovered from something, exposed to sky within the last few decades. The rock is fractured rhyolite and fresh basalt, and where it splits it reveals faces that have never oxidized, surfaces that still carry the mirror-shine of deep earth. Water runs in threads between the plates — not streams exactly, more like the seepage of a thing that hasn't yet decided to become a stream. It pools in the cracks, in the shallow bowls left by overturned equipment, in the footprints of whatever walked here last. The footprints are mostly your own.

The vegetation is the first thing that stops your understanding of the place. It shouldn't be here — this much green, this much *weight* of life, on stone this young and soil this thin. But it is everywhere, dense as woven fabric, layered in canopy over canopy over carpet, and it grows with a patience that matches the slow processes around it. Mosses first — thick, emerald, ankle-deep — then ferns with fronds the size of your hand, then a low scrub of something with waxy leaves and pale roots that grip the rock like fingers. Higher up the slope, where the terraces step upward toward the ridgeline, the vegetation thickens into something almost impenetrable: a tangle of climbing vines, epiphytes, and small trees whose trunks are wrapped in living green so completely you can't tell where bark ends and moss begins. The density is such that the air inside the thickets is a different air — heavier, wetter, warmer by a degree or two, and so still that a single drop falling from a leaf takes a long time to reach the ground.

And yet the fauna is almost absent. You see no birds. No insects cross your path, though you find their traces — tiny bore-holes in dead wood, the faintest silk-threads between ferns, a single translucent wing caught in a spider's web that itself is so fine you almost miss it. The silence is not empty silence. It is the silence of a place that has decided, for now, to be quiet. The water makes no sound as it seeps. The vegetation makes no sound as it grows. Even the wind, when it comes, moves through the canopy with a softness that produces only the faintest rustle, like cloth on cloth.

The built structures are scattered across the landscape like bones half-buried in green. They are industrial — unmistakably so — but they have been *absorbed*. A processing tower stands on the next terrace up, its steel frame still intact but its walls long since removed or rotted, and the interior is a vertical garden: vines threading through conveyor housings, moss filling the gaps in grating floors, a small tree growing out of what was once a control panel. The machinery is still there — you can see gears, pistons, the hulking shapes of engines — but they are *still*. Not rusted into immobility so much as *held*, as if the vegetation has simply decided to keep them in place. Some of the gears are half-turned, frozen mid-rotation, with roots wrapped around their teeth. A pipeline runs along the terrace edge, and where it has cracked, water seeps out in a thin, constant sheet that feeds a curtain of moss below.

Further out — and the landscape extends further than you can easily see, rolling in terraces and shallow valleys toward a hazy distance where the ridgeline blurs into the bright sky — there are more structures. A long, low building with a collapsed roof, its interior now a greenhouse of unplanned ecology. A set of rail tracks, still straight, still level, running between two points that no longer exist, their rails green with oxidation and their ties buried in soil that has accumulated over decades of slow, patient deposition. A chimney, tall and solitary, standing at the center of a clearing where nothing grows — the only place on the entire terrace where the ground is bare, and the rock beneath is blackened and fused, as if something burned there for a very long time and the earth has not yet forgiven it.

The strangeness of the place is not dramatic. It accumulates. It is in the way the water sometimes runs *uphill* — not far, not fast, but enough that you notice a wet patch above a dry one and cannot find the source. It is in the way the vegetation grows in patterns that almost resemble circuitry, almost resemble pipe-routing diagrams, as if the plants are following the ghost-logic of the infrastructure that preceded them. It is in the silence, which is not the silence of absence but the silence of *attention* — as if the place is listening to something you cannot hear. It is in the light, which is too even, too constant, too *present*, as if the sky itself is a kind of machinery that has not yet been turned off.

You walk. The ground is soft underfoot — not muddy, but yielding, the moss and accumulated organic matter compressing slowly beneath your weight and not springing back. Your footsteps leave impressions that will last for hours, maybe days, in this slow world. The air is damp on your face. The green is everywhere, dense and layered and patient. The structures stand among it like monuments to a process that has been interrupted but not ended — the industrial and the organic not at war but in some long, slow negotiation whose outcome is not yet determined.

Somewhere ahead, on a terrace you haven't reached, there is a sound. Not a loud sound. Not even a clear one. A low, rhythmic pulse, like a heartbeat, like a machine cycling, like the deep respiration of something very large and very slow. It might be water. It might be the settling of the young rock. It might be the place itself, breathing.

You walk toward it. The moss receives your footsteps. The light holds everything in its pale, even gaze. The silence continues, and continues, and continues.
