# The Groaning Gardens of Vaelys

## First Impressions

You descend into it before you arrive. The world opens downward — a vast bowl of saltwater and living architecture, a flooded basin so wide that its far shores are only suggested by a slight darkening of the horizon, a line where the overcast sky meets a slightly-different shade of grey-green water. This is Vaelys, and it has been dying for so long that its death has become a kind of country.

The water is cold. Not bitterly, not immediately — it seeps into clothing with a patient, unsettling intimacy, as if it has been waiting. Eight degrees Celsius, roughly, though your instruments falter here. The salinity is unusual. Not pure ocean, not brackish river-mouth, but something older — mineral-heavy, carrying dissolved silicates and traces of metals that your spectrometer flags but cannot name. The water has a faint opacity to it, a cloudy translucence like skim milk, so that visibility extends perhaps thirty meters before the world softens into a greenish murk. Light passes through it but seems reluctant, as if the photons are negotiating with every suspended particle.

## The Terrain Below

What you're standing on — or rather, what your vessel floats above — is the surface of a drowned mountain range. This was once serious elevation: the basin rim still shows peaks that break the surface as sharp islands, black rock worn to soft roundness, their tops barely clearing the waterline like the heads of sleeping animals. But the interior, the vast interior you've entered, has been flattened by so much time and so much water that the ancient peaks here are now seamounts — submerged hills rising from a silty plain at forty, sixty, eighty meters depth. They've been worn down to nubs, to gentle domes. Erosion has had its way with everything. The rock faces are smooth in the way that only extremely old rock can be smooth — not polished, but *resolved*, as if the mountains have accepted their fate and stopped fighting the current.

The sediment on the basin floor is extraordinary. Meters upon meters of accumulated silt, organic detritus, calcium carbonate from ancient shell-beds, volcanic ash from eruptions so old they predate any human record. Your sonar reads it as a soft, layered blanket — compressed in places, fluffy and unsettled in others. When the current stirs it (and the current is always stirring), the water clouds further, and the world goes from murky to opaque. Navigation becomes a matter of instruments and faith.

## The Gardens

And then there is the vegetation. This is what makes Vaelys extraordinary, what draws the expeditions, what keeps the research platforms floating here despite the cold and the poor visibility and the constant mechanical problems.

The seamounts are gardens. Every single one.

They are covered — utterly, impossibly covered — in aquatic plant life that has no clear analog in any botanical taxonomy. The dominant organism is something like kelp but behaves like coral: it builds structure. Stalks as thick as your forearm rise from the sediment in dense stands, each one branching into fractal fans of ribbon-like fronds that sway in the current with a slow, hypnotic rhythm. The color is deep olive at the base, shading to a near-luminous jade at the tips, where the fronds are newest and most translucent. In the down-filtered light from above, the gardens glow faintly — not bioluminescence, exactly, but a kind of brightness of saturation, as if the green has been concentrated past the point where it can stay dark.

Between the kelp-analogues, other things grow. Thick mats of something mossy and blue-black drape over the older rock faces. Colonial organisms — maybe animal, maybe vegetable, the distinction blurs here — form crusts and shelves in vivid orange and pale violet, accreting slowly onto any hard surface. Anemone-like blooms cluster in the lee of the stalks, their tendrils extended into the current, capturing the constant rain of organic particles from above. The density is staggering. On the flanks of the seamounts, the plant life is layered so thickly that you cannot see the rock beneath it. Your vessel's hull scrapes against soft, yielding surfaces as you navigate between the gardens — not rock, never rock, always living or once-living material compressed into a yielding, fibrous mat.

The sound is what gets you first. The gardens *groan*. The stalks, under tension from the constant current, produce low-frequency creaks and groans as they bend and recover. It's not a sound you hear so much as feel — a subsonic vibration in your chest, a pressure against your eardrums. Add to this the constant patter of organic debris falling through the water column (the "marine snow" that is the ecosystem's main energy input), the clicking and tapping of the abundant crustacean fauna, the occasional deep *thrum* of something large moving in the murk below, and the white-noise rush of the current itself, and you have a soundscape that is ceaseless, enveloping, and deeply strange. Vaelys is never quiet. Vaelys is never still.

## The Fauna

The animal life is not separate from the gardens — it is woven through them, dependent on them, nearly identical to them in places. The most visible are the grazers: things like flatfish, but larger, slower, their bodies patterned to match the blue-black moss so perfectly that you see them only when they move. They drift along the seamount flanks like living leaves, mouthparts working ceaselessly against the algal crusts.

Smaller things swarm in the fronds. Shrimp-analogues in extraordinary numbers — thousands per square meter, their bodies transparent except for a bright red dot at the center of each one (an organ no one has successfully explained). They move in coordinated clouds, flashing from transparent to crimson as they shift orientation, so that the garden appears to pulse with red light. It's not light — it's just reflection, just pigment — but the effect is disorienting, like watching a living heartbeat spread through the vegetation.

Larger predators exist but are rarely seen. Sonar picks them up as shadows at the edge of range — torpedo-shaped, moving fast, then gone. The researchers have named them *vaelys* fish, unoriginally, and know almost nothing about them. They are heard more than seen: a sudden rush of displaced water, a brief turbulence in the current, and then the shrimp-clouds scatter and the garden goes momentarily still before resuming its slow sway.

## The Current

The current is the engine of everything here. Vaelys sits at the confluence of deep ocean circulation patterns — cold water sinking along the basin rim, warmer water rising from hydrothermal vents in the deepest parts of the plain, tidal forces from the planet's large moon pulling the entire water mass in a slow, twelve-hour breathing cycle. The result is a constant, complex, multi-directional flow that never stops and never simplifies. At the surface, the current runs roughly east to west at about one knot. At ten meters depth, it reverses. At thirty meters, it spirals. At the bottom, it is nearly still, except where the vents create localized upwellings of warm, mineral-rich water that the gardens cluster around with obvious preference.

This turbulence is what makes navigation difficult and what makes the ecosystem possible. The current delivers nutrients, disperses spores and larvae, keeps the water oxygenated, and prevents the sediment from settling into an anoxic dead zone. It is also what makes the gardens groan, what keeps the stalks under constant tension, what drives the shrimp-clouds into their coordinated dances. Remove the current and Vaelys dies. The current is Vaelys.

## The Built Things

There are structures here, but they are old and few and strange.

The most prominent is the Platform — a research station, industrial in its bones, that has been anchored to the largest seamount for eleven years. It is a squat, ugly thing: steel and composite, barnacle-crusted, its lower levels entirely submerged and now functionally part of the garden. Kelp-analogues grow through its lower grating. The orange colonial organisms have colonized its anchor chains. The shrimp live in its ballast tanks. It groans with the same frequency as the gardens, and after a few weeks aboard, the researchers stop being able to tell the sound of the station from the sound of the sea.

Above the waterline, the Platform is more recognizably human: a cluster of habitation modules, a crane, a desalination plant that runs constantly and breaks down weekly, a communications array that works intermittently due to the mineral content of the water interfering with radio transmission. There is a small industrial workshop where they repair the remotely operated vehicles that do most of the actual exploration — the water is too cold and too murky for comfortable diving, and the current is too unpredictable for safety. The ROVs are tethered, and their tethers snag constantly on the gardens, and the gardens grow back over the tethers within days, and the whole process of exploration is one of constant, patient, mechanical struggle against a living environment that is not hostile but is absolutely indifferent.

There are older things too. On the basin rim, where the peaks break the surface, there are ruins — not human ruins, or at least not human in any way the researchers can confirm. Stone foundations, worn to stumps. Carvings on rock faces that have been smoothed by centuries of wave action into near-illegibility. A few metal artifacts, so corroded that they crumble at touch, their original function unguessable. Whoever built here did so when the water was lower, or when the land was higher, or when the world was different in some fundamental way that the geology has since smoothed over. The ruins are old in the way that the mountains are old — resolved, accepted, nearly gone.

## The Light

The sky is overcast. It is always overcast. Vaelys sits in a meteorological depression that seems semi-permanent — a zone of low pressure where moist air from the surrounding ocean converges, rises, and condenses into a flat, featureless cloud layer that extends from about three hundred meters altitude to the edge of space. The light that reaches the water surface is diffuse, directionless, grey. It does not change much with the time of day — dawn and dusk are barely perceptible shifts in a brightness that is always moderate, always even, always slightly insufficient.

Underwater, this means that the gardens exist in a perpetual twilight. The upper ten meters are dimly lit — you can see shapes, colors, movement. Below that, the light fades rapidly. At thirty meters, you are in a blue-grey world where the gardens are silhouettes. At fifty meters, you are in darkness, and the only light is what you bring with you — the ROV's LEDs, which illuminate a small cone of vivid, artificial brightness that makes the gardens look garish and wrong, their greens too green, their oranges too orange, the shrimp-clouds scattering from the beam like startled stars.

## The Feeling of the Place

Vaelys is not hostile. It is not welcoming. It is *occupied* — by the gardens, by the current, by the fauna, by the slow geological processes that have been running here for longer than any human civilization. The researchers who work here describe a particular feeling that sets in after a few weeks: a sense of being *tolerated*. The Platform is not attacked, not rejected, not consumed. It is simply... incorporated. The gardens grow over it. The fauna lives in it. The current works around it. The water seeps into it. Everything that is not maintained with constant, active effort is gradually absorbed into the living system of the basin.

And the system is alive in a way that is difficult to articulate. Not conscious — there is no evidence of intelligence, no sign of purpose or design. But *active*, ceaselessly active, in a way that makes the human presence feel small and temporary and slightly absurd. The gardens grow. The current flows. The shrimp swarm. The grazers feed. The predators hunt. The sediment accumulates. The stalks groan. The water moves. Everything is in motion, everything is connected, everything is part of a cycle that has been running for millennia and will continue running long after the Platform has rusted through and sunk into the silt and become, itself, a seamount for new gardens to colonize.

Vaelys is what the world looks like when it has been left alone for a very long time. It is not pristine — it is *mature*. It has passed through its early chaos and settled into a state of dynamic equilibrium that is stable not because nothing changes, but because everything changes in balance. The current shifts, and the gardens sway. A stalk breaks, and a hundred larvae settle in the gap. A predator takes a grazer, and the moss it was eating grows back. The system absorbs, adapts, continues.

It is beautiful, in the way that very old things are beautiful — not bright, not dramatic, but deep, and patient, and slightly sad, and absolutely, unassailably real.
