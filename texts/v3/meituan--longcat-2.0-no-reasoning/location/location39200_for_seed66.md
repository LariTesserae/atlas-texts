The rain arrives like a decision that was made long ago. It does not fall from any particular sky—though there is a sky, or something in that direction, a permanent umber overcast that might be cloud or might be smoke or might be the breath of the place itself. The rain simply *is*, the way this place is: a given, a constant, a fact you must build around.

You stand on a surface that used to be a road, or still is a road, depending on how you define the term. It is a continuous expanse of basalt cobble, each stone worn to the shape of a flattened egg by the passage of things. The water runs in the gaps between them, always moving, never quite pooling, following channels that are not carved but *remembered*—this basalt was once a flow, and the water returns to the old paths, the ancient downhill.

---

**The built world** announces itself first as an alteration in the sound. The rain on open ground is a high, diffuse hiss. The rain on the city is *organized*. It becomes a roar of ten thousand timbres as it strikes the corrugated iron of rooftops, the glassless windows of upper floors, the great sheet-metal cisterns that crown every third or fourth structure, the brass pipes that connect building to building in webs of rusted tubing, the hissing steam vents that erupt from below the cobbles at irregular intervals, sending up columns of white vapor that merge with the rain and become indistinguishable from it.

The city rises from the basalt plain in terraces. Not planned terraces—*settled* terraces, the way sediment settles, each level finding its own equilibrium over long centuries. The lowest structures are heaviest: thick walls of the same black basalt, mortared with something that might be volcanic ash and might be lime and might be a cultivated fungus that mineralizes into a substance indistinguishable from stone. These are the old buildings. Their roofs are gone—taken by time, taken by weather, taken by the deliberate dismantling of previous generations who needed the iron and the timber and the copper wiring. What remains are the walls, and the doorways, and the great arched openings where machinery once passed through.

Into these hollowed structures the water has moved without resistance. The lowest terraces are part-flooded, not in the way of a disaster but in the way of a *negotiation*. The water comes in; the inhabitants have raised their floors. Walkways of timber and salvaged steel span the dark pools below, and from these walkways, steps climb to the next level, and the next. Everything is on stilts, or becomes on stilts, or was built on stilts from the beginning because everyone who has ever lived here knew about the water.

---

**The fauna** is what you notice next, because it is impossible not to notice, because it is *everywhere*, because it is the reason the city exists and the reason the city is the way it is.

They are not animals in any sense that a biologist would recognize, though they move and feed and die. They are *colonies*—vast, slow, colonial organisms that occupy the same ecological niche that a city's infrastructure might occupy elsewhere. Each one is a network of filaments, some as fine as spider silk, some as thick as a forearm, spreading through the basalt, through the water, through the walls of the buildings themselves. They are the color of wet iron, of rust, of old blood, and they pulse—not visibly, not at first, but if you press your hand against a wall you can feel it, a slow rhythmic contraction, like a heartbeat measured in minutes rather than seconds.

The filaments are the organism's body. What you see above the surface are its *organs*—specialized structures that emerge from the network to perform specific functions. There are the **collectors**: broad, flat, frond-like plates that rise from the water on flexible stalks, oriented to catch the rain and channel it downward. There are the **processors**: dense, knotted masses of filament, warm to the touch, where something is being broken down or built up—the smell near them is complex, metallic, organic, like a brewery and a forge and a compost heap all at once. There are the **connectors**: long, taut cables of bundled filament that stretch between buildings, between terraces, between the city and the plain beyond, carrying something—nutrients, signals, both—through the network at a pace you can almost watch if you stare long enough.

And there are the **breeders**, though that word implies more intention than is accurate. They are the largest structures, rising three or four stories from the flooded lower terraces, shaped like inverted cones or like the fruiting bodies of enormous fungi, their surfaces pocked with openings from which a constant fine mist emerges—spores, or eggs, or something that is neither and both. The mist drifts in the rain and the steam and settles on every surface, and where it settles, new filaments begin to grow, slowly, so slowly, over years and decades, extending the network by inches.

The organisms are not hostile. They are not friendly. They are *infrastructure*. The inhabitants of the city have learned to live with them the way a river-dweller lives with the river—respectfully, attentively, with an understanding that the relationship is not one of control but of cohabitation. The filaments carry water; the inhabitants tap into them. The filaments generate heat through their metabolic processes; the inhabitants build their dwellings around the warmest clusters. The filaments, in their slow pulsing, create a vibration in the basalt that can be felt through the soles of your feet—a constant, subsonic hum that the inhabitants say you stop noticing after a while, though newcomers claim they never stop noticing, that it gets into your bones and your teeth and your dreams.

---

**The inhabitants themselves** are human, or were human, or are something that has been human-adjacent for so long that the distinction has become academic. They are small—not genetically, but practically, shaped by a lifetime of navigating narrow walkways and low doorways and the spaces between the filaments. They move carefully. They move *slowly*, not from infirmity but from the understanding that haste in this place is dangerous—the walkways are slick, the water is deeper than it looks, and the filaments, while not aggressive, do not appreciate being stepped on and will respond to damage with a slow, inexorable constriction that can trap a foot, a leg, a body if you don't know how to wait it out.

They dress in layers of oiled fabric and treated leather, not because the rain is cold—the temperature is warm, almost uncomfortably so, the air thick with moisture and the heat of ten thousand metabolic processes—but because the filaments are mildly caustic to bare skin, and because the steam vents erupt without warning, and because the water that runs through the lower terraces is not clean in any sense that matters.

Their tools are iron and brass and copper, worked by hand in small forges that glow orange in the perpetual dimness. They make things: fittings for the walkways, containers for water, instruments for measuring the pulse of the filaments (because the pulse changes, slowly, over seasons, and the changes mean things—the network is growing, or stressed, or preparing to breed, and the inhabitants must adjust their own patterns accordingly). They make things, and they repair things, and they dismantle things that are no longer needed and return the materials to the network, because the network will take them—will slowly, over months, absorb iron and brass and copper into its filaments, incorporating them, and this is not waste but *exchange*, the fundamental economy of the place.

---

**The scale** is difficult to hold in the mind. The city extends to the horizon in every direction, or seems to—the rain and the steam and the umber sky make distance uncertain. You can see the terraces rising and falling, the great breeder-structures punctuating the skyline like cathedral spires, the connector-cables arcing between them against the dim sky. But you cannot see the edge. The inhabitants say there is no edge, or that the edge is very far away, or that the edge moves—that the network grows outward at its own pace, and the city grows with it, and the plain beyond is slowly being colonized, the basalt being threaded with filaments, the water being channeled into new paths, the old giving way to the new in a process so gradual that no single generation perceives it.

The weather is the city's own. The steam from the vents, the mist from the breeders, the heat from the processors—all of it rises and condenses and falls again, a closed loop, a local hydrology that has nothing to do with any ocean or any distant mountain range. The rain is the city's exhalation. The city breathes water, and the water falls, and the network drinks it, and the city breathes again.

---

**The sound** is constant and layered. The rain is the base note—a roar, a hiss, a drumming, depending on what it strikes. Above that, the hum of the filaments, felt as much as heard, a vibration that lives in the chest and the jaw. Above *that*, the sounds of the inhabitants: voices, tools, the creak of walkways, the splash of water, the hiss of steam, the clang of a forge-hammer, the wet slap of a collector-frond shedding its load. And above all of it, or beneath all of it, a sound that might be wind or might be the network itself, a low, slow, almost musical tone that rises and falls with the pulse, that changes with the seasons, that the inhabitants can read like a farmer reads the sky.

---

**The light** is normal, which is to say it is the light of an overcast sky in a temperate latitude, filtered through rain and steam and the mist of ten thousand breeders until it becomes a diffuse, directionless glow that casts no shadows and reveals no distances. It is enough to see by. It is enough to work by. It is not enough to see the sky clearly, or to know what is above the clouds, or to remember what direct sunlight looks like. The inhabitants do not miss it. The inhabitants, most of them, have never seen it.

---

**The erosion** is the deepest thing about this place, and the hardest to see. The basalt is old—not geologically old, but *worn* old, shaped by the patient work of water and filament and time into forms that are smooth and rounded and organic. The cobbles of the road are not sharp; they are eggs, pebbles, shapes that fit the hand and the foot. The walls of the old buildings are not angular; they are softened, their corners rounded, their surfaces pocked and channeled by the slow work of the filaments that have grown into them and around them and through them, dissolving some minerals and depositing others, turning stone into something that is part stone and part filament and part water and part time.

The city is not decaying. The city is *settling*. It has found its shape, or is finding it, or has been finding it for longer than anyone can remember. The balance between the built and the grown, between the iron and the filament, between the water and the stone, is not stable in the way that a building is stable—it is stable in the way that a river is stable, always moving, always the same, always becoming what it already is.

---

You stand on the road. The rain falls. The filaments pulse beneath your feet. Somewhere, a forge-hammer rings against brass. Somewhere, a breeder releases its mist into the warm, wet air. The city breathes. The water falls. The network grows, slowly, in the dark, in the warm, in the rain, in the sound, in the vast and patient and overwhelming life of the place.
