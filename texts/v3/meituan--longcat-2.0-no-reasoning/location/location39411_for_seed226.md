# The Hanging Gardens of Veth-Koram

---

You arrive at the edge of a cliff that isn't finished yet.

The rock here is young — not in the geological sense, but in the sense that it still remembers being something else. The basalt columns rise in hexagonal stacks, but they're soft at the edges, still rounding themselves, still deciding what shape they want to be. Where you press your palm against one, it yields slightly, like clay that has been left to cure in fog. The whole escarpment is in the process of becoming stone. You can see the layers: at the bottom, true rock, dark and cold and ancient; above that, a transitional zone where mineral and something more organic blur together; and at the top, where the city begins, the stone is almost alive — warm to the touch, faintly veined with moisture, breathing.

The city of Veth-Koram is built on this unfinished cliff face, and it is enormous in ambition but small in footprint — a single vertical settlement, perhaps four hundred meters tall and no more than sixty meters wide, clinging to the rock like a vine. It is a city in the way that a termite mound is a city: dense with intention, layered with construction, but occupying a space you could walk around in an hour. The buildings are carved from the cliff itself and then built back up with the same semi-cured stone, mixed with a pale fibrous material that the Korami harvest from the moss-beards that hang from every ledge. The architecture is organic, rounded, windowless on the exterior — from a distance the city looks like a series of blisters on the rock face, or like the clustered nests of some vast swallow.

Water is everywhere, but not in the way you'd expect. There are no rivers here, no streams cascading down the cliff. Instead, the rock itself weeps. The semi-cured stone is porous, and deep below, an aquifer presses upward against the cliff's interior, and the moisture migrates through the mineral layers and emerges on every surface as a fine, continuous condensation. The walls of Veth-Koram are always damp. The air is thick with it — not raining, not misting, but *present*, a humidity so complete that your skin never dries. The Korami don't collect water; they live inside it. Their homes are designed around the weeping walls, with channels carved into the floors that guide the moisture into central basins, where it pools and is used. The sound of it is constant: a low, wet, percussive dripping that echoes through every corridor and chamber, amplified by the stone into something that resembles music if you listen long enough. It is the loudest quiet place you've ever been.

The vegetation grows in the water's path. Everything that can hold a root in the weeping stone does. The moss-beards are the most spectacular — hanging curtains of deep emerald and black-green, some of them thirty meters long, swaying in the updrafts that rise from the valley below. They are not moss, exactly, but something older and simpler, a colonial organism that is half plant and half fungus, and it is this that the Korami harvest for building material. Beneath the beards, the cliff face is upholstered in layers of liverwort and hornwort and things that have no name in any language you know — flat, wet, overlapping growths that feel like velvet and smell like the inside of a cellar. In the crevices, small ferns have established themselves, their fronds perpetually beaded with water, and here and there a flowering plant pushes through — pale, almost white, with petals so thin they are translucent, adapted to the low light that filters through the perpetual humidity.

The light is strange. It is bright — the sky above the cliff is clear, the sun unobstructed — but by the time it passes through the water-heavy air and reaches the city, it has been diffused into something soft and directionless. There are no harsh shadows in Veth-Koram. Everything is evenly lit, as if the light comes from everywhere and nowhere. The Korami have no word for "glare." Their eyes are large and dark, adapted to the perpetual softness, and they find direct sunlight painful and alien.

The temperature is cool — not cold, but the kind of cool that makes you aware of your own warmth. The weeping stone draws heat from your body. The air moves slowly, heavy with moisture, and it carries the smell of mineral and growth and something faintly metallic, like blood or like the air before a thunderstorm that never comes. You are never uncomfortable, but you are never quite warm enough. The Korami wear layers of woven moss-fiber, thick and soft, dyed in muted colors with pigments extracted from the lichens that grow on the cliff's north face.

The city is organized vertically. The lowest levels, where the stone is oldest and hardest, are the workshops — the Korami are stone-workers, not in the sense of carving finished rock, but in the sense of *guiding* the unfinished rock as it cures. They are, in a way, farmers of geology. They understand the process by which the cliff's upper layers transition from soft to hard, and they manage it — adding moisture here, scraping away excess growth there, shaping the stone as it sets the way a potter shapes clay on a wheel. Their tools are simple: blades of sharpened bone, scrapers made from the shells of the valley's large freshwater mussels, wooden mallets carved from driftwood. They have no metal. They have no fire — the stone is too damp, the air too wet. They work by hand, slowly, and the work is never finished because the cliff is always growing, always producing new soft stone from below as the old stone cures above.

The middle levels are the living quarters — small, round rooms carved into the cliff, each one centered on a weeping wall. The Korami sleep on beds of dried moss-fiber, and they eat a diet of the freshwater mussels and the pale, starchy roots of the cliff-face ferns. They are few — perhaps three hundred in total — and they move through their city with a quiet, unhurried purpose. You rarely see them. The density of population is low, the spaces between them vast. A corridor might stretch twenty meters between one occupied chamber and the next. The city feels empty even when it is fully inhabited, because the Korami are small and quiet and the stone swallows sound even as it amplifies the dripping.

The highest levels are the gardens. This is where the name comes from, though the Korami don't call it that — their word translates roughly to "the place where the cliff breathes out." The uppermost terraces are open to the sky, and here the Korami cultivate the moss-beards and the flowering plants, tending them with a care that borders on devotion. The gardens are the city's only source of color — the pale whites and faint pinks of the cliff-flowers, the deep greens of the moss, and, in the highest and most exposed beds, a lichen that produces a vivid, almost electric blue, which the Korami use for dye and, it is said, for medicine. The gardens are also where the Korami go to hear the wind, which is the only sound in Veth-Koram that is not water. It moves across the open terraces with a low, sustained note, like a finger drawn around the rim of a glass, and the Korami stand in it with their eyes closed, faces upturned, listening.

There are animals, but not many. The valley below the cliff is home to a species of large, slow-moving amphibian — something like a salamander the size of a dog — that the Korami hunt with nets woven from moss-fiber. They are rare, these creatures, and the Korami take no more than they need. In the gardens, small insects move among the flowers — winged things, pale and slow, that seem to be in the process of adapting to the perpetual humidity. You see them struggling through the air as if the thickness of the water-heavy atmosphere is a burden they haven't yet learned to carry. The Korami watch them with interest. They believe the insects are becoming something new.

The strangeness of this place is not dramatic. It is subtle, pervasive, and it accumulates. It is in the way the stone is not quite stone. It is in the way the water comes from everywhere and nowhere. It is in the way the city is simultaneously ancient and unfinished, as if it has been here forever and is still being built. It is in the way the Korami speak — slowly, with long pauses, as if they are listening to something you cannot hear. It is in the way time moves here: not fast, not slow, but *thick*, the way the air is thick, the way the stone is thick with moisture. A day in Veth-Koram feels longer than a day anywhere else. Not because the hours are extended, but because each moment is so full of small, wet, quiet detail that your mind cannot move through it quickly. You find yourself standing still, watching water bead on a wall, for what feels like minutes and might be hours.

The Korami do not welcome visitors, but they do not turn them away. They regard you with their large, dark eyes, and they wait. They are patient in the way that stone is patient, in the way that water is patient. They will show you the weeping walls. They will show you the gardens. They will give you moss-fiber to wear and mussel-meat to eat, and they will answer your questions slowly, in their thick, slow language, and you will understand that they are not primitive — they are *deliberate*. They have chosen this pace. They have chosen to live inside a process rather than to complete it. The cliff is still becoming stone. The city is still becoming a city. The Korami are still becoming whatever they are becoming.

And the water drips, and the moss grows, and the pale flowers open in the directionless light, and the sound of it all — the wet, percussive, echoing, musical sound of it all — fills the empty spaces between the Korami and their unfinished walls, and it is loud, and it is the only song this place has ever known.
