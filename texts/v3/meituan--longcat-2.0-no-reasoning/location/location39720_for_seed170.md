# The Calcination Fields of Vethruun

---

You stand on a plain of fused mineral that extends in every direction until the heat makes the horizon swim and dissolve. The ground is not rock in any familiar sense — it is *calcined*, baked so thoroughly over so long that it has become something between ceramic and glass, a pale grey-white surface shot through with veins of darker material: iron, perhaps, or something that was iron before the heat changed its mind about what it wanted to be. The surface is cracked into plates the size of rooms, the size of buildings, and the cracks between them glow faintly orange from the heat below. Not magma. Something else. Something that moves too slowly to be liquid and too freely to be solid — a deep mineral exhalation, a slow sweating of the world's interior through pores that have been open for geological ages.

The elevation is significant. The air is thin enough that your lungs work for what they're given, and the sky above is a deep, bruised violet even at midday, because there is not enough atmosphere left at this height to scatter light the way it does in the lowlands. The sun — and it is a sun, or at least it occupies that role — hangs in the sky like a white-gold coin pressed into cloth. It does not feel distant. It feels *present*, a physical weight on the exposed surfaces of things, and the calcined ground returns that heat as a shimmering, visible distortion that rises in columns and sheets, so that the entire landscape appears to be gently steaming, though there is nothing to steam. No water. There has not been water here for longer than the concept of water has had meaning.

And yet the place is not still.

---

The fauna — and this is where the first fracture appears in your understanding of what you are seeing — is *overwhelming*. But the word "fauna" implies biology, and what moves across the Calcination Fields is not biological in any way you can immediately parse. They are *presences*. They are *occupants*. They move through the landscape the way weather moves through a valley, except they are discrete, they are countable, and they are aware.

The closest analogy, and it is a poor one, is this: imagine a herd of animals the size of grain silos, but made of the same calcined mineral as the ground, moving on limbs that are not limbs but rather *folds* in their own bodies, so that walking looks like a slow origami of self-reconfiguration. They are pale. They are cracked like the ground. They glow faintly at the seams. And there are *so many of them* — not packed together, but distributed across the visible landscape with the density of trees in a sparse forest, each one hundreds of meters from its nearest neighbor, and yet the cumulative effect is of being *surrounded*, of being in a space that is fully occupied by slow, enormous, mineral attention.

They do not eat. They do not breathe. They *process*. Each one is, in some sense that you can feel but not articulate, a slow industrial act — a walking refinery, a mobile calcination event. Where they stand for long enough, the ground beneath them changes. It darkens. It develops new crystalline structures. It becomes *more itself*, more thoroughly baked, more completely transformed from whatever it was into whatever the Fields want it to be. They are not creatures that live in this landscape. They are the landscape's method of continuing to exist.

---

The sound is constant and loud, but it is not the sound of the fauna. The fauna move in near-silence — a low grinding, a subsonic settling, felt in the sternum more than heard. The loudness comes from the *ground itself*, from the deep slow exhalation of heat through the cracks, which produces a continuous, resonant hum, a chord made of dozens of frequencies that are almost but not quite harmonic, so that the sound is always on the verge of resolving into something musical and never quite gets there. It is the sound of a vast structure under thermal stress. It is the sound of a world-sized engine idling. And beneath it, at the very threshold of perception, there is a rhythm — slow, so slow, with a period of minutes rather than seconds — a pulse in the heat, a fluctuation in the glow of the cracks, as if the entire plain is breathing on a timescale that makes geology look hasty.

---

The weirdness is not a feature of this place. It is the *medium* of this place, the way water is the medium of an ocean. Reality here is not broken — it is *bent*, consistently and according to rules that are not your rules. The most immediate manifestation is this: distance does not behave. The fauna that appear to be a kilometer away may be ten kilometers away, or they may be much closer than they appear, and the relationship between apparent distance and actual distance changes depending on the temperature of the air between you and them, which changes depending on which direction you face, which changes depending on factors you cannot identify. You learn, quickly, to navigate by the *sound* of things rather than their appearance, because the hum of the ground is more honest than the light.

Time, too, is unreliable, though in a way that is subtle enough to miss if you are not paying attention. The slow pulse of the heat — that minutes-long rhythm — does not affect all things equally. The fauna move at their own pace, which is constant and stately and apparently immune to the pulse. The ground's glow fluctuates with the pulse. The shimmering heat-columns rise and fall with the pulse. But *you* — your perception, your cognition, your sense of how long things take — you are caught in the pulse like a leaf in a tide, and there are moments when a single heartbeat feels like an hour and moments when an hour passes like a heartbeat, and the only reliable clock is the grinding, patient movement of the fauna, who have been walking these fields since before the fields had a name.

---

There are structures here, but they are not *built*. The parameter says none, and that is correct — no one made these things, no hand placed these stones, no intention shaped these forms. And yet there are *forms*: arches of fused mineral that rise from the plain like the ribs of buried giants, natural bridges spanning cracks that descend into the glowing deep, towers of calcined material that have been *grown* rather than constructed, deposited layer by layer by the slow mineral exhalation over ages so long that the word "age" loses its meaning. They are industrial in character — they look like the ruins of factories, like the skeletons of refineries, like the frameworks of vast machines that have been stripped of everything but their most essential geometry. But they are not ruins. They are not remnants. They are *ongoing*. The mineral exhalation still flows through them. They are still growing, still accreting, still becoming more complex, and if you watch one for long enough — and "long enough" here means something different than it means anywhere else — you can see it *change*, a new strut extending from an existing joint, a new arch beginning to curve upward from a base that has been preparing for this addition for centuries.

This is the industrial character of the place: not the industry of makers, but the industry of *process*. The Calcination Fields are a factory without workers, a refinery without engineers, a production line whose product is *itself* — more calcined ground, more mineral transformation, more of the slow, hot, patient conversion of raw material into whatever this place is becoming. The fauna are part of this process. The heat is part of this process. The weirdness is part of this process. Everything here is a component in a machine that has no designer and no off switch and no purpose beyond its own continuation.

---

The ecology — if you can call it that — is a ecology of *transformation*. The fauna process the ground. The ground, processed, changes the behavior of the heat. The heat, changed, alters the rate of mineral exhalation. The mineral exhalation builds the structures. The structures, by their geometry, channel the heat in new ways. The channeled heat affects the fauna, who move differently through the altered thermal landscape, who process the ground differently in response, who change the ground in new ways, and the cycle continues, not in a circle but in a slow spiral, each iteration producing a landscape that is slightly more complex, slightly more structured, slightly more *intentional* in its appearance, though there is no intention behind it. There is only process, feeding on itself, growing like a crystal grows — not toward a goal, but toward *more*.

And at the edges of perception, at the limits of what the shimmering heat and the unreliable distance will allow you to see, there are *larger* things. Not fauna. Not structures. Something else. Shapes that may be geological or may be alive or may be industrial or may be all three or none, so far away that they exist only as interruptions in the horizon, as places where the swimming, dissolving edge of the world becomes *specific*, becomes *angled*, becomes *architectural*. They do not move. Or they move so slowly that their motion is indistinguishable from the motion of the ground itself, from the slow tectonic patience of a world that has been doing this for longer than you can calculate and will continue doing this for longer than you can imagine.

---

The light is normal, and this is perhaps the strangest thing of all. After everything — the heat, the mineral vastness, the grinding hum, the reality-bending distance, the slow enormous fauna, the factory-without-workers — the light is simply *light*. It falls from the sun in straight lines (mostly). It illuminates surfaces in a way that your eyes understand. It casts shadows that are dark and sharp and behave as shadows should. It is, in this one respect, a place that meets you halfway, that offers you a single familiar thing to hold onto while everything else slides and shifts and grinds and glows and pulses and transforms around you. The shadows of the fauna move across the calcined plain with a slowness that matches their owners, and in those shadows the ground is darker, cooler by fractions of degrees, and the cracks between the plates glow less fiercely, and for a moment — just a moment — the place almost makes sense.

Then the pulse shifts, and the shadows stretch or compress, and the distance rearranges itself, and the hum of the ground rises or falls by a quarter-tone, and the nearest fauna folds itself into a new configuration that brings it a step closer or a step further or a step in a direction that doesn't correspond to any direction you know, and the place becomes itself again: hot, high, loud, slow, vast, empty of everything you recognize and full of everything you don't, a landscape that is not a landscape but a *process*, not a place but an *event*, still ongoing, still calcining, still becoming.
