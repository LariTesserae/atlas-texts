# The Breeding Flats

You descend through a crack in the bedrock that never fully closes — a throat of stone that swallows you into the warm dark. The air hits first: thick, wet, almost chewable, carrying the mineral taste of deep earth and something else, something organic and urgent, like the breath of a body far larger than your own.

This is the Flats.

The terrain extends in all directions — not a cave, not a cavern, not any single definable space, but a *region* of open underground, a landscape that should not be able to exist at this scale below the surface. The ceiling is irregular, sometimes close enough to touch, sometimes vanishing upward into haze and distance. The floor is a lattice. That's the word that comes closest — a lattice of pale, porous stone that looks almost like calcified foam, honeycombed with cells and channels and passages, each one slightly different, the whole structure creaking and settling with a low groaning sound that never quite stops. The stone is warm. Not hot — warm, like skin. Like the inside of a mouth.

Water is everywhere and nowhere. It beads on the underside of the lattice in fat, trembling droplets that catch the light — and there is light, diffuse and sourceless, a warm amber glow that seems to radiate from the stone itself, or from the moisture, or from the air. Rivulets run through channels carved into the floor-stone, connecting pools and seeps in a constantly shifting network. You watch one channel: thirty seconds ago it was dry. Now it carries a thin stream of mineral-rich water that wasn't there before. The lattice is *drinking* and *exhaling* simultaneously. Condensation forms, falls, collects, evaporates. The humidity is such that your clothes are damp within minutes, and the sound — the sound is constant. Dripping, trickling, the soft percussion of water on stone, but also something louder: a deep, rhythmic thudding, almost tidal, coming from somewhere deeper below. The Flats breathe. You can hear it.

There is no green here. No moss, no fern, no fungus in any form you'd recognize. The lattice-stone is the only structure, and it is not alive in any botanical sense — it is mineral, crystalline in places, sedimentary in others, constantly accreting and dissolving at its edges. Where it is newest, at the growing margins of the Flats, the stone is raw and pale, almost white, crumbling under pressure. Where it is older, it has darkened to amber and ochre, saturated with mineral deposits. The channels deepen. The structure complexifies. It is, in some sense, *building itself* — or being built by the water, or by the fauna, or by some process that doesn't fit neatly into any category you have.

Because the fauna.

They are *overwhelming*. The word doesn't do it justice. Every cell of the lattice, every channel, every pool — occupied. The creatures are small. Most are smaller than your thumb. Some are smaller than a grain of rice. They are arthropodal, or annelid, or something that shares features with both and belongs to neither — segmented bodies, translucent or pale amber, with too many legs or no legs at all, with mouthparts that look like tools, like tiny pickaxes and chisels and probes. They move through the lattice like blood through capillaries, and they are *doing things*. Chewing stone. Depositing it. Shaping it. You watch one creature — pale, six-legged, no eyes — methodically scraping material from the wall of a channel and packing it into a growing ridge. It is building. Or repairing. Or eating and excreting in a way that is indistinguishable from construction.

The lattice is their work. The Flats are their work. This entire landscape is a colony-structure, a megaproject maintained by billions of tiny bodies, and it has been growing for a long time. How long? The oldest sections of stone, deep in the structure, are dense with layered deposits — geological time compressed into the architecture of a living (not-living? what is the word?) system. The creatures maintain it, extend it, remodel it constantly. Sections collapse. Sections are rebuilt overnight. The thudding you hear is not tidal — it's the aggregate sound of billions of mandibles and claws working in overlapping rhythm, a construction-site the size of a valley, running without pause.

They do not flee from you. They do not acknowledge you. They are occupied with their work in a way that suggests you are simply irrelevant — a stone that has fallen into the wrong place. But when you shift your weight and a section of lattice cracks underfoot, you watch the response: within seconds, a swarm of repair-bodies converges on the damage, and you can hear them — a sound like dry leaves, like static, like a thousand tiny hammers — patching, packing, rebuilding. The break is gone in minutes. The lattice doesn't remember it happened.

Scattered through the Flats are structures that are not lattice. These are the *built* things — and they are strange. Humps of shaped stone, low walls, partial enclosures, platforms raised a foot or two above the floor. They look like they were made by hands, or by something close to hands, but they are crude. Stacked stone. Mud-mortar. Some have fragments of worked material embedded in them — things that might be tools, or might be fossils, or might be the shed exoskeletons of the fauna repurposed as building material. The structures are old but not ancient, and they are half-consumed by the lattice, which grows around and through them like a reef absorbing a shipwreck. Were they shelters? Ritual sites? Storage? There is no way to know. Whoever or whatever built them is gone, or transformed, or *underneath* — because some of the structures cover openings in the lattice, deep shafts that drop into red-lit darkness, and from those shafts comes the deepest sound of all: a vibration you feel in your sternum, a hum that is almost below hearing.

The warmth increases as you move toward the shafts. The water there is hotter — not scalding, but distinctly warm, almost body-temperature, welling up from below in pulses that match the thudding. The creatures cluster thickest around these vents, their bodies slightly different here — darker, harder, with mineral crusts on their carapaces. They are working faster. The lattice here is newer, rawer, pale white and crumbling, and the sound is louder — a frantic chittering, the scrape of countless limbs, the soft collapse of new stone not yet set.

This is the growing edge. The Flats are expanding.

You stand at the boundary between the old lattice and the new, and you watch the frontier advance. Not quickly — you cannot see it move — but you can *feel* it. The ground is slightly less solid here. The stone gives slightly under your weight, and you can hear the creatures beneath you, inside the lattice, inside the very ground you stand on, working. Chewing. Building. The air shimmers with warmth and moisture. Water condenses on your face. A drop falls from the ceiling and strikes your hand, and you notice it is slightly viscous, carrying a trace of dissolved mineral that catches the amber light and gleams.

Something moves in your peripheral vision — larger, faster than the usual fauna. You turn, but it's gone. A shadow in the lattice. A ripple in the channel-water. The creatures around you pause — all of them, simultaneously, for perhaps two seconds — and then resume their work.

The Flats settle. The Flats throb. Somewhere, a section of ceiling lets go and crashes down, and the sound is enormous, a detonation of stone that echoes for a full minute before the repair-bodies reach it and the clicking begins, and the dust settles, and the lattice is whole again.

You are standing inside something that is *becoming*.
