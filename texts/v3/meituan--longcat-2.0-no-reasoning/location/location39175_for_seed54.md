# The Drownings of Keth-Avarath

---

You arrive at the edge of a city that is still being born.

Not metaphorically. The western districts are *literally* under construction — not by hands, not by machines in any sense you'd recognize, but by a process that resembles crystallization more than building. Structures rise from the wet stone in slow, patient increments: a wall thickens by the width of a fingernail over the course of an hour. A tower's upper floors are still translucent, not yet fully committed to solidity, their edges soft as candle wax held just below melting. The material is something between mineral and computation — a dense, dark substrate that drinks light and sweats water continuously, every surface beaded and streaming.

The city is enormous. It sprawls across a high plateau — you can feel the elevation in the thinness of each breath, the way sound carries strangely, the faint pressure behind your eyes. The plateau itself is raw, geologically young, as though the land was pushed up only recently and hasn't decided what it wants to be yet. In places the ground is still *forming*: you can see strata organizing themselves in exposed cross-sections, layers sorting by density with the patience of sediment in a glass of water that no one will ever finish. The rock here is dark and fine-grained, almost likeite but warmer than it should be, and everywhere — *everywhere* — it weeps.

Water is the medium of this place. It condenses out of the air in fat, slow droplets that never quite fall, just accumulate until they're heavy enough to slide. It seeps from every joint in the stone, every seam in the construction, pooling in the streets which are designed — if *designed* is the right word — to channel it into rivulets, then streams, then the deep arterial canals that run through the city's lower levels. The canals are the oldest parts of Keth-Avarath, or perhaps the most fundamental: they predate the buildings the way a skeleton predates flesh. The water in them is black and moves slowly, thick with dissolved minerals, and it is *warm* — not hot, but warmer than the air, warmer than the stone, as though something deep below is breathing.

The air itself is heavy. Not humid exactly — *dense*. It has a texture, a resistance, like moving through a room filled with invisible gauze. Condensation doesn't just form on surfaces; it forms *in* the air, tiny suspended droplets that never coagulate into fog but never disperse either, so that visibility is always slightly compromised, everything beyond a hundred meters softened and blurred as though seen through old glass. The light — what little there is — doesn't penetrate this haze so much as *diffuse* through it, arriving from no particular direction, sourceless and gray.

Because it is dark. Deeply, thoroughly dark. There is no sun here, or if there is, it doesn't reach this plateau. The sky — you can sense it above the haze, a vast and lightless ceiling — provides nothing. The city's illumination comes from the structures themselves, a faint bioluminescence that isn't biological at all: the building-substrate emits a dim, blue-gray glow at its edges and seams, the way a crack in a rock might glow if the rock were full of slow fire. It's enough to navigate by, barely. Enough to see the next wall, the next canal, the next figure moving through the wet streets.

And there are *figures*. So many figures.

This is the thing that strikes you after the water, after the dark, after the slow patient construction of a city that is still deciding on its own shape: the sheer, overwhelming *density* of living things. Keth-Avarath is not a city built for humans. It was not built for anything you have a name for. The streets — narrow, winding, layered with bridges and tunnels and half-formed passages that dead-end into walls not yet finished — are *thick* with fauna.

They move in layers. On the ground, things like centipedes the size of your arm but with too many legs, each leg jointed in too many places, skittering across the wet stone in overlapping waves that flow around your feet like water around a stone. Above them, at waist height, a constant drift of flying things — not insects, not birds, something with translucent bodies and internal structures you can see through their skins, organs pulsing in colors that don't have names, wings that beat at frequencies just below hearing so that you feel them in your teeth more than your ears. Higher still, clinging to the walls and ceilings, colonies of sessile organisms that might be animals or might be something else entirely: they have the texture of coral, the patience of fungi, and they *watch* you pass with structures that might be eyes or might be something more alien — sensory organs tuned to wavelengths you can't produce.

The sound is immense. Not loud in the way of machinery or storms, but loud in the way of *density* — so many individual sounds layered on top of each other that they merge into a continuous, shifting roar. The skittering of a million legs. The wet clicking of things feeding on the canal walls. A deep, subsonic hum that comes from the city itself, from the slow growth of its structures, a sound felt in the sternum more than heard. And above it all, threading through it like a melody through noise, the calls of the larger fauna — things that move through the upper levels of the city on limbs like inverted cathedral arches, their voices a chord of three or four tones simultaneously, harmonizing with each other across vast distances in patterns that might be language or might be something more fundamental.

There is no plant life. None. The city is a world of animal and mineral, flesh and stone, with nothing green, nothing rooted, nothing photosynthetic. The ecology here runs on chemosynthesis, on the warm mineral water rising from below, on the slow decay of the building-substrate itself which the sessile organisms mine and process and excrete in forms that other organisms consume. It is a food web built on stone and heat and chemistry, and it is *ancient* — far older than the city, which is merely the latest expression of a biological imperative that has been building structures on this plateau for longer than your species has had a word for *time*.

The technology of Keth-Avarath is not separate from its biology. This is the thing that makes it *weird* — not alien, not incomprehensible, but *weird* in the way that a sentence in a language you almost understand is weird. The city is built by the same organisms that inhabit it. The sessile wall-colonies *are* the construction process: they secrete the building-substrate, layer by layer, in patterns that encode — something. Information, maybe. Instructions. Memory. The structures of the city are a physical record of decisions made over millennia, and the city *reads* itself, the way a body reads its own DNA, growing and adapting and repairing according to plans stored in the molecular architecture of its own walls.

And the plans are sophisticated beyond anything your physics can fully describe. The building-substrate has properties that shouldn't coexist: it is simultaneously rigid and self-healing, conductive and insulating, opaque and — at certain frequencies — transparent. It responds to the presence of the fauna, thickening where traffic is heavy, thinning where it is not, opening passages and closing them with the slow deliberation of a living thing deciding where to grow a vein. The canals are not just waterways; they are *circulatory systems*, and the warm mineral water they carry is not just water but a medium for chemical communication, a slow neural network that connects every part of the city in a web of molecular signals that propagate at the speed of diffusion.

Time moves strangely here. Not because of any relativistic effect, but because everything is so *slow*. The city grows at the speed of geology. The fauna live at the speed of — not insects, not reptiles, something in between, metabolisms tuned to a world where a day is a brief event and a year is a breath. The water in the canals takes a decade to travel from one end of the city to the other. The walls thicken over seasons. The great arch-limbed creatures in the upper levels move with the patience of tides, each step a considered event, each call a message that will take hours to reach its destination and hours more to receive a reply.

And yet it is not still. It is *dense* with motion, layered with life, loud with sound and wet with water and dark with a darkness that is not empty but *full* — full of presences, full of processes, full of a city that is not a city in any sense you were taught but is unmistakably, undeniably a place where things *live* and *build* and *decide*.

You stand at the edge of the western districts, where the construction is newest, where the walls are still translucent and the canals are still finding their paths through stone that hasn't finished forming. The water runs over your feet — warm, mineral-heavy, faintly luminous with the organisms that live in it. Something large moves in the darkness above you, and its call arrives a moment later, three tones harmonizing with the hum of the city itself, and for a moment the sound and the stone and the water and the dark are all one thing, one continuous note, and you understand that Keth-Avarath is not a place that contains life.

It is a place that *is* life. All of it. Every wall, every canal, every drop of water, every creature in its layered millions. A single organism the size of a city, still growing, still deciding what it wants to become, patient beyond comprehension, loud beyond bearing, wet and dark and dense and *alive* in a way that makes your own aliveness feel like a rough draft.

The wall beside you thickens by a fraction of a millimeter while you watch.

The city is not finished.

The city will never be finished.

That is the point.
