# The Canopy Flats of Aethon

From up here, the air is thick enough to chew.

You stand on what the locals call a *table* — one of perhaps forty thousand vast stone platforms jutting from the mountainside at two thousand nine hundred meters, each one tilted at a slightly different angle, each one veined with channels that carry water not downward, not really, but *outward*, laterally, in defiance of the slope. The water moves in sheets across the stone, perhaps a finger's depth, warm as blood, carrying with it a faint iridescence — not oil, not mineral, something else, some suspended particulate that catches light at angles that shouldn't exist given the geometry of the surface.

The tables are old. Old the way a tooth is old — worn to its essential shape, not accumulating, not degrading, simply *being*. The stone has been smoothed by water over spans no one agrees on. You can run your hand across a channel and feel the direction of flow embossed in the rock like muscle striation, like the grain of something that was once alive and decided to stay that way in outline only. Erosion here has long since finished its argument with the landscape. This is the settled state. This is what won.

---

Between the tables, vegetation fills the gaps with an almost architectural insistence. Not forest — there is no canopy overhead, no trunks, no forest floor in any recognizable sense. Instead: *mass*. Dense, horizontal, layered growth that spans the vertical spaces between platforms like a green soffit, like the underside of a bridge designed by something that had never seen a bridge but understood the concept of spanning. Broad-leafed things, some leaves a meter across, their surfaces beaded with condensation that rolls upward in fat droplets before falling — or trying to fall — or simply moving toward the nearest channel to rejoin the lateral water. There are bromeliads the size of bathtubs perched on stone shelves, holding reservoirs of water that hum with life. Filamentous mosses drape between structures in curtains so dense they read as solid, breakable only to reveal more moss behind them, and behind that, a geometry of roots that have long since replaced the original stone with something else — a lithified organic composite that is neither rock nor wood but holds the compressive strength of both and the slow metabolism of neither.

The heat is remarkable. Not the dry heat of altitude, not the thin-air burning of high exposed ridges, but a dense, wet, pressing heat that sits on you like a hand. Thirty-seven degrees, maybe thirty-eight, and the humidity so near saturation that sweat becomes a theoretical concept — your body knows what it wants to do but the air will not permit evaporation. The result is a strange thermal equilibrium. You do not cool. You do not, over time, overheat. Your body simply *joins* the temperature of the place, the way a stone does, and you become ambient.

Water is everywhere in this condition. It condenses on your forearms. It beads in your eyebrows. It runs in constant sheets across every horizontal surface and pools in every depression and seeps from the faces of rocks that have no visible source — no aquifer, no spring, just stone that weeps, steadily, as though the mountain itself is perspiring. The channels carry it laterally across the tables, and at the edges of each table the water simply *leaves* — not falling, not flowing over a lip, but departing at the edge of the stone and continuing through the air in a coherent sheet, a liquid bridge spanning the gap between one platform and the next, sometimes two or three platforms, before it touches down and resumes its surface flow. The physics of this is not capillary action. It is not surface tension at any scale you can measure with instruments designed for this planet. The water *chooses* its path. Or something in the stone instructs it. Or the geometry of the place is such that "down" is a local variable, and the water is simply following the gradient that actually exists.

---

The sound is constant and layered. Water, first — the hiss of sheets moving over stone, the deeper note of channels at capacity, the occasional *snap* of a liquid bridge forming or breaking. Then the biological sound: a dense, overlapping chorus of calls, clicks, rustles, and something that might be song or might be echolocation or might be communication in a register that your ears interpret as music because they have no other category for it. The fauna is abundant and largely unseen. You catch movement in the moss curtains — something scaled, something feathered, something that is neither and moves with the fluid certainty of a creature that has never been prey. Insects, or insect-analogues, move in clouds near the water surfaces, their wings producing a collective drone that shifts pitch as the swarm turns, a living theremin. Larger things move in the deep vegetation between tables — you hear them, a displacement of mass, a rustle that starts low and moves upward through the layered growth like a wave, and then silence, and then the water sounds resume as though nothing happened.

There are structures. Not many. A wall here, the corner of a building there, made of the same lithified composite as the root-stone, half-swallowed by growth. They are old in the way the tables are old — not ruins, not decayed, but *settled*, integrated, as though the buildings grew here rather than being built, or as though building and growing were the same activity performed by something that did not distinguish between them. You find a doorway once, standing open, leading into a space that is now entirely filled with bromeliads, their reservoirs connected by the same lateral water system that runs across the tables, so that the interior of this structure is a kind of hydraulic garden, a room that has become a watershed. There are no tools. No artifacts you can identify as tools. No writing. Whatever built these structures left only the structures themselves, and the structures have been so thoroughly recontextualized by time and growth that they read as geological features, as natural as the tables, as the channels, as the weeping stone.

---

The light is normal. This is perhaps the most unsettling thing. Given the strangeness of the water, the density of the growth, the heat, the altitude, the sound — you expect the light to be wrong. Filtered green, or amber, or polarized, or somehow *more*. But it is simply daylight, full-spectrum, slightly diffused by the humidity, casting shadows that fall in the correct direction, illuminating surfaces with the honest flatness of ordinary photons. The normalcy of the light makes everything else stranger. It is as though the world is saying: *this is not a dream, this is not a hallucination, this is simply what is, and the light confirms it.*

You walk. The tables extend in every direction, each one slightly different in size and tilt and the pattern of its channels, but all recognizably the same *kind* of thing, the way all hands are hands. The gaps between them are bridged by vegetation, by liquid, by root-stone, by the accumulated detritus of growth so dense it has become structural. You can cross from table to table without descending, without climbing, moving laterally across the mountainside at a constant elevation, following the water, which is following something else, which is following the shape of the stone, which is following a logic that is not geology and not biology and not engineering but some older category that includes all three.

The air tastes of minerals and green and something faintly sweet, like the smell of warm stone after rain, except it has not rained, it is always like this, the water is always at the surface, the stone is always weeping, the growth is always dense, the heat is always pressing, the sound is always loud, and the place is always, has always been, will always be exactly this: a high wet impossible garden on the side of a mountain, where water moves sideways and buildings grow and the light is normal and nothing is normal and you are ambient now, you are the temperature of the place, and the place does not notice you, and the place does not need to.
