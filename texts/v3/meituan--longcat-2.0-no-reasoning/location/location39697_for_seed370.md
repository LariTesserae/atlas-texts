# The Lithodermis

You stand inside a body. Not a body that was ever alive in any way you'd recognize, but a body nonetheless—one that grew, calcified, and then simply *was*.

The ceiling is low enough that you could touch it if you reached up: a vault of fused stone plates, each one no larger than a shield, overlapping like the scales of some unimaginable pangolin. They fit together with a precision that should be impossible without measurement or tool, yet here is the place—built, every molecule of it, by something that knew exactly what it was doing. The seams between plates are filled with a dark mineral glue, something between resin and lead, pressed in by enormous force and now harder than the plates themselves.

The walls are the same. The floor is the same. Everything is scales.

Each plate is carved—no, *pressed*, you can tell—with a shallow spiral groove that runs from one edge to the other, like a fingerprint magnified to the size of a door. The grooves are filled with a faintly luminous paste that provides the only light in the place: a dull amber glow that makes everything look submerged in honey or old urine. It is not enough to see clearly by. Shadows pool in the corners where the spiral grooves run shallow, and the far end of this chamber—it's hard to say how far—fades into a brownish murk that might be ten feet or fifty.

The sound is the reason your hands went to your ears the moment you arrived.

Every scale vibrates. Not visibly—you can't see the movement—but you can feel it in your teeth, in the bones of your forearms, in the soft tissue behind your eyes. Each plate hums at a slightly different pitch, and the pitches are *dense* against one another, packed so close in frequency that they blur into a single roar of almost-information. It is not music. It is not noise. It is the sound of a structure under every possible load simultaneously, the resonant signature of a thing that was built to bear weight from every direction at once and never, ever collapse.

You realize, after the first minute or so, that the sound has an interior geography. There are places in the room where it intensifies into a physical pressure—you can feel it pushing against your chest like the air before a thunderstorm. There are other places, near the floor, in the angles where walls meet, where the sound partially cancels and you get a pocket of relative quiet that feels almost sacred, almost stolen. You press yourself into one such pocket and the relief is so acute it nearly makes you dizzy.

There are no doors. No windows. The place is sealed—has been sealed for longer than you can construct a meaningful number for. Whatever process built this lithodermis is finished. The scales stopped growing. The paste in the grooves stopped being replenished. The mineral glue hardened for the last time. And now the thing simply persists, in equilibrium, humming its overlapping frequencies into a space with no listener.

On the floor, half-buried in a fine dust of spalled stone—no wind to carry it, no rain to pack it, just the slow self-abrasion of mineral against mineral over an incomprehensible duration—you find tools.

They are small. They fit a hand. They are made of bone, or something that has replaced bone, a white and slightly translucent material that might be calcium phosphate or might be something else entirely that occupies the same niche. The tools are simple: scrapers, points, a curved blade with a handle shaped for a grip that is almost but not quite human. The thumbs are in a different place. The fingers are too many, or too few—it's hard to tell, because the handles have been pressed to fit *exactly*, and your hand slides into them with a discomfort that is almost recognition.

There are also, scattered among the tools, fragments of the same white material that are clearly not tools. They are shaped like the spiral grooves on the walls—curved, shallow, precise. They are, you realize, *fossils*. Not of organisms, but of the process that carved the grooves. Impressions left in a softer material that has since been stripped away or dissolved, leaving only these negatives behind. Evidence of a construction technique. A lost craft.

No water has ever been here. No water will ever be here. The chemistry of this place is entirely anhydrous—the mineral glue, the luminous paste, the bone-tools, the dust—all of it exists in a dry equilibrium that has no pathway to anything else. There is no rust, no rot, no mold, no stain. There is only the slow, slow settling of one mineral surface against another, and the sound, and the amber light, and the silence between the sounds.

You are the first thing to move in here in longer than the structure has existed.

The scales hum. The paste glows. The tools wait in the dust, shaped for hands that are not yours, built by a process that is not alive, in a place that is not a place—just a sealed chamber in a body of stone that grew and finished and stopped, and has been holding its breath ever since.
