# The Grotto of Unfinished Tides

---

You are inside a lung.

Not a metaphor — or not *only* a metaphor. The space is roughly the size of a large room, though it doesn't hold still enough to measure. The walls are wet stone, but the stone is warm, and it breathes. Slowly. A cycle of perhaps forty minutes: the walls contract, the water level rises by a hand's breadth, and then the walls relax and the water recedes, leaving everything slick and gasping. You can feel the pressure change in your sinuses. The grotto is alive in the way that a body is alive — not with intention, but with process.

The water is everywhere. It fills the space to about two-thirds, dark and faintly brackish, carrying a mineral taste like old copper and something sweeter underneath — almost floral, almost like overripe stone fruit. It is not seawater. It is not freshwater. It is *body* water. The grotto sweats it from its walls in a perpetual slow weep, and the water has nowhere to go, so it simply *is* — a standing pool that rises and falls with the breathing, that laps against surfaces with a sound like someone running their tongue across their teeth.

The light is almost absent. What exists comes from the water itself — a faint bioluminescence, not from organisms but from the water's own chemistry, a cold blue-green glow that makes everything look like it's been photographed through deep ice. You can see perhaps three meters. Beyond that, the darkness is total and textured, like velvet pressed against your eyes. Occasionally something in the water flares brighter — a brief pulse, like a nerve firing — and for a moment you see the full shape of the space: the vaulted ceiling, the hanging formations, the dense growth. Then it subsides and you are blind again.

---

The vegetation is the most startling thing. In a place this dark, this wet, this enclosed, you would expect moss at most. Instead, the walls are *packed* with growth — a dense, interlocking mat of organisms that have no business existing together. Ferns with fronds like black lace. Fungi in shelf formations, pale and translucent, their undersides dripping. Something that looks like seaweed but grows on stone, its fronds swaying in the breathing current. Liverworts in thick carpets, their surfaces beaded with water that doesn't roll off but clings in perfect spheres, trembling. And threading through all of it, a fine mycelial network visible as silver lines on the rock surface, pulsing faintly — not with light, but with *movement*, a slow peristalsis, as though the grotto's walls are digesting something.

The growth is so dense that in places you cannot see the stone beneath it. The walls appear to be made of living tissue — layered, damp, faintly warm to the touch. When you press your hand against it, it yields slightly, and the mycelial threads brighten under your palm before fading. The grotto notices you. Not with awareness. With chemistry.

---

The formations on the ceiling are young — geologically, impossibly young. Stalactites that should have taken millennia to form hang in clusters, but they are soft. When you touch one, your finger sinks in slightly, and the surface dimples like flesh. They are growing visibly, if you watch long enough — a millimeter per breathing cycle, mineral-laden water dripping from their tips and building them outward in shapes that are almost but not quite regular. Some of them have begun to branch in patterns that look disturbingly like bronchi. The grotto is building a respiratory system on its ceiling. It has been doing this for what might be decades. It might be doing it for the first time. Time moves strangely here.

The floor is uneven — sharp ridges of newly deposited mineral, still angular, not yet smoothed by the water that laps over them. In the shallow pools between ridges, the water has a surface tension that seems too high, too elastic. It dimples when something touches it but doesn't break easily. Small bubbles rise from fissures in the floor and don't pop when they reach the surface — they sit there, trembling, accumulating in clusters that drift slowly toward the walls and are absorbed.

---

There is one structure. Or the remains of one. In the deepest part of the grotto, where the water is waist-deep and the breathing current is strongest, there is a low wall — perhaps a meter long, half a meter high — made of stones that have been deliberately placed. The stones are not local. They are smooth river rock, foreign to this mineral environment, and they have been stacked with care. But the wall is old in a way that doesn't match the grotto's youth. It is half-dissolved, its surfaces pitted and softened, the gaps between stones filled with the same dense vegetation that covers everything else. Something grew a shelf fungus directly on top of it, incorporating the structure into itself.

There are no tools. No other artifacts. Just this one wall, this one act of arrangement, now being slowly unmade by the grotto's patient chemistry. Whoever built it — if anyone built it — is gone. The grotto is taking their wall back.

---

The fauna is rare but present. In the water, occasionally, something moves — a flicker of motion at the edge of your vision, gone when you turn. Not fish. Something simpler. Translucent, multi-segmented, perhaps a centimeter long, moving with a slow undulation that suggests it has never known a predator. There are perhaps a dozen of them in the entire grotto. They feed on the mineral content of the water, or on the biofilm that coats every surface, or on something else entirely. They have no eyes. They do not need them.

On the ceiling, in the driest pockets where the breathing cycle exposes the stone to air for a few minutes, there are tiny snails — their shells spiraled in a direction that is wrong, that doesn't match any chirality you know. They move so slowly that watching them is like watching the stalactites grow. They leave trails that glow faintly for a few minutes after they pass, then fade.

And sometimes — rarely, unpredictably — something larger moves in the water. You feel it as a displacement, a sudden rise in the level that doesn't match the breathing cycle, a current that pushes against you from no particular direction. Then it passes. You never see it. You are not sure it is fauna. You are not sure it is singular.

---

The weirdness is in the water's behavior. It does not always obey the walls. Sometimes a tendril of water detaches from the surface and moves across the air — not falling, not spraying, but *traveling*, a sinuous column that navigates the space like something searching, touching surfaces, tasting them, then returning to the pool. Sometimes the water in one part of the grotto is warmer than in another, with no source for the heat. Sometimes the breathing cycle stutters — the walls contract and then hold, hold, hold, and the pressure builds in your ears and the water rises and the light flares and you think the grotto is going to *do something*, and then it releases, and everything subsides, and the quiet returns.

The mycelial network is not fungal. Or not only fungal. When you look at it closely — in the brief flares of bioluminescence — you can see that the silver threads connect not just the vegetation but the *formations*, the stalactites, the mineral ridges on the floor, the foreign stones of the wall. The grotto is one organism. Or it is becoming one. The boundaries between stone and water and growth and air are not where you expect them to be. The stone is alive. The water is structural. The air tastes of chlorophyll.

---

The sound is mostly the breathing. A low, wet, rhythmic sound — not quite a sigh, not quite a heartbeat. The drip of water from the stalactites, each drop arriving at a slightly different interval, creating a pattern that almost resolves into rhythm and then doesn't. The faint crackle of mineral deposition, audible if you hold your breath — a sound like ice forming, like tiny structures locking into place. And underneath everything, below the threshold of hearing but present in your chest, a subsonic hum that might be the grotto's resonance frequency or might be your own blood moving through your veins. It is impossible to tell.

---

You are very small here. The grotto is intimate, enclosed, complete. It does not need you. It was here before you arrived — or it wasn't, and it assembled itself in the time it took you to find the entrance, which you can no longer locate. The walls have shifted. The breathing continues. The water laps. The stalactites grow. The wall dissolves. The silver threads pulse.

The grotto is patient. It has been doing this for a very long time, or for no time at all. It will continue. You can feel it in the pressure, in the warmth, in the way the water holds you — not supporting, not drowning, just *present*, fully present, occupying every available space between you and the stone.

You are inside something that is still deciding what it is.
