# The Forge-Cleft of Unending Churn

---

You stand — or rather, you *crouch*, because the ceiling is low — inside a city that has no sky.

The walls on every side are fresh-riven mineral, not quite stone, not quite metal. They have the color of blackened copper and the texture of something that was torn open yesterday and has not yet decided what it is. In places the cleft-wall still weeps a thin metallic exudate that runs in rivulets no thicker than a hair, freezing in mid-drip into a lace of crystalline alloy. The geology here is *young* — not millions of years old, not thousands. This rift opened its jaws within living memory, maybe within the last few decades, and it has not yet learned to settle. The surfaces are knife-edged. Footing is treacherous. Every surface is a serration.

And into this wound, people have built.

Not people, exactly. **Bodies.** Small, dense, numerous. They move in currents along the walls themselves, gripping the fresh stone with hands or hooks or magnetic pads, and they *build* as they go — welding struts into the wall face, bolting platforms that cantilever out over the narrow dark, stringing conduits and cable-veins that throb with a dull amber pulse. The construction is not a single architecture. It is *competing* architectures — one faction's scaffold bolted directly through another's, a third group's pipe-network threaded through the gaps, a fourth group's habitation pods crammed into any concave surface large enough to hold a curled body. The density of it is staggering. You cannot look at any square meter of wall without seeing seven layers of construction, each one partially obscuring the others, each one vibrating at a slightly different frequency.

The air is cool. Not cold — cool, like the inside of a mine, like the temperature of deep rock that has never known sun. It carries a taste of ozone and hot solder and something organic that you cannot quite name, a musk that rises from the bodies themselves. There is no water anywhere. The inhabitants do not drink. They do not seem to need to. Their biology — if it is biology — runs on something else. Mineral exchange, perhaps. Electrochemical. You see them press their palms against the fresh stone and *absorb*, their skin flushing briefly with the same copper-dark color as the wall before fading back to their own pallid tones.

The light is dim. It comes from the conduits — that amber pulse — and from the welding arcs that flare and die and flare again in a constant staccato along every surface. The effect is of a city lit by its own nervous system. Shadows are deep and numerous. Things move in them.

And there are *so many things that move.*

The fauna — if that word even applies — is abundant and strange. The walls are alive with small creatures that may be animals or may be machines or may be something that predates the distinction. They are the size of your hand, flat-bodied, many-legged, and they move with the purposeful industry of ants in a colony. They carry things: fragments of fresh mineral, scraps of wire, droplets of the metallic exudate held in their cupped limbs. They flow along the walls in rivers of chitin or ceramic or something between. They interact with the builders — sometimes cooperatively, sometimes not. A builder will reach down and pluck one from the wall and press it into a junction box where it will live for a while, processing something, before it crawls back out and rejoins the wall-river. Other creatures are larger — the size of a dog, the size of a person — and they move through the cleft's central space on cables or in free fall, catching themselves on far walls, traversing the void with a boneless ease.

The sound is mostly quiet, but the quiet is *textured.* A low hum runs through everything — the resonance of the cleft itself, the vibration of all that construction, the pulse in the conduits. Against this, small sounds emerge and subside: the tick of cooling metal, the skitter of many legs on stone, the brief hiss of a welding arc, the murmur of voices speaking a language that sounds like it was designed to be heard in enclosed spaces — short, clipped, with long vowels that carry in the cool air. Occasionally a section of wall *cracks* — a new fissure opening, a fresh surface revealed — and there is a sound like a bell being struck underwater, and every creature in range freezes for half a second before resuming.

Because the cleft is still opening. That is the turbulence. The walls are not stable. New surfaces emerge constantly — the rift widening by millimeters per hour, the floor dropping, the ceiling rising, new branches splitting off into darkness. And the city must *follow.* Builders swarm to any fresh surface within minutes, claiming it, bolting to it, beginning the work of making it habitable before the next crack renders their previous work obsolete. Entire sections of construction are abandoned daily — left to the wall-creatures, who dismantle them with patient efficiency and carry the materials to the new front. The city is not a place. It is a *process.* It is a migration that never arrives.

You notice, after a while, that the walls are not random. The fresh mineral that emerges has *patterns* in it — not natural strata, not quite. Repeating geometries. Circuit-like traces in the stone itself, as if the rock were trying to become something, as if the rift were not tearing open dead matter but *hatching* something that was always encoded in it. The builders seem aware of this. They orient their construction to align with the patterns. They treat certain wall-surfaces with reverence, building around them rather than through them. The wall-creatures, too, avoid certain geometries, flowing around them like water around stones.

There is a sense — and you cannot prove this, but you feel it — that the city and the cleft and the creatures and the builders are all part of a single system. That the rift is not destroying a mountain but *birthing* something. That the construction is not shelter but *metabolism.* That the cool air and the dim light and the constant churn are not conditions to be endured but the necessary environment for a process that has been running for longer than anyone remembers and will not stop.

A new crack opens somewhere above you. The bell-sound rings through the cleft. Amber light flickers. The wall-creatures surge toward the sound like a tide. The builders follow, already welding, already bolting, already building the next layer of the city on the wound's newest lip.

The place is small. You could walk its full length in an hour, maybe less. But it contains *everything.* Every surface is alive with purpose. Every shadow holds a body or a creature or a machine in the middle of its task. The density of intention here is almost unbearable — the sense that every cubic centimeter of this cleft is *claimed*, is *used*, is *in process.*

There is no water. There is no green. There is no sun. There is no rest.

There is the forge-cleft, and it is growing, and the city grows with it, and the creatures move through it like blood through a vein, and the walls keep opening, and the walls keep revealing their patterns, and the builders keep building on the edge of the new.

It is cool. It is dim. It is loud with quiet.

It is not finished. It may never be finished. That is the point.
