# The Dripworks of Keth-Avul

---

You arrive at the edge of a city that should not exist.

The plateau is vast — a tableland of dark basalt, perhaps forty kilometers across, lifted high enough that the air has thinned to a permanent metallic taste. The sky is the color of a bruise healing: deep violet-grey, lit from within by a sun that never fully breaks through the haze. It is not dark, exactly. It is *dim* — a perpetual late-afternoon that never resolves into evening, the light sourceless and directionless, as though the atmosphere itself has learned to glow faintly and forgotten how to stop.

The heat is the first thing your body understands. It comes from below. The rock is warm underfoot — not scalding, but *alive* with warmth, the way a sleeping animal is warm. Steam rises from cracks in the basalt in slow, fat curls, and where the steam meets the cooler air of the plateau's edge, it condenses into a perpetual weeping. The cliff faces weep. The buildings weep. Everything here is damp to the touch, coated in a film of mineral water that never quite dries, that beads and runs and drips in a constant, arrhythmic percussion — *tik, tik, tik-tik, tik* — the sound of the city's heartbeat, if it has one.

---

## The Water

There is no river. No lake. No visible source. And yet water is *everywhere*.

It seeps up through the basalt from some deep aquifer heated by the plateau's volcanic roots, emerging as warm springs that pool in natural depressions and carved channels. The people of Keth-Avul — if they are people — have built their entire civilization around the management of this weeping. Channels cut into the stone, some ancient and smooth as glass, some newer and rough with tool marks, carry warm water in slow, deliberate circuits through the city. The water is mineral-heavy, faintly orange with iron, and it leaves deposits everywhere: terraced crusts of calcium and silica that build up over years into strange, coral-like formations on every surface the water touches. These deposits are the closest thing to organic growth you will see here. They branch. They layer. They *accumulate* with a patience that mimics life.

In the lower districts, where the channels converge, the air is thick with warm mist. Visibility drops to a few meters. You hear the city before you see it — the drip, the slow rush of channeled water, the distant mechanical groan of something large and purposeful.

---

## The City

Keth-Avul is built *into* the weeping, not against it.

The structures are massive and low, constructed from the same dark basalt as the plateau itself, cut in thick blocks and fitted without mortar — the water does the sealing, depositing its minerals into every joint until the walls are monolithic, fused, *grown together* over decades. The architecture is industrial in its logic: functional, repetitive, scaled for processes rather than inhabitants. Enormous rectangular blocks, some the size of city blocks themselves, are connected by covered channels and elevated walkways. Smokestacks — though they emit steam, not smoke — rise at irregular intervals, some straight, some leaning, all crusted white and orange with mineral deposits that make them look like the bones of some vast, calcified creature.

The city is not dense. It is *open* — sprawling across the plateau in a loose, deliberate scatter, with enormous empty spaces between districts. These gaps are not wasteland. They are functional: collection fields where the weeping rock is left exposed, where water is allowed to seep and pool and be harvested. The basalt in these open areas is carved into shallow basins, terraced like rice paddies, each one a collection point. The terraces are old — centuries old — and the mineral crusts have built up into ridged, undulating surfaces that look, from a distance, like frozen waves. Walking across them is treacherous. The crusts are brittle, and beneath them the warm water moves in hidden channels, and a misstep can break through into a pocket of steam hot enough to blister.

The built environment has a quality of *layered time*. The oldest structures are almost geological — their edges softened by mineral accretion, their surfaces indistinguishable from natural rock formations. Newer constructions are sharper, their basalt blocks still dark and angular, their channels still clean. But even the new buildings are designed to be *consumed* by the water over time. There is no resistance to the weeping here. The city is a negotiation with it.

---

## The Industry

Something is being made here. Something requires heat, water, and time.

The industrial heart of Keth-Avul lies in the city's center, where the largest structures cluster around a deep vent in the basalt — a natural fissure from which superheated water and steam erupt in slow, rhythmic pulses, like breathing. The machinery here is old and enormous: piston-driven pumps, filtration arrays, distillation columns that rise three stories high, all fabricated from iron and copper and a dark, oily ceramic that resists mineral buildup. The machines are loud — or rather, they are *present*, their deep rhythmic clanking and hissing cutting through the ambient quiet of the plateau like a second, mechanical pulse layered over the first organic one of dripping water.

What they produce is not immediately clear. The distillation columns output a clear, viscous fluid that is collected in ceramic basins and carried away in sealed channels. The filtration arrays process the mineral-heavy water into something purer, something that is piped to the outer districts. There are storage facilities — vast, low warehouses with thick walls — but their contents are hidden. The industry of Keth-Avul has the feel of a *process* rather than a product: something cyclical, self-sustaining, more like metabolism than manufacturing.

The workers — and here the word becomes uncertain — move through the industrial district in small groups, wrapped in heavy, damp clothing that has been mineral-stiffened into something almost like armor. Their movements are slow, deliberate, adapted to the heat and the wet. They do not speak, or if they do, their voices are lost in the machinery and the water. They tend the machines with a familiarity that suggests *relationship* rather than operation — adjusting a valve here, clearing a channel there, listening to the rhythm of a pump with their hands pressed against its housing.

---

## The Strange

There is something wrong with the water. Or something *other* about it.

In the collection fields, in the quiet hours between the machinery's pulses, the water in the terraced basins sometimes *moves* against the gradient. A pool that should flow downhill instead ripples inward, toward its center, as though something beneath the surface has shifted. The mineral crusts in certain areas grow in patterns that are too regular to be accidental — spirals, branching fractals, lattices that repeat with mathematical precision. The oldest residents of Keth-Avul — the ones who have lived here longest, whose skin has taken on a faint orange tint from years of mineral exposure — say the water is *learning*. That the deep aquifer is not merely a geological feature but a *system*, one that has been shaped by centuries of human engineering into something that now shapes them back.

In the deepest district, near the central vent, there is a structure that is not a building. It is a *formation* — a tower of mineral accretion that has grown, over who knows how many decades, into something that resembles architecture. It has openings. It has channels. It has a shape that suggests *intention*. The people of Keth-Avul do not go near it. They have built their newest district on the opposite side of the city, as though slowly, incrementally, retreating from something they do not wish to name.

The steam that rises from the vent carries, at certain times, a sound that is not mechanical. A low, resonant hum, felt more than heard, that vibrates in the chest and the teeth. It is not constant. It comes and goes in cycles that do not match the machinery, do not match the weather, do not match anything observable. The old residents say it is the plateau *thinking*. The new arrivals say it is gas moving through subterranean channels. Both groups continue their work.

---

## The Ecology of Absence

There is no green here. No leaf, no blade of grass, no moss even — the heat and the mineral saturation have prevented any conventional plant life from taking hold. The plateau is a world of stone and water and steam, and the only living things that have adapted to it are *small* and *hidden*.

In the warm, wet channels, there are films — bacterial mats, perhaps, or something adjacent to them — that coat the stone in patches of iridescent sheen. They are not colorful in any conventional sense; they shimmer in oil-slick rainbows over surfaces of black and orange, beautiful and faintly nauseating. In the deepest, hottest channels near the central vent, there are *things* that move — small, pale, multi-legged creatures that skitter across the mineral crusts and retreat into cracks when disturbed. They are rare. You might go hours without seeing one. But they are *there*, a thread of animal life woven through the mineral world, adapted to heat and wet and darkness in ways that would be impossible almost anywhere else.

The air, thick with moisture, carries almost no sound beyond the immediate. The plateau is quiet in the way that a cathedral is quiet — not silent, but *hushed*, the ambient noise of water and machinery and wind absorbed by the mist and the soft, damp stone. Sounds do not carry. A conversation at normal volume reaches perhaps ten meters before it dissolves into the general murmur of dripping and hissing. The city feels *private* in this way — each district, each structure, each individual wrapped in their own small sphere of sound, isolated by the very atmosphere that sustains them.

---

## The Edge

At the plateau's rim, the weeping intensifies. The cliff face is a curtain of warm water, running in continuous sheets down the basalt to the lowlands far below — lowlands you cannot see through the mist and the haze. The channels of Keth-Avul terminate here, spilling their mineral-heavy water over the edge in slow, broad waterfalls that dissolve into spray before they have fallen ten meters. The sound at the edge is different: a soft, continuous roar, like breathing, like the plateau exhaling.

There are structures at the edge — old ones, half-dissolved by the water they were built to channel. Watchtowers, perhaps, or collection points, or something else entirely. Their original purpose has been eroded along with their edges. They stand like monuments to a question no one remembers asking.

The mist rises from the edge and drifts across the plateau, feeding the weeping, feeding the channels, feeding the city. The cycle is closed. The water falls and rises and falls again, and the city tends its machines and its terraces and its slow, strange industry, and the mineral crusts grow, and the deep vent breathes, and the plateau — hot, wet, high, old, and patient — continues whatever it is doing.

Whatever it has been doing for a very long time.
