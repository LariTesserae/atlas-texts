# The Condensation Cathedral

---

You are standing inside a single room. That is the first thing to understand — the entire world you can perceive is one room, and it is enormous.

The ceiling is somewhere above you, lost in a haze of warm vapor that catches and scatters the light from hundreds of industrial fixtures bolted into girders you cannot quite see. The light is not dramatic. It is the light of a factory floor at midmorning — flat, functional, slightly amber, the color of old sodium vapor lamps that have been running so long their warmth has become indistinguishable from the warmth of the air itself. Everything is lit evenly. There are no shadows deep enough to hide in.

The room is perhaps two hundred meters across. You know this not from measuring but from the way sound behaves — a low, omnidirectional hum that seems to come from the walls and floor and air simultaneously, punctuated by rhythmic mechanical events: a deep *chunk* somewhere to the left, a hiss of released pressure somewhere above, the distant clatter of something metallic tumbling down a chute. The sound is enormous. Not loud in the way of a scream or an explosion, but loud in the way of a river — a constant, physical presence that you feel in your sternum, that makes your own breathing feel small and private. You have to raise your voice to speak to someone standing next to you, and even then the words arrive softened, as though the air itself is reluctant to carry them.

The floor is metal grating. Beneath it, you can hear water moving — not flowing in any particular direction, but *present*, a dark sheet of it maybe three meters down, absolutely still except where condensation drips from the grating above and sends out slow, concentric rings. The water beneath the floor is black. It has been there a long time.

---

The walls, such as they are, are machines.

Not walls *with* machines on them. The machines *are* the walls. They rise from the floor-grating on all sides — or at least on the sides you can see, which may be all of them, since the room's far edges dissolve into the haze and you cannot tell where machinery ends and distance begins. The machines are built from iron and brass and a dark, oily stone that might be basalt or might be something poured and set. They are tall. They are complex. They are covered in pipes, valves, pressure gauges, riveted panels, access hatches, and at intervals, large spinning wheels whose purpose you cannot determine. Some of the pipes are thick as a person's torso. Some are thin as fingers. All of them sweat.

This is where the water comes from. Not from the reservoir below — that is something else, something older — but from the machines themselves. They are warm. The air is warm. And where warm metal meets warm, moisture-heavy air, condensation forms, and the entire surface of every machine is beaded with water. It collects in rivulets. It runs down pipes and pools in the joints of valve assemblies and drips from every horizontal surface in a constant, irregular patter that is one of the room's fundamental sounds — not loud, but *dense*, a texture rather than a noise, like static made of water.

The machines are not all running. Some are still, and on the still ones, the condensation has had time to do its work. Mineral deposits crust the joints in white and pale green. Patinas of verdigris spread across brass plates like continents on a map. And in every crack, every seam, every place where a bolt meets a plate and a tiny pocket of persistent moisture has gathered — there is green.

---

The vegetation is the thing you did not expect.

It is not decorative. It is not incidental. It is *lush* — aggressively, almost confrontationally lush, given where it is growing. Ferns erupt from pipe junctions in cascading fountains of dark green, their fronds six feet long, heavy with moisture, dripping. Moss covers every horizontal surface that isn't actively too hot to touch — thick, emerald, ankle-deep in places, so dense it has the texture of a living carpet. You can see where it has been stepped on and slowly, over what must have been months, rebounded. Vines — thick-stemmed, waxy-leaved — climb the structural columns in tight spirals, and where they reach the warmer pipes near the ceiling, they bloom with small, pale flowers that open and close on a cycle you can't quite time. The flowers are white. They smell like nothing. Or perhaps the smell is there but is buried under the mineral-and-oil-and-wet-stone smell of the room itself, which is the dominant smell, the smell of everything.

There are trees. This is the strangest thing. In three places in the room, where structural columns widen into what might be load-bearing pylons, trees have taken root — not in soil, but in accumulated organic matter, in centuries of fallen fern fronds and moss and the slow compost of things that grew and died and were not removed. They are not tall. The ceiling, or the haze, or something about the light limits them. But they are broad, and their canopies spread horizontally, pressing against pipes and conduits, and the machines have been built *around* them, or the trees have grown *into* the machines, and it is impossible to tell which came first. The bark is wrapped in wire-thin epiphytes. The roots are visible above the grating, threading through it, reaching down toward the black water below.

The ecology is abundant but quiet. Insects — small, dark, unfamiliar — move through the moss in purposeful lines. Something chirps in the canopy of the nearest tree, a sound like a tiny, irregular machine. There are birds, or things like birds: small, quick shapes that move between the ferns and the pipe-networks, nesting in the junction boxes of electrical conduits, their feathers carrying a faint oily sheen. They watch you. They are not afraid. There is no reason for them to be afraid. You are the anomaly here, not them.

---

The strangeness is not in any single thing. It is in the *fit*.

This room should not exist. The parameters are wrong for each other. A fully built, industrial, mechanized environment at extreme elevation — you can feel the elevation in the slight thinness of the air, in the way sound carries differently than it should at sea level, in the faint, persistent pressure behind your eyes. This is a place that is *high*. Impossibly high. The machines are doing something that requires them to be here, at this altitude, in this single vast room, running and dripping and condensing and running again. The water cycle is closed. The moisture that rises from the machines condenses on the cooler surfaces near the ceiling, drips down, is caught by the vegetation, is transpired back into the air, condenses again. The system has been running for a very long time. The erosion parameter is not about rock here — it is about *everything*. The machines are weathered. The stone is weathered. The grating under your feet has been worn into subtle curves by the passage of... someone. Something. The traffic patterns are visible in the metal, smooth channels where feet or wheels or something else has passed and passed and passed, and the condensation has had time to work on these paths differently than on the surrounding metal, creating tiny channels, tiny streams, a micro-hydrology of foot traffic.

The room is empty of people. That is the density parameter — the vast space between presences. The machines run. The water cycles. The ferns grow. The birds nest. But there is no one here. There is evidence of someone — a tool left on a grating panel, a ladder leaning against a column at an angle that suggests recent use, a panel removed and set aside with care, the machinery behind it exposed and complex and still running — but no one. The room is a cathedral to a process whose operators have stepped out, or died, or never existed in the way you understand existence, and the process continues without them, patient, slow, deafening, warm, wet, green, and strange.

---

You stand on the grating. Water drips around you. A fern frond brushes your shoulder, heavy with condensation, and you feel the cold of it through your clothing. Somewhere above, a pressure valve releases — a long, descending hiss that ends in a single, resonant *clang* that reverberates through the floor and the walls and the water below and takes a very long time to fade. The birds go quiet. Then they don't. The machines hum. The moss grows, imperceptibly, in every direction at once.

The room has been here a long time. The room will be here a long time yet.
