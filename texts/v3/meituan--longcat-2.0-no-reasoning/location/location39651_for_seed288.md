# The Root-Halls of Vethara

---

You descend.

Not through soil — there is no soil here, not in any sense you'd recognize — but through a gradual loosening of the world above, a thinning of stone into something more porous, more willing. The ceiling recedes. The walls pull back. And then you are *inside* something vast.

The first thing that strikes you is the sound. It is not loud in the way a storm is loud, or a machine. It is loud the way a cathedral is loud when every surface is singing. A deep, continuous, layered drone fills the space — the resonance of the living structures themselves, thousands upon thousands of them, each one humming at its own frequency, and all of those frequencies combining into a wall of sound that presses against your chest, that you feel in your teeth, that makes the air itself seem to thicken. It is not unpleasant. It is the sound of a city breathing.

And it *is* a city.

---

The structures rise from the floor and descend from the ceiling in columns of pale, fibrous material — not wood, not stone, not bone, but something that partakes of all three. They are the color of old ivory, of dried tendon, of the inside of a shell. Some are as thin as your wrist. Others are massive, ten meters across, fluted and ridged with growth-lines that record centuries in their grain. They are not built. They are *grown*. Every wall, every arch, every bridge spanning the gaps between pillars, every spiraling staircase that coils up a column's surface — all of it is biological. Living tissue, slow and mineral-rich, deposited over epochs by organisms that no longer move, that have become their own architecture.

The city has no streets in the conventional sense. Instead, the floor is a continuous surface of the same pale material, worn smooth by passage — but the passage of what? You look down and see the tracks: shallow grooves, parallel sets, some wide, some narrow, pressed into the surface like fossils of movement. The floor has partially healed over many of them, the living material slowly filling in old paths, and new grooves have been pressed beside them. The city is re-routing itself, slowly, over centuries, following the traffic of its inhabitants.

---

The inhabitants.

They are everywhere. That is the first impression — *everywhere* — and yet the space is so large, so open, that they never feel crowded. They move through the halls in streams and clusters, in pairs and alone, and the sound of their movement adds to the drone: the click of chitin on living stone, the rustle of membranous wings, the wet percussion of soft bodies against hard surfaces, the scrape and tap of limbs too numerous to count.

They are arthropodal, mostly. Not insects — nothing here is quite an insect — but built on the same deep body plan: segmented, jointed, armored in plates that range from translucent amber to matte black. Some are small, no larger than your hand, and they move in glittering clouds around the bases of the great columns, feeding on the nutrient-rich secretions that bead on the living walls. Others are large — two meters long, low-slung, multi-legged — and they move through the halls with the slow deliberation of grazing animals, their broad mouths pressed against the floor, rasping up the thin film of organic material that constantly sheds from the city's surfaces.

There are flyers, too. Not many — the ceiling is high but not infinite — but enough that the upper air is never still. They have wings like stained glass, panels of chitinous membrane stretched between rigid struts, and when the dim light catches them they throw faint patterns on the walls below. They navigate by sound, mostly, clicking and chirping in frequencies that layer over the city's drone, adding complexity, adding information. You realize, after a while, that the sound is not just resonance. It is *language*. The city is talking to itself.

---

The light comes from the walls.

Not from windows — there are no windows, no openings to the surface, no sky. The light is bioluminescent, produced by organisms embedded in the living architecture: thin veins of phosphorescent tissue that run through every surface like circuitry, glowing in pale blues and greens and, in some of the older structures, a deep amber that suggests age, that suggests the light itself is tired. The illumination is dim — you can see perhaps thirty meters in any direction, and beyond that the halls dissolve into a warm darkness that is not threatening, just *deep*. The city extends further than the light reaches. Much further.

In the dimness, the luminescent veins create a kind of map. Brighter lines mark the main thoroughfares, the paths most traveled, where the biological activity is highest and the light-producing organisms are most nourished. Dimmer lines branch off into side chambers, into quiet spaces, into the residential warrens where the smaller creatures nest in alcoves grown specifically for that purpose. The city lights itself where it needs to be lit, and leaves itself dark where it doesn't.

---

The vegetation — and here is where the parameters begin to resolve into something coherent, something that makes sense of the contradiction between *no water* and *lush growth* — is not plant life as you know it. There are no leaves, no roots in soil, no photosynthesis. Instead, the city itself is the vegetation. The living architecture is a single organism, or perhaps a colony of organisms so deeply integrated that the distinction has ceased to matter. It grows. It metabolizes. It draws its energy not from sunlight but from the slow geothermal heat of the deep rock, channeled up through root-like structures that descend kilometers below the floor, into the warm places where the planet's interior still moves.

The "lushness" is in the density of biological activity. Every surface is alive. The walls are furred with fine cilia that wave constantly, filtering the air, exchanging gases, maintaining the atmosphere. Sheets of fungal-like growth hang from the ceilings in curtains, processing minerals, breaking down waste, recycling everything. In the larger chambers, great gardens of filamentous organisms spread across the floor in mats of deep green and rust-red, feeding on the detritus of the city, and in turn being fed upon by the grazing arthropods, and the grazers being fed upon by the predators, and the predators' waste being broken down by the fungal mats, and the cycle closing, and the city breathing, and the sound continuing.

It is lush the way a coral reef is lush — not with plants, but with *life*, dense and interdependent and self-sustaining, a closed loop that has been running for longer than you can easily comprehend.

---

The erosion parameter — 2.7, ancient — is visible everywhere, once you know to look for it. The city is old. Not old in the way that ruins are old, crumbling and broken, but old in the way that a mountain is old: settled, patient, wearing its age as a kind of smoothness. The columns have been growing for so long that their surfaces have been polished by the constant passage of bodies, by the slow rain of organic particles, by the patient work of the cilia that cover everything. The original shapes — whatever forms the organisms first grew into — have been softened, rounded, worn into organic curves that no designer would have planned but that have the inevitability of water-worn stone.

There are places where the city has grown so old that it has begun to *settle*. Not collapse — the living material is self-repairing, within limits — but settle into a final form, a stable configuration that it will hold for millennia. In these places, the halls are lower, the columns thicker, the light dimmer. The sound is deeper here, slower, a bass note that you feel more than hear. Fewer creatures move through these spaces. They are the city's old quarters, its quiet places, and they have the feel of a library, or a cathedral nave, or a forest floor in late autumn — a stillness that is not empty but *full*, full of time, full of the accumulated weight of everything that has happened here and left its trace in the living walls.

---

The scale is open. You walk for what feels like hours and the city does not end. It changes — the halls widen into chambers the size of cathedrals, then narrow into corridors where you must turn sideways to pass, then open again into spaces so large that the ceiling is lost in darkness and the far walls are only suggestions, faint lines of luminescence in the gloom. Bridges span gaps in the floor where the living stone has grown away from itself, creating chasms that drop into deeper darkness, and you can hear, from far below, the sound of water — no, not water, something else, some dense fluid that moves slowly through the city's lowest levels, carrying nutrients, carrying waste, carrying the chemical messages that coordinate the organism's growth across its entire vast body.

There are districts. You begin to recognize them by their sound, by their light, by the density and type of inhabitants. The market-districts, where the air is thick with pheromones and the clicking language reaches a frenzy of exchange. The nursery-districts, where the light is warmer and the walls are softer, grown into cushioned surfaces, and the small creatures cluster in their thousands, tended by adults who move among them with a patience that looks almost like love. The quiet districts, the old districts, the districts where the light has gone almost entirely and the only sound is the deep bass drone of the city's oldest structures, still alive, still growing, still humming their single sustained note.

---

The tech level is zero. There are no tools, no devices, no fire, no metal. Everything here is biological. The creatures that inhabit the city are not its builders — they are its *symbionts*, its inhabitants, as dependent on the city as the city is on them. They feed it with their waste, aerate it with their movement, prune it with their grazing, and in return it shelters them, feeds them, lights their way, and speaks to them in the continuous drone that is the sound of its living body. There is intelligence here — the clicking language is complex, the social structures are intricate, the city itself responds to stimuli in ways that suggest something like cognition — but it is all biological intelligence, evolved, not invented. No one made anything. Everything grew.

---

The temperature is cool. Not cold — the geothermal heat keeps the deep city well above freezing — but cool in the way that a cellar is cool, a steady 12 or 13 degrees, unchanging, the temperature of deep rock and slow metabolism. The air is still. There is no wind this far below. The atmosphere is maintained by the cilia on the walls, by the fungal curtains, by the slow gas exchange of the living architecture, and it is thick, humid, rich with organic compounds that smell like nothing you have ever encountered — not unpleasant, not pleasant, just *other*, the smell of a biology that has never touched the surface, that has been evolving in isolation since before your species existed.

---

The dynamics are slow. Nothing here happens quickly. The creatures move at a walking pace, or slower. The city grows at a rate measurable in millimeters per year. The light pulses — you notice this after a long while — in cycles that last hours, a slow brightening and dimming that corresponds to some metabolic rhythm of the organism, a breathing-in and breathing-out of luminescence that is too gradual to perceive in real-time but that, over the course of your visit, you begin to feel as a kind of tide, a rise and fall of visibility that shapes the city's days.

There are no seasons. There is no weather. The surface world — with its sun and rain and wind and the violent churn of weather and erosion — might as well be on another planet. Down here, time moves differently. The city has been growing for millions of years. It will grow for millions more. The creatures that live in it are born, live, die, and are recycled into its walls, and their descendants are born, and the cycle continues, and the sound continues, and the light pulses, and the city breathes.

---

You stand in a large chamber — one of the great halls, where the columns are spaced far apart and the ceiling is a vault of living stone traced with veins of pale green light — and you listen. The sound is overwhelming. It is the sound of a world that has found its final form, that has been eroding toward stability for so long that it has arrived, that has become what it will always be. It is the sound of ten thousand species singing in concert, not because they chose to, but because they are part of the same organism, the same system, the same slow and patient and ancient and *alive* thing.

The city hums.

The city breathes.

The city is.
