# The Gullet of Hask

---

You are standing in the throat of something.

Not a creature — or at least, not one that has a name you'd recognize as naming a thing. The walls curve inward on either side like ridged flesh, pale and striated with mineral bands in ochre, ash-gray, and a deep plum that bruises toward black. They rise perhaps forty meters, which is to say: cathedral scale. The space between them narrows and widens as you move forward, and you understand with the part of your brain that maps spaces that this is a passage, a single feature, bounded. The walls will not go on forever. They close behind you too — you've already noticed that. The way in is not the way you came.

The light is wrong. Dim, but sourceless. It comes from the stone itself, a faint phosphorescence in the plum-colored veins, as though the walls are infected with slow light. It never flickers. It never brightens. You could read by it, if you had something to read, but your eyes never quite adjust. Everything stays at the threshold of legibility, and you find yourself leaning forward to see your own hands.

There is no water here. None. The air is dry as calcined bone, and your lips have already begun to crack — you can feel the tight skin pulling when you open your mouth. But the air is warm. Not hot. Warm the way a body is warm. The walls, when you touch them (and you will; everyone does), radiate a steady low heat, maybe a degree above your own skin temperature. It's not comfortable exactly. It's *insistent*. The warmth presses against you from both sides, and you become aware of your own coolness, your own foreignness, in this place.

And yet: vegetation.

It shouldn't grow here. There is no water, no soil that you can see, no light worth photosynthesizing by. But it does grow, and it grows *lush*. Thick ropes of something like fern frond but waxy, rubbery, hang from the upper reaches of the walls in curtains of deep burgundy and copper-green. They don't reach for light. They reach for *each other*, intertwining overhead in a canopy so dense that it breaks the sourceless glow into a mottled ceiling, like being underwater in green glass. Below the fern-canopy, lower growth: carpets of low, broad-leafed things that pulse. Actually pulse. A slow, rhythmic swelling and contraction, like breathing, like the peristaltic movement of something swallowing. They're warm to the touch. Some of them are wet — not with water. With a clear, viscous fluid that smells faintly of copper and crushed tarragon. It evaporates almost as fast as it beads on the surface.

Between the growth, the floor. A long-existed floor, weathered smooth, worn by something's passage over and over until the stone has taken on the patina of old wood. You can see the erosion in the walls too — the mineral bands have been worn into soft undulations, as though the rock were once fluid and froze mid-swell. The whole passage has the settled quality of a place that has been itself for a very long time. It knows what it is. It is not becoming anything else.

Sparse, though. Despite the lush growth, the space between living things is wide. The fern curtains hang in distinct columns, separated by gaps of bare, glowing stone. The ground-cover clusters in irregular patches, leaving stretches of that polished floor open and empty. There is room here. Breathing room, walking room. The density of presence is balanced against the density of absence, and you find that the empty spaces are not less alive than the full ones. They hum.

The sound. God, the sound.

This place is *loud*. Not in volume exactly — though there is volume — but in saturation. Every frequency is occupied. The lowest register is a subsonic throb you feel in your sternum, the pulse of the walls themselves. Above that, a constant susurration from the fern curtains as they sway in currents of warm air you can barely feel. A clicking, high and irregular, from somewhere ahead — not mechanical, not exactly biological, something between the two. And threaded through everything, a kind of *breathing*. Long, slow inhales that seem to come from the passage ahead of you, and long slow exhales from behind. The Gullet breathes. In and out. You are inside something that is breathing.

The fauna.

You see them first as movement in the peripheral vision. Quick, low shapes that vanish between the ground-cover patches. They're the size of large rats, maybe, but wrong in their proportions — too many joints in their legs, heads that seem to rotate independently of their bodies. They have pelts that match the mineral bands in the walls: ochre, ash-gray, plum. They are watching you. There are dozens of them, maybe hundreds, distributed sparsely through the passage the way the vegetation is — clusters of activity separated by stretches of still, watching nothing. They make the clicking sound. Not with their mouths. With their joints. Each movement produces a sharp, ceramic click, and because there are so many of them moving at once, the sound becomes a constant rain of tiny percussions, like a room full of people typing.

One approaches you. It has six legs, a blunt head, and eyes — far too many eyes — arranged in a spiral around what you think of as its face. It stops at arm's length and performs a gesture that you will later think of as *reading*: its head tilts, its eyes dilate in sequence, and it makes a sound like a wet finger on a crystal rim. Then it turns and walks away, clicking, and you feel *seen* in a way that has nothing to do with sight.

The built things.

Scattered. Sparse. They are here, but you almost miss them because they are made of the same materials as the walls. Low structures — knee-high, waist-high — roughly rectangular, made of stacked and fused stone that doesn't quite match the passage walls. They have openings. Doorways. Some are empty. Some contain objects: a bowl-shaped depression filled with that clear viscous fluid. A arrangement of small, smooth stones in a spiral pattern. A piece of worked metal — copper, maybe, or bronze — bent into a shape that could be a tool or could be art or could be a prayer.

Someone was here. Someone *made* things here, with hands, with simple tools. The structures are old but not ancient — they haven't weathered the way the walls have. They sit in the passage like someone set them down yesterday and simply never came back. Or like they were set down and then the passage *grew around them*, the way a tree grows around a fence post.

And now the turbulence.

Because this place is not stable. It is not calm. It is in constant, chaotic change, and you are only now beginning to notice it because you've been here long enough for the pattern to emerge. The fern curtains rearrange themselves. Not slowly — *suddenly*. One moment they hang in one configuration, and the next (no transition, no wind, no warning) they have shifted, interwoven differently, opened a new gap, closed an old one. The ground-cover patches migrate. They flow across the floor like slow liquid, and where they pass, the polished stone briefly shows fresh erosion marks — new grooves, new patterns — before the next patch flows over them and the marks are hidden.

The walls are growing. Not quickly. But you can see it if you watch a single point: a new mineral band forming, a new ridge pushing outward by millimeters. The passage is *building itself*. And simultaneously, in other places, it is *unbuilding* — sections of wall crumbling to fine powder that the warm air currents carry away, the passage widening, opening, becoming something other than what it was.

The fauna participates. The creatures move in patterns that are not random but not predictable — they swarm and disperse, swarm and disperse, and where they cluster, the vegetation thickens. Where they leave, it thins. They are gardening. They are *editing*. They click their approval or disapproval, and the passage responds.

You realize, standing here in the warm dim breathing loudness of this place, that you are not in a landscape. You are in a *process*. The Gullet of Hask is not a place that exists. It is a place that is *happening*. It has been happening for a long time — the erosion, the settled forms, the weathered stone all testify to that — but it is not finished. It will never be finished. It is a bazaar of geological and biological and architectural and faunal activity, all negotiating, all building and unbuilding, all breathing in and out.

And you are in it now. Part of the negotiation. The creatures are watching you with their spiral eyes, clicking their ceramic judgment, and the walls are warm against your back, and the fern curtains are rearranging themselves around you, and the passage is breathing, and you understand that you will not leave the way you came.

The way in is not the way you came.

The way forward is the way forward.
