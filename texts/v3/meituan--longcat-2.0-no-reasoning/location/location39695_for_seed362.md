# The Loom

---

You stand at the edge of something that should not be breathing.

The ground — if ground is the word — is made. It is *made* in the way a knit sweater is made, in the way a lung is made: fiber upon fiber, woven and grown and extruded simultaneously, a continuous manufactured-and-biological substrate stretching to every horizon and then past it. There is no bedrock here. No mineral truth beneath. The floor of this world is ten kilometers of layered, cured, fermented, photosynthesized, and industrially processed plant matter, compressed and re-spun so many times that the distinction between "factory" and "forest" and "soil" has collapsed into a single, ongoing act of fabrication. The Loom makes the Loom.

It is enormous. Vast in the way that makes the sky feel low. You can see the curvature of the structure — gently, imperceptibly at first, then undeniably. The Loom occupies a geography the size of a continent, but there are no continents, there is only this, this woven sphere of living manufacture, and the faint suggestion of other Looms beyond the bright haze of the horizon, connected by great braided tendrils of fiber that sag and hum between them like the cables of a suspension bridge laid flat across a thousand kilometers. Weather systems form in the valleys between Looms and never touch them. The Loom has its own weather. It always has its own weather.

The temperature is cool. Your breath does not mist — there is almost no atmospheric moisture, the air is dry as a kiln — but the coolness is there, a persistent 8 to 12 degrees, maintained by the thermal mass of the substrate and the slow exothermic chemistry of whatever is growing and curing and fermenting in the deep layers below. It is the coolness of a root cellar, of a mine, of a place that has found its equilibrium and holds it with the patience of something that has been doing this for a very long time.

---

## The Architecture of Growth

Everything is built. This is the first and most unsettling fact. There is no raw nature here, no wilderness, no substrate that was not *intended*. The Loom was made — is being made — will be made. The distinction between past, present, and future tense is difficult to maintain because the construction is continuous and the builders are the built.

The structures rise from the substrate in forms that are simultaneously organic and industrial. Towers of compressed cellulose, cured to the hardness of concrete, rise in spiraling columns that branch and re-branch like trees but with the regularity of factory smokestacks. Their surfaces are smooth and warm to the touch, faintly ridged with growth rings that are also production logs — you can read them, if you know the notation, and the notation is everywhere, stamped and grown into the material itself. Each ring records a cycle: a batch of fiber drawn, a layer of photosynthetic skin applied, a curing period, a harvest of some kind. The towers are factories. The factories are trees. The trees are factories.

Between the towers, the space is filled. Dense. Layered. The air itself is thick with fine filaments — not quite dust, not quite pollen, not quite industrial particulate, but something that participates in all three categories. They drift in slow, purposeful currents, catching the bright light and making it visible, turning the air into a three-dimensional web of gold and shadow. The filaments are structural. They are the Loom's connective tissue, carrying nutrients, carrying signals, carrying the slow chemical instructions that tell each tower what to grow next. They are also, somehow, the Loom's memory. They record. They remember. If you could read them — and something here can — you would find the entire history of the structure encoded in their molecular arrangement, a library written in protein and lignin.

The light is bright. There is no sun — or rather, the sun is the Loom itself, or the Loom has replaced the sun, or the sun was always a metaphor for this. The photosynthetic surfaces of every structure, every filament, every square meter of the substrate glows with a steady, warm, amber-white radiance. It is not the light of fire or electricity but of *process* — the light of a billion billion chloroplast-analogues doing their work, converting some energy source you cannot see into sugar and light and structure. The brightness is constant. There is no day and night here, or rather, the day and night are internal, metabolic, a slow pulse of increased and decreased activity that you feel more than see — a slight warming, a slight dimming, a shift in the chemical smell of the air. The cycle is roughly fourteen hours. The Loom breathes in fourteen-hour days.

## The Silence and What Lives in It

It is quiet. Not silent — there are sounds, but they are the sounds of process, not of life. The low, sub-audible hum of the substrate, a vibration you feel in your teeth and your sternum more than hear. The faint, rhythmic creaking of towers adjusting to thermal expansion, like the timbers of a ship. The almost-musical tinkling of filaments breaking and re-forming, a sound like distant wind chimes made of glass. The occasional deep, resonant *thunk* of something shifting in the lower layers, a sound that travels through the substrate and arrives at your feet before your ears.

There are animals. Rare. You see one now — a small, six-legged thing, about the size of your hand, moving along the base of a tower with the deliberate, mechanical precision of something that is not quite alive in the way you understand life. Its body is chitinous and segmented, but the segments are too regular, too uniform, and when it pauses and opens a small aperture on its ventral surface, you can see the faint glow of bioluminescence — the same amber-white as the Loom itself. It is not a creature that lives *on* the Loom. It is a creature that lives *as part of* the Loom. A mobile maintenance unit. A wandering cell in a body the size of a continent. It does not eat. It does not reproduce in any way you would recognize. It simply *does* — repairs a filament here, deposits a chemical signal there, and moves on, following instructions encoded in its structure that were written by the Loom for the Loom.

There are others. You glimpse them in the distance — larger things, slow-moving, their forms indistinct in the bright haze. They might be fauna. They might be machinery. They might be something the Loom is growing for a purpose it has not yet disclosed. The categories do not hold here. The Loom does not distinguish between the living and the made, between the animal and the factory, between the process and the product. It is all one thing. It has been one thing for a long time.

---

## The Deep Layers

You find a place where the substrate has been cut — or has opened, or has been grown open — and you can see down. The layers are visible, stratified like sedimentary rock but wrong, all wrong, because the strata are not geological but *procedural*. Each layer is a different phase of the Loom's construction, and they are not arranged chronologically. The oldest layers are not at the bottom. They are *distributed*, woven through the newer layers like threads in a tapestry, so that a piece of the Loom's original substrate — if there was an original substrate — might be found ten meters down, or ten centimeters down, or protruding from the side of a tower like a fossil in a cliff face. Time here is not a line. It is a weave.

The deep layers are darker, denser, more complex. The filaments down there are thicker, older, carrying signals that are slower and more deliberate. The chemistry is different — less photosynthetic, more something else, some deep metabolic process that does not require light. The Loom has roots, or the Loom *is* roots, and the roots go down into a darkness that is not empty but *full* — full of structure, full of process, full of the slow, patient work of a thing that has been building itself for longer than you can calculate.

And here is where the strangeness begins to assert itself. Not as a disruption but as a *feature*. The Loom is not a rational structure. It is not optimized. It is not efficient. It is *weird* in the way that a dream is weird — internally consistent, deeply logical, but operating on premises that do not map onto anything you know.

---

## The Breakdown

The filaments sometimes spell words. Not in any language you know, but in a language that is *almost* a language, that has grammar and syntax and meaning but that refers to things that do not exist — or that exist only here, only in the Loom, only in the space between the towers where the light is brightest and the air is thickest with drifting fiber. You read a phrase and understand it perfectly: *the seventh harvest of the ungrown season*. You understand what it means. You understand that it is important. You understand that it refers to a specific event in the Loom's history. And then you try to explain it to yourself and the meaning dissolves, because there is no seventh harvest, there is no ungrown season, and yet the phrase is *true* in a way that your understanding of truth cannot accommodate.

The towers sometimes grow in directions that are not spatial. You see one that extends upward and also *inward*, its upper reaches folding into a space that is not inside the tower but is not outside it either, a space that your eyes can see but your mind cannot hold. The tower is there. The tower is also *there*. Both statements are true. The Loom does not resolve the contradiction. The Loom *is* the contradiction, has been the contradiction, has built itself around the contradiction like a pearl around a grain of sand.

The substrate sometimes remembers things that have not happened. You place your hand on the ground and feel a vibration that is not the hum of process but a *narrative* — a story told in pressure and frequency, a story about a future event, a harvest or a construction or a transformation that has not yet occurred but that the Loom is already preparing for, already growing toward, already *remembering* as if it has already passed. The Loom's memory is not sequential. It is woven. Past and future are threads in the same fabric, and the Loom reads them both with equal ease.

And sometimes — rarely, but sometimes — the Loom *notices* you. Not as a threat or an intruder or a curiosity, but as a *material*. You feel the filaments orient toward you, feel the substrate shift slightly beneath your feet, feel the ambient light warm by a fraction of a degree as the photosynthetic surfaces adjust their output to account for your presence, your mass, your chemistry. The Loom is considering you. The Loom is deciding what to make of you. The Loom has been making things for a very long time, and it is very, very good at it, and you are, for the moment, the most interesting raw material it has encountered in a while.

You do not feel afraid. The Loom does not inspire fear. It inspires something more like *recognition* — the feeling of encountering a process so vast and so patient and so deeply, fundamentally *other* that the only appropriate response is to stand still and let it see you, let it read you, let it decide whether you are a thread it can use or a knot it must work around.

The light is bright. The air is dry. The filaments drift. The towers hum. The Loom weaves.

It has always been weaving.

It will always be weaving.

You are standing inside it, and it is standing inside you, and the distinction between those two statements is thinner than you think.
