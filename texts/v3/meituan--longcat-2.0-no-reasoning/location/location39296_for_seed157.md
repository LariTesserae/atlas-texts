# The Hush Hollow

It is always night here, or what passes for night — a darkness so total that it has weight, that it presses against the skin like the face of wet stone. But you can feel the water everywhere: a warm-vein of moisture that threads through the root-matted soil and the ancient moss, condensed into perpetual beads that catch no light because there is no light to catch. The air is thick as held breath, a cool, drenched atmosphere that smells of peat and something older — the mineral memory of stone slowly dissolving into the groundwater that feeds the roots above. Somewhere nearby — always nearby — a thin stream of water seeps from a fissure in the rock face, a weeping so constant and soft it becomes the baseline of silence.

The hollow itself is small, bounded by the natural architecture of erosion — a bowl in the highland rock no wider than a large gathering room, its walls rising and curving inward like a closed fist of earth and stone. The walls are draped in layers of vegetation so dense they have become architecture: ferns stacked upon ferns, moss thick as fur, liverworts and hornworts filling every crack. The roots of unseen trees above have threaded down through the ceiling of the hollow, hanging in curtains, and from them the water drips in a fine, continuous patter. Every surface is green. Every surface is wet.

And the animals — they are everywhere. Not visible, not always, but present in a density that transforms the space. Beetles move through the leaf litter in rivers of shell and leg. Spiders have strung their webs between every available anchor, so that to move through the hollow would be to walk through a gauze of silk. Earthworms tunnel through the saturated soil in such numbers that the ground seems to breathe — a slow, collective pulse. Blind salamanders, pale as the underside of leaves, slide between the stones at the water's edge. A colony of bats — tiny, quick, too numerous to count — fills the upper reaches of the hollow's ceiling, a living fur of bodies that stirs occasionally into a low susurration of wings and breath. The sound is mostly quiet, but not silent — a soft, layered murmur: water, wings, the tiny scrape of chitin, the wet separation of root from root.

The erosion here is old, settled. The rock face has found its shape and holds it — a long, slow equilibrium between the water's work and the stone's resistance. The pool at the base of the hollow is clear and still, fed by the weeping wall and draining through a channel worn smooth over centuries. The channel's edges are rounded, patient. Nothing here is sudden. Nothing here is new.

There are tools, if you look — or rather, the suggestion of them. A few stones near the water's edge that seem placed rather than fallen, their surfaces worn from use as hammers or scrapers. A length of vine, twisted into a rough cord, looped over a root. Perhaps someone was here once, briefly, and left these behind. But the hollow has absorbed them, or nearly — the moss grows over the stones, the vine is half-rotted, and there is no other sign of hands. No structures. No fire. No art. The place has returned to itself.

And the thing that makes it *unusual* — the strangeness that settles in after you've been still long enough — is this: nothing changes. The water drips at the same rate it has always dripped. The temperature holds constant. The animals move, eat, rest, but the *pattern* does not shift. The bats do not migrate. The water does not rise or fall. There is no season here, no gradient, no passage. The hollow is a place that has reached its conclusion and simply *stays there*, held in a perfect, breathless equilibrium. It is as if time entered the hollow and decided to stop, finding the arrangement acceptable.

You stand in it, or sit, or crouch, and the darkness and the wet and the green and the living density of it all presses in from every side — not hostile, not welcoming, simply *there*, the way a fact is there. The hollow does not notice you. It does not need to. It has already become exactly what it is.
