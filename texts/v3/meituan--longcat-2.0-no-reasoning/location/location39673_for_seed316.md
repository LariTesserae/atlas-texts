# The Dissolution Fields

---

You stand at the edge of something that has forgotten it was ever solid.

The ground beneath your feet is a memory of ground. It holds weight — your weight, right now — but only through a kind of exhaustion, the way a very old bridge remains standing because it has simply stopped trying to fall. Beneath the thin skin of what you'd call soil, there is a wetness so pervasive it doesn't behave like water anymore. It behaves like atmosphere that became confused about which direction it was supposed to move. The earth weeps continuously, not from any specific point but from *everywhere*, the way a body sweats when it is dying. Shallow pools have no edges. Puddles merge into puddles. The concept of "dry" has become theoretical, a word that might once have meant something in some other geological era.

The horizon is wrong.

Not distorted in any way you can immediately name — there is no shimmer, no visible bending, no lensing of light through heat. The wrongness is more fundamental. The horizon is *too many*. It layers. You can see a far line where land meets sky, and behind it another line, and behind that another, each one slightly more saturated, slightly more *real*, as though the distance is not a distance but a stack of nearly-identical worlds pressed together like pages of a book fanned open. You are not looking out. You are looking *through*. And somewhere in the middle distances, maybe three or four of the horizon-lines in, something is breathing. The whole landscape inhales and exhales on a cycle of about eleven seconds. When it breathes in, the pools on the ground pull tighter against themselves, surface tension becoming visible, and the air thickens to the consistency of warm syrup. When it breathes out, everything loosens, and for a half-second the ground feels like it might simply let go of itself and dissolve into the water beneath it, and you feel yourself almost falling — not downward but *inward*, toward some interior the world has that you don't.

The sound.

The sound is not loud in the way a storm is loud, or a waterfall, or a city. It is loud in the way that *total presence* is loud. Imagine every molecule of air in your vicinity is speaking simultaneously, each one at a volume just below what you could hear individually, and their combined voice is a roar that has texture — not a hiss, not a rumble, but something like a choir performing in a language made entirely of consonants. The water contributes its own voice, a thick gurgling that rises and falls with the breathing cycle, and occasionally the ground contributes a sound that is almost geological — a deep, slow crack that you feel in your teeth before you hear it with your ears — as though some enormous structure far below is adjusting its weight. The fauna, what little there is, does not add to the sound so much as *subtract* from it. You notice them by the way the roar dips for a half-second in one particular direction, a momentary silence-shaped-hole in the noise, and you turn and see something that might be a bird but is more accurately described as a *decision* the air made to briefly hold a shape. It is pale, and it moves without disturbing the air around it, and when you look directly at it you cannot tell if it has wings or if the air near it is simply moving in a way that resembles wings, and then the sound fills back in where it was and it is gone.

The vegetation is not so much sparse as *tentative*. Things grow here the way thoughts grow in a half-sleep — partially, without commitment. There are stalks, or stalk-like structures, emerging from the wet ground at irregular intervals. They are the color of bruised celery. They are hollow. When the landscape breathes out, they exhale from their tops a fine mist that smells like copper and something older than copper, something that might be the smell of iron before it became a concept. They have no leaves. They may never have had leaves. Or the leaves may have been absorbed back into the stalks in some reversal of growth that this place performs casually, the way other landscapes shed bark. Around their bases, the water is slightly darker, tinged a deep amber, and if you were to kneel and look closely you would see that the water there is *moving against the breathing*, flowing inward toward the stalks even as the ground exhales outward, a small rebellion against the physics of this world that no one has corrected because no one is in charge of correcting it.

The structures — the few that exist — are not ruins, exactly. They are *interruptions*. A wall, standing to about waist height, made of a material that might be stone but feels, when you touch it, like dense rubber that has calcified. It is warm. It is slightly wet. It follows a curve that suggests it was once part of a circular enclosure, but the rest of the curve does not exist — not because it was destroyed but because it was *never completed*, or because completion is a state this place does not permit. Near the wall, half-sunk in the wet ground, there is what appears to be a tool — a long shaft of some dark material, smooth and slightly curved, tapered at one end. It is not metal. It is not wood. It has the grain of bone but the weight of stone. It may have been held once. It may have been used to dig, or to mark, or to point. Now it is simply *present*, like everything else here — not abandoned, not preserved, just occupying the space it occupies, which is all this world seems to ask of anything.

The light is dim, but there is no visible source of darkness. The sky — if it is a sky — is a uniform gray that is not the gray of cloud cover but the gray of *distance made visible*, as though the air itself becomes opaque simply by being far away and there is so much far-away air that the near part of it has begun to give up. The light comes from everywhere and nowhere. It does not cast shadows. Or rather, it casts shadows that are slightly *offset* from the objects that produce them — a stalk casts a shadow that falls two degrees to the left of where geometry says it should, and the wall's shadow appears to pool *upward* onto a section of empty air above it, a darkening of nothing. This is not unsettling in the way you'd expect. It is unsettling in a deeper way, the way a wrong note in a familiar song is unsettling — a wrongness that implies the whole thing was wrong and you only just noticed.

The air is warm and heavy and tastes like the inside of a closed room that has been closed for a very long time. Breathing it is not unpleasant. It has a sweetness to it, faintly vegetal, but underneath the sweetness there is a mineral quality, a taste like licking the underside of a stone in a cave, cool and ancient and tasting of nothing that has ever been alive. The humidity is near-total. Your skin does not dry. Your clothes do not dry. Everything you carry becomes, over the course of minutes, saturated, and the saturation does not feel like dampness so much as *participation* — your things are becoming part of the wetness here, the way a drop of water becomes part of the ocean not by entering it but by being entered *by* it.

The erosion. The erosion is what holds all of this together, or rather what holds it in its current state of almost-coming-apart. This landscape has been wearing down for longer than landscapes normally get to wear down. It has passed through youth and maturity and the equivalent of old age and has arrived at something past old age, a state that has no name — a geological geriatric state where the land has been smoothed so thoroughly that it has almost become liquid, where every peak that ever existed here has been not just worn away but *forgotten*, where the original tectonic violence that built whatever this was has been sanded down to a whisper, a faint memory in the grain of the wet ground. And yet it has not flattened entirely. There are low rises, barely perceptible, gentle undulations in the terrain that move like the surface of a slow liquid, and they are not static — they shift, almost imperceptibly, over the course of hours, migrating across the landscape like sleeping animals turning in their sleep. The whole vast plain is *slowly rearranging itself*, a tectonic restlessness operating at the speed of continental drift, and you are standing on it, and it is moving under you at a rate too slow to feel but too fast to be truly eternal, and this contradiction — the ancient and the ongoing, the final form that is still changing — is the central fact of this place.

The density. Everything here is *close*. The wetness is close — to the air, to the ground, to you. The vegetation is close together in its sparseness, clustered in ways that suggest conspiracy. The horizons are close behind each other. The sound is close to your ears, inside your ears, in the spaces between your eardrums and your brain. Even the light feels close, pressed against your skin like a membrane. There is no emptiness here, no void, no clean space. Every cubic meter of this world is *occupied* — by water, by sound, by the thick warm air, by the faint mineral taste, by the slow movement of the ground, by the presence of the thing breathing in the middle distance. You cannot stand here without standing *in* everything. There is no room that is not also a room in something else.

And it is vast. The Dissolution Fields extend in every direction until the horizons begin to fold over each other and distance becomes meaningless, and even then they continue, layer upon layer, a plain so large it has developed its own weather — which is to say, the breathing is the weather, the eleven-second cycle is the day-night cycle, and the slow migration of the low rises is the seasonal shift. It is a world wearing the mask of a landscape. Or it is a landscape that grew so large it became a world and now doesn't know what it is. Or it is a world that is slowly, patiently, with the calm of something that has nothing but time, dissolving itself back into the water from which it came, and the dissolution has been going on for so long that it has become the new normal, the way things are, the settled state.

You are standing in it. It is warm. It is loud. It is wet. The ground shifts beneath you, almost imperceptibly, and the horizon breathes in, and the air thickens, and somewhere a pale shape that is not quite a bird moves through the sound without disturbing it, and the dark shaft half-buried near the wall catches the dim light in a way that suggests it is wet even though everything here is wet and so calling it wet is redundant.

Stay as long as you like. The place will not notice you, but it will absorb you, the way it absorbs everything. Slowly. Patiently. With the calm of something that has already won.
