# The Calcination Rooms

---

You are standing inside something that has no outside.

The space is roughly the volume of a large chapel, though "chapel" implies a congregation, and what gathers here is not congregating — it is *occluding*. The walls, floor, and ceiling are all the same substance: a pale, crystalline calciumite, leached and re-leached over epochs until the surface has the texture of old bone that has been handled by many hands. It is cool here. Not cold — your breath does not mist — but cool the way a cellar is cool, the way deep limestone holds a temperature that predates the concept of weather. The air tastes of mineral dryness so absolute that the inside of your mouth feels lightly abraded, as though you've been breathing powdered chalk.

But you are not breathing chalk. You are breathing *them*.

They are the Griddle.

No — that's not right. They don't have a name. Names require language, and language requires the patience to listen for a response, and the Griddle do not respond. They *overlap*. They exist in a state of permanent mutual crowding, a fauna so dense that the concept of individual organisms becomes a polite fiction you maintain only because counting them would take longer than your life.

They are small. Most of them are the size of a lentil, some the size of a fingernail, a few — rare, ancient, almost stationary — the size of a fist. Their bodies are compressed, flattened things, like dried leaves that have been pressed between the pages of a book for so long that the book has begun to press back. They move across every surface in sheets. The floor beneath your feet is not calciumite — it is calciumite *under* six layers of Griddle, each layer moving in a slightly different direction, so that standing still means slowly being rubbed in six different directions at once. They make a sound. Oh, they make a sound.

It is not the sound of insects, though people who have heard it and then tried to describe it later reach for that comparison first. Insect sound is rhythmic — chitin on chitin, wing-beat, the click-and-pause of biological timing. The Griddle sound is *continuous*. It is the sound of every surface being touched by every other surface simultaneously. Imagine holding a handful of dry lentils and letting them pour over each other in an unending cascade — not fast, not slow, just *perpetual*. A low, dense, granular hiss that fills the room the way water fills a bowl, that enters your ears and doesn't leave, that you stop noticing only because your brain reclassifies it as silence after about nine minutes. But it is never silent. It is the loudest quiet you will ever hear.

The light is normal. That is perhaps the strangest thing. The light is normal — the flat, directionless illumination of an overcast afternoon, as though the room exists beneath a sky that is always at exactly 4:00 PM in a temperate October. There are no visible light sources. The calciumite walls emit nothing. The Griddle themselves are a dull, translucent grey-green, the color of lichen that has forgotten it was ever alive. But the light is *here*, ambient and even, casting no shadows because there is nothing tall enough to cast one. You have not yet looked up. When you do, you will see the ceiling is also covered — also moving — and the light comes from everywhere and nowhere, as though the air itself has a very slight luminous charge, a bioluminescence of nothing.

This place is old. Not in the way that mountains are old, which is to say *younger than they look* — old in the way that a footprint in clay is old, meaning it was made once and then left alone for so long that the clay forgot it was ever soft. The erosion here is not erosion in the geological sense. Nothing is being worn down because nothing is being worn *against*. The calciumite walls are in their final form. They have been smoothed past the point of change. Run your hand along a wall — careful, the Griddle will part around your fingers and then flow back, curious, tasting your skin with whatever sense they use in place of taste — and the surface is so polished by time that it has a faint *give*, almost organic, like pressing your thumb into a well-rested loaf of bread. The room has settled into what it is. It will not become anything else.

And yet it is active. Deeply, overwhelmingly active. The Griddle are not static. They are engaged in something — a process, a behavior, a cycle so large and so slow that you cannot perceive its shape from inside it. Watch one patch of floor for long enough — minutes, an hour — and you will see the density shift. Layers thin and thicken. The collective mass migrates in slow tidal patterns toward the walls, up the walls, across the ceiling, down again. Not all of them. Not all at once. But the room breathes. The fauna breathes. Something is being *maintained*.

There are structures here. Scattered. Sparse. They rise from the calciumite floor like the ruins of an intention — low, broad platforms of the same mineral substance, perhaps a meter across, worn to illegibility. On two of them, you can see the remains of fittings: corroded metal bolts, the ghosts of where something was attached. Industrial. Someone — something — built here, once. The bolts are iron, or were iron, and they have rusted to a dark red so deep it is almost black, and the Griddle crawl over them with what might be indifference or might be maintenance, the way coral tends its substrate. There are perhaps a dozen of these platforms in the room. None are near each other. They are placed with the irregular spacing of objects that were positioned for a purpose now forgotten, or perhaps with the deliberate randomness of a system that wanted no two points to be equidistant from a third.

There is no water. There has never been water here, or if there was, it was so long ago that the calciumite has consumed every memory of it, every hydrogen bond, every trace of dissolution. The room is anhydrous to its mineral bones. The Griddle do not drink. What they do — what sustains them in this closed, dry, ancient space — is not something you can see. They feed on the calciumite, perhaps. Or on each other. Or on the slow, ambient energy of the light that comes from nowhere. Or on something else entirely, something for which there is no analog in any biology you know. They are not animals. They are not fungi. They are not machines, though the old industrial fittings suggest someone once thought of them that way. They are a *fauna* — a living crowd, an overwhelming presence of small bodies doing something that looks like living, in a room that looks like it was built by hands that have been gone longer than the room has existed.

The density is the thing you cannot escape. Every cubic centimeter of this space contains life — not potential life, not dormant life, but *active, moving, touching, sounding* life. The air is not empty. The walls are not surfaces. The floor is not a floor. It is all substrate, all medium, all occupied. You are the only thing in this room that is not part of the Griddle's world, and they know it. They don't react to you the way animals react — no alarm, no curiosity, no aggression. They react the way a river reacts to a stone: they part, they flow, they fill in behind you. You are a disruption in their density, and they are correcting for you in real time, constantly, with the mindless persistence of something that has been doing this since before there was a word for *persistence*.

The room is low. You feel this in your body before your mind names it — the slight pressure of enclosure, the sense that the ceiling is closer than ceilings should be, that the walls are nearer than walls ought to be. It is not claustrophobic. It is *intimate*. The space has been compressed by its own age, or it was built small, or it exists inside something larger and you are only seeing a fraction of it — a pocket, a cell, a single chamber in a structure that extends in directions you cannot access. The Griddle don't seem to notice the boundaries. They reach the walls and climb them. They reach the ceiling and traverse it. They do not seek exits. There may not be exits. There may not be anything outside this room at all.

The sound rises. You stopped noticing it, and now you've started again, and it is louder than you remember — or you are more attuned, or the density has shifted, and there are more of them between you and the walls now, more bodies per cubic centimeter, more surfaces touching surfaces, more of that granular hiss filling the air like static, like the room is slowly tuning itself to a frequency just below hearing.

Something on one of the platforms is moving differently than the rest. A cluster of the larger Griddle — the fist-sized ones, the old ones — are arranged in a pattern. Not a shape you recognize, but a *pattern*, which is different. A shape implies design. A pattern implies repetition, and repetition implies process, and process implies that this room, this dry, ancient, overcrowded, lightless-lit, cool and low and mineral-soft place, is not a ruin.

It is running.
