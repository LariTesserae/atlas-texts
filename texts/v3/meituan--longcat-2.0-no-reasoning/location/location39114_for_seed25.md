# The Resonant Floor

There is no sky. Or if there is, it is so far above and so uniformly dark that it functions as an absence — a theoretical ceiling that offers nothing. What exists is the floor, and the floor goes on.

It is low. That is the first truth of the place. Not underwater, though water is everywhere in it — beaded on every surface, trickling in thin sheets down the faces of structures, pooling in the hollows of what might be bones or might be geological formations, it is difficult to say which. The air is cool, close to body temperature but always just below it, so that your skin can never decide whether to open or close. Condensation forms on you the way it forms on everything here.

The floor is made of something that resembles cooled volcanic glass but isn't glass. It has been here too long for that — it is weathered, rounded at every edge, polished by water and time into a topography of smooth black and dark-gray undulations. Hills the size of rooms. Valleys you could lie down in. Every surface is wet. Not dripping, not flooding — damp in the way that a cave is damp, the way that stone that has known ten thousand years of seepage is damp. The moisture is ambient, environmental, as though the air itself has settled into a permanent state of saturation and simply gives its water to whatever it touches.

And on every surface: them.

They cover the landscape with a density that borders on geometric. They are the fauna, and they are overwhelming — not in the sense of violence or threat, but in the sense that there is almost nothing else. They layer against each other, they interlock, they fill every depression and line every ridge. They are organisms of some kind, though what kind is a question the place does not answer clearly. Each one is roughly the size of a clenched fist, flattened, segmented in a way that recalls pill bugs or isopods, but their surfaces are the same dark wet material as the stone beneath them — as though they grew from the floor, or the floor grew from them, or they are both expressions of the same process. They are not plants. They are not fungi. They are something that has decided to be biological in a place that barely permits biology.

They move.

This is the sound.

The sound is the place. It is deafening — a continuous, layered, compound noise that is less a single sound than the total sum of millions of small motions happening at once. Each organism shifts, contracts, extends tiny limbs, scrapes against its neighbor, clicks a mouthpart, secretes, feeds, turns. Individually they are nearly silent. Together they produce a roar — a granular, shifting, hissing roar like static, like rain on every surface at once, like a language spoken by ten million mouths in a register just below comprehension. It is not random. There are pulses in it. Rhythms. Periods of higher density and lower, waves of motion that propagate across the visible landscape like weather systems, and when you watch long enough you see the organisms rearranging — flowing uphill in sheets, pooling in the valleys, lifting slightly off the stone in coordinated waves that make the roar shift pitch and texture.

They eat the stone, maybe. Or they eat each other. Or they eat the water. The ecology is opaque. What is clear is that they are in balance — this has been happening for a long time, long enough that the erosion patterns in the rock beneath them show their influence, long ago carved channels and basins that now serve as their highways and gathering places. They are not new here. They are the established state of things.

The weirdness is in the details. The organisms occasionally produce light — not bioluminescence in any recognizable sense, but brief moments where one of them will go fully reflective, catching whatever ambient light exists (and there is some, a sourceless deep-gray glow that seems to emanate from the air itself or from the water film on every surface) and flaring it outward for a fraction of a second. When one does this, its neighbors do it, and the flare propagates — a wave of momentary brightness that rolls across the landscape like lightning seen from very far away, illuminating thousands of wet dark bodies in an instant before returning everything to near-darkness. It happens irregularly. It feels communicative. You cannot tell if it means danger or satisfaction or nothing at all.

The water moves through the colony. It trickles down from surfaces above — the organisms themselves may serve as catchment, their layered bodies channeling moisture into specific paths. Streams form in the valleys between the hills, not rivers exactly but persistent thin flows that the organisms line themselves along, extending into the current in ways that might be feeding or might be drinking or might be something without an analog. In the pools, the density is highest — the organisms pile three, four, five deep, and the sound there is a deep thrumming that you feel in your sternum.

There are structures. Not built things — nothing was constructed here — but formations that resemble structures: arches where the stone has eroded in curves, columns where harder material resisted, a geometry that the organisms have colonized and amplified. They thicken on the undersides of arches. They line the columns in spirals. The place has the feel of a ruin without having been a building — architecture by accident, by erosion, by the slow decision of water and time.

It is cool and dark and wet and loud and alive, alive beyond any reasonable expectation, a place that should not support this much motion and yet does, that has found its equilibrium not in stillness but in continuous, deafening, collective movement. The organisms do not seem to notice you. They are busy with whatever they are doing, which has been happening here longer than you can measure, in the low dark damp, on the resonant floor, in the sound that never stops.
