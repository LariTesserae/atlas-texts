# The Lithic Grove

You stand at the edge of something that should not be called a grove, but the word arrives anyway, as if your mind is trying to find a container for what it sees.

The ground beneath you is not soil. It is a bed of fine, pale aggregate — crushed and recrushed stone, particles ranging from sand to fingertip, all of it the color of old bone or bleached driftwood. It has been here so long that it has forgotten its origin. There is no memory of mountains in it. No geological ambition. It has been weathered down, ground down, re-arranged by forces so patient that the arrangement has become final. The surface is level. Not flat — level, in the way that a still pond is level, with the suggestion that something once moved here and left equilibrium as its residue.

Sparse things rise from this bed. You might call them trunks, or columns, or fingers, or the masts of ships sunken into a pale sea. They are vertical mineral formations, perhaps a meter and a half tall, perhaps two meters, tapering upward and narrowing into soft rounded tips like sealed candle wicks. Their surfaces are smooth but not polished — they have the matte finish of things that have been touched by air for longer than air remembers. They are scattered across the floor with the kind of spacing that feels intentional but is not: each one stands six, eight, ten meters from its nearest neighbors, and between them the pale aggregate stretches unbroken. They do not cluster. They do not compete. They have found their distances and hold them.

The columns are not growing. They are not dying. They are not doing anything. They have settled into their being the way a rock settles into a riverbed — not at rest, exactly, but in a state where rest and motion have become indistinguishable.

There is warmth here. Not heat — warmth. The air has a low, even temperature, perhaps that of skin, perhaps slightly above. It does not move. It does not circulate. It simply *is* warm, the way a closed room holds the warmth of its last occupant. The warmth comes from below, or from the columns themselves, or from the aggregate, or from nothing at all. There is no source you can find. It simply saturates the space the way a color saturates a canvas.

And there is almost no light. What light exists comes from nowhere you can point to. It is not ambient. It is not directional. It is more like a faint luminescence held in the air itself, just enough to give the columns edges, just enough to let you distinguish vertical from horizontal, near from far. The pale aggregate picks up whatever faint radiance exists and returns it as a ghost-glow, so the ground seems to produce a soft, directionless shine — not bright enough to read by, bright enough to walk by. The columns are slightly darker than the ground, their surfaces drinking a fraction of what little light there is, so they appear as grey vertical interruptions in a slightly luminous field. The effect is of standing in a room after everyone has gone to sleep, when the only light is the memory of light, held in objects that have absorbed it all day.

The space has boundaries. Not walls — boundaries. At a certain distance in every direction, the columns become less distinct, the glow fades, and the air thickens into something that is not darkness exactly but an absence of the conditions that let you perceive. You could walk toward it, but something in the quality of the silence would stop you before you arrived. The grove is perhaps fifty meters across. Perhaps sixty. It is small enough that you sense its completeness. This is not a piece of a larger landscape. This is *the* landscape. Everything that happens here happens within this circle.

The silence is not empty. It is full in the way that a held breath is full. There is no wind. No insect hum. No creak or settling or drip. The aggregate is so fine and so dry that it makes no sound underfoot — or rather, it makes a sound so subtle, so distributed across so many particles, that it resolves into texture rather than noise. When you shift your weight, the particles rearrange themselves with a softness that is almost organic, like the rearrangement of scales. The sound, if it is a sound, is below hearing. It is something you feel in your feet, in your shins, in the small bones of your ankles — a whisper of contact that never becomes a word.

The ecology of this place, if it can be called ecology, is mineral. The columns are not alive. The aggregate is not alive. Nothing grows. Nothing decays. There is no cycle because there is no time, or because time moves so slowly here that a thousand years passes like a held note. The columns may have been here for ten thousand years. They may have been here for a hundred thousand. They are not fossils — they are not the remains of something that was. They are the persistent form of something that simply continued. Warm, dry air. Ground level. No water has touched this place in so long that the concept of wetness has no reference here. There are no stains, no mineral deposits, no efflorescence. Nothing crystallizes. Nothing dissolves. The chemistry of this place has reached a point where it has nothing left to say.

And yet — and this is what makes it a grove rather than a desert, a place rather than a void — there is a *presence* here. The columns are spaced like trees. The ground is textured like a forest floor. The dim, sourceless light falls like light through a canopy. Your mind keeps insisting on the analogy, and the place does not contradict it. It is as if a forest lived here once, long ago, before water, before soil, before the concept of growing things, and what remains is the *shape* of that forest, preserved in mineral, stripped of biology, reduced to the essential gesture of vertical things standing in a horizontal space under a ceiling of dim air.

You could sit here. You could sit on the pale aggregate — it would be warm, slightly yielding, fine enough to accept your weight without complaint — and you would feel the silence settle around you like a held breath that never exhales. You would watch the columns not move. You would feel the warmth not change. You would wait for something to happen and nothing would happen and the nothing would not be disappointing because there is no mechanism here for disappointment, no gradient of expectation, no passage of time against which delay could be measured.

The place is complete. It has been complete for longer than completeness has had a name.

You are the first thing here that has arrived.
