# The Grotto of Ten Thousand Throats

You descend through a seam in the rock that shouldn't exist — a vertical crack barely wider than your shoulders, warm air pressing against your face like a living thing trying to decide whether to let you pass. The walls are not stone. They are *grown*: a dense, interlocking lattice of ceramic-white filaments, each no thicker than a finger, woven so tightly that the surface reads as smooth until you're close enough to see the individual strands breathing. They expand. They contract. A slow peristaltic rhythm, as though the passage itself is swallowing.

The water is everywhere and nowhere. It doesn't pool. It doesn't run. It *condenses* — beading on every surface in fat, trembling droplets that never quite fall, held by some surface tension that seems to exceed what physics should allow. The air is thick with it. You breathe water. Not uncomfortably — it's warm, almost body-temperature, and somehow your lungs accept it — but the sensation is unmistakable: each inhale is a drink. The humidity is so total that distance becomes uncertain. Shapes ten meters away soften into suggestion.

Then the passage opens.

---

The chamber is small. That's the first thing that strikes you — not vast, not cathedral-scale, but *contained*. Perhaps forty meters across, roughly circular, the ceiling lost in a haze of warm condensation that glows faintly amber from sources you cannot locate. The light doesn't come from any single point. It *seeps* from the walls themselves, a bioluminescence so dim it barely qualifies as illumination, turning everything into a study in browns and deep ochres and the wet gleam of surfaces that shouldn't be wet.

The walls are the same filament-lattice as the passage, but here they have been *shaped*. Over what must be an immense span of time, the woven material has been sculpted — or has sculpted itself — into ribs, buttresses, flowing organic arches that recall the inside of a massive heart. The erosion patterns are ancient and deliberate: channels worn into the lattice by water that has been dripping, dripping, dripping for longer than you can meaningfully contemplate. The channels branch and rebranch, a river system in miniature, and every channel terminates in a small rounded cavity no larger than a cupped hand.

Each cavity holds water. Each water-surface *vibrates*.

---

The sound is the thing that will break you if you let it.

It is not loud in the way a machine is loud, or a storm is loud. It is loud in the way a *crowd* is loud — a dense, layered, overlapping cacophony of individual voices, each one distinct, each one *communicating*. The water in each cavity is resonating at a slightly different frequency, and the frequencies interact, interfere, harmonize, clash. The result is a sound like language. Like ten thousand throats all speaking at once in a language built from overtones and beats and the wet click of droplets striking surfaces at precise intervals.

It is deafening. Not painfully so — your ears don't ring, your head doesn't ache — but the sound fills every available space in your perception. You cannot think over it. You can only think *within* it, the way you might think inside a river's current, letting the sound carry your thoughts in directions you didn't intend.

And the sound is *structured*. There are patterns. Phrases that repeat. Call-and-response sequences between cavities on opposite walls. A low, almost subsonic pulse that seems to originate from the floor — if there is a floor; the ground is a shallow pool of warm water no more than ankle-deep, and beneath it you can feel the same filament-lattice continuing downward, pulsing with that same slow peristaltic rhythm.

---

The fauna announces itself in movement.

At first you think the walls are *shifting* — the ribbed lattice seeming to rearrange, the channels appearing to migrate. Then you see them: organisms the size of your thumb, thousands of them, moving through the lattice like blood cells through capillaries. They are translucent. You can see their organs — or what serve as organs — pulsing with the same amber light that illuminates the chamber. They have too many legs, or perhaps no legs at all and simply *flow*, their bodies deforming to pass through gaps that should be too narrow, then reconstituting on the other side.

They are everywhere. Clustered in the water-cavities, their bodies half-submerged, their movements creating the vibrations that produce the sound. Crawling across the walls in dense rivers of pale flesh. Hanging from the ceiling in living curtains that drip not water but a clear, viscous fluid that smells faintly of copper and warm bread.

Some of them notice you.

They don't react with alarm. They *adjust*. The sound shifts — a new voice entering the chorus, a frequency that seems to be directed at you, probing, testing. The organisms nearest to you orient their bodies in your direction. They have no visible eyes, but they *perceive* you with absolute certainty. The sound they produce in response to your presence is lower, slower, almost interrogative.

You realize, with a slow creeping certainty, that the organisms are not *in* the structure.

The organisms *are* the structure.

The filaments, the lattice, the channels, the cavities — all of it is their bodies, or their secretion, or their *architecture*, built over spans of time that make the concept of "building" meaningless. They are the walls. They are the water-management system. They are the acoustic array. They are the warm, wet, dim, dense, deafening, living *place* you have entered, and they have been here so long that the distinction between organism and environment has dissolved entirely.

---

The water near your feet moves differently now. It is flowing *toward* you, pooling around your ankles with what feels like intention. The organisms in the shallow pool are climbing your boots — not aggressively, but with the same exploratory purposefulness with which a tongue finds a new tooth. Their bodies are warm. They pulse against your skin in rhythm with the chamber's sound.

The air thickens. The condensation increases. The amber light brightens by a fraction, and you can see deeper into the haze above — the ceiling is not a ceiling but a *convergence*, thousands of filaments drawing together into a central mass that hangs like a chandelier, or a heart, or a brain. It pulses. The entire chamber pulses with it. The sound swells and resolves, for just a moment, into something that is unmistakably, terrifyingly, *beautifully* a single coherent statement — a sentence in a language made of water and vibration and the bodies of ten thousand tiny throats —

And then it fragments again into the chorus, and the moment passes, and you are standing ankle-deep in warm water in a small, dense, living room that is deciding what to do with you.
