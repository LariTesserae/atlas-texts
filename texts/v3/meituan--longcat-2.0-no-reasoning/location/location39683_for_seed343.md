# The Braidwork Plateau

---

You are standing on a place that should not still exist.

The elevation registers first as a pressure behind the eyes — a thinness in the air that makes every breath feel incomplete, that makes the distance to the horizon seem simultaneously infinite and intimate. You are approximately four thousand meters above the last meaningful ground, on a plateau so ancient that the word "plateau" is a polite fiction. This land has been settling for a hundred million years. The mountains that once stood proud here are memories of memories, worn down to suggestions, then worn down again, then worn down until even the suggestions have forgotten themselves. What remains is a vast, flat-leaning expanse of exposed stone that barely undulates — shallow rises and shallow bowls separated by such gradual transitions that you don't notice the change in grade until you've walked for twenty minutes and realize the world has tilted three degrees beneath you.

The stone is the first thing that wrongs you about the place. It is not smooth. It is not polished. It is *worked* — carved by wind and water and time into a network of shallow channels, basins, ridges, and honeycomb cells so dense and so fine that the entire surface of the plateau reads as a single continuous relief sculpture. Running your hand across it feels like running your hand across a brain. The channels are millimeters to centimeters deep. They catch water.

And there is water. Not much. Never much. The plateau sits in a climatic corridor where warm, moisture-bearing air masses occasionally arrive from lowland basins to the east — great sluggings of humid air that climb, lose their hold on themselves, and deposit their contents in brief, intense showers that last minutes. On the plateau's surface, the water does not run off. The channels hold it. The basins pool it. The honeycomb traps it. For days after each rain, the entire surface glistens, and in the thousand tiny depressions, water sits — shallow mirrors reflecting the overcast sky, each one no larger than a cupped hand. Between storms, the plateau dries to a pale grey-green from the living things that coat it.

And that is the second thing that wrongs you.

The vegetation is not on the plateau. The vegetation *is* the plateau.

The dominant organism — though "organism" is also a fiction here, because it may be millions of organisms or one or something else entirely — is a low, spreading, net-like growth that coats every exposed surface of stone in a living skin. It is not a lichen, not exactly, though it shares their patience. It is not moss, though it has moss's tenacity. It is a layered, interwoven mat of pale green, yellow-green, and rust-colored filaments, leaves, and root-like holdfasts that together form a continuous membrane across the stone surface, conforming to every channel and basin and honeycomb cell. In places the mat is two centimeters thick; in others it has grown into bushy, fingered clumps the size of a fist, raised above the surface on stubby, woody bases that have slowly, over centuries, compressed and lignified into tiny trunks. These clumps cast shadows. The shadows are millimeters deep. But there are billions of them, and the effect across the surface is that the plateau appears to shimmer with micro-relief, every point of texture exaggerated into a tiny crag and a tiny valley.

The mat is alive in a way that feels excessive. It grows. You can almost see it growing if you watch a basin of standing water for long enough — the surface film of green creeping outward at the pace of hours, not days. When the rains come, the mat swells visibly, its colors deepening from dusty sage to vivid, almost garish emerald, and it releases spores — not in clouds but in slow, deliberate pulses, like breathing. You see the spore events as faint golden hazes rising from the surface in the warm air, catching the light, drifting in the plateau's constant slow wind.

There are other plants. At the edges of the channels, where moisture lingers longest, narrow reedy things grow — stiff, jointed stems no taller than your knee, topped with brush-like seed heads that rattle in the wind. In the deeper basins, where water pools persist for weeks after rain, there are floating sheets of a duckweed-like plant so fine it looks like spilled paint on the water's surface. And in the deepest depressions — the rare natural bowls in the stone that are deep enough to hold water for months — there are small, gnarled trees. Actual trees. Twisted, sprawling things with bark like paper and leaves like needles, no taller than a person, rooted in accumulated soil that has built up over millennia from the decomposition of the mat itself. They are the oldest things here. Some of them are probably thousands of years old. They are the plateau's only concession to verticality, and they are modest about it.

The warmth is not oppressive but it is constant. The sun at this elevation is strong — the air is thin and gives little protection — but a persistent cloud layer often sits just above the plateau, diffusing the light into a soft, even glow that never fully commits to either brilliance or gloom. It is the light of a hospital corridor stretched across the sky. Shadows are soft-edged and brief. The temperature hovers in the high teens to low twenties Celsius, warm enough to be comfortable in still air, but the wind — the constant, patient wind that has been eroding this place for geological ages — takes the warmth off your skin and replaces it with a feeling of exposure.

And then there are the animals.

The sound arrives before you understand what it is. It is not a single sound. It is the accumulated output of a biosphere that has been running at full capacity for so long that the plateau's ecology has achieved a kind of steady-state roar. The dominant fauna are arthropods — insects, primarily, though the lines are blurry. Beetles with iridescent shells that march in columns along the channels, following the moisture gradients. Flies that hover in shimmering columns above the wet basins, feeding on the spore haze. Spiders — enormous, fat-bodied spiders with leg spans as wide as your palm — that have built webs not between branches (there are no branches) but between the raised clumps of the living mat, creating vast, low, horizontal web-fields that catch dew and insects alike and glisten like nets of silver thread spread across the stone.

The spiders are the plateau's apex predators, and they are everywhere. You walk among their webs constantly. They are too low to see until you're nearly on them — a sudden flash of geometry at knee height, the web vibrating with the tension of something caught, the spider already moving along its radial threads toward the struggling point. The spiders are slow, deliberate, patient — they have the tempo of the plateau itself. They do not rush. They do not need to.

Below the spiders in the hierarchy are the millipedes — long, segmented, dark-backed things that process the dead mat and fallen organic matter into soil, working in slow grinding lines along the channels. Below them are the larvae — thousands of species of larvae, burrowing in the mat, feeding on it, being eaten by it (the mat, you realize, is not purely plant; it absorbs, digests, incorporates — the boundary between autotroph and heterotroph is negotiable here). The fauna is so dense that when you press your hand against the mat, it moves. Not the mat itself. The things inside the mat. A millimeter-thick layer of living, squirming, clicking, chewing, crawling biomass between your fingers and the stone.

The sound is the sound of all of them at once: the chitinous clicking of beetle mandibles, the high thin whine of flies, the almost subsonic thrum of spider legs on web silk (a sound you feel in your chest more than hear), the rustle of millipede bodies over stone, the constant, papery *tick tick tick* of things moving through the mat overhead and beside you and beneath your feet. It does not stop. It does not vary in volume. It is the background radiation of this place, as constant as the wind, and it is *loud* — not painfully so, not sharply so, but comprehensively so, filling every frequency band from the audible low hum to the insectile high buzz until silence is a concept that belongs to some other world.

The wind itself is the other constant sound — a long, slow, unbroken exhale across the stone surface, carrying spores and dust and the faint chemical smell of the mat's metabolic processes (something like wet cardboard, something like iron, something like honey left too long in the jar). The wind erodes. That is its function. It has been the primary sculptor of this plateau for longer than there has been a word for "plateau." It carries abrasive dust — ground from the stone itself by previous winds, in a cycle of erosion that is the oldest active process here — and it works on the channels, deepening them by microns per year, maintaining the honeycomb, keeping the surface texture alive and changing even as it is, in the largest sense, finished.

And there are the people.

Not many. A handful. They live in the deep depressions, near the old trees, where water lingers and soil has accumulated enough to support small gardens of cultivated plants — domesticated variants of the mat's weedy relatives, grown in careful plots for food and fiber. Their shelters are low, wide structures built from stacked stone slabs and roofed with the dried, compressed mat — which, when dead and dry, is a surprisingly effective building material, light and insulating and fireproof. The shelters are scattered across the plateau with a density of roughly one per square kilometer, each one occupied by a small family group, each group connected to the others by faint paths worn into the mat — not roads, just slightly more compressed and slightly less vegetated corridors that follow the easiest routes between the deep depressions.

The people are tool-users. They have stone knives for cutting the mat. They have bone needles for sewing it. They have fire, which they use carefully — the mat is flammable when dry, and a fire on the plateau would be catastrophic, so their fires are small, contained in stone-ringed pits, fed with dead wood from the old trees (which they prune, never fell, with a reverence that has the weight of deep custom). They eat the beetles. They eat the spiders (roasted, which makes the legs crispy and the body soft, apparently). They eat the young shoots of the mat, which taste, by all accounts, like a bitter celery. They drink water from the deep basins, which they keep covered with mat-sheets to reduce evaporation.

They are quiet. Their voices are soft and low, adapted to a world where the ambient noise is so constant that loud sounds are not drowned out but *absorbed*, swallowed into the existing roar like a pebble dropped into a river. They speak in short, economical phrases. They listen to the plateau — to the changes in the insect sound that indicate weather, to the vibration in the spider webs that indicates prey availability, to the smell of the mat that indicates moisture. They are as tuned to this environment as the spiders are to their webs.

There are faint marks of older habitation — stone arrangements that might be ceremonial, might be practical, are probably both. Cairns on the highest points of the plateau's barely perceptible rises, visible from great distances across the flat expanse. Carved channels in the stone that redirect water flow in ways that benefit the deep depressions. A few half-buried stone slabs carved with patterns — spirals, mostly, and branching forms that echo the channel networks, as if the people who made them saw in the plateau's erosion patterns a mirror of their own vascular systems, their own branching thoughts.

The plateau extends. You can walk for hours and the character of the place does not change — the mat, the channels, the basins, the scattered shelters, the old trees in their depressions, the spiders in their web-fields, the beetles in their columns, the wind, the sound, the thin warm air, the soft light. It is a regional-scale homogeneity, a landscape that has achieved its final form and now simply *maintains* — not static, not dead, but in equilibrium, every process matched by a counter-process, every growth matched by an erosion, every birth matched by a consumption. It is a place that has been doing this for so long that it has become good at it.

At the edges of the plateau — far from where you stand — the mat thins. The channels deepen into actual gullies, then into ravines, then into canyons that drop away into the lowlands below. From these edges, the plateau looks like a green-grey cliff face, the mat forming a hanging garden on vertical stone, the spiders building their webs in defiance of gravity, the beetles marching along the cliff edge in their columns as if the drop means nothing to them. Which it doesn't. They have wings. Most of them. Not all. Some fall. The spiders at the bottom of the canyons are fat with the falling.

You are standing on the Braidwork Plateau, and it is beautiful in the way that very old things are beautiful — not because of what they are, but because of what they have survived to become. It hums. It clicks. It rustles. It glows faintly green in the diffused light. The wind moves across it, and the channels catch the air and sing — not loudly, just a thin, reedy note, the voice of wind through carved stone, added to the chorus of beetles and flies and spiders and millipedes and the slow, green breathing of the mat.

You breathe the thin warm air. You listen. You do not need to be told that you are welcome or unwelcome. The plateau does not have opinions about you. It is busy with its own business, which is the business of continuing, which it has been conducting for a hundred million years, and which it will conduct for a hundred million more, with or without your presence.

The wind picks up slightly. The spore haze thickens. The mat exhales.

You walk on.
