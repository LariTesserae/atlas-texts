# The Nursery of Walls

---

You are inside a lung.

Not a metaphor. The breathing is real, and it comes from every surface.

---

The chamber is small — no more than four meters across at its widest point, though "widest point" is a generous description because the space is never the same shape twice. It is more like a fist: curled, clenched, with walls that inch closer or farther apart on a rhythm you can feel in your teeth when you stand still long enough. The ceiling is low. The floor is soft. Everything is wet.

This is a submerged place. The water is not flooding in from outside — it *is* the place. The walls weep it continuously, thin veils of cold water sliding down surfaces that look, at first, like calcified bone but behave, up close, like something that has not yet decided what it will become. The floor is a shallow pool perhaps ankle-deep, opaque, the color of milk mixed with a very faint blue — like glacial runoff, like the discharge from a wound that is healing wrong. It is 0.5°C above freezing. Your feet go numb within minutes. The air — if you can call it air, this warm wet breath that fills the upper part of the curved ceiling — tastes of mineral and salt and something sweeter underneath, like rotting fruit, like a placenta.

There are no devices here. No metal. No plastic. No marks of tool-use. But this is not an untouched place. The built parameter is high, and the reason is that *something is building*, and it has been building for a very long time, and it is almost finished, and the architecture is biological.

---

The walls.

The walls are the first thing you notice and the last thing you understand. They are covered — densely, almost oppressively — in structures that resemble urban construction but are not made by hands. They are made by bodies.

Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. Each no larger than your thumbnail.

**Tubular towers.** Built from a material that is part calcium, part secretion, part something that glows faintly in the dimness — not bioluminescence exactly, more like the memory of light, a phosphorescence that lingers in the material itself. The towers cluster. They share walls. They are stacked unevenly, sometimes five or six high, sometimes collapsing into one another and being rebuilt within hours. Between them run channels — deliberate, organized — that carry the weeping water from the ceiling down to the floor pool in slow spiraling routes. Some channels are lined with a smooth glossy substance, others are rough and freshly extruded, still soft to the touch. The construction is ongoing. You can watch, in real time, as a new wall extends itself — a pale filament emerging from a pore in an existing structure, growing at perhaps one centimeter per minute, curving toward a neighboring tower, and *bonding* to it with a faint click you feel more than hear.

The builders are everywhere. They are small — segmented, pale, eyeless, numerous beyond easy counting. Each one is perhaps the length of a rice grain, pale gray-white, with a soft body that leaves a faint trail of mucus wherever it moves. They have no faces that you would recognize as faces, but they have *fronts*, and the fronts are busy: manipulating material, testing joints, carrying tiny loads of dissolved mineral in soft pouches beneath their segments. They move in dense carpets across the walls, over and under and through one another, and the sound they make — if sound is even the word — is not silence. It is a texture. A dry, continuous, microscopic rustling, like the sound of a book's pages being turned by a patient ghost. It is so quiet that when you hold your breath, you can feel it on your skin before you hear it in your ears.

They do not avoid you. They do not acknowledge you. You are terrain to them. Several are walking across your boot right now.

---

The dimness. There is no light source you can point to. The faint glow from the wall-structures provides the only illumination — a blue-green so faint that your eyes, after ten minutes, stop trying to parse color and begin interpreting everything in a flat monochrome. Shapes become more felt than seen. The ceiling above you is invisible; you know it's there because the water drips from it, but you cannot see it. The space feels both intimate and vertiginous — a small room that extends into darkness in every direction beyond the reach of the glow.

Your own body becomes part of the ecology almost immediately. Within minutes of arrival, the temperature differential between you and the environment begins to matter. Your warmth is a resource. Small organisms — not the builders, something else, something more opportunistic — begin to gather along the warmer surfaces of your clothing. They are transparent, nearly invisible, and they move with a slow sinusoidal drift, like sea grass in a current, except there is no current. They are orienting to your heat. They leave no mark. You feel a faint tightness on your skin, as if the air itself is drawing closer.

---

The sparse vegetation. It is here, but it is strange. Growing from crevices in the walls — places where two structures meet and leave a gap just wide enough for root-analogues — there are organisms that are neither plant nor fungus but something older in concept. They are dark, almost black, and they grow in thin flat sheets that press against the wall like lichen, except they *pulse*. A slow rhythm, synchronized across all of them, a contraction and expansion that takes approximately twelve seconds per cycle. When they contract, they darken further, almost disappearing. When they expand, they reveal a faint interior structure — a web of filaments that glow, briefly, a deep arterial red, before the light fades and they go dark again.

They are breathing. They are doing something with the water. Filtering it, maybe. Or *reading* it. They seem to respond to the presence of the builder-organisms, contracting more sharply when large numbers pass over them, as if recoiling, or as if *communicating in a language of tension*.

There are also, in the pool at the floor, something that resembles kelp. Three or four strands, each no longer than your forearm, pale green, tethered to the soft floor by holdfasts that look disturbingly like tiny grasping hands. They drift in the still water with a motion that suggests current, but there is no current. They are moving on their own. Slowly. Purposefully. Trailing their free ends along the pool's surface like fingers testing the temperature of a bath.

---

The erosion parameter is low, and this is visible. Nothing here is old. The walls are *emerging*. In places — and you find these if you look carefully, if you press your palm flat against the surface and wait — you can feel the material *still forming*. It is soft. It yields slightly under pressure, then firms. There are places where the wall is simply *not finished yet* — where you can press your finger into the surface and leave an impression that slowly, over minutes, fills back in as new material is deposited from within. The place is under construction at the most fundamental level. The geology — if it is geology — is embryonic. It has the texture of something that will, in time, become hard and permanent and ancient, but right now it is young. It is still deciding what it wants to be.

And the fauna — the abundance of it — is not just the builders. The builders are the *citizens*. But there are others.

**The grazers.** Larger than the builders — some as long as your thumb — flat, dark-backed, moving slowly across the walls in wide lazy arcs. They eat the dark sheets. You can see their mouthparts — a complex spiral of tiny chitinous teeth — working at the edges of the vegetation, and where they feed, the sheets contract violently and then, over minutes, regrow. The grazers are not molested by the builders. They move through the urban lattice unimpeded, and the builders adjust their paths around them with the ease of long accommodation.

**The swimmers.** In the pool. Small, fast, translucent — you mostly see them as refractions, as distortions in the milk-blue water. They are perhaps the size of minnows, but their body plan is wrong: too many segments, too many legs, a head that seems to face in two directions at once. They move in schools that coil and uncoil with a liquid precision that is beautiful and deeply unsettling. They feed on something in the water — you cannot see what. Occasionally one surfaces, breaks the tension of the pool with a tiny sharp *pop*, and submerges again.

**The watchers.** There is — you think there is — something larger. You have not seen it. But twice now, the builder-organisms on the far wall have *all stopped at once* — a ripple of stillness passing across the colony like a wind through grass — and resumed after approximately four seconds. As if something passed behind the wall. As if something in the unfinished geology of this place is aware of you, and chose, briefly, to announce itself.

---

The dynamic quality is the thing that holds it all together. This place is not static. It is not a tableau. It is a system in motion, and the motion is *fast* by the standards of what is being built. Structures rise and fall. The floor pool rises and falls with the breathing of the walls. The dark sheets pulse. The channels reroute themselves when a tower collapses — which happens, on average, once every twenty minutes in your field of vision — and the water finds a new path within seconds, and the builders begin again, and the new tower is slightly different from the old one, as if the colony is iterating, is *designing*, is learning from each failure.

The cold slows you. The dimness narrows your world. The wet saturates everything — your clothing, your skin, your breath. The silence — that near-silence, that texture of tiny motion — fills your ears until you cannot tell if you are hearing the place or your own blood.

And the strangeness. The strangeness is not in any single element. It is in the *combination*. A city, but built by things without minds you can recognize. A geology, but one that is still wet, still soft, still becoming. An ecology, but one that operates at a scale just below the threshold of your attention, so that you are always aware of it but never quite able to hold the whole of it in your mind at once. It is a place that is *almost* familiar — almost a reef, almost a cave, almost a city, almost a body — but is none of these things, and is instead something that has no name, something that is still, itself, emerging.

---

You stand in the shallow pool. The water is at your ankles. The walls breathe. The builders build. The dark sheets pulse their slow red rhythm. Something moves behind the wall, or doesn't. The cold climbs your legs. The glow fades and does not return, or returns and you cannot tell the difference.

The place is small. The place is alive. The place is not finished.

It is waiting for something. Perhaps it is waiting for you to leave, so it can continue becoming what it is.

Perhaps it is waiting for you to stay.
