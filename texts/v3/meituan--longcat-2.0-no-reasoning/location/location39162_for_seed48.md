Somewhere in the lowlands, where the ground never fully dries, the city of Vael Koth sits in a permanent state of sweating.

The terrain is flat and saturated — not a marsh, not a floodplain, but something more like a vast mineral basin that has learned to weep. The ground is a dark, fine-grained stone, almost black, veined with pale mineral deposits that glisten perpetually. Water does not pool so much as it *emerges* — seeping up through hairline fractures in the stone, beading on every surface, running in thin sheets down walls and the sides of structures. The air is thick with it. Not raining. Not foggy. Just *wet* — the kind of wet where your skin never feels dry, where metal is always slightly slick, where the boundary between solid and liquid feels negotiable. The humidity is so high that condensation forms on every cool surface and drips in irregular rhythms, a thousand tiny waterfalls no taller than a hand's width.

The city itself is old. Not ancient-ruins old, but *settled* old — the kind of old where the buildings have found their final shapes and stopped changing. Structures rise three to eight stories, built from the same dark stone as the ground, cut in massive blocks that have been worn smooth at their edges by centuries of moisture and slow mineral accretion. There are no sharp corners anymore. Everything has softened. Arches have sagged into something more organic. Cornices have grown stalactite-like drips of calcium and iron deposits that hang like frozen tears. The architecture is dense in places — blocks of buildings pressed together, narrow streets between them, covered walkways and bridges connecting upper stories — but the overall feeling is not claustrophobic. The city is *open* in its sprawl, extending in every direction across the basin, thinning at its edges into scattered industrial structures and then into the bare weeping stone of the lowlands.

And it is industrial. This is not a city of markets and temples. This is a city of production. The soundscape is dominated not by voices but by machinery — the low, rhythmic pounding of presses, the whir of belt drives, the hiss of steam venting from pipes that run along every major wall like metal ivy. Factories occupy entire blocks, their interiors lit by the dull orange glow of furnaces and the blue-white arc of electrical junctions. The power grid is old but functional — thick cables strung between buildings, transformers humming on rooftops, the whole city vibrating at a frequency just below conscious hearing. Smokestacks rise at irregular intervals, but what they emit is mostly steam, white and voluminous, adding to the already saturated air. The industrial processes here involve heat — significant heat — and the temperature in the factory districts is oppressive, the air shimmering above metal surfaces, workers moving slowly and deliberately in the thermal haze.

The streets are paved with the same dark stone, but worn into channels by centuries of foot traffic and water flow. Every street is also a stream, or nearly so — water runs in the grooves, ankle-deep in places, clear enough to see the stone below but carrying a faint mineral tint, pale green or rust-orange depending on which district you're in. The drainage is not engineered so much as *accepted* — the city was built to accommodate the water, not to fight it. Channels are carved deliberately into the street surfaces. Buildings have gutters that are really small aqueducts, directing runoff into cisterns or simply channeling it to the next block. In the lower-lying areas, the streets become canals, and flat-bottomed boats — pushed by poles or pulled by animals — serve as the primary transport.

There are no plants. None. No trees in courtyards, no moss on walls, no weeds in cracks. The stone surfaces are bare — not sterile, exactly, but *mineral*. In places, thin films of bacteria or slime grow in the wet areas, iridescent sheets of color on the stone, but nothing that would be recognized as vegetation. The absence is striking once you notice it. No green anywhere. The palette is black stone, rust metal, pale mineral deposits, the white of steam, the orange of furnace light, and the dark, dark wet.

But the city is not empty of life. The fauna — and this is the thing that defines Vael Koth more than its industry, more than its water, more than its age — the fauna is *overwhelming*.

They are arthropods, mostly. Not insects exactly, not crustaceans exactly, but something in that lineage, adapted over millennia to the hot, wet, mineral-saturated environment. They range in size from barely visible to the size of a large dog. The most common are the *keth* — flat, multi-legged creatures about the size of a hand, with carapaces that range from matte black to deep iridescent blue. They move in loose aggregations across the wet stone surfaces, feeding on the bacterial films and mineral deposits. They are everywhere. The ground ripples with them. Walls are textured with them. They cluster around the warm pipes of the industrial district in such numbers that the pipes appear to be *moving*, a slow peristalsis of living bodies.

Larger ones occupy the canal systems — long, segmented creatures that undulate through the shallow water, their bodies pale and translucent enough to see the dark line of their digestive tracts. They are scavengers, mostly, feeding on the organic detritus that washes through the city's water system. They are not aggressive, but they are *numerous*, and the canals are thick with them, the water's surface dimpled with their movement.

In the factory districts, the heat draws different species — hard-shelled, compact creatures that nest in the warm spaces behind furnaces and along steam conduits. They click. The factories have a constant undercurrent of clicking, thousands of small hard bodies shifting and scraping against metal and stone. Workers move among them with practiced indifference, stepping over clusters, brushing them off ledges, occasionally crushing one underfoot with a wet pop that has become so routine it doesn't register.

The largest fauna are the *vren* — slow, heavy, six-legged creatures the size of small cattle, with broad flat heads and mouthparts designed for scraping mineral deposits from stone. They are used as draft animals, sort of, though "used" implies a level of control that doesn't quite exist. The vren move through the city on their own routes, following the mineral-rich seepage patterns, and the city has simply been built around their paths. Streets widen where the vren regularly pass. Doorways are tall enough to accommodate them. They are not domesticated so much as *accommodated* — a negotiation between species that has been ongoing for longer than the city has existed. They are slow and deliberate and utterly indifferent to the humans who move around them, stepping over their legs, ducking under their bodies, waiting patiently while a vren finishes scraping a particular patch of wall.

The sound of the city is mostly quiet, in the way that a place can be mostly quiet while still being full of sound. There is no roar, no cacophony. The machinery provides a constant low hum. The water provides a constant low shushing. The fauna provide clicking, scraping, the occasional heavy footfall of a vren, the soft collective rustle of keth moving across stone. Human voices are present but not dominant — people speak at normal volumes, and the sound doesn't carry far in the humid air. Conversations are intimate by necessity. The overall effect is of a place that is *busy* but not *loud*, active but not frantic.

The light is dim. The sky is overcast — not with storm clouds, but with a permanent layer of high humidity and industrial steam that diffuses whatever sunlight reaches the basin into a uniform gray-white glow. There are no sharp shadows. Everything is lit evenly, flatly, the way things are lit underwater. At night, the city's furnaces and electrical lights provide pools of warmer illumination, but between these pools, the darkness is deep and wet and absolute. The keth are more active at night. The clicking gets louder. The vren move more freely through the streets when the human population thins.

The people of Vael Koth are adapted to all of this — the wet, the heat, the fauna, the dimness, the mineral taste of the water. They dress in close-fitting garments that don't trap moisture, in materials that dry quickly or don't absorb water at all. They move through the city with a particular gait — careful on the wet stone, watchful of the larger fauna, comfortable in the dark. They maintain the machines, manage the water flow, harvest the mineral deposits that the vren and keth help to concentrate, and produce goods that are traded to the highlands in exchange for materials the basin cannot provide.

The city is not beautiful in any conventional sense. It is dark and wet and hot and full of clicking, scraping life. But it is *coherent*. Every element — the water, the stone, the heat, the industry, the fauna, the people — has found its place in a system that has been running for a very long time. Nothing is wasted. The water feeds the bacterial films, which feed the keth, which concentrate minerals, which the vren scrape and further concentrate, which the people harvest. The industry produces heat, which shapes the fauna's behavior, which shapes the city's layout, which shapes the industry's placement. It is a closed loop, or nearly so, and it hums along at its own pace, indifferent to the world beyond the basin.

The basin itself extends for kilometers in every direction — flat, wet, dark stone, with the city at its center and the industrial structures thinning outward into scattered extraction sites and processing stations. Beyond the city's edges, the fauna thin out too, though they never fully disappear. The bare stone stretches to the horizon, weeping its slow mineral tears, empty and alive at once.
