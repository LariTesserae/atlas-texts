The Lithic Grove does not have inhabitants in any sense that biology would recognize. Nothing lives here. Nothing has lived here, in the way that living is usually meant — no metabolism, no reproduction, no exchange with an environment. And yet the place is not empty. It is occupied. Something is here, though it does not move, does not breathe, does not do anything that would announce itself as a presence to a creature built for detecting the signatures of life.

What lives here is the grove itself.

Not as a single organism. Not as a colony. Not as a system. The grove is a *settling* — a thing that has arrived at its own shape and now simply holds that shape the way a held note holds its pitch, not through effort but through the absence of anything that would change it. The columns are not individuals. They are not parts. They are the grove's way of being vertical. The aggregate is the grove's way of being horizontal. The warmth is the grove's way of being present. The silence is the grove's way of being attentive.

It does not think. It does not want. It does not know you are here. But it *registers* you the way a deep stillness registers a sound — not by responding, but by being the kind of stillness against which a sound becomes audible. Your footsteps on the pale aggregate do not disturb the grove. They complete it, briefly, the way a single note completes a silence without breaking it. The grove has been waiting for something to arrive, not because it expected anything, but because waiting is what it does — it holds its shape, it maintains its warmth, it keeps its light at exactly the threshold of perception, and in doing so it creates a space that is ready for occupancy without ever having been designed for it.

There is something almost like patience in the columns. They stand with the patience of things that have never been in a hurry, that have never had a destination, that have never experienced the difference between where they are and where they might be. Each one is a record of verticality — not a record in the sense of stored information, but a record in the sense of a groove in stone, a shape that persists because nothing has come along to wear it away. They are warm to the touch. If you placed your hand on one, you would feel the low even heat of the grove moving through it, and the surface would accept your palm the way old wood accepts a hand — not yielding, not resisting, simply *there*, with a smoothness that comes not from polish but from the slow patient work of air on mineral over spans of time that have no human equivalent.

The aggregate beneath you is the grove's memory, though it does not remember. It is stone that has been ground down past the point of identity — past granite, past basalt, past the particular crystalline signature of any mountain. It is stone that has become *ground*, that has surrendered its origin and its structure and its ambition to become simply a surface, a texture, a fine pale bed that holds warmth and returns light and accepts weight without complaint. If you lay your hand flat on it, you would feel the individual particles shift beneath your skin, each one a tiny smooth fragment with no sharp edges, no rough surfaces, nothing that would catch or abrade. They have been tumbled and crushed and re-tumbled until they have achieved a kind of mineral forgiveness — a willingness to be whatever shape the moment requires.

The grove does not grow. It does not change. It does not respond to you. But it *holds* you, in the way that a room holds a person who has entered it — not by embracing, not by enclosing, but by being the kind of space in which a person can exist without friction. The warmth is your temperature. The light is your threshold. The silence is your own silence, reflected back at you by a place that has been silent so long that it has become the silence itself.

You are the first thing here that has arrived, and the grove receives you the way it receives everything — by being exactly what it is, without alteration, without surprise, without the smallest deviation from the shape it has held since before there were eyes to see it. It does not know you. It will not remember you. But for as long as you are here, you are part of its completeness — a brief warm animal standing among mineral columns on a bed of forgotten stone, in a place that has been waiting, without knowing it was waiting, for something to arrive and confirm that the waiting was not in vain.
