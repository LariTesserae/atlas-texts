The Keeper does not have a name, or if it does, the name is not a sound. It is a smell — warm clay and lamp oil and the particular musk of a small body that has lived its entire life in a single room. It is a texture — the soft rasp of claws on stone, the whisper of a tail dragging through moss. It is a rhythm — the slow, patient pulse of a creature that has never known urgency because nothing here has ever required it.

The Keeper is small. Smaller than you expect, when you finally see it — and you will see it, eventually, because it allows itself to be seen on its own terms, which is to say: gradually, in pieces, over time. A flash of pale fur at the edge of a shelf. A pair of dark eyes reflecting lamplight from behind a stack of unfired pots. A shape that resolves, when you are not looking directly at it, into something like a large rat and something like a small monkey and something like neither — a compact, low-slung body covered in fine grey-brown fur that is almost exactly the color of the stone, with long delicate fingers and a face that is startlingly expressive, almost human in its capacity for what you can only read as *attention*.

It is old. Not old in the way that the gallery is old — the gallery is old in layers, in strata, in the accumulated weight of maintenance and repair — but old in the way that a single living thing is old: worn smooth, economical, precise. Its movements are minimal. It does not waste motion. When it crosses the gallery floor, it moves in a low, fluid scuttle that keeps it in the channels between the stones, in the shadow-lines, in the spaces where the lamplight does not quite reach. When it climbs the walls — and it climbs constantly, with a spider's ease, finding holds in the carved shelves and the gaps between stones that you cannot see — it moves along the routes that the lizards do not use, the narrow vertical paths that run between the lizard-tessellations like roads through a city.

The Keeper tends. That is what it does. That is what it *is*. Tending is not a behavior the Keeper performs; it is the shape the Keeper's life takes, the way a river's life takes the shape of its channel.

It tends the lamps. This is its primary work, the work that structures its days — though there are no days here, only the slow flicker-cycle of the flames and the intermittent pulse of rain from above. The Keeper moves along the walls in a circuit that covers every lamp in the gallery, and it tends each one with the same sequence of gestures: it reaches up with one long-fingered hand, pinches the wick between thumb and forefinger, adjusts the flame by a fraction, then tilts the lamp-dish slightly to one side and back, checking the oil level by the angle at which the surface catches the light. When a lamp is low, the Keeper carries oil in a small clay jar — the jar with the fitted stone lid — and refills it with a precision that suggests it has done this ten thousand times, a hundred thousand times, and will do it ten thousand more. When a wick is spent, the Keeper pulls the old one out with a quick, practiced motion and threads a new one from the coil of fresh fiber it keeps in a niche near the center of the gallery. The old wicks are not discarded. They are carried to a shelf near the ceiling and added to a dense mat of used fiber that the Keeper is slowly, slowly weaving into something — a new ceiling-panel, perhaps, or a replacement section of the reed matting, or simply a nest that grows larger with each passing year until it is indistinguishable from the architecture.

It tends the water. The channels are its particular concern. The Keeper knows every channel in the gallery — knows where they run, where they narrow, where they are prone to blockage. It clears blockages with its fingers, reaching into the thin streams and pulling out accumulations of moss, shed lizard skin, mineral deposits, the tiny bodies of dead crayfish. It does this constantly, almost unconsciously, the way you might brush hair from your face. When a channel begins to erode its banks — when the packed earth starts to crumble and the water begins to spread rather than flow — the Keeper repairs it with small stones and fresh clay, pressing the clay into place with its palms and smoothing it with the flat stone from its toolkit. The repairs are crude but effective, and they are everywhere: if you look closely at the channels, you can see the patches, the places where the clay is newer and lighter in color, and you can trace the Keeper's work in a web of small interventions that hold the entire water system together.

It tends the plants. Not all of them — the moss tends itself, and the ferns in their cracked pots tend themselves, and the succulents with their thick pale leaves are so thoroughly adapted to this place that they require nothing. But there are certain plants, in certain pots, that the Keeper attends to with a particular care: trimming a dead leaf here, pressing a wayward root back into the soil there, turning a pot so that the plant's growth is balanced, symmetrical. Some of these plants are very old. Some have been repotted so many times that the ceramic is more repair than original — a patchwork of clay and cord and resin that holds the plant like a fist holds a bird, gently, firmly, with the understanding that the bird will eventually fly and the fist will remain.

It tends the lizards. This is the thing that is hardest to see, because it looks like nothing. The Keeper does not feed the lizards or shelter them or protect them from predators. The lizards are self-sufficient, a closed system, a living machine that runs on insects and moss and warmth. But the Keeper *watches* them. It sits, sometimes, on a shelf near the ceiling, in the space between two rows of lamps, and it watches the lizards on the wall below with an intensity that is almost meditative. And sometimes — rarely, but sometimes — it reaches out and touches one. A single finger, pressed gently against the lizard's back, and the lizard goes still, and the Keeper holds the touch for a long moment, and then withdraws. What passes between them in that touch is not something you can name. It is not affection, exactly, and not communication, exactly. It is *recognition*. The acknowledgment of a shared life, a shared space, a shared duration. The Keeper and the lizards have been here together for a very long time, and the touch is the only language they need.

The Keeper does not tend the crayfish or the snails or the insects. These are beneath its notice, or beyond its concern, or simply *other* — part of the gallery's life but not part of the Keeper's work. The Keeper's work is the lamps and the water and the plants and the lizards and the slow, patient maintenance of the gallery itself: the ceiling, the walls, the shelves, the floor, the basin at the center with its stratified layers of clay. The Keeper is the gallery's immune system, its repair mechanism, its memory. It remembers where every channel runs and which lamps burn low and which pots are cracked and which beams in the ceiling are beginning to fail. It remembers these things not in its mind — or not only in its mind — but in its body, in the patterns of movement that carry it through the gallery on its endless circuits, in the calluses on its fingers from pressing clay, in the particular angle at which it holds its head when it listens to the water.

Because the Keeper listens. It listens constantly, with an attention that is almost unbearable in its completeness. It listens to the water in the channels and can tell, by sound alone, where a blockage is forming. It listens to the lamps and can tell, by the quality of the flicker, which ones need oil and which ones need new wicks. It listens to the rain above and can tell, by the pattern of drumming, how heavy the fall is and how much water will reach the gallery and how long it will last. And it listens to the darkness at the far end of the gallery, to the low opening where the water disappears, to the deep resonance that echoes back from whatever space lies below. It listens to this more than anything else. It sits near the opening — not too close, never too close — and it listens, and its ears flatten slightly, and its eyes narrow, and its body goes very still.

The lizards near the opening turn their heads toward it when the Keeper is near. They do this for the Keeper more than for anything else. The Keeper is the thing they orient to, the fixed point in their slow kaleidoscopic world, the center around which their patterns organize. When the Keeper moves, the lizards adjust. When the Keeper is still, the lizards are still. When the Keeper listens to the darkness, the lizards listen too — or seem to listen, or simply go still in a way that resembles listening, which may be the same thing.

What the Keeper hears in the darkness, it does not share. What it knows about the space below — the larger chamber, the falling water, the pool that may or may not have a bottom — it keeps to itself, in the private interior of its small, old, patient body. But it is afraid. Not afraid in the way you understand fear — not the sharp, electric fear of immediate danger — but afraid in the way that a very old thing is afraid of change, of disruption, of the possibility that the system it has maintained for so long might shift in a way it cannot repair. The darkness below is the one thing in the gallery the Keeper does not tend. It is the one thing the Keeper cannot reach, cannot fix, cannot control. It is the wound in the gallery's body, the place where the water leaves and does not return, the place where the system opens onto something unknown.

The Keeper has been here longer than the current ceiling. Longer than the current shelves. Longer than the current pots, the current lamps, the current channels. It has rebuilt the gallery around itself so many times that the gallery is, in a sense, its body — an externalized physiology, a life-support system made of clay and wood and stone and water and light. The gallery keeps the Keeper alive: the warmth, the water, the food (the Keeper eats the snails, sometimes, and the crayfish, and certain parts of certain plants, and things that grow in the moss that you cannot identify). And the Keeper keeps the gallery alive: the lamps, the channels, the repairs, the slow patient work of maintenance that never ends because the gallery is always, always falling apart, and the Keeper is always, always putting it back together.

There may have been more than once. There may have been a time when the gallery was tended by many, a community of small careful creatures moving through the lamplight in a coordinated dance of repair and maintenance. Or there may have been a pair, once, and one of them went into the darkness and did not return, and the remaining one continued alone. Or there may have always been only one — a single Keeper, born in this gallery, destined to die in this gallery, sustained by the gallery's warmth and the gallery's water and the gallery's light, a closed system within a closed system, a life that is indistinguishable from the life of the place it inhabits.

The Keeper does not think about these things. The Keeper does not think about anything, in the way you understand thinking. The Keeper *does*. It tends. It listens. It moves through the gallery on its circuits of maintenance, and the lamps burn, and the water flows, and the lizards shift on the walls, and the moss grows, and the insects swarm, and the crayfish crawl, and the snails trace their glacial paths across the leaves of ancient plants, and the gallery breathes, and the Keeper breathes with it, and the darkness at the far end listens back.
