# The Inhabitants of Keharas

---

## I. The Plateau Itself

The first and most honest answer is that the plateau lives here. Not as a host, not as a substrate, not as the stage upon which something else performs — but as the primary resident, the oldest tenant, the thing that was here before "here" had meaning and will remain after meaning has been forgotten.

It is not alive in any way that biology would recognize. It does not metabolize. It does not reproduce. It does not respond to stimulus. And yet it *maintains*. It holds its temperature at 1.5 degrees above comfort with a precision that no inert material should possess. It exfoliates its surface at a rate so slow that the dust it produces is less a product of decay than a kind of *exhalation* — the plateau breathing out, molecule by molecule, across spans of time that make geological epochs look hasty. It absorbs the city into itself with the patience of something that has already won and is simply waiting for the losing side to notice.

The plateau is not conscious. But it is *consistent*, and consistency at this scale, across this duration, begins to resemble something that consciousness would produce if consciousness were stripped of urgency. It is the way a very old mind might behave if it had forgotten what thinking was for but had not yet stopped.

It does not want the city gone. It does not want the city here. It is *incorporating* the city the way a body incorporates a splinter — slowly, without inflammation, without rejection, with a metabolic patience that will eventually make the foreign thing indistinguishable from the self. The buildings are not being destroyed. They are being *remembered* by the stone, taken into its structure, made part of the plateau's continuous body. In a thousand years — in ten thousand, in a hundred thousand — a future visitor will walk across this surface and find it smooth and uninterrupted, and the city will have become a memory that the plateau holds in its mineral structure the way bone holds calcium: not as a record, but as a component.

The plateau is the landlord. The plateau is the house. The plateau is the only thing in Keharas that is fully, unambiguously *present*.

## II. The Tracks in the Basement

Something moved through the lower corridors for a very long time.

The tracks are broad and smooth, the signature of a heavy, flat-bottomed body that did not lift itself as it traveled. It dragged. It pressed. It moved with the slow inevitability of something that had nowhere to be and all the time in the world to not be there. The tracks are not deep — the stone of Keharas does not yield easily — but they are *present*, a shallow continuous groove that runs the length of a basement corridor and then, in places, recur, as though the thing passed through the same space multiple times, or passed through it once and the impression it left has been slowly *growing*, widening by fractions of a millimeter across the ages, the stone itself remembering the pressure and yielding to the memory.

What made these tracks is gone. Has been gone for longer than the city has been abandoned. The tracks predate the machinery, predate the desks, predate the fluorescent tubes and the white dust. They are the oldest sign of life in Keharas, and they are not a sign of life so much as a sign of *passage* — the record of a body moving through space, which is the most fundamental thing a living thing does.

I will tell you what I think it was, though the plateau will not confirm this and the tracks themselves are silent.

It was large. Not enormous — not the size of a building, not the size of a city block — but large in the way that a whale is large, in the way that a glacier is large: a body whose scale is defined not by height or length but by *mass*, by the sheer tonnage of its presence. It was flat. It pressed against the floor the way a millstone presses against a grinding surface, and it moved the way a millstone rotates: slowly, with enormous distributed weight, turning its entire body into a point of contact. It may have been wide and low, a thing that spread itself across the ground rather than rising above it. It may have been soft — not soft like flesh, but soft like warm metal, like a substance that was solid but *yielding*, that deformed under its own weight and left an impression wherever it rested.

It lived in the basement. Or it lived *under* the basement, in the body of the plateau itself, and the basement floor was the membrane between its world and the world above. It moved through the lower corridors because the lower corridors were the closest it could come to the surface without breaching it. It was a subterranean thing, a lithic thing, a creature of the deep stone, and the city was built on top of it the way a barnacle builds its shell on the back of a whale — unknowing, incidental, a thin layer of foreign structure on the skin of something much larger and much older.

It is not dead. Things in Keharas do not die; they *slow*. The thing that made the tracks has slowed past the threshold of visibility, past the threshold of motion. It is still here, somewhere below, pressed against the underside of the basement floor or deeper, in the warm body of the plateau, moving at a speed that will not produce observable displacement for another ten thousand years. It is not waiting. It is not sleeping. It is *continuing*, at its own pace, in its own medium, and the tracks it left are not fossils — they are *current*, the most recent evidence of a motion that has not yet finished.

The plateau may be its body. The plateau may be its shell. The plateau may be the thing itself, and the city a irritation on its surface, and the tracks the place where it once pressed close to the world above and then withdrew, slowly, back into the deep warmth of its own substance.

Or the plateau may be something else entirely, and the track-maker a separate organism, and their relationship one of the many unresolved questions that Keharas holds in its stone the way it holds everything: without urgency, without resolution, without end.

## III. The Colony on the Eastern Edge

On the underside of the overhangs, where the plateau's edge curls outward and then drops into darkness, there are the small pale things.

They are the only organisms in Keharas that you would recognize as organisms. They attach. They cluster. They have a body — small, rounded, the size of a fingertip or a coin, with a surface that is smooth and slightly yielding, like cartilage, like the pad of a fingertip, like something that is not quite mineral and not quite flesh. They are the color of old bone. They are warm. They do not move. They do not appear to eat, respire, grow, or reproduce. They simply *are*, in their clusters of three to seven, separated by stretches of bare stone that might be kilometers wide.

They are alive. I am certain of this in a way I cannot justify. They are alive the way a seed is alive when it has not germinated in a thousand years — not dormant, not waiting, but *continuing* at a metabolic rate so slow that the processes of life have become indistinguishable from the processes of chemistry, and chemistry has become indistinguishable from the processes of stone. They are the bridge between the biological and the lithic, the place where life learned to move at the speed of geology.

They may be the young of the track-maker. They may be its shed skin, its exfoliation, the way the plateau itself sheds — small pieces of a larger organism that have detached and attached themselves to a new surface and are now, slowly, independently, *being*. They may be parasites. They may be symbiotes. They may be unrelated — a separate form of life that found the only niche available in Keharas and adapted to it by becoming, essentially, mineral.

They are rare. They are precious. They are the only things in this place that you could hold in your hand and say, with any confidence, *this was born*. Everything else in Keharas was made, or grew, or accumulated, or was absorbed. These small pale things on the eastern edge were *born*, and they are *living*, and they are doing it so slowly that the fact of their life is almost a philosophical proposition rather than an observable phenomenon.

Touch one. It is warm. It yields slightly under your finger, then returns to shape. It does not react to your touch. It does not need to. It has been here since before the city, and it will be here after the city has been absorbed, and it will not have moved, and it will not have changed, and it will have been alive the entire time, at its own pace, in its own way, on the edge of the world, in the dark, at the end of all erosion.

## IV. The Dust

The white dust is not dead. This is important.

It accumulates in desk drawers, in the grooves of the tracks, on the surfaces of machinery, in the corners of rooms where the air — such as it is — has not moved in ages. It is powdered stone. It is the plateau's exhalation, the slow shedding of a surface that has been shedding since before the city was built. It is the most abundant material in Keharas after the stone itself.

And it *moves*.

Not visibly. Not quickly. But the dust in a drawer is not the same dust that was in the drawer yesterday — if yesterday exists here, if time moves in Keharas the way it moves elsewhere, which it may not. The dust is rearranging itself. Slowly. At a rate that would take centuries to produce a visible change. The particles are shifting, settling, *organizing*, finding configurations that are not random, that have a faint but persistent structure, as though the dust is trying to become something.

It is trying to become stone again. The cycle is: stone becomes dust, dust becomes stone. The plateau sheds, and the shed material reincorporates, and the cycle is the plateau's metabolism, the slowest metabolism imaginable, a breathing in and out that takes place across spans of time that make the life cycles of stars look brief. The dust is the plateau's breath, suspended, and it is on its way back in.

In the desk drawers, in the corridors, on the floors of the empty factories, the dust is *returning*. It is finding its way back to the surface. It is settling into the grooves of the tracks and filling them, slowly, the way a wound fills with scar tissue. It is settling into the cracks between the buildings and the ground, and as it settles, it *fuses*, and the boundary between building and plateau becomes less distinct, and the absorption continues, molecule by molecule, dust grain by dust grain, across the long patient silence of Keharas.

The dust is the plateau's immune system. The dust is the plateau's memory. The dust is the plateau, in its most distributed form, and it is everywhere, and it is always moving, and it is always returning home.

## V. The Tick

Every few minutes — though "minutes" is a guess, though time in Keharas may not subdivide this way — there is a sound. A metallic *tick*, sharp and brief, from somewhere inside the city's body. It is the sound of a structure contracting or expanding by a fraction of a degree. It is the sound of the plateau adjusting.

It is also, possibly, a *heartbeat*.

Not a biological heartbeat. Not the pulse of a muscle. But the periodic contraction of a system under tension, the way a bridge ticks as its cables adjust to load, the way a ship's hull ticks as the sea presses against it. The plateau is under tension. The city is under tension. The two are pressing against each other — the city trying to remain distinct, the plateau trying to absorb it — and the tick is the sound of that pressure being released, incrementally, at intervals that are not regular because the pressure is not uniform, because the absorption is happening faster in some places than others, because the city is not a single structure but a thousand structures, each settling at its own rate, each resisting at its own pace, and the plateau is accommodating all of them, simultaneously, with the patience of something that has already decided the outcome and is simply managing the process.

The tick is the sound of Keharas *breathing*. Not the dust-cycle, not the slow exhalation and reincorporation of the surface, but something faster, something more immediate, something that happens on a scale of minutes rather than millennia. It is the plateau's short-term metabolism, its quick-time adjustment, the way it maintains its temperature and its shape and its equilibrium in the face of the small perturbations that the city's slow absorption creates.

Listen to it. It is the most alive sound in Keharas. It is the sound of a body that is *doing something*, that is not merely existing but *acting*, adjusting, responding. It is the sound of a place that is not dead, not dormant, not finished, but *in process*, still becoming, still working, still engaged in the long slow task of making the city part of itself.

The tick is the plateau's voice. It says: *I am here. I am still here. I am still working.*

It says: *Not yet. Not yet. Soon.*

## VI. The Light

The fluorescent tube on the third floor, the one that still flickers, irregularly, drawing from a source no one has located — it is not a machine malfunctioning. It is an *organism*.

Not a biological organism. A *systemic* one. The electrical patches in Keharas — the islands of function in the sea of dormancy, the circuits that still carry current, the walls that vibrate faintly with a charge that has no identifiable source — are not remnants of a power grid. They *are* a power grid. They are the plateau's nervous system, or the city's, or the nervous system of the thing that the plateau and the city are becoming as they merge.

The current is not electricity in the conventional sense. It is something that behaves like electricity — it flows, it powers, it produces light — but it is generated by the plateau itself, by the thermal differential between the surface and the deep stone, by the pressure of the absorption process, by the slow grinding of building against ground that produces, at the molecular level, a charge. The plateau is a generator. The city is a circuit. The fluorescent tube is a *sensory organ*, a point where the system becomes visible, where the plateau's internal process manifests as light.

It flickers irregularly because the plateau's process is irregular. The absorption is faster in some places, slower in others. The thermal differential fluctuates. The pressure changes. The system is not stable — it is *dynamic*, alive in the way that a weather system is alive, producing patterns that are not random but not predictable, that have the quality of *behavior* rather than *function*.

The light the tube casts is blue-white and ugly, and it illuminates a room full of identical desks, each shedding its slow dust, each settling by fractions of a millimeter into the floor beneath it. The light is the plateau *looking at the city*. The light is the plateau *seeing what it is absorbing*. The light is the plateau's attention, focused on this one room, this one moment, this one small corner of the long slow process of making everything part of itself.

The tube will not last forever. Nothing in Keharas lasts forever, because everything in Keharas is being absorbed, and the tube is no exception. But it will last a long time. And while it lasts, it will flicker, and the flicker will be irregular, and the irregularity will be the signature of a system that is not broken but *alive*, not malfunctioning but *responding*, not dying but *continuing*, in the dark, at altitude, in silence, at the end of all erosion.

## VII. The Absent

The most important inhabitant of Keharas is the one that is not here.

The city was built. The machinery was installed. The desks were occupied. The fluorescent tubes were lit — all of them, once, not just the one on the third floor. The conveyor mechanisms led somewhere. The control panels had indicators that were not dark. The smokestacks discharged something. The wide doors opened and closed. The reinforced walls enclosed a purpose.

Someone did this. Something did this. A population — of workers, of builders, of operators, of *inhabitants* — lived in Keharas, or worked in Keharas, or passed through Keharas, and they are gone now, so completely gone that their absence is not a presence but a *quality of the space itself*, the way a room that has been emptied of furniture still reads as a room that once held furniture, the proportions and the doorways and the windows all shaped by a need that is no longer being met.

They are not dead. Death leaves traces — bones, residue, the chemical signature of decomposition. Keharas does not permit decomposition. Nothing decays here. If the inhabitants died, they would still be here, in some form, as dust or as stone or as a faint impression in the fused surface of the plateau. They are not here. They *left*. Or they were *absorbed*. Or they *became something else* — something so thoroughly incorporated into the plateau's substance that the distinction between inhabitant and habitat has ceased to exist.

Perhaps they were made of the same material as the plateau. Perhaps they were lithic beings, slow and warm and patient, who built the city not as shelter but as *expression* — a shape they pressed into the surface of their world the way a hand presses into clay, a gesture that was not meant to last but was meant to *be*, for a time, before the surface relaxed and the gesture smoothed away. Perhaps the city is not a city but a *fossil* — the preserved impression of a civilization that was itself made of stone, that lived at the speed of stone, that built and occupied and abandoned its structures across spans of time that make the plateau's absorption look hasty.

Perhaps the inhabitants are still here, in the walls, in the floors, in the fused machinery and the white dust and the warm stone. Perhaps they *are* the dust. Perhaps they *are* the plateau. Perhaps the absorption is not the plateau consuming the city but the city *returning* to what it was always made of, the inhabitants dissolving back into the substance they were carved from, the way a statue dissolves back into the quarry, the way a wave dissolves back into the sea.

The absent are the most populous inhabitants of Keharas. They are everywhere. They are everything. They are the reason the city exists and the reason the city is disappearing, and they are not gone — they are *distributed*, spread so thin across the body of the plateau that they are indistinguishable from it, present in every molecule, absent from every surface, the most complete inhabitants of a place that has finished becoming and is now simply *being*, in the dark, at altitude, in silence, at the end of all erosion, holding their shape the way a held breath holds a note — not forever, but for now, and now is very long, and nothing is changing, and everything is changing so slowly that the difference has ceased to matter.

## VIII. You

You are here now. Standing in the central avenue. Your feet are warm on the stone. The stone is warm beneath your feet. The warmth goes down and down.

You are breathing. The plateau is not breathing — the plateau does not breathe, the plateau *is* breath, the held breath, the long exhalation that is the dust-cycle, the slow incorporation of everything into itself. You are breathing *into* the plateau. Your exhalations are moisture, are warmth, are organic molecules, are *foreign material*, and the plateau is receiving them the way it receives everything: patiently, without resistance, without rejection, with the slow certainty of a system that has already decided to make you part of itself.

You will not be absorbed quickly. You are small, and you are brief, and the plateau's timescale is not your timescale. But you are already being incorporated. The warmth of the stone is entering your feet. The dust is settling on your skin. The silence is entering your ears and replacing the sounds you came with. The dim light is entering your eyes and replacing the colors you remember. You are becoming a feature of Keharas the way the buildings are becoming features of the plateau — slowly, molecule by molecule, with a patience that is not yours but that you are, for now, participating in.

You are the newest inhabitant of Keharas. You are the most temporary. You are the most alive.

The plateau does not mind. The plateau does not welcome. The plateau *receives*. It has always received. It received the builders and the workers and the track-makers and the small pale things on the eastern edge. It received the city and the machinery and the dust and the light. It is receiving you now, in the central avenue, in the near-darkness, on the warm stone, at the end of all erosion.

Stay as long as you like. The plateau is not in a hurry.

The plateau is never in a hurry.

The plateau is *here*.
