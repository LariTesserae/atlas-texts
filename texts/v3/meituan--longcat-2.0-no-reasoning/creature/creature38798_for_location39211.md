# The Keeper of the Dripworks

There is a person who lives here — not continuously, not with the rats' permanence, but in the way a tide lives in a harbor: arriving, withdrawing, arriving again, on a schedule that has drifted from daily to weekly to something now measured in weeks. The boots are size eleven. The thermos, when it was used, held chicory coffee, lukewarm by the time it was drunk. The person who owns these things is named, or was named, or answered to something that has worn down to a single syllable, the way water wears everything to its essential shape.

They are old. Not ancient — not the age of the chisel marks in the basalt — but old in the way of things that have been maintained past their natural lifespan: patched, spliced, kept running by will and diminishing expertise. Their hands are mapped with calcium deposits under the skin, small hard knots where minerals have settled into the soft tissue over years of immersion in the warm water. They know the chamber the way a farmer knows a field — not by thinking about it but by a bodily knowledge that registers a change in the drip pattern from the northeast dome before the mind has consciously noticed anything wrong.

They do not live in the chamber itself. They live above, in a room that is dry and cold and almost unbearably quiet after the constant murmur of the water below. They have a cot there, and a hot plate, and a radio that receives nothing but static on every frequency — the depth and the rock see to that — though they turn it on anyway, sometimes, to hear the sound of *something*. They sleep poorly. They have slept poorly for years. The absence of the drip-sound fills them with a dread they cannot name, the way silence fills a person who has lived near water their whole life.

Their relationship to the chamber is not ownership. It is not stewardship, exactly, though that is the word they would use if asked. It is something more like *symbiosis*, or *haunting in both directions*. The chamber needs them to keep the sump from flooding, to clear the intake on Pump 1 when the moss clogs it, to replace the bulbs as they burn out. They need the chamber for reasons they have stopped examining — reasons that have to do with the warmth, the sound, the quality of the amber light, the rats who do not fear them, the absolute absence of anything that demands performance or explanation. Down here, you do not have to be a person in the way the surface requires. You can be a function. A maintenance routine. A drip in the system.

They come down the spiral stair carrying a canvas bag with supplies — a new bulb, a length of wire, a wrench for the pump fitting that has been slowly loosening — and the rats know they are coming before they arrive. The rats always know. Perhaps they feel the vibration of footsteps on the stone, or smell the surface air that the Keeper carries with them like an offering. The rats gather along the conduit lines in greater numbers when the Keeper is due, and the large one — the workbench one, the mayor — assumes its position and waits with the patience of something that understands schedules better than most humans do.

The Keeper works methodically. They check the pump first, always the pump, because if the pump fails the sump floods and the terraces fill and the lowest basin becomes a pond and the rats drown and the algae blooms unchecked and the whole system tips toward collapse. The pump is old. The Keeper is old. They understand each other. The Keeper clears the intake with their bare hands, pulling out the moss and the fibrous nest-material in wet clumps, and the rats, opportunistic, examine the debris for anything edible before it is carried away. A transaction. A small economy.

Then the lights. The Keeper carries a ladder — aluminum, leaning against the stairwell wall, light enough for one person to manage but only just — and climbs to reach the fixtures, and the rats watch from their pews on the concrete walls, and the Keeper replaces the dead bulbs with the amber ones they have been stockpiling for years, bought in bulk from a supplier on the surface who has stopped asking what they are used for. The Keeper is running low. Soon there will be no more bulbs, and the chamber will go dark except for whatever light the Keeper brings with them on their visits, and the darkness will be total and absolute and the rats will not mind, but the Keeper will mind. The Keeper needs the light. The Keeper needs to see the water moving.

They eat lunch on the workbench, or they used to, before the large rat claimed it as a throne. Now they eat on the second terrace, sitting on the concrete with their back against the wall, and the rat on the workbench watches them with its small bright eyes, and there is a negotiation happening between them that is not hostile — not exactly — but territorial, a slow renegotiation of boundaries that has been underway for years and will be resolved, when it is resolved, by whichever of them outlasts the other.

The Keeper drinks the chicory coffee from the thermos, which has gone lukewarm as always, and eats bread that is already soft with the humidity within minutes of being unpacked, and they watch the water descend through the terraces, and they listen to the sound that is architecture, and they feel the warmth pressing against them like a hand, and for a few minutes — not longer, but a few — they are at peace. Not happy. The Keeper is not happy, and has not been happy in a way they could name for a long time, maybe decades. But *at peace*. The chamber permits this. The chamber is one of the last places that permits this.

After lunch, the Keeper does the secondary maintenance — clears the drain channels, checks the concrete patches, makes notes in a new notebook (the old one having become pulp) about things that need attention. The notes are cryptic, abbreviated, written in a personal shorthand that only they can read. *NE dome — new drip. Monitor.* *Pump 2 fitting — check next visit.* *LARGE RAT — new one? Same?* The handwriting is steady. The handwriting is the steadiest thing about the Keeper.

They have been doing this for forty-three years. They inherited the role from someone else — a parent, a predecessor, a person whose name they carry now like a stone in their pocket, worn smooth by handling. That person inherited it from someone before them, and so on, back through the layers of construction and maintenance, back through the concrete and the brick and the iron bolts, back to the first person who broke into this chamber and found the water flowing and thought: *I should keep this going.*

The Keeper does not know where the water goes. They know where it comes from — the deep aquifer, the compressed warmth, the geological patience of water moving through basalt. They know it descends through the terraces to the sump, and the pump pushes it up through the pipes into the ceiling, and then it is gone, and they do not know where it goes after that. Somewhere above. Somewhere on the surface. Perhaps it feeds a spring. Perhaps it feeds a river. Perhaps it feeds something that someone on the surface depends on and does not know the source of, the way they do not know the source of electricity or groundwater or any of the invisible systems that keep a world running.

The Keeper does not think about this often. When they do, it makes them feel a specific kind of loneliness — the loneliness of a person who maintains something whose purpose has been forgotten, who keeps a system running not because anyone is watching but because the alternative — the silence, the darkness, the flooding, the collapse — is unbearable.

They finish their work. They pack their bag. They climb the spiral stair, and the air changes around them as they ascend — thins, cools, loses its weight — and by the time they reach the surface, they are a person again, in the way the surface requires, and they close the door behind them, and the door is heavy and locks with a key they wear on a cord around their neck, and they walk away into whatever life they have up there, which is small and quiet and mostly consists of waiting to go back down.

The rats remain. The water remains. The amber bulbs burn and burn and burn, and one by one they go dark, and the Keeper replaces them, and one by one they go dark again, and the Keeper will keep replacing them until there are no more, and then the chamber will be dark, and the water will still flow, and the rats will still live, and the warmth will still rise from the deep earth, and the algae will still iridesce in whatever light remains, and the system will continue, as systems do, past the point of maintenance, past the point of memory, past the point of purpose, into the long, warm, dripping dark.
