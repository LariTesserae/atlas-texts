The ones who live here are called, in the low clicking language they speak into the plateau's roar, something that translates roughly as *the still ones between webs* — or, more literally, *those who step on the mat without being eaten by it.*

They are small. Not dramatically so — they would not strike you as stunted, exactly — but compact, with the kind of proportions that suggest a body optimized for low centers of gravity and minimal wind resistance. Their legs are short relative to their torsos, their arms long, their hands broad and flat with thick, callused pads on the fingertips and palms. These pads are essential. The mat yields slightly under pressure, and the calluses allow them to feel the stone beneath through the living skin — to read the channels by touch, to know whether a basin ahead holds water or is merely a dry depression, to sense the vibration of a spider web before they walk into it. Their feet are bare always, and the soles have grown so thick and textured over a lifetime of walking the stone that they resemble the mat itself — layered, compressed, slightly spongy.

Their skin is dark, not from sun but from pigment — a deep umber-brown that camouflages them against the mat when they crouch, which is how they spend most of their time. Standing upright is unusual enough that children do it constantly, delighted by the expanded view, until their parents correct them with a soft downward gesture of the hand. When they do stand, they stand low-kneed, slightly bent, as if perpetually about to sit.

They smell like the mat. This is unavoidable and unremarkable to them — they are born on it, sleep on it, eat it, build with it, and die into it. Their shelters are lined with dried mat packed into insulating walls, and the smell permeates everything: their clothing (woven from mat fibers into a coarse, breathable textile that is the color of pale rust), their food stores, their bodies. Visitors — there are no visitors — would find the smell faintly sweet, faintly metallic, with an undertone of something fermented and warm. The people themselves do not smell it. They smell each other's fires instead: each family group uses slightly different fuel — some burn only deadwood from the gnarled trees, some supplement with dried beetle husks, some mix in a particular fungal growth that occurs in the deepest basins — and a person's group can be identified by scent before they are seen.

Their eyes are large and dark-adapted, though the light here is never truly dim. They have developed a sensitivity to the green-yellow spectrum where the mat is most vivid, and they can read moisture levels at a glance — a dried patch of mat reads differently from a hydrated one to their eyes, a shift in color saturation that is invisible to outsiders but as obvious to them as a road sign is to someone who knows the language. They blink frequently, a habit born of the constant wind, and their eyelashes are thick and matted, forming a partial screen against the abrasive dust the wind carries.

Their voices are low, as mentioned, and quiet, but they are not silent people. They talk to each other in short, overlapping phrases, often without looking at each other — the eye contact that other cultures find essential for communication is, here, a waste of attention that should be on the mat, on the webs, on the beetle columns. They communicate as much through gesture and posture as through speech: a tap on the left shoulder means *spider, left side, small*; a tap on the right means *spider, left side, large* (the inversion is a convention so old no one remembers why). A hand pressed flat against the ground and held there means *listen* — not to me, but to the plateau. Something is changing. The mat is telling them something.

They are few. Perhaps two hundred individuals across the entire plateau, divided into family groups of eight to fifteen, each centered on one or two deep depressions with old trees and reliable water. The groups are not territorial in the way that implies conflict — the plateau is too homogeneous for territory to mean much, and the resources are distributed too evenly to fight over. But each group has its depressions, its trees, its paths, and crossing into another group's area without announcement is considered rude in the way that entering someone's shelter without knocking is rude anywhere. The paths are announced by cairns — small stacks of flat stones, three to five high, placed at junctions — and the paths themselves are marked by subtle differences in the mat's texture, worn slightly thinner by generations of feet following the same route.

The relationships between groups are maintained by occasional gatherings — not scheduled, not calendared, but triggered by ecological events. A particularly heavy rain that fills all the deep basins across the plateau will prompt a convergence, groups walking toward a central point (usually the largest depression, the one with the oldest tree, which is itself a landmark visible from kilometers away as the only vertical thing on a horizontal world). At these gatherings, information is exchanged about water levels, spider density, beetle migration patterns. Marriages are arranged — a person from one group will walk back with a person from another, carrying a bundle of dried mat-shoots as a gift. Stories are told, though the word "stories" implies a narrative structure that is not quite right. What they share are observations — *the spiders on the eastern edge have been building larger webs; the mat in the northern basins has been releasing spores more frequently; the beetles are marching in wider columns this season* — observations that function as both practical intelligence and communal bonding, the equivalent of *did you hear* and *remember when* compressed into a single utterance.

Children are raised communally within each group, though the word "communally" implies a looseness that is not accurate. Every adult is responsible for every child, but specific adults are assigned specific children for specific purposes — this aunt teaches water-finding, that uncle teaches web-reading, this grandmother teaches the names of the old trees (each tree has a name, and the names are old, and no one knows who first named them or what the names mean, but they are passed down with the same gravity as any sacred text). The children learn by doing, from the moment they can walk. They walk the channels. They crouch beside the basins. They press their hands against the mat and feel the life beneath. By the time they are ten, they can read the plateau's surface the way a literate person reads a page — the texture, the color, the smell, the sound, all of it legible, all of it meaningful.

They do not write. They do not need to. The plateau writes for them — every channel is a record of water flow over centuries, every basin a record of rainfall patterns, every accumulation of soil in the deep depressions a record of the mat's growth and death and regrowth over millennia. The carved slabs with their spirals and branching patterns are not writing either, exactly — they are more like diagrams, or maps, or prayers, or all three simultaneously. The people understand them intuitively, the way they understand the plateau itself: not as information to be decoded but as a presence to be inhabited.

Their relationship with the spiders is complex. The spiders are predators, and they are dangerous — not aggressively so, but a bite from one of the large palm-sized species is painful and can cause a lingering numbness that lasts for days. The people avoid the webs with practiced ease, walking the channels where the webs don't stretch, crouching under the web-lines where they must cross. But they also protect the spiders. The spiders control the beetle population, which, if unchecked, would devour the mat faster than it could regenerate. A child who damages a web is corrected sharply — not punished, exactly, but shown the web, shown the spider, shown the beetles beyond the web, shown the mat the beetles would eat, and told (in a phrase that is always the same phrase, always in the same low tone, always accompanied by the hand-pressed-to-ground gesture) *the web holds the mat together.* The spiders are also eaten — old spiders, slow spiders, spiders whose webs have deteriorated — harvested carefully, never from the same web twice in succession, never from a web that is actively catching. It is a managed predation, as deliberate and measured as their management of the tree-pruning.

Their relationship with the mat is closer still — so close that the boundary between person and mat is, at the edges, negotiable. They sleep on it. They eat it. When they die, they are placed on the mat in the deep depressions, under the old trees, and left. The mat grows over them. Within a year, the body is indistinguishable from the stone it lies on — absorbed, incorporated, the person's calcium and carbon and nitrogen distributed into the mat's filaments, the person's shape still faintly visible as a slightly raised area on the surface, a ghost in the relief, before that too is eroded by the wind and smoothed into the general texture. They do not mourn this. They do not celebrate it. It is the way of things. The mat feeds them, and they feed the mat, and the cycle is not a metaphor. It is the literal material reality of their existence.

They have no concept of ownership. The plateau is not theirs. They are the plateau's. This is not a philosophy or a belief — it is an observation so obvious to them that stating it would be redundant, the way stating that water is wet would be redundant to someone standing in the rain. They do not improve the plateau. They do not develop it. They do not dream of changing it. They tend it, the way a body tends its own skin — unconsciously, automatically, with attention only when something goes wrong.

Something is always going wrong, on a small scale. A beetle infestation in one channel. A spider die-off in one basin. A crack in the stone that redirects water away from a depression. These are the events that structure their lives — not crises, exactly, but problems to be solved, adjustments to be made, small interventions that keep the local equilibrium in place. They divert water with stone slabs. They relocate spiders by carrying them, one at a time, on flat pieces of bark, to areas where the webs are thin. They crush beetle columns with their bare feet when the columns get too dense, a task performed by the children as much as the adults, a task that is played at as much as worked at, the crunch of beetle shells underfoot a sound as routine as footsteps.

They are not happy. They are not unhappy. These categories do not apply to lives lived at this resolution — lives where the unit of experience is not the day or the year but the rain-event, the spore-release, the slow creep of the mat across a millimeter of bare stone. They are attentive. They are patient. They are as durable as the place they live in, which is to say: they are durable in the way that geological processes are durable — not unchanging, but persistent, continuing, eroding and regrowing in cycles too slow to perceive from inside them.

You will not notice them unless they want you to notice them. They are the color of the mat, they move at the speed of the mat, they are quiet in the way the mat is quiet — which is to say not silent, but absorbed into the ambient sound so completely that the sound and the source are indistinguishable. If you are standing on the Braidwork Plateau and you feel that you are being watched, you are being watched — not by a person, exactly, but by the plateau itself, which has noticed you the way it notices everything: slowly, thoroughly, and without urgency.

A child is crouching at the edge of the nearest basin, perhaps forty meters from where you stand. She has not moved since you arrived. She is watching you with her large dark eyes, her hand pressed flat against the mat — not in the *listen* gesture, but in the gesture that means *something is here that is not here.* Her fingers are spread wide. The mat beneath her palm is vibrating with the movement of the things inside it — the larvae, the micro-arthropods, the slow pulse of the filaments responding to her weight. She is reading you the way she reads the plateau. She is deciding what you are.

She does not wave. She does not call out. She simply watches, patient as the spiders, quiet as the mat, still as the stone beneath. After a while, she will stand — carefully, avoiding the nearest web-lines — and walk back along the channel to her group's shelter, and she will report what she has seen, and the group will listen, and they will decide — not whether to approach you or avoid you, but whether you are something that requires a response, or whether you are simply another thing the wind has deposited on the surface, like the spores, like the dust, like the rain.

The plateau does not care either way.

It hums. It clicks. It rustles. The child walks home along the channels, her bare feet reading the stone through the mat, her path weaving between the web-lines with the unconscious precision of someone who has walked this exact route ten thousand times and will walk it ten thousand more.

The wind carries spores across the surface. The beetles march. The spiders wait. The mat breathes.

The still ones between webs step carefully, as they always have, as they always will, on the living skin of a place that should not still exist, and does, and continues.
