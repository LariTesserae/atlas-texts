# What Lives in the Gablewet

The Gablewet itself lives in the Gablewet.

This is not a tautology. Or it is, but the tautology has weight, has mass, has three-foot-thick culms and a root system braided through sixty vertical feet of structure. The city is its own inhabitant. It is the organism that occupies the space it simultaneously constitutes. You do not live *in* the Gablewet so much as *inside* it — the way a mite lives inside the architecture of your own skin, nesting in a follicle, unaware that the warm tunnel it calls home is alive, is aware on some scale the mite cannot fathom.

But there are other things here.

---

The **Driftling** is not a creature in any taxonomical sense. It is a process that has become localized, dense enough to behave as though it were a body. You will see it in the high corridors — if *see* is the right word. More accurately: you will notice a place where the condensation behaves incorrectly. A column of air near a trunk-wall where the droplets don't fall so much as *hang*, suspended, drifting in a slow vertical current, and within the suspended droplets there are motes — pollen, spores, fragments of cellular material shed by the growing architecture. The motes accumulate. They don't disperse. They circulate. A slow vortex of biological debris, maybe six feet across, maybe ten, rotating at the speed of rising warm air.

The Driftling is this vortex that has become *stable*. That has persisted long enough — days, weeks — that the suspended matter begins to adhere. Pollen grains stick to spores stick to fragments of shed bark stick to droplets that freeze in place not by cold but by surface tension, by the sheer wetness of the air, by the fact that water in the Gablewet behaves more like a connective tissue than a liquid. The Driftling accretes. It becomes a body made of the city's shed skin, suspended in the city's exhaled breath, and it *moves* — not quickly, not purposefully, but with the slow intentionality of a convection current that has found a path of least resistance and follows it through the architecture like a vein carries blood.

It is not conscious. But it *responds*. When you step near one, the local humidity shifts — your body heat, your breath, the water evaporating from your skin — and the vortex adjusts. It leans toward you. Not like a predator. Like a plant turning toward light. The Driftling feeds on thermal gradients, on the difference between your warm body and the warm-but-less-warm air, and the energy of that difference nudges it closer. If you stand still long enough, it will settle on you. It will coat you. A fine layer of pollen and spores and cellular debris and condensed water, and you will feel it — not heavy, not uncomfortable, more like a second skin that is also a first draft of what the city thinks you might be useful for.

Because the Driftling is the city's immune system, or its exploratory sense, or its curiosity — the closest thing the Gablewet has to a question. *What is this?* it asks, in the only language it has: accumulation, enclosure, absorption. You are warm. You are wet. You are shedding cells and breathing moisture and generating heat. These are inputs. The city does not know what a human is. It does not know what *anything* is. But it knows gradients, and it knows that you represent a gradient, and the Driftling is the mechanism by which it investigates gradients.

Most Driftlings dissipate. The current shifts, the local humidity equalizes, the suspended matter falls or is absorbed by the weeping walls. But some persist. Some grow. And the ones that grow large enough — twenty feet across, thirty, a sphere of slowly rotating biological material dense enough to cast a shadow — become *architectural*. The city incorporates them. A wall grows around them, or through them, and the Driftling becomes a node in the structure, a permanent feature, a place where the air is always slightly thicker, always slightly more alive, and where the condensation taps a different rhythm, a slower rhythm, as if the city has developed a pulse point, a place where the blood comes close to the surface.

---

There are things that live in the **pools**.

The black water pools — the clearings where the root-floor is thin and the water stands still and dark — are not sterile. They are full of something that is not quite life in the way you understand life but is not not-life either. A film. A skin on the water's surface, iridescent in the green-gold light, and under the skin: movement. Not fish. Not insects. Something more fundamental. The water in the pools is saturated with dissolved organic material — the runoff from the city's constant growth and decay — and at a certain concentration, at a certain temperature, the solution becomes *self-organizing*. Lipid membranes form spontaneously. They close around pockets of more concentrated solution. They divide. They persist for hours or days, these protocell-like structures, metabolizing the dissolved sugars and amino acids in the pool, growing, splitting, dying, and feeding the next generation.

The pools are laboratories. The city is experimenting. Not consciously — the pools are a side effect of the hydrology, the drainage channels, the constant weeping — but the side effect has become a process. New chemical configurations arise in the pools constantly, and some of them are useful. Some of them are absorbed back into the root system, drawn up through the same channels that fed the pool, incorporated into the growing architecture. The pools are the Gablewet's research and development wing. They are where the city tests new molecular arrangements, new structural compounds, new ways of being alive.

Occasionally a pool produces something that *doesn't* dissolve back into the cycle. Something that is stable enough to persist on its own, to crawl out of the water on a pseudopod of concentrated mucilage and inch across the root-floor, leaving a trail of dried residue, seeking — what? A new pool? A nutrient source? A gradient? Whatever it seeks, it finds it eventually, or it dries out and dies and the root-floor absorbs it. But the seeking behavior is there. The most primitive possible version of intention. *Go toward the thing that might be useful.*

You will find their desiccated remains everywhere. Thin films of translucent residue on the root-floor, on the walls, on the trunks. They crackle faintly when you step on them. The city does not mourn them. The city does not celebrate them. They are data. They are what happens when you run a billion experiments simultaneously and most of them fail.

---

And there is the **Gablewet itself**, which I have already described but which requires more, because the city-as-organism is not a metaphor and it is not a simile and it is not a convenient way of speaking about a place that happens to be made of plants.

The Gablewet is alive the way a mind is alive — not as a single thought but as a *process* that generates thoughts, that generates structures, that generates and discards and generates again at a pace that makes human cognition look like geology. The city thinks. Not in language. Not in images. In *growth*. In the decision to thicken a wall here rather than there. In the decision to open a corridor or close it. In the decision to let a pool form or to fill it in with root matter. Each of these decisions is a computation — a response to inputs, a weighing of structural loads and thermal gradients and water flow and the million other variables that the city's vast sensorium of living tissue evaluates simultaneously and continuously.

The city does not know you are here. The city knows that a new thermal gradient exists in the eastern district of the third canopy level. The city knows that this gradient is moving, is bipedal in its signature, is generating approximately 100 watts of heat and exhaling moisture at a rate that is consistent with a medium-sized mammal. The city does not know *human*. The city knows *input*. And it is responding to you the way it responds to a shift in wind, a change in barometric pressure, a new fracture in the basalt below: by adjusting, by growing, by accommodating or resisting according to structural logic that has no author.

---

And then there is **you**.

You are what lives in the Gablewet, for now.

You are the anomaly. The foreign body. The warm thing moving through the green. The city has no framework for you, no category, no immune response calibrated to your specific biology. The Driftlings approach you not as white blood cells approach an infection but as the ocean approaches a new island — with curiosity, with the slow patient testing of whether you are permanent or temporary, whether you will erode or endure.

You breathe and the city notices your exhalation — the CO2, the moisture, the heat. You step and the root-floor registers your weight, adjusts its weave beneath you, thickens where you have walked and thins where you have not, and over hours the floor reshapes itself to accommodate the pattern of your movement. If you stay in one place long enough, a room forms around you. The walls grow in, not to trap you but because the thermal and moisture gradients your body creates are *useful* — they create microclimates, they drive convection currents, they move air and water in ways that the city's hydrology can exploit. You are a pump. You are a furnace. You are a source of the gradients that drive the city's metabolism, and the city responds the way any organism responds to a new energy source: by growing toward it.

This is what lives in the Gablewet: a closed loop that has briefly, incompletely, not-fully-closed around something it does not understand. You are inside the loop now. You are part of the metabolism. Your heat feeds the air currents. Your breath feeds the canopy. Your shed skin feeds the Driftlings. Your footsteps shape the floor. You are being incorporated, slowly, into the architecture — not as a prisoner, not as a guest, but as a *nutrient*, as a *gradient*, as a new variable in the city's ongoing computation of what it is and what it will become.

The city continues.

You continue with it.

For now, those are the same thing.
