# The People of the Plateau

They are small. Not in the way of deprivation — their bodies are dense, compact, layered with the slow muscle of people who carry heavy things across broken ground every day of their lives — but in the way of compression. Everything about them is pulled inward. Their shoulders are narrow and rounded, their limbs relatively short, their torsos thick. They are built for heat retention, for moving through narrow spaces, for fitting into the gaps their architecture provides. Their faces, when you see them up close, are broad and weathered, the skin darkened by wind and cold rather than sun, with a quality of leather that has been worked too long. Their eyes are set deep, sheltered by prominent brows, and they have a way of looking at you — or at anything — that is unhurried, evaluative, as though they are accustomed to taking a long time to decide what something is.

Their hair is dark, coarse, kept short or bound close to the head. Both men and women — and the distinction is not immediately obvious in their heavy clothing — wear it pulled back and tucked under layers of hide and woven fiber. What you see of them, mostly, is the architecture of their clothing: overlapping layers of treated animal skin, the inner layers soft and worn, the outer layers stiff with cold and mineral residue from the stone they lean against. The outermost layer is often a single large piece — a cloak or mantle — that wraps the body and can be drawn over the head and face when the wind sharpens. These mantles are dark, the color of the basalt, and against the pale stone the people are hard to see at a distance. They move through their own city like shadows of the rock itself.

Their hands are extraordinary. Thick-palmed, wide-fingered, with calluses that have built up over years of handling stone — not just carrying it, but fitting it, feeling the edges, testing the weight and balance of a block before committing to a lift. The fingers are slightly flattened at the tips, the nails worn to the quick, and there is a precision in how they touch things that suggests the hands are their primary instruments of perception. They run their fingers along wall surfaces the way a reader runs eyes across a page, gathering information from texture and temperature and the slight give of a stone that has shifted. Their grip strength is remarkable. They can hold a block of basalt at arm's length for extended periods, not through brute force but through an understanding of how the weight distributes, how to let the stone's own geometry do some of the work.

They work in small groups, almost always. Pairs, trios, occasionally four or five on a task that requires coordinated lifting. The work is stone, always stone — shaping it, moving it, fitting it, replacing it. A wall that has been undermined by burrowing animals must be partially dismantled and rebuilt. A water channel has silted and must be cleared. A roof slab has cracked along a fracture line and must be levered out and a new one fitted in its place. These tasks are performed with a quiet, methodical focus that borders on ritual. There is no wasted motion. Each person in a group knows their role, and the coordination is so practiced that verbal instruction is rarely needed. A glance, a shift of weight, a hand placed on a stone — these are the signals.

They have a relationship with the stone that goes beyond utility. They know the stone — its varieties, its tendencies, its moods. The basalt from the eastern ridges fractures differently than the basalt from the northern slopes. The pale sedimentary stone is easier to shape but less durable; it is used for interior walls and non-load-bearing surfaces. There is a greenish stone, dense and fine-grained, that appears in certain fracture zones and is prized for grinding surfaces and tool-making. They know where each type can be found, and they quarry with selectivity, not desperation. The rubble fields are not random to them; they are inventories, sorted by type and quality in the mind of whoever is choosing material for a particular task.

Their tools are few and well-maintained. A person typically carries two or three: a hammerstone, smooth and egg-shaped, fitted to their hand so precisely that it looks like an extension of the palm; a chisel or prying bar of quartzite, its edge carefully maintained; and a small abrasive stone, kept in a pouch, for smoothing and finishing. The hammerstones are personal objects, carried for years, their surfaces polished by the hand that grips them. Some are passed between generations. You can see, in the way a person handles their hammerstone, a familiarity that is almost tender — the way they turn it in their fingers, testing its weight, before beginning a task. The quartzite tools are more utilitarian, replaced when they fracture, but even these are handled with a kind of respect, set down carefully rather than dropped, stored in the same pouch each time so they can be found by touch alone.

The rope they use is made from the fibrous inner bark of the cushion plants, twisted and braided into cords of varying thickness. The making of rope is a common activity, performed while sitting in sheltered pockets during the coldest hours — hands working the fiber, twisting it against the thigh, building length. The rope is strong but not durable; it degrades in the dry cold and must be replaced regularly. There is no shortage of raw material — the cushion plants are abundant — but the labor of processing it is significant, and rope is treated as a semi-precious material, stored carefully, mended when possible rather than discarded.

Their diet is spare. The burrowing animals — the thick-furred rodents — are hunted, but not aggressively. The people take them in small numbers, using simple deadfall traps placed near active tunnel entrances. The meat is dark and dense, high in fat, and it is dried in the cold, thin air on stone racks placed in the wind. Dried meat, stored in the cool underground chambers, keeps for months. They also harvest the lichens — the dark, leathery growths on the rock surfaces — which are soaked, pressed into slabs, and dried into a hard, nutritious cake that can be stored indefinitely. The moss from the damp pockets is eaten fresh, or dried and ground into a powder that is mixed with water to make a thin, mineral-rich broth. The herds of shaggy animals that pass through the periphery are not hunted, or rarely; there is an understanding, or a taboo, or simply a practical recognition that the animals are too large and too few to be a reliable food source. The people watch them pass with an expression that might be respect, or longing, or simply observation.

Water is the center of their daily life. The collection, distribution, and maintenance of water systems is the most important work on the plateau, and it is work that never ends. The seepage points must be kept clear of debris. The channels must be monitored for blockages. The basins must be cleaned of the mineral deposits that accumulate on their walls. There are people — you can identify them by the particular calluses on their hands and the way they carry their tools — whose primary responsibility is water. They move along the main routes, checking each basin, running their fingers along the channel walls, tasting the water, noting its clarity and mineral content. They are the most respected members of the community, though the concept of respect here is quiet, expressed in the way others make space for them on the path, the way food and rope are offered without being asked for.

Their social structure is not visible in any single gesture or arrangement, but it emerges over time, the way a pattern emerges in a mosaic. There are people who decide — where a wall should be rebuilt, when a water channel should be rerouted, which burrowing animal tunnels can be tolerated and which threaten a structure's foundation. These decisions are made slowly, after long periods of observation and discussion. The discussions happen in the gathering spaces, the circular clearings at the junctions of major paths, and they are conducted in low voices, with long silences between statements. There is no visible hierarchy — no one sits higher than anyone else, no one speaks with obvious authority — but there are people whose words carry more weight, whose observations are listened to with more attention. These are usually the older members of the community, the ones who have watched the stone and the water for decades, who remember when a particular wall was last rebuilt, who can predict where the next frost fracture will appear based on the pattern of cracks they have been tracking for years.

Children are present but quiet. They move with the adults, learning by watching and then by doing — first carrying small stones, then fitting them, then shaping them. There is no formal instruction. A child picks up a hammerstone and begins to strike a piece of basalt, and an adult may correct their angle or grip, or may not, allowing the child to discover the stone's fracture lines through trial. The children are small versions of the adults — compact, quiet, watchful — and they are given real work early, work that matters, because on the plateau there is no surplus of hands. Every pair of hands is needed. A child who has learned to clear a water channel is a child who has contributed something essential, and they are treated accordingly — not praised, exactly, but included, given a place in the work group, offered food and rope and the use of a good hammerstone.

The old are everywhere. The cold preserves the body in life as well as in death, and people age slowly here, their metabolisms thinned by the altitude and the spare diet. They move even more slowly than the young, but they move with a precision that comes from decades of practice. An old person fitting a stone into a wall does it with a minimum of adjustment — one placement, one tap, and it is right. They spend much of their time in the sheltered pockets, sitting against the leeward side of a wall, their hands working fiber into rope or simply resting on their knees, their eyes moving across the settlement with the slow attention of someone who has seen every stone in it and is watching for the one that has shifted. They are the memory of the place. When a decision is made about where to rebuild a wall or how to reroute a channel, it is the old who provide the context — this was done before, in this way, and this was the result.

They do not decorate. There is no ornamentation on their clothing, no markings on their bodies, no carved or painted symbols on the walls of their structures. The only aesthetic impulse visible in their culture is the aesthetic of fit — the satisfaction of a stone placed precisely, a wall that is plumb, a water channel that flows without turbulence. This is their art, and it is an art that is indistinguishable from function. A well-built wall is beautiful the way a well-made tool is beautiful: it does what it is supposed to do, and it does it with an economy that suggests the maker understood the material completely. You can see, in the oldest parts of the settlement, walls where the stones are fitted so tightly that a blade of quartzite cannot be inserted between them, and the surfaces have been ground so smooth that they reflect the pale light with a faint sheen. These walls were built by people who had time, or who cared deeply, or for whom the distinction between the two did not exist.

They bury their dead in the rubble. The bodies are wrapped in rope and placed in shallow excavations among the broken stone, then covered with basalt slabs and rubble. There are no markers. The graves are indistinguishable from the surrounding terrain within a few seasons, as the frost and the wind and the slow creep of lichen reclaim the disturbed stone. The dead become part of the plateau in a literal way, their bodies cooling and drying in the thin air, preserved by the cold, slowly mineralizing, returning their substance to the rock from which the settlement is built. There is no visible mourning, no ritual that you can observe from outside. But the old, sometimes, will sit near a place where the rubble looks slightly newer, slightly less settled, and they will work their rope and say nothing, and the quality of their silence is different from their usual silence — heavier, more deliberate, a silence that is doing work.

They have a relationship with the birds. The raptors that ride the thermals along the ridge lines are watched — their patterns of flight, their numbers, the times of day they are most active. The people read the birds the way they read the stone: as indicators of conditions, as signals of something larger. When the raptors circle in a particular way, it means something — a thermal shift, a change in air pressure, perhaps a storm forming in the distant lowlands. The smaller birds that nest in the walls are tolerated to the point of encouragement. Their droppings streak the stone, and the people do not clean them. There is a practical reason — the droppings, mixed with water, create a weak acidic solution that can be used to soften certain types of stone for shaping — but there is also something else, something that looks like companionship. The birds come and go through the walls of the settlement, and the people move around them, and there is a mutual accommodation that suggests a long cohabitation. The birds are not pets. They are neighbors.

The burrowing animals are more complicated. They are food, but they are also a threat — their tunnels undermine walls, collapse passages, destabilize the ground on which the settlement is built. The people manage this relationship with a pragmatism that is almost philosophical. They trap the animals for food, but they do not trap them to extinction. They build walls over active burrows, but they leave gaps — small openings at the base of walls, barely visible, that allow the animals to pass through. The animals move through the lower levels of the settlement as though it were part of the landscape, and the people move through the upper levels, and the two populations coexist in a vertical stratification that neither planned but both maintain. When a tunnel collapses a wall, the people rebuild the wall and leave a new gap. When an animal is caught in a trap, it is killed quickly and completely, with no waste. The relationship is not warm, but it is respectful, in the way that a relationship between two species competing for the same limited resources can be respectful.

They do not count time in the way that lower-dwelling peoples might. There are no calendars visible in their architecture, no astronomical alignments, no markers of seasonal cycles. The seasons on the plateau are subtle — the cold is constant, the light varies only slightly, the plants grow so slowly that their changes are imperceptible. Instead, they seem to track time through the settlement itself. A wall was built in the time of someone's grandmother. A water channel was rerouted after a particular fracture event that split a ridge in the eastern quarter. The settlement is their calendar, its layers of construction and reconstruction a record of duration that is read by the old and passed to the young not as history but as context — this is how things are, because this is how they have been done, and this is what happened when they were done differently.

They are not isolated. At the edges of the plateau, where the land drops away, there are paths — steep, treacherous, winding — that descend to the lower lands. These paths are used, though not frequently. The people who descend carry dried meat and lichen cakes and return with things that the plateau cannot provide: wood, perhaps, or plant fibers of a different kind, or materials they do not produce themselves. The trade is minimal, cautious, conducted with the same economy that characterizes everything they do. They do not depend on the lower lands, and the lower lands, presumably, do not depend on them. The plateau is self-sufficient in the essentials — water, stone, food, shelter — and whatever is gained from below is supplementary, not critical.

They are not happy, in the way that word is usually meant. They are not unhappy either. They are *situated* — placed in a relationship with their environment that is so complete, so thorough, that the question of happiness does not arise. They do what the plateau requires of them, and the plateau provides what they need, and the exchange is so balanced that it has the quality of a natural process rather than a human one. They are not at war with their environment. They are not conquering it, or being conquered by it. They are part of it, the way the lichen is part of it, the way the burrowing animals are part of it, the way the frost and the wind and the sharp stone are part of it. They have found the shape that fits the place, and they have held that shape for so long that it has become indistinguishable from the place itself.

When you watch them — from a distance, from the edge of a ridge, where your presence has not yet registered — they move through their city of stone like a slow circulation, like blood through capillaries, like water through channels. They are carrying, fitting, clearing, mending. They are checking a wall, tasting water, replacing a roof slab. They are sitting in a sheltered pocket, working rope, watching the raptors circle. They are leading a child's hand along a fracture line, showing them where the stone will split. They are placing a body in the rubble and covering it with slabs and saying nothing. They are doing the things that the plateau requires, and they have been doing them for longer than anyone can remember, and they will continue to do them after everyone who remembers now is gone.

The wind moves across the stone. The ice cracks in the shallow pools. The birds call from the ridge lines. And the people move through it all, small and dark and slow, fitting themselves to the world with the same precision with which they fit one stone to another — patiently, exactly, without haste, without end.
