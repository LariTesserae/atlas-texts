# The Dwellers of Keth-Ammon

---

They are small. That is the first thing.

Not small in the way of children or in the way of things that are diminished — small in the way of creatures who have learned, over generations uncounted, that the space between roots is the only space that matters, and that the body must conform to the architecture or the architecture will kill you. They are perhaps a meter and a half tall when they stand fully upright, which they rarely do. Their natural posture is a kind of low, fluid crouch — spine curved, head forward, weight balanced on the balls of their feet and the pads of their fingers simultaneously. They move through the hanging gardens the way the furred things move along the root-bridges: with an ease that looks like grace but is really something older and more desperate than grace. It is the ease of a body that has been shaped by its environment so thoroughly that the boundary between organism and habitat has begun to blur.

Their skin is pale. Not white — there is no white here, nothing has ever been white in Keth-Ammon — but a deep, warm ivory, the color of old bone or the inner bark of the pale-wood trees that grow in the upper root-mass. In the amber light, their skin takes on the same tone as the stone and the fluid and the fungal glow, so that when they are still, when they press themselves against a root or stand in shadow, they become nearly invisible. Their skin is smooth and largely hairless, but it is not delicate — it has a thick, almost leathery quality, toughened by constant contact with root-bark and stone, and it is marked everywhere with faint patterns, not tattoos but something closer to the grain in wood, lines and whorls that follow the contours of muscle and bone and that shift subtly as they move. These patterns are unique to each individual. They use them to identify one another in the dark.

Their eyes are large. Not grotesquely so, but large enough to be the first feature that registers — dark, dark irises, nearly black, with pupils that can open wide enough to swallow almost the entire visible surface of the eye. In full dark, their eyes catch the fungal light and throw it back as a faint amber gleam, the same color as the insect-carapaces, the same color as everything else in Keth-Ammon. They have no need for color vision in the way that surface creatures do. Their eyes are built for luminance, for the detection of movement in low light, for reading the subtle shifts in the amber glow that tell them where the fungal colonies are fruiting, where the root-bridges are thickest, where the fluid is flowing strongest. They see in shades of gold.

Their hands are their most remarkable feature. Long-fingered, double-jointed at the second knuckle, with an extra range of motion that allows the fingers to curl backward as easily as forward. The fingertips are broad and flat, covered in a dense array of ridges — not fingerprints, something more like the pads of a gecko's foot — that allow them to grip root-bark with extraordinary precision. They can hang from a root-bridge by one hand for minutes at a time, the ridged pads finding purchase on surfaces that would be slick and unholdable to anyone else. Their fingernails are thick and slightly translucent, more like hooves than nails, and they grow continuously; they are kept short by constant wear against stone and root, and the dwellers use them as tools — for prying, for scraping, for the delicate work of grafting and pruning that maintains the living architecture of their settlement.

They have no name for themselves that you would recognize. The sounds they make are not language in the way you understand language — they are closer to music, or to the clicking of the insects, a complex system of clicks and hums and breath-patterns that carries meaning not just in the sounds themselves but in the resonance of the space around them. They speak, and the roots carry the sound, and the stone shapes it, and the listener hears not just the voice but the voice *in this place*, in this specific configuration of root and chamber and fungal light, and the meaning is inseparable from the medium. To speak in Keth-Ammon is to play the chamber like an instrument. They have been doing it for so long that they no longer distinguish between communication and the act of being present in the space. To be silent is to be absent. To make sound — any sound, a footfall on a root-bridge, a hand against stone, a breath drawn in or let out — is to announce yourself, to say *I am here, I am part of this, I belong.*

---

They live in groups of twelve to twenty. Not families, exactly — the concept of family as you understand it does not map cleanly onto their social structure. They are bound by something more fluid and more practical: shared responsibility for a section of the root-network. Each group tends a cluster of platforms and bridges, maintains the grafting, monitors the fluid-flow, manages the fungal colonies that provide their light. The work is constant but not burdensome — the gardens are mature, stable, and the maintenance required is more like gardening in the old sense, the sense of keeping a living thing in balance, than like labor. They prune. They redirect. They watch for signs of disease or decay in the root-mass, and when they find it, they cut it out and seal the wound with a paste made from crushed insect-carapace and the amber fluid, which has properties that promote healing in the root-tissue. They are not farmers. They are not builders. They are something in between — caretakers of a living structure that is also their home, their food source, their light source, and their world.

They eat what the gardens provide. The root-flesh of certain species, carefully harvested so as not to damage the structural roots. The seeds and fruits of the fern-like plants that grow in the upper canopy. The insects — they eat the insects, or rather they eat certain insects, the large slow ones with the thick carapaces, which they roast over the coals of their root-fire until the shells crack and the flesh inside is soft and warm and tastes, by all accounts, of copper and green. They drink the amber fluid. It is not water — it is something thicker, richer, carrying dissolved minerals and organic compounds in a suspension that the dwellers' bodies have evolved to process. They need it the way you need water. Without it, they would die. The fluid is the true currency of Keth-Ammon, more valuable than food or light or shelter, because it is the one thing that cannot be replaced. It flows from the deep stone, from sources that the dwellers do not fully understand, and its flow is slow and constant and finite. They monitor it obsessively. Every group knows the flow-rate of every channel in their section. A drop in flow is a crisis. An increase is a cause for celebration — a rare, bright event, greeted with a chorus of clicks and hums that reverberates through the root-network and can be felt in the stone.

---

They do not age the way you do. Or rather, they do, but the process is so slow that it is nearly imperceptible across a single human lifetime. They live for a very long time — how long, no one can say, because they do not count years, because years have no meaning in a place where the light does not change with the seasons and the temperature never varies and the only cycles are the slow, deep cycles of root-growth and fluid-flow that unfold over spans of time too long to perceive. The oldest among them are not revered for their age — age is not a concept they have a word for — but for their knowledge of the root-network. The oldest ones know the gardens the way a conductor knows an orchestra: every voice, every resonance, every place where the structure is strong and every place where it is weak. They can press their ear to a root and hear the fluid moving deep inside it. They can feel, through the soles of their feet, the stress-load on a bridge three platforms away. They are the memory of the settlement, and when they finally die — slowly, quietly, curling into a tight ball on a root-platform and simply stopping, the way a clock stops, the way a fire goes out — their body is placed in a crevice in the stone wall, and the roots grow over it, and within a year there is nothing left but a slight thickening of the root-mass, a place where the bark is a little smoother, a little warmer, and the fungal colonies cluster a little more densely, feeding on what the body has become.

---

They are not peaceful. This is important to understand. They are not some idyllic root-dwelling people living in harmony with nature, whatever that means. They are survivors in a place that is generous but not infinite, and generosity without infinity is just slow starvation. They fight — not often, but viciously when they do — over fluid-channels, over root-sections, over the right to harvest from a particular fungal colony. Their weapons are simple: sharpened bone, weighted fiber-nets, and their own bodies, which are stronger than they look, the muscles dense and ropy from a lifetime of climbing and gripping and hanging. They fight in the root-canopy, in three dimensions, swinging and dropping and grappling in a space where a fall means death and the ability to move through the hanging gardens faster than your opponent is the difference between life and death. They do not kill each other often — the population is too small, too precious, too hard-won against the attrition of a hostile world — but they maim. They drive each other off. They claim and reclaim and claim again, and the borders of each group's territory shift like the borders of root-growth itself: slowly, then all at once, then slowly again.

But they also cooperate. They must. The root-network is a single organism, or close enough to one that the distinction doesn't matter, and a disease in one section will spread to all sections, and a structural failure in one bridge can cascade through the entire settlement, and so they share knowledge, share labor, share the deep and intimate understanding of the gardens that is their most precious possession. When a new root-bridge must be grown — a process that takes years, that requires the careful grafting of a structural root to a new anchor-point, the patient training of its growth with fiber-guides and weighted ties, the constant monitoring of its health and tension — groups work together, pooling their knowledge and their labor, and the bridge that results belongs to no one and everyone, and the ceremony of its completion is the closest thing they have to a shared ritual: a long, sustained chord of clicks and hums, produced by every dweller in the settlement simultaneously, that resonates through the root-network and the stone and the fluid and the air, and that says, in a language that is not words but is unmistakable: *we are here, we are together, we endure.*

---

There is one more thing.

In the deepest part of the chamber, where the root-curtains are thickest and the fungal light is dimmest and the amber fluid flows in a slow, thick stream down a channel worn so deep into the stone that it is more canyon than groove — there is a root that is different from the others.

It is old. Older than the settlement, older than the dwellers, older perhaps than the chamber itself. It descends from the ceiling in a single massive trunk, as wide as three dwellers standing side by side, and it does not branch. It simply descends, straight and true, into the floor of the chamber, where it plunges into the stone and disappears into the deep rock below. Its bark is not like the bark of the other roots. It is smooth, almost polished, and it is warm — warmer than the ambient air, warmer than the stone, warm the way a living body is warm. And it pulses. Slowly, rhythmically, with a cadence that does not match the fluid-flow or the fungal cycle or any other rhythm in the chamber. It pulses the way a heart pulses. The dwellers can feel it in their feet when they stand on the root-bridges nearest to it. They can feel it in their hands when they touch the walls. They can feel it in their chests, in the space behind their sternum, a slow and steady throb that is not their own heartbeat but that their heartbeat has, over generations, synchronized with.

They do not worship it. They do not have a concept of worship. But they orient toward it. Their platforms are arranged in concentric rings around it, the oldest and most central groups living closest, the youngest and most peripheral groups living farthest away. When they are sick, they press their bodies against it, and the warmth seeps into them, and they get better — not always, not always, but often enough that the practice is universal. When a dweller dies, their body is placed in the stone wall nearest to it, so that the roots that grow over the body are roots of *it*, the great root, the central root, the root that is the spine of Keth-Ammon. And sometimes, in the deepest part of the long dim cycle when the fungal light is at its lowest and the fluid flows slowest and the insects are quiet and the chamber is as close to silent as it ever gets — sometimes, if you press your ear to the great root and listen, you can hear something.

Not a voice. Not a word. Not a thought.

A presence. Vast and slow and patient and aware. Not conscious in the way that the dwellers are conscious, not conscious in the way that you are conscious, but *aware* — aware of the weight of the stone above, aware of the fluid in its channels, aware of the roots that branch from it and the dwellers that live among the roots and the insects that live among the dwellers and the fungal colonies that light the whole interdependent web of life that hangs inside the mountain like a heartbeat hangs inside a chest.

It is not a god. It is not a mind. It is a root — the first root, the deepest root, the root that found the chamber when the chamber was new and the stone was soft and the fluid was abundant, and that grew and grew and grew until it filled the space and became the space and made the space into a place where life could take hold and hold on and endure.

The dwellers do not know this. They do not theorize about the great root. They do not tell stories about it. They simply live in relation to it, the way a cell lives in relation to the bone it calcifies around, the way a note lives in relation to the chord it completes.

They are part of it. It is part of them. The gardens hang, and hold, and endure.

And in the amber light, in the warm air, in the slow pulse of the deep root, the dwellers of Keth-Ammon crouch on their root-bridges and click and hum and tend their gardens, and they are home.
