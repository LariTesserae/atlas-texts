# The Keepers of Vael Korum

They do not have a name for themselves. Names are for things that need to be called across distances, and no one here is far from anyone else.

---

There are seven of them. Or there were seven, the last time anyone thought to count, which was years ago, or perhaps longer — time moves differently when you are part of a system that breathes on the scale of hours. They are not a family, not a crew, not a council. They are more like organs in a body that has learned to think: each one necessary, each one performing a function that has become, over the long years, indistinguishable from identity.

They are old. Not ancient — not yet — but old in the way that the Quarter itself is old, which is to say they have been worn to their final forms. Their skin has taken on the color of the place: a pale, warm brown with an undertone of green, as though the chlorophyll of the walls has leached into them over decades of contact. Their hair, where they still have it, is matted with the fine spores that hang perpetually in the air, and it has a faintly luminous quality in the green-filtered light, a soft phosphorescence that is not quite biological and not quite mineral. Their hands are the most changed. The pads of their fingers have thickened into something like the root-mats beneath the floor — dense, porous, sensitive to moisture and pressure and the chemical signatures of the nutrient solutions that flow through the pipes. They can read the health of a system by touch. They can feel a blockage in a pipe three rooms away through the vibration in the substrate. They have, in a very real sense, grown into the Quarter's nervous system.

They do not wear much. The warmth makes heavy clothing unnecessary, and the moisture would ruin anything that wasn't already part of the ecology. They wrap themselves in woven fungal fiber — the shelf fungi, harvested and dried and spun into a cloth that is soft and slightly damp and smells of the earth — in loose garments that can be shed quickly when they need to climb, to crawl through the narrow passages between structural columns, to press their bodies against pipes and walls and root-mats in the intimate diagnostic contact that is their primary work.

---

Their work. This is what they are.

The Quarter is alive, but it is not self-maintaining in any simple sense. It is a system of such complexity — biological and mechanical, grown and built, organic and engineered — that it requires constant, attentive adjustment. Valves must be opened and closed on schedules that shift with the seasons, with the slow changes in the root-mat's density, with the mineral content of the water that seeps up from below. Pumps must be monitored for the subtle changes in rhythm that signal wear, or blockage, or the slow calcification of their internal gears. Nutrient solutions must be mixed — this is the work that takes the most time, the most knowledge — combining the outputs of different parts of the system in precise ratios to feed other parts. The Quarter digests itself, recycles itself, and the Keepers are the ones who manage the digestion, who ensure that what is broken down is rebuilt in the right place, in the right form, at the right rate.

They know the system the way a body knows itself — not through maps or diagrams (though there are diagrams, scratched into the soft bark of certain columns, faded and overwritten and scratched again across generations) but through a felt sense, a proprioception of the whole. When a pump in the eastern cluster begins to labor, they feel it as a tightness in their own chest. When a section of root-mat begins to compact beyond its optimal density, they feel it as a stiffness in their joints. The Quarter speaks to them through their bodies, and they respond with their bodies, and the conversation has been going on so long that neither party can clearly distinguish where one ends and the other begins.

---

They live in the spaces between the machinery. Their dwellings are not separate structures but *niches* — hollows in the root-mats, alcoves formed by the natural clustering of structural columns, small platforms built up from compressed fungal matter and sheltered by the overhanging shelves of growth. They are warm. They are wet. They are dark in the way that the inside of a body is dark — not the darkness of absence but the darkness of enclosure, of being held. Each Keeper's niche is personalized in small ways: a particular arrangement of dried mosses, a collection of smooth stones taken from the deepest layers of the root-mat, a length of pipe that has been tuned — by careful filing of its openings — to produce a specific tone when water flows through it. These are not decorations. They are *markers*. Each Keeper's niche sounds different, smells different, feels different underfoot, and the others can navigate to it in absolute darkness by the unique sensory signature of the place.

They eat what the Quarter provides. The fungal shelves produce fruiting bodies — dense, pale, nutritious — on a cycle that the Keepers have learned to predict and encourage. Certain of the epiphytic plants produce small fruits, tart and mineral-rich, that grow in clusters along the structural columns at heights that require climbing to reach. The water, filtered through the root-mats and mineral-enriched by its passage through the pipe systems, is drinkable and warm and faintly sweet, and they drink it constantly, their bodies requiring the moisture the way the Quarter itself requires it — not as a supplement but as a medium, as the substance in which all their processes occur.

They do not hunt. There is nothing to hunt, and the Quarter does not welcome the disruption that hunting would bring. The pale salamander-centipedes that move along the pipes are tolerated — they eat certain molds that would otherwise accumulate in the pipe junctions — but they are not pursued, not harvested. The birds that occasionally pass through the upper canopy are watched with a mild, detached interest, the way one might watch a cloud. They are not of the Quarter. They are of the sky, and the sky is a different country.

---

They speak, but not often, and not in the way that people in drier, louder places speak. Their language has contracted over the years, refined itself, shed the vocabulary of things that do not exist here. They have few words for emotions that are not also descriptions of the system's state. To say that one is content is to say that the pumps are running smoothly. To say that one is troubled is to say that a section of root-mat has gone sour, has begun to decompose rather than compost, has developed a smell that is wrong. Their conversations are technical and intimate simultaneously — a discussion of valve timing is also a discussion of how one feels, and a report on the state of the nutrient solutions is also a confession, a sharing of the self.

When they do speak, their voices are low and unhurried, shaped by the acoustics of the Quarter — the way sound is absorbed by the moss curtains and reflected by the structural columns and dampened by the constant patter of condensation. They do not need to raise their voices. The Quarter carries sound the way it carries water: everywhere, evenly, through every surface. A whisper spoken in one niche can be heard, if one attends to it, in any other part of the Quarter. They have learned to modulate not their volume but their *intention* — to speak in ways that are meant to travel, or in ways that are meant to stay close, and the Quarter obeys this distinction with the same fidelity with which it obeys the opening and closing of valves.

---

They touch each other often. This is not social in the way that touching is social in other places — it is *diagnostic*. They press their hands against each other's arms, necks, the soft spaces between the ribs, reading the state of the body the way they read the state of the pipes. A slight elevation in skin temperature signals a change in the local nutrient mix. A change in the texture of the skin — a drying, a roughening — signals that the humidity in a particular section has dropped, that a moss curtain has thinned, that something in the system's balance has shifted. They maintain each other the way they maintain the Quarter: constantly, attentively, with a tenderness that is not sentimental but *structural*, a recognition that the health of each is the health of all.

They sleep in shifts, though the word "shift" implies a formality that does not exist. Someone is always awake, always moving through the Quarter, always attending. The others rest in their niches, and their rest is not unconsciousness but a *deepening* — a sinking into the substrate, a slowing of the pulse, a state in which the boundary between the body and the root-mat becomes permeable, in which they dream the Quarter's dreams, which are slow dreams of growth and flow and the patient accumulation of mineral and fiber.

---

There is a question that sometimes surfaces in the long quiet hours, when the pumps are steady and the valves are all in their proper positions and the Quarter is, for the moment, in perfect balance. The question is this: *What happens when we are gone?*

They have no children. The Quarter has not been designed for children — its spaces are too narrow, its surfaces too demanding, its ecology too finely tuned to accommodate the chaos of a growing body that does not yet know the system's language. The Keepers themselves were not born here. They came — drawn by rumor, by necessity, by the slow gravitational pull of a place that needed hands and minds and bodies willing to be absorbed into its processes. They came from the outside, from the dry places, from the places where the sky is open and the ground is ground and things are separate from each other. They came, and they stayed, and they were changed, and now they are of the Quarter in a way that makes the question of their absence almost nonsensical — you cannot remove an organ from a body and ask what the body will do without it. The body will fail. The body will find a new equilibrium, lower, slower, diminished, and it will persist in that diminished state for a long time, the pumps still turning, the valves still opening and closing, but without the fine adjustment, without the diagnostic touch, without the intelligence that knows when a sound is wrong and a smell is wrong and a texture is wrong.

Or perhaps the Quarter will find new Keepers. Perhaps, at the edges, where the structures thin and the root-mat shallows and the pipes give way to open water, someone is even now feeling the pull — a restlessness, a sense that the dry places are too dry, that the open sky is too open, that there is a warmth and a wetness somewhere that calls to something in the body that the body does not yet have a name for. Perhaps they will come, as the current Keepers came, and they will be absorbed, and the Quarter will continue.

The Keepers do not dwell on this. It is a question for the long cycles, for the seasons measured in years, and they are occupied with the shorter cycles — the hours, the days, the slow pulse of sap behind translucent bark. They move through the Quarter. They open valves. They close valves. They press their hands against pipes and walls and each other. They listen to the pumps. They drink the warm water. They eat the pale fruiting bodies. They sleep in their niches and dream the Quarter's slow dreams.

They are the Keepers of Vael Korum, and they are keeping it, and it is keeping them, and the distinction between the two has long since ceased to matter.
