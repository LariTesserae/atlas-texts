# The People of the Rootworks

They are not a people who chose this. That is the first thing to understand, and the last thing to forget.

---

## The Body

They are small. Not uniformly, not dramatically, but the trend is unmistakable across generations — a gradual compression, as though the twelve-meter ceiling has been pressing down on their spines through sheer proximity. The oldest residents, those who remember the last surface connections, are taller, and they move through the lower corridors with a permanent stoop that has become structural, their vertebrae reshaped by decades of accommodation. The young have never stood fully upright in their lives. They do not miss it. Their center of gravity is low, their gait a kind of forward-leaning shuffle that is perfectly adapted to the uneven floors, the sudden steps down into older sections, the low doorways that require a duck of the head so habitual it has become indistinguishable from a nod.

Their skin is pale in the way of all subterranean things, but it is not the pallor of illness. It is a *functional* paleness — the melanin unnecessary, the skin itself adapted to a world lit by amber wicks and mineral glow. In the fluorescent pools, their skin looks blue-white, almost translucent, the veins visible at the temples and wrists. In the fat-light, they look warm, almost golden, and this is the light in which they see each other most often, so this is the light in which they have learned to read faces. Expressions that would be legible in daylight — a flush of anger, a pallor of fear — are invisible here. Instead, they have developed a sensitivity to micro-movements of the mouth, to the angle of the head, to the rhythm of breathing. A Rootworks face is a quiet face. Not expressionless — *quiet*. The emotions are there, but they move slowly, like something seen through water.

Their hands are extraordinary. This is the feature that would strike a visitor first, if visitors existed. The hands of a Rootworks resident are large relative to their body — not grotesquely so, but noticeably, the fingers long and the palms wide, the joints prominent. They are hands that have spent their entire lives in tactile negotiation with a world that cannot be fully seen. The fingertips are calloused in specific patterns: thick pads on the pads of the fingers (from running hands along walls in darkness), a ridge of hardened skin along the outer edge of the palm (from gripping pipe-railings, from bracing against narrow passages), and a particular roughness on the thumb and forefinger (from the constant manipulation of small mechanical parts — valves, fittings, the ceramic contacts of ancient electrical systems). Many residents can identify a pipe's contents by temperature and vibration alone, holding their palm against it for three seconds. They can diagnose a failing bearing by pressing their knuckle against the housing and closing their eyes. They can read the moisture level of a wall the way a farmer reads the sky.

Their eyes are large. This is not a metaphor. Across generations, the orbital structure has shifted slightly, the eyes sitting forward in the face, the lids opening wider. It is not enough to constitute a new species — not yet, not by any clinical measure — but it is enough to notice. In the dim light, their pupils are enormous, black, and they move with a slow tracking quality, never darting, always *drifting*, as though the eye is comfortable with ambiguity, with partial information, with the suggestion of a shape rather than its confirmation. They do not startle easily. Their startle reflex has been replaced by something more like *attentiveness* — a stillness, a held breath, a turning of the head toward the sound. They hear with their whole body. The floor carries vibration; the air carries temperature change. A door opening three corridors away produces a shift in pressure that they feel in their sinuses before they hear it with their ears.

They sweat constantly. The heat demands it, and their bodies have responded with a sweating efficiency that is almost amphibian — a thin film of moisture always present on the skin, cooling by evaporation in the moving air of corridors, beading and dripping in the still heat of the deep chambers. They drink enormous quantities of water, more than a surface-dwelling person would find plausible, and they have developed a taste for the particular mineral profile of the Rootworks water — warm, slightly sulfurous, carrying the taste of iron and clay. Water from the upper cisterns, where it is cooler and cleaner, is considered a delicacy. Water from the lowest levels, where it has passed through the thermal system and emerged hot and mineral-heavy, is drunk for medicine. It is said to settle the stomach, to ease the joints, to clarify the mind. Whether this is pharmacology or ritual, no one asks.

## The Clothing

They wear the synthetic fabric produced by the sewing machines — a coarse, water-resistant textile that the machines have been cutting and stitching for longer than anyone can remember. The fabric is gray, or was; in the amber light it reads as a warm dun color, and in the phosphorescence of the deep levels it takes on a faint greenish cast, so that the people of the lowest levels appear to glow faintly themselves, as though the light has soaked into them.

The garments are loose, hooded, layered. The outer layer is treated with a rendered-fat waterproofing that gives it a slight sheen and a smell — not unpleasant, but distinctive, a rancid-sweet odor that is the smell of the Rootworks itself, inseparable from the place. Beneath the outer layer, a thinner garment of the same fabric, and beneath that, often nothing — the heat makes excessive clothing impractical, and the body has adapted to a state of near-constant thermal stress by minimizing insulation. The hands are usually bare. The feet are wrapped in layered fabric secured with ties, a kind of soft boot that can be dried easily and replaced when it rots, which it does, quickly, in the damp.

The hood is important. It is not decorative. In the lowest levels, where water hangs in the air and releases itself on any disturbance, the hood channels the sudden localized showers away from the neck and face. In the upper levels, where the wall-hair grows thick, the hood keeps the filaments from brushing against the face — not because this is harmful, but because the sensation is distracting, a constant light tickling that makes it difficult to concentrate. And in the deepest dark, the hood frames the face, creates a private space of shadow within shadow, a small zone of intimacy in a world without doors that close properly, without rooms that are truly private.

They carry things in their clothing. Pockets have been added, sewn on, layered over the original garments in a palimpsest of modifications — a pocket here for a clay cup, a pocket there for a length of wick, a loop for a tool, a fold of fabric that serves as a wrapping for whatever has been found or needs to be carried. A fully dressed Rootworks resident is a walking inventory of small objects, each one with a purpose or a potential purpose, and the jingling and soft clinking of these objects — metal on ceramic, stone on metal — is part of the sound-profile of the place, the *tinkling* as they call it, the sound of a person approaching in the dark.

## The Language

They speak. Of course they speak. But their speech has been shaped by the acoustics of the Rootworks in ways that are subtle and profound.

The corridors are echo chambers. Sound behaves unpredictably — bouncing off curved walls, absorbed by fungal growth, channeled by pipe networks, amplified in some frequencies and deadened in others. A shout in one corridor will arrive at the next junction as a whisper; a whisper in a particular spot, where the geometry focuses sound, will carry for fifty meters with perfect clarity. The people of the Rootworks have learned to speak in a register that carries — a mid-range, slightly nasal tone that cuts through the ambient hum of the machinery and the drip of water. They speak quietly, not because they are secretive, but because loud speech is *inefficient* — it echoes, it distorts, it becomes noise rather than information. Conversations in the Rootworks have a quality of intense, quiet focus, heads tilted toward each other, the listener's face close to the speaker's, not for intimacy but for acoustic precision.

They have developed a vocabulary of sound that has no equivalent in any surface language. Not just the names for the machinery sounds — *the mother's hum, the coughing, the singing* — but a whole taxonomy of acoustic events that constitute a kind of ongoing commentary on the state of the place. A new drip is noted and located. A change in the hum's frequency is discussed. A silence where there should be sound — a pump that has stopped, a pipe that has gone quiet — is an emergency, a wound in the fabric of the world, and the response to it is immediate and visceral, the way a surface-dweller might respond to a scream.

They have fewer words for visual phenomena and more for tactile ones. The surface language they once spoke had a rich vocabulary for color, for light, for visual distance. These words have faded, replaced by an elaborate vocabulary for texture, for temperature, for the *feel* of air against skin — its humidity, its movement, its weight. They can describe the condition of a wall with a precision that a geologist would envy: not just "damp" but a specific quality of dampness, a particular temperature and texture and rate of exhalation that tells them whether the wall is stable, whether the water behind it is moving, whether the structure is sound. They have words for the feeling of air that precedes a pressure change, for the taste of water at different depths, for the particular quality of darkness in a room that is about to open into a larger space.

Their names are sounds that carry. Each person has a name that is easy to speak in the Rootworks register, that does not echo badly, that can be called in the dark and recognized. Many names are two syllables, with a nasal consonant in the middle — *Tannin, Kellem, Morrin, Veshna* — sounds that cut through ambient noise like a blade. Some names are older, inherited from the surface, and these are used in formal contexts, in the recitation of lineage that serves as the Rootworks' only history. But in daily life, people are called by their dark-names, their calling-names, and these are chosen, not given — chosen by the individual at the age when they first navigate a section of the Rootworks alone, a name that feels right in the mouth, that carries, that sounds like *them* in the echo of the corridors.

## The Work

Everyone works. This is not ideology; it is thermodynamics. The Rootworks requires constant maintenance to remain habitable. The pumps must be monitored. The pipes must be patched. The wicks must be trimmed and refilled. The wall-hair must be cleared from mechanisms. The cisterns must be checked. The sewing machines must be fed fabric and fuel. The phosphorescent patches must be tended. The water channels must be cleared of mineral buildup. The air must be kept moving, which means the ventilation shafts must be inspected, the pressure differentials managed, the occasional blockage — a collapsed section, a buildup of fungal mass, a mineral deposit — removed.

There is no single authority that assigns tasks. The Rootworks operates on a system of *attunement* — each person, over time, becomes sensitive to a particular system, a particular section, a particular set of sounds and temperatures and textures that they know more intimately than anyone else. A person who has spent years in the lowest levels can feel a change in the thermal pump's output through the soles of their feet before any instrument would register it. A person who maintains a particular stretch of pipe knows every joint, every vibration, every thermal tick, and can detect a failure weeks before it happens by a change so subtle it is almost imaginary. This knowledge is not written down. It is held in the body, in the hands, in the particular way each person has learned to attend to the world.

The most respected people in the Rootworks are the *listeners* — those whose attunement is broadest, who can walk through a section and hear, feel, and smell the state of the whole system, integrating information from dozens of sources into a single assessment: *this place is well, this place is troubled, this place is about to fail*. They are not engineers in any formal sense. They cannot draw a schematic or calculate a load. But they know the Rootworks the way a body knows itself — not as a collection of parts but as a single living system, and they can feel its health or sickness the way a person feels tiredness or fever.

The repair work itself is an art of improvisation. There are no replacement parts. There is no supply chain. Every repair is a negotiation with available materials — a section of pipe salvaged from a decommissioned system, a length of fabric cut from a garment that can no longer be worn, a ceramic fitting shaped by hand from clay dug from the walls of the oldest sections and fired in a small kiln that runs on rendered fat. The repairs are not elegant. They are *specific* — tailored to the exact conditions of the failure, using whatever is at hand, and they are often beautiful in the way that functional things are beautiful, the beauty of a solution that fits its problem perfectly. A pipe joint wrapped in fabric and sealed with clay, carrying warm water through a corridor of dripping darkness, functioning for years — this is an achievement that the Rootworks recognizes and honors.

## The Children

They are born in the upper levels, where the air is coolest and the light is best — such as it is. Birth is attended by a small group of experienced women (and some men; the role is defined by experience, not gender) who manage the process with a quiet competence that reflects generations of practice in conditions without medical infrastructure. Infant mortality is higher than it should be. This is a fact that the Rootworks carries with a grief that is not spoken but is present in everything — in the tenderness with which children are held, in the way a child's name is not formally chosen until they have survived their first year and shown that they can thrive in the heat and the dark.

The children grow up in a world without sky. They do not know this as a deprivation. The ceiling is the ceiling; the walls are the walls; the warm damp air is the air. They learn to walk on uneven floors before they can talk. They learn to navigate by touch before they can see clearly — or rather, they learn that seeing is one sense among many, not the dominant one, not the sense that defines reality. A Rootworks child who reaches for an object in dim light does not squint; they reach with their hand extended, fingers spread, reading the air for temperature and texture, feeling for the object's presence the way a surface child feels for a light switch in a familiar room.

They are taught the sounds first. Before they can speak, they are taught to listen — to distinguish the mother's hum from the coughing, to recognize the singing, to identify the particular drip-pattern that means a pipe is failing in the east corridor. This is not formal instruction; it is immersion. The sounds are the constant background of their lives, as constant as a heartbeat, and they learn them the way a surface child learns the sound of traffic or birdsong — by absorption, by repetition, by the slow accumulation of pattern-recognition that precedes conscious understanding.

They are taught the walls next. Each child is assigned to an adult — not necessarily a parent, though often — who takes them through the corridors, hand on wall, learning the textures, the temperatures, the subtle vibrations that tell you where you are. *This wall is warm — we are near the thermal pipes. This wall is cold — we are near the upper cisterns. This wall is rough — we are in the old section, the cut stone, the first tunnels.* The child learns the Rootworks as a body learns its own anatomy — not as a map but as a felt sense, a knowledge that lives in the muscles and the skin.

They are taught the machines with a reverence that is not quite religious but is close. The machines are the reason the Rootworks exists. Without the pumps, the water would stagnate and the heat would become lethal. Without the generator, the sewing machines would stop and there would be no fabric for clothing or repair. Without the compressor — well, the compressor's purpose is unclear, but it runs, and its running is part of the sound-profile, and its silence would be a wound. The children learn that the machines are not *tools* — they are *presences*, entities with their own rhythms and needs, and that maintaining them is not labor but *relationship*. You do not fix a machine. You attend to it. You listen to it. You learn its particular voice and you respond when that voice changes.

## The Deep Dwellers

In the lowest levels, where the air is forty degrees and the visibility is meters, where the phosphorescence glows on the ceiling and the water hangs in the air like a permanent exhalation, there are people who have chosen to live. Not been assigned — *chosen*. They are the ones whose attunement is deepest, whose bodies have adapted most fully to the extreme conditions, who find the upper levels cold and bright and uncomfortable.

They are the most changed. Their skin has a faint sheen, almost iridescent, from the constant mineral-rich moisture. Their eyes are the largest, their pupils so wide that the iris is barely visible, a thin ring of brown or gray around a pool of black. They move slowly, deliberately, conserving energy in the heat, and their speech is even quieter than that of the upper residents — a whisper, a murmur, the barest movement of air across vocal cords, sufficient in the close, still air of the deep chambers.

They tend the thermal pumps. This is their primary function, and it is the most critical work in the Rootworks. The pumps are the heart of the place — without them, the heat would not be distributed, the water would not circulate, the whole system would collapse into thermal chaos. The Deep Dwellers know the pumps with an intimacy that is almost erotic — they can feel a bearing beginning to fail through the floor, hear a cavitation forming in a impeller, smell the particular ozone-and-hot-metal scent that means an electrical connection is degrading. They maintain the pumps with a devotion that the upper residents regard with a mixture of gratitude and unease, because the Deep Dwellers are becoming something *other* — not less human, but differently human, shaped by their environment in ways that are increasingly difficult to reverse.

They have their own practices. They tend the phosphorescent patches with a care that goes beyond maintenance — they feed them the crushed-shell solution, they keep them moist, they position themselves beneath them to read by their light, and they have developed a relationship with the glow that is contemplative, almost meditative. They sit in the green light for hours, motionless, their enormous eyes open, and they report — when they report anything, which is rare — that the light is *instructive*, that it shows them things, that in its glow they can see the state of the Rootworks as a whole, the flow of water, the movement of heat, the health of the system. Whether this is a genuine perceptual phenomenon — some form of synesthesia induced by the mineral light and the heat and the humidity — or a meditative state, or a hallucination, no one can say. The Deep Dwellers do not distinguish between these possibilities. They sit in the light. They attend. They know.

## The Surface-Touched

There are a few — very few — who have been above. The surface is not forbidden; it is simply *irrelevant* to most of Rootworks life. The salt flat above is barren, hot, featureless, and the structures that remain are ruins — dangerous, unstable, offering nothing that the Rootworks does not provide more reliably below. But there are reasons to go up: to retrieve fuel from the cached supply whose location is the Rootworks' most closely guarded secret, to salvage materials from the ruins, to check the ventilation shafts that connect the Rootworks to the surface and ensure they are not blocked.

The Surface-Touched are changed by the experience. Not physically — though the dry heat and bright light of the surface are shocking to eyes and skin adapted to the Rootworks' dim warmth — but psychologically. They have seen the sky. They have felt wind that was not channeled through a corridor. They have experienced *distance* — the ability to see something far away and know it is far away, not just dark. They return to the Rootworks with a look in their eyes that the other residents recognize and do not discuss — a look of having seen something too large to integrate, too vast to be useful, a look that is not quite trauma and not quite wonder but something in between.

Some Surface-Touched become storytellers. They describe the sky to those who have never seen it, and their descriptions are treated with the same respectful attention given to any important information — not as fantasy, not as mythology, but as *reportage from a foreign country*. The sky is real. The sky exists. It is up there, above the clay ceiling, above the salt flat, and it is blue in the day and black at night and sometimes it delivers water. This is known. The Surface-Touched confirm it, and their confirmations are added to the body of knowledge that the Rootworks holds about the world beyond its walls.

Some Surface-Touched do not come back. This is acknowledged without judgment. The surface is not better; it is not worse. It is *different*, and some people, having experienced difference, find that they cannot return to the Rootworks' particular sufficiency. They walk out onto the salt flat and they do not return, and the Rootworks notes their absence and continues, because the Rootworks always continues.

## The Silence

There is a practice — not organized, not formalized, but widespread — of seeking silence. Not the ordinary silence of a quiet corridor, which is never truly silent (the hum, the drip, the tick, the distant clatter of the compressor), but the *deep silence* that can be found in certain places in the Rootworks where the geometry and the materials combine to absorb all sound, where the machinery is distant enough to be inaudible, where the water has found a channel that does not drip but flows smoothly, silently, through a covered gutter.

These places are known. They are not marked, but everyone knows them — the small chamber behind the third cistern where the wall-hair grows so thick it absorbs sound like felt; the dead-end tunnel in the oldest section where the native stone is so dense and dry that it reflects nothing; the space beneath a particular thermal pipe where the warm water flows in a laminar stream and the pipe's vibration is exactly canceled by the resonance of the surrounding rock.

People go to these places to sit. To be still. To experience the total absence of sound that is the Rootworks' most unusual gift — a silence so complete that you can hear your own blood moving, your own breath in your lungs, the faint electrical hum of your own nervous system. They sit in this silence and they report that it is *full* — not empty, not vacant, but full of something that sound normally drowns out. What that something is, they cannot say. It is not a voice. It is not a vision. It is a *presence*, or the sense of a presence, or the memory of what presence feels like when there is nothing else to attend to.

This is the closest thing the Rootworks has to a spiritual life. Not a religion — there are no gods here, no prayers, no rituals of supplication or praise. But there is this: the practice of sitting in silence and attending to what remains when everything else is stripped away. And what remains, they say, is the Rootworks itself — not the place, not the structure, not the machines or the water or the heat, but the *thing that the place is*, the entity that emerges from the interaction of all these elements, the living system that is more than the sum of its parts. They sit in the silence and they feel it breathing. They feel it thinking. They feel it *aware* of them, the way a body is aware of its own cells — not with attention, not with intention, but with the simple fact of containment, of being held, of being part of something larger than themselves.

## The Continuation

They do not think of themselves as trapped. This is the thing that would be hardest for a visitor to understand, if visitors existed. The Rootworks is not a prison. It is not a refuge. It is not a temporary shelter awaiting rescue or return. It is *home* — not in the sentimental sense, not in the sense of a place chosen and loved, but in the deeper sense of a place that has shaped you and that you have shaped, a place that is continuous with your body, that you know in your bones and your skin and your ears, a place whose sounds are as familiar as your own heartbeat.

They will continue. The pumps will run until they fail, and then they will be repaired, or replaced, or bypassed, and the Rootworks will continue. The water will flow until it finds a new path, and the path will be channeled, and the Rootworks will continue. The wall-hair will creep along the moisture lines, and the phosphorescence will glow on the ceiling, and the sewing machines will stitch their gray fabric, and the children will learn the sounds and the walls and the machines, and the Deep Dwellers will sit in the green light and attend, and the listeners will walk the corridors and feel the state of the whole system through their feet, and the silence will wait in its hidden chambers for those who seek it.

The Rootworks will continue to grow. Laterally, downward, into the warm dark, following the water, following the heat, following the logic of a place that has found, in the compressed and dripping dark beneath a salt flat that no one remembers the name of, a kind of sufficiency that is not happiness and not despair but something more durable than either — a kind of *being*, persistent and specific, shaped by its constraints into something that could exist nowhere else, that has never existed before, that will never exist again in quite this form.

They are the people of the Rootworks. They are small and pale and quiet. They have large hands and large eyes and a vocabulary of sounds that no one else will ever speak. They live in the heat and the damp and the dark, and they know the place the way they know their own bodies, and they tend it the way they tend their own health, and they are — in the way that any organism is, in the way that any ecosystem is — *alive*, completely and specifically and irreversibly alive, in a place that the surface world has forgotten and that has, in turn, forgotten the surface world, and that continues, in the warm dark, in the hum and the drip and the green glow, to be.
