# The Inhabitants of the Cascades

## The Cascadeborn

They are not tall. That is the first thing you notice — or rather, the first thing you would notice if you spent long enough among them to stop noticing everything else. The adults are compact, dense-bodied, with the kind of physical economy that comes from a life lived entirely on grating and stairs and ladders: forearms that are disproportionately strong, calves that are broad and sure-footed, hands that are wide and surprisingly gentle. Their skin tends toward a deep warm brown that the green-gold light renders almost bronze, and their hair — worn long, usually, in braids or twists or tight coils bound with wire and plant-fiber — is threaded with the same fine metallic dust that coats every surface. It glints when they move. They do not cut it short. Hair serves functions here: insulation against the cold of the upper levels, padding for the loads they carry on their heads (and they carry an extraordinary amount on their heads, balancing baskets and bundles and coils of cable with a steadiness that seems effortless until you try it and find yourself staggering), and — more quietly — a kind of social vocabulary. The bindings, the ornaments woven into it, the way it is dressed: these convey information about clan, trade, marital status, and something the Cascadeborn call *depth*, which is not merely how many levels a person has lived on but a measure of their structural knowledge, their understanding of the Cascades as a living system.

Their eyes are striking. The pupils tend to be large, adapted to the dim mid-levels where most life is lived, and the irises are often very dark, nearly black, which makes the moments when they look up into direct light seem almost painful — a flinch, a narrowing, a hand raised to shade the face. But there is a subset of the population, those whose work takes them regularly to the upper levels — the Windworkers, the Sun-tenders, the Cable-riders — whose eyes have adapted differently: lighter in color, more sensitive to brightness, and these people are valued for their ability to read the upper structure, to see stress fractures and corrosion and cable fatigue in full sunlight where the rest of the Cascadeborn would be squinting and half-blind.

They move through the Cascades the way water moves through the Cascades: finding the path of least resistance, always, but with an understanding that least resistance is not the same as shortest distance. A Cascadeborn person climbing from the Deep Markets to the Mid-gardens does not take the nearest ladder. They take the route that passes through the warm corridor near the Number Seven engine house, because the warmth loosens the joints after a long day, and because the route passes by the evening food-stalls near the junction of Walkway Twelve and the Old Vine Bridge, and because that route allows them to stop and speak with the Cable-riders who gather at the junction to trade news from above. Efficiency here is social and thermal and nutritional, not merely geometric. A visitor who tries to move through the Cascades the way they would move through a normal city — directly, impatiently, always taking the fastest apparent route — will exhaust themselves, overheat, become dehydrated, and arrive at their destination in a state of furious bewildered sweat, having climbed three times as many meters as necessary because they did not understand that the fastest route down is almost never a ladder.

Their language is layered. There is a base tongue, spoken in the mid-levels, that is fluid and tonal and rich with onomatopoeia — it sounds, to an outsider, almost like the sound of the Cascades itself: a running, dripping, chiming, resonant speech with hard stops and long vowels. But there are registers. There is the Deep Speech, spoken at the lowest accessible levels, where the language becomes slower, more deliberate, shaped by the acoustics of the stone chambers and the constant low-frequency hum of the geothermal vents — a bass-heavy mode of communication that uses chest resonance and subvocalization and is partly felt rather than heard, transmitted through the feet and the hands on the railings. There is the High Speech of the upper levels, clipped and bright, shaped by wind-noise and the need to communicate across open air — more consonantal, more staccato, with a range of whistles and clicks that carry farther than voiced sounds. And there is the Root Speech, which is not spoken at all but conducted through touch: a system of pressure-patterns applied to the structural framework — the beams, the cables, the load-bearing creepers — that transmits information through vibration. The oldest Cascadeborn can feel an engine malfunction three levels below them through the soles of their feet. They can read the water level in a distant cistern by pressing their palm to a pipe. They can tell you which sections of walkway are overcrowded, which cables are under unusual strain, which gardens are too dry, all without speaking, without looking, simply by standing still and attending to the structure's continuous subtle narration of its own state.

They do not write, exactly. They *mark*. The surfaces of the Cascades are covered in inscriptions — scratched into metal, carved into wood, etched into stone — but these are not texts in the way an outsider would recognize. They are structural annotations. A symbol scratched into a beam near a joint indicates the date and method of the last repair. A series of marks on a cistern indicate capacity, current fill level, and the identity of the last person to service it. The plant-trough channels are marked with notches that encode flow rate and direction. The deepest stone levels bear carvings that are simultaneously structural diagrams, historical records, and something that might be prayer — geometric patterns that map the water-courses through the foundation, annotated with symbols that refer to events, or people, or processes so old that no one living can read them with certainty. The Cascadeborn do not think of this as writing. They think of it as maintenance. To inscribe a mark is to care for a structure. To read a mark is to understand a structure. The two activities are the same activity.

## The Clans

The social structure of the Cascades is organized not by geography but by function — or rather, by *relationship to the structure*. There are no neighborhoods in the conventional sense. There are trades, and the trades are hereditary not by rigid rule but by the simple logic that a child raised in the engine houses learns the engine houses, and a child raised among the gardens learns the gardens, and the skills are so specialized and so deeply embodied that switching trades in adulthood is possible but rare, like learning a new language with your muscles rather than your mind.

The **Engineers** maintain the mechanical systems: the steam engines, the water turbines, the lifts, the pumps, the generators. They are the loudest clan, the most physically imposing, and the most insular. They live in the engine-house levels, surrounded by heat and noise, and over generations they have developed a culture that is half mechanical and half mystical — they speak of the engines as living things, which, given that the engines are partially integrated with the plant ecology (cooling systems that use living root-masses as heat exchangers, exhaust scrubbers that are vertical gardens, fuel systems that process composted plant matter), is less metaphorical than it sounds. The senior Engineers can diagnose a problem by smell — the particular hot-oil-and-wet-stone scent of a bearing beginning to fail, the sweet-rotten undertone of contaminated fuel, the sharp ozone tang of an electrical fault. They are respected and slightly feared. Their work is dangerous. Engine failures in the Cascades are not abstract inconveniences; they are structural events. When an engine goes down, the water stops pumping, the lifts stop running, the ventilation shifts, and the entire section of the Cascades that depends on that engine begins, slowly, to change — to warm, to still, to stagnate. The Engineers carry this weight. They are quiet about it.

The **Gardeners** are the most numerous and the most politically powerful, though they would resist that word. They maintain the plant systems — the load-bearing creepers, the air-channeling broad-leafed forms, the water-channeling vine-troughs, the insulation plants in the wall cavities, the bioluminescent growths in the deep levels, the tough silver-leaved things at the top. Their knowledge is encyclopedic and intuitive in equal measure: they know the tensile strength of each species of creeper at each stage of its growth cycle, the water requirements of each garden zone, the way a plant's root structure will interact with the metal it clenches, the seasonal rhythms of growth and dormancy that must be accounted for in structural load calculations. A senior Gardener can walk a section of the Cascades and tell you, by the color and posture of the leaves, the health of the structure beneath — because the plants are the structure's skin, and they show stress before the metal does. They are the ones who train the young creepers along their cables, who graft new growth onto old, who decide when a plant has served its structural purpose and must be carefully, respectfully retired and replaced. They are patient people. They think in years.

The **Waterkeepers** are the smallest clan and the most essential. They manage the cascade system itself — the distribution of water from the upper catchments through the thousands of channels, gutters, troughs, pipes, and cisterns that make up the Cascades' circulatory system. They are the ones who know where every drop of water goes, who can trace a leak from the sound it makes, who understand the hydraulic logic of the entire structure the way a physician understands the logic of blood. They are also, by necessity, the clan with the broadest spatial knowledge, because water connects everything — a blocked gutter on Level Forty will manifest as a dry garden on Level Thirty-five and a corroded beam on Level Twenty-eight, and the Waterkeeper who is called to investigate must understand the entire chain of causation. They tend to be quiet, observant, and possessed of an almost supernatural calm, because panic is useless when you are standing in a cistern chamber three hundred meters above the nearest exit and you have just realized that a main channel has fractured and the water is going somewhere it should not be. They fix it. They always fix it. They climb out, eventually, wet and steady, and they file a report in the marks on the nearest beam, and they move on.

The **Cable-riders** maintain the tension systems — the cables, the suspension bridges, the hanging walkways, the cable-nets that support the hanging gardens. They are the most visible clan, the ones you see swinging through the green depths of the Cascades on ropes and harnesses, checking cable tension by plucking the strands and listening to the note, replacing worn sections, adjusting the counterweights that keep the walkways level. They are agile, fearless in a casual way that is more unsettling than bravade, and they have a reputation for being slightly mad — which they enjoy and do nothing to dispel. They are the Cascades' connection to the outside world, too, because they are the ones who maintain the cables that anchor the structure to the cliff faces, and who venture out onto the rock itself to inspect the anchor points, to check for geological shift, to ensure that the stone spine of the Cascades is still sound. They are the only Cascadeborn who regularly see the raw cliff, the unmade world, and they bring back stories that the others half-believe: stories of the vast dark river that still runs at the very bottom of the gorge, far below the lowest accessible levels, and of the things that live in the water there.

The **Mark-keepers** are the closest thing the Cascades have to a scholarly or priestly class. They maintain the inscription system — the marks, the carvings, the structural annotations that constitute the Cascades' collective memory. They are historians, cartographers, and archivists, and they spend their lives moving through the structure, reading the marks, updating them, and — crucially — interpreting the old ones. The deepest stone levels bear inscriptions that no one living fully understands, and the Mark-keepers are the ones who study them, who debate their meanings, who construct theories about the original builders and the earliest days of the Cascades. They are the ones who know that the structure is older than anyone can prove, that the stone foundation predates the iron by centuries at least, and that the iron predates the steel by a similar margin, and that the original builders — whoever they were — were not the Cascadeborn's ancestors but something else, something whose relationship to the current population is unclear and possibly uncomfortable. The Mark-keepers do not talk about this with outsiders. They do not talk about it much with anyone. They keep the marks. They keep the silence.

## The Visitors

There are always a few. Traders from the flatlands below, who climb the long exterior stairways with goods the Cascades cannot produce — grain, salt, certain metals, medicines, books. Diplomats from other settlements, come to negotiate water rights or trade agreements or the perennial question of whether the Cascades will allow a new cable-anchor to be driven into the cliff face on the eastern side. Refugees, occasionally, from places that have dried up or blown away or simply failed, who arrive at the base of the Cascades exhausted and desperate and are — after a period of assessment that can last weeks — admitted, given a compartment in the mid-levels, and assigned to a clan based on aptitude and need. The Cascadeborn are not unkind to refugees, but they are not sentimental either. The Cascades has limited capacity, and every new mouth requires water, food, and structural accommodation, and these things must be calculated. A refugee who is admitted is admitted because the Gardeners have determined that the plant systems can support another person, and the Waterkeepers have confirmed the water budget, and the Engineers have verified that the relevant section of structure can bear the additional load. It is a practical welcome. It is, in its way, more honest than most.

There are also the rare, strange visitors — the ones who come not to trade or negotiate or seek refuge but simply to *see*. Scholars, wanderers, the curious and the lost. The Cascadeborn regard these with a mixture of amusement and wariness. They are harmless, mostly, but they are disruptive in the way that all outsiders are disruptive: they move wrong, they breathe wrong (too shallow, too fast, not attuned to the spore-load), they ask questions that reveal how little they understand, and they want things done by tomorrow. The Cascadeborn accommodate them. They assign a guide — usually a young Cable-rider, because Cable-riders enjoy the performance of it, the chance to show off their agility and their knowledge simultaneously. The guide leads the visitor through a curated route: the Mid-gardens, the Deep Markets, perhaps a glimpse of an engine house from a safe distance, certainly the upper levels at sunset, because the light at the top of the Cascades at sunset is the thing that breaks every visitor open, that makes them understand — for a moment, before the understanding fades — what the Cascades is.

Most visitors leave. Some stay. The ones who stay are the ones who, on the third or fourth day, stop flinching at the hammering. Stop holding their breath in the engine houses. Stop looking for the ground. The ones who, on the fifth or sixth day, begin to feel the structure through their feet — the faint pulse of the engines, the subtle sway of the cables, the deep slow breathing of the metal as it warms in the sun. The ones who realize, with a shock that is almost physical, that they have been living their entire lives in dead structures — buildings that did not breathe, did not grow, did not respond to their presence — and that the Cascades is not a building at all but a *being*, a vast slow composite organism of stone and metal and water and plant and human, and that to live here is to be incorporated into it, to become part of its metabolism, to be — in the most literal sense — structural.

These are the ones who are offered a clan. These are the ones who, years later, are Cascadeborn.

## The Deep Ones

No one talks about them. This is not evasion or superstition — or rather, it is not *only* evasion or superstition. It is a practical silence, maintained for the same reason you do not discuss the load-bearing capacity of a beam that is currently holding up your home: because the discussion itself implies a doubt, and the doubt, if it spreads, becomes a kind of stress that the structure does not need.

The lower levels of the Cascades — below the lowest accessible platforms, below the stone foundation, below the point where the warm water rises and the bioluminescent growth gives way to a darkness that is not merely the absence of light but something thicker, something that has *weight* — are inhabited. The Cascadeborn know this. They hear the heavy deliberate shifting at night. They have found, on the lowest accessible platforms, marks that are not their own: deep gouges in the stone, too regular to be natural, too large to be made by any tool they recognize. They have found, in the warm water that rises from below, organic material — scales, fragments of shell, a dark fibrous substance that might be hair or might be something else — that does not match any known species. They have found, on the rare occasions when they have descended to the absolute lowest point of the accessible structure and pressed their ears to the stone, a sound that is not the engines and not the water and not the wind: a low, rhythmic, biological sound, like breathing, but too slow and too deep to be human.

The charms that are hung on the lower hatches are not charms. They are living plant-forms — a species of creeper that is sensitive to vibration and that responds to large masses moving nearby by contracting its leaves and emitting a sharp hissing sound, a release of volatile compounds that is acrid and unmistakable. The Cascadeborn check these plants every morning. If the leaves are contracted, they know that something large was moving in the deep levels during the night. They note it. They do not investigate.

There are theories. The Mark-keepers have theories, encoded in their most guarded inscriptions, accessible only to the senior members of the clan. The theories suggest that the original builders of the Cascades — the ones who laid the stone foundation, who carved the deep channels, who inscribed the oldest and most incomprehensible marks — were not human. Or were human once, and became something else, over the centuries of living in the deep warm dark, tending the roots of the structure, maintaining the water system at its source, slowly adapting to an environment that would kill an unadapted human in hours. The theories suggest that the Deep Ones are still there, still maintaining, still performing the foundational work that keeps the Cascades alive — the work that the Cascadeborn's own maintenance systems depend on but do not fully understand, the work that happens below the lowest accessible level, in the warm dark, where the water is hottest and the stone is oldest and the air tastes of iron and wet stone and something green underneath.

The theories suggest that the Deep Ones and the Cascadeborn are not separate populations but a single population, split by depth and time and adaptation, still connected by the structure itself — by the water that flows down and the warmth that rises up and the vibrations that travel through the stone and the metal and the living tissue, a continuous conversation in a language that is not spoken but *felt*, a language of pressure and temperature and flow and load, the oldest language in the Cascades, the language of the structure itself.

The theories suggest that if the Deep Ones ever stopped their work, the Cascades would die. Not quickly. Not dramatically. But slowly, over years, the way a body dies when its blood stops circulating. The water would stop rising. The warmth would fade. The stone would cool. The plants would wither. The metal would rust. The structure would groan, and settle, and groan again, and eventually it would be silent, and the green-gold light would be gone, and the sky above would be just sky, and the gorge would be just a gorge, and the Cascades would be a ruin.

The Cascadeborn do not talk about this. They maintain their levels. They tend their gardens. They keep their water flowing. They trust the Deep Ones to keep theirs.

It is, when you think about it, the most extraordinary act of faith in the world: to live your entire life inside a structure that you know, with absolute certainty, is being maintained at its deepest level by something you have never seen, something you cannot fully understand, something that breathes in the dark below you, slow and warm and patient, and that has been breathing there longer than your people have had a name for themselves.

## The Children

They are everywhere. They are the Cascades' most visible and most chaotic element, and they move through the structure with a fearlessness that terrifies visitors and delights their parents. A Cascadeborn child of six can climb a cable-ladder faster than an adult visitor. A child of eight can read the water-sound well enough to know, by ear alone, whether the nearest cistern is full or empty. A child of ten can navigate from the Deep Markets to the upper gardens without a guide, using routes that are not on any map — shortcuts through wall cavities, across cable-nets, through the interior of the engine houses (which are warm and loud and smell of oil and are, for reasons that are not entirely clear, the Cascadeborn children's favorite places to play).

They are raised communally, as much by the structure as by their parents. Every adult in a clan is responsible for every child. A child who is found on a walkway that is marked as structurally compromised will be corrected not only by their parents but by every adult who witnesses it, because structural safety is not a parental concern but a communal one — a child who does not learn to read the marks is a child who will, eventually, step onto a walkway that cannot bear their weight, and the failure of that walkway will affect everyone below it. The education of a Cascadeborn child is, from the beginning, an education in *relationship to structure*: this beam is strong, this cable is frayed, this plant is load-bearing and must not be climbed, this plant is decorative and is fair game, this water is clean, this water is not, this sound means the engine is healthy, this sound means something is wrong and you should find an adult immediately.

They play games that are, to outsiders, indistinguishable from maintenance. A group of children will spend an afternoon "checking" a section of walkway — tapping beams, examining joints, reporting findings to a patient senior Engineer who treats their reports with perfect seriousness and corrects their terminology with gentle precision. They build miniature structures from scraps of metal and plant-fiber, and the structures must bear weight, and the child whose structure bears the most weight is the winner, and the prize is a piece of fruit from the upper gardens, where the light is brightest and the fruit is sweetest.

They are happy. This is the thing that visitors notice last and remember longest. The Cascadeborn children are happy in a way that seems, to people from the flatlands, almost archaic — a physical, embodied happiness that comes from being *inside* something, from being held and supported and necessary, from knowing that they are part of a structure that is larger than themselves and that will outlast them and that needs them, specifically them, to continue. A Cascadeborn child knows, by the time they are five years old, that they are load-bearing. Not metaphorically. They know that the work they do — the small maintenance tasks, the plant-tending, the water-monitoring, the mark-reading — is real work, that the structure depends on it, that they are, in the most literal sense, holding the Cascades together.

It is a heavy knowledge for a child to carry. But the Cascades is heavy. And the children carry it the way the Cascades carries everything: slowly, patiently, with the understanding that the weight is shared, and that it has always been shared, and that it will always be shared, by the stone and the metal and the water and the plants and the people and the things in the deep, all of it, all of them, holding each other up.
