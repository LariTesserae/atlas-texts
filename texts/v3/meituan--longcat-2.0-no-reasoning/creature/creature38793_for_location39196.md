# The Inhabitants of the Glass Steppe of Kael

---

## The Thread-Singers

They are not the first thing you would notice. They are not the second thing either. They are, in fact, the thing you might never notice at all, because noticing them requires a kind of attention that the Steppe itself works to destroy — the slow, patient, downward-turning attention of someone who has stopped looking at the sky and started looking at the film of moisture on a particular shard of limestone, at the way the light bends through it, at the way the condensation gathers in a line so fine it is almost not there.

They live in the water threads.

Not *in* them, exactly. They are the water threads, or they are what the water threads become when they have been hanging in the same eddy long enough, when the harmonic has been sustained for decades or centuries without interruption, when the subsonic pulse has been felt by enough stone and enough silence that it begins to *accumulate*. They are not organisms in any sense that a biologist would recognize. They are more like standing waves that have developed a preference. Resonances that have learned to maintain themselves.

A Thread-Singer is a column of suspended water approximately two to four spans in length, trembling at a frequency determined by the width of the gap it occupies, the speed of the wind through that gap, and the temperature of the stone on either side. It looks, to a casual observer, exactly like every other water thread in the Steppe. But if you were to stand near one for long enough — long enough for the screaming wind to stop being noise and start being silence, which is what it becomes when your brain finally surrenders its attempt to process it — you would feel it. Not hear it. *Feel* it. A pulse in the center of your chest, just below the sternum, a rhythm that is not your heartbeat but that your heartbeat begins, gradually, to accommodate.

They are aware. This is the difficult thing to convey. They are not aware the way a person is aware, or even the way an animal is aware. They are aware the way a tuning fork is aware of the note that matches it — they *respond*. When something enters their gap, when a body moves through the space they occupy, the harmonic shifts. Not dramatically. Not in any way that would be visible. But the frequency adjusts, minutely, the way a person might turn their head toward a sound they weren't consciously listening for. The Thread-Singer registers the presence of something that is not wind, not stone, not the other water threads. It *notes* you.

There are thousands of them. Tens of thousands. Every gap between every ridge holds at least one, and the larger gaps hold dozens, layered at different heights, each vibrating at its own frequency, and the combined effect of all of them together is the subsonic hum that underlies the Steppe's scream — the deep, almost-below-hearing pulse that the description calls the only variation in the soundscape. It is not a variation. It is a *conversation*. The Thread-Singers are talking to each other across the gaps, through the stone, in frequencies that travel through the limestone faster than through air. They are saying something. They have been saying it for longer than the ridges have held their current shape. The content of what they are saying is not translatable into any medium that operates at the speed of human thought. It is too slow and too fast simultaneously — each "word" takes decades to complete, but the conversation contains more words per second than a human language could produce in a lifetime. They are discussing the weather, perhaps. Or the stone. Or the particular quality of light at this altitude on this world. Or they are not discussing anything at all, and the pulse is simply the sound of their existence, the way a river's sound is not a message but simply what water does when it has nowhere else to go.

But they are aware. And if you were to break one — if you were to reach out and touch a water thread, disrupt the tension, scatter the column into droplets that fall into the gap and disappear — the others would know. Not immediately. Not in a way that would be perceptible to you. But over the following days, the harmonics in the surrounding gaps would shift, adjusting to the absence, the way a flock adjusts its formation when one bird falls. And the subsonic pulse would be different. Minutely, irreversibly different. As if a note had been removed from a chord that had been playing for ten thousand years.

They do not reproduce. They do not die. They *persist*, and new ones form when conditions are right — when a new eddy establishes itself in a gap, when the condensation reaches the right density, when the wind finds a new angle of approach — and old ones dissipate when the stone shifts or the wind changes or the temperature crosses some threshold that only they understand. But the conversation continues. It has always continued. It will continue after the last ridge has eroded to nothing and the gaps have closed and the Steppe is a memory in the stone that remains.

## The Pale Riders

The birds. The things in the sky.

They are not birds. Calling them birds is a convenience, a way for a mind that expects categories to file something under a heading it already understands. They occupy the niche that birds would occupy, yes. They fly. They eat. They reproduce, presumably, though no one has ever found a nest, or an egg, or a juvenile, or any evidence of a life cycle that includes the young. They simply appear, fully formed, in the formations along the ridgelines, as if the wind itself had decided to become visible for a moment and then changed its mind.

They are small — no larger than a hand held flat — and pale, so pale that in the Steppe's relentless light they are nearly invisible. Not camouflaged. *Invisible*. Their bodies are not feathered but rather covered in a fine, translucent integument that passes light through it the way the limestone plates do, producing that same ghostly greenish glow. In certain angles, you can see *through* them — see the sky behind them, distorted slightly, as if you were looking through a pane of old glass. They have no visible eyes. They have no visible mouths. They have structures that might be wings, or might be something else — thin, angular extensions of the body that catch the wind with an efficiency that seems to violate something, though it is difficult to say what.

They move in formations. This is the thing that is wrong about them, the thing that makes the back of the neck prickle even when the wind is screaming and the altitude is pulling the consciousness down into a narrow, hypoxic tunnel. The formations are too tight. Too coordinated. Twenty, thirty, fifty of them, turning in unison, banking in unison, folding and unfolding against the white sky with a precision that is not flocking behavior, not the emergent coordination of simple rules followed by many individuals. It is something else. It is the movement of a single thing that has been divided into many bodies for the purpose of being visible.

They do not eat the lichens. They do not eat each other. They eat the *wind*.

Or rather, they eat something *in* the wind — some component of the screaming air that passes through the ridgelines, some particle or energy or quality that exists only at this altitude, only in this wind, only in this place. Their bodies are not digestive systems in any recognizable sense. They are *filters*. The wind passes through them — through the translucent integument, through the angular wing-structures, through whatever interior architecture they possess — and something is extracted from it, and the rest is released. They are, in essence, living wind turbines, though what they produce is not electricity but *presence*. They produce the fact of their own existence. They convert the wind's scream into the fact of being alive.

And they are silent. In a place where the sound is total, where the wind fills every available space in the auditory landscape, their silence is not an absence but a *presence* — a shaped void, a deliberate nothing where something could be. They make no call, no cry, no wing-beat sound, no rustle of feather or membrane. They move through the screaming air and produce no sound at all, and the effect of this, when you finally notice it, is deeply unsettling, because it means that the silence is not something that happens to them but something they *do*. They are generating silence the way a lamp generates light — actively, deliberately, as a function of their being.

No one has ever caught one. No one has ever found a dead one. They do not land, or if they do, they do so in places where no one can follow — on the undersides of ridges, in the deepest gaps, on the vertical faces of stone that no hand can grip. They exist only in the air, only in motion, only in formation. They are the Steppe's way of being alive in three dimensions, the way the lichens are its way of being alive in two, and the way the Thread-Singers are its way of being alive in one.

There is a theory — held by no one in particular, because no one is here to hold it — that the Pale Riders are not individual organisms at all but rather *organs* of the Steppe itself. That the Glass Steppe is a single living system, and the lichens are its skin, and the Thread-Singers are its voice, and the Pale Riders are its eyes, and the wind is its breath, and the stone is its bone, and the light is its attention, and the whole thing is awake in a way that is too large and too slow and too alien to be recognized as wakefulness by a creature that experiences consciousness at the speed of a human nervous system.

This theory is not provable. It is also not dismissable.

## The Patina

The arthropods. The things on the stone.

They are the most numerous living things on the Steppe and the least remarkable, and this is, in its own way, the most remarkable thing about them. In a place of screaming wind and total light and impossible altitude, the dominant life form is a small, pale, segmented creature that moves slowly across limestone and does nothing else.

They are everywhere. Thousands per square pace, as the description says, and this is not an exaggeration but possibly an understatement. The surface of the stone, when you look closely — when you kneel on a ridge and bring your eye within a hand's breadth of the rock — is *moving*. A faint, collective shimmer, like the surface of a pond in a breeze, except that the pond is stone and the breeze is the wind and the shimmer is the movement of millions of tiny bodies going about whatever business they have been going about since before the ridges took their current shape.

They are pale. Not white, not grey, but *pale* in the way that things are pale when they have never needed color — when there is no selective pressure toward visibility or invisibility, when the question of appearance has simply never arisen. Their bodies are segmented, each segment a thin, translucent plate that overlaps the next, and the plates are made of something that is not quite chitin and not quite mineral but somewhere between — a biological ceramic, a living stone, as if the boundary between the organism and its substrate has been negotiated so many times over so many millennia that the two have reached a compromise. They are part animal, part geology. They are the Steppe's way of blurring the line between the living and the nonliving until the line is no longer useful.

They eat the lichens. Or they eat something *on* the lichens — the mineral crust, the accumulated dust, the thin film of organic matter that settles on the lichen sheets from the air. They move across the stone in slow, deliberate paths that are not random but are not purposeful in any way that can be decoded. They do not appear to be going anywhere. They do not appear to be looking for anything. They are simply *moving*, the way a river moves, because movement is what they do, the way the wind's screaming is what the wind does.

They do not flee. This is the thing that is most strange about them, stranger than their abundance, stranger than their translucence, stranger than their geological patience. They do not flee from your shadow. They do not react to your presence at all. They do not speed up or slow down or change direction or stop. They simply continue their slow, deliberate movement across the stone as if you were not there, as if nothing could be there, as if the concept of a large, warm, oxygen-breathing visitor from lower altitudes were so far outside their experiential framework that it did not register as a stimulus.

This is not fearlessness. Fearlessness implies an awareness of danger and a decision to disregard it. The Patina does not disregard danger. It does not *perceive* danger. It does not perceive *anything* beyond the stone beneath it and the lichen beside it and the moisture that condenses around it. Its sensory world is so narrow, so focused, so perfectly adapted to the single task of existing on this stone in this wind at this altitude, that everything else — everything that is not stone and lichen and moisture — simply does not exist. You do not exist. The Pale Riders do not exist. The Thread-Singers do not exist. The screaming wind does not exist, or if it does, it exists only as a condition of the stone's temperature, a variable in the condensation equation, nothing more.

They reproduce, presumably. They must. But no one has observed it. No one has observed feeding, or mating, or death, or birth, or any of the biological processes that would explain how a population of millions maintains itself on a surface that produces almost no biomass. They simply *are*, in the same way that the stone simply is, and the light simply is, and the wind simply is. They are a fact of the Steppe, as fundamental and as unremarkable as the limestone itself.

And yet.

If you were to watch them for long enough — long enough for the wind to become silence and the light to become darkness and the altitude to pull your consciousness down to a single, narrow point of attention — you would notice that their movement is not random. It is not purposeful, either. It is *patterned*. The paths they trace on the stone, when viewed from above, when viewed over time, form shapes — not geometric shapes, not symbols, not language, but *patterns* that repeat across the surface of the ridge the way a motif repeats across a textile. The same curves, the same intersections, the same densities and sparsities, appearing again and again, as if the collective movement of millions of tiny bodies were producing a design that no individual body was aware of.

The design says nothing. It means nothing. It is simply what happens when millions of small, pale, translucent creatures move slowly across stone for longer than the stone has held its current shape. It is the Steppe's handwriting, written in the language of patience, in a script too large to be read from any single vantage point.

## The Stone Itself

This is the thing that is most difficult to say, and most necessary.

The stone is alive.

Not in the way that the lichens are alive, or the Patina, or the Pale Riders, or even the Thread-Singers. Not in the way that a forest is alive, or a coral reef, or a mycelial network stretching across miles of soil. The stone is alive in the way that a *place* is alive — in the way that a river delta is alive, or a weather system, or a city. It is alive at a scale and a speed that makes biological life look like a brief, frantic interruption in a much longer conversation.

The limestone is old. It is so old that it has begun to behave like a different substance, the description says, and this is true in a way that is more literal than it sounds. The stone of the Glass Steppe is not merely eroding. It is *thinking*. Not thinking in the way a brain thinks — not with neurons, not with electricity, not with anything that could be detected by an instrument designed to detect thought. It is thinking the way a crystal thinks, the way a mineral deposit thinks, through the slow accumulation and dissolution of structure, through the patient rearrangement of molecules over timescales that make geological epochs look like afternoons.

The translucent plates — the bone-glass — are not merely a geological curiosity. They are *memories*. Each plate is a record of a period when the stone was different, when the conditions at the surface were different, when the moisture and the temperature and the pressure conspired to produce a layer of calcium carbonate so pure and so thin that light could pass through it. The plates are stacked in the stone like pages in a book, like layers in a sediment core, like the rings of a tree, except that what they record is not years but *states* — states of the Steppe, states of the atmosphere, states of the light. The greenish glow that passes through them is not refracted sunlight. It is *stored* sunlight, light that was absorbed by the stone centuries ago and is being released now, slowly, the way a battery discharges, the way a memory surfaces.

The ridges themselves are not merely geological formations. They are *arguments*. The stone has been arguing with the wind for longer than there has been wind to argue with, and the ridges are the conclusion of that argument — the shape that the stone has arrived at after eons of being blown, the shape that cannot be improved upon, the shape that is the stone's final answer to the question of how to exist in a place where the wind screams forever. The gaps between the ridges are not absences. They are *concessions* — the stone acknowledging that the wind must pass through, that the air must move, that the space between solid things is as important as the solid things themselves.

The Glass Steppe is not a place where life happens to exist. It is a place that *is* life — that has achieved a form of existence so total, so complete, so perfectly balanced between the living and the nonliving, that the distinction has ceased to matter. The lichens are the stone's way of being soft. The Patina are the stone's way of being mobile. The Pale Riders are the stone's way of being airborne. The Thread-Singers are the stone's way of being heard. And the screaming wind is the stone's way of breathing.

You will not stay here. No one stays here. But the Steppe will stay in you — not as a memory, not as an experience, but as a *frequency*. The subsonic pulse that you felt in your sternum, the harmonic of wind through water in empty space, will continue in you long after you descend, long after the air thickens and the sounds become small and the light becomes gentle. It will be there in quiet moments, in the space between heartbeats, in the pause between one thought and the next. A faint vibration. A deep hum. The sound of a place that has found its voice and will not stop speaking.

The sound of the stone, thinking.
