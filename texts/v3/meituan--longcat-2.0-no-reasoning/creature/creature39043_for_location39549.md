The Griddle are not a species. They are a *condition*—the condition of a surface that has been touched so many times it has begun to touch back.

Each one is a flattened, compressed body, roughly lentil-sized, though size is a spectrum here rather than a category. The smallest are barely visible—specks of dull grey-green that catch the ambient light like dust on a still pool. The largest, the ancient ones, have the heft and stillness of river stones, their edges worn to soft curves by the constant friction of their own kind. Their bodies are translucent in the way that old wax is translucent: not clear, but *thin*, so that light passes through them and comes out changed, slightly warmer, slightly more tired. They are the color of lichen that has been dead for a long time but has not yet fallen off the rock. They are the color of the inside of a closed eye.

They have no faces. They have no limbs that you would recognize as limbs. What they have is *surface*—an enormous amount of it, relative to their volume, because they are so flat, so pressed, so thoroughly two-dimensional in a three-dimensional world. Each one is essentially a disc, a plate, a flake, and they stack and slide and overlap with the ease of pages rifled by wind. When they move, they do not walk or crawl or swim. They *shear*. One layer slides over another, and the friction between them produces that sound—that dense, granular, perpetual hiss that is the room's true voice, the voice of ten million surfaces in constant, gentle, unresolvable contact.

They are not individuals. This is the first thing you must understand, and the last thing you will fully accept. Each Griddle is a body, yes—a discrete, bounded, physical thing you could theoretically pick up and hold between your fingers. But it is not *separate*. They exist in a state of permanent mutual occlusion, each one partially covered by its neighbors, each one partially covering others, so that the entire mass functions as a single continuous sheet of living tissue that happens to be composed of countable units the way a beach is composed of countable grains of sand. You can count them. You would die counting them. But the counting would be a lie, because the thing you are counting is not a collection of individuals—it is a *surface that has become alive*.

They feed. This is the part that resists understanding. In a room with no water, no visible nutrients, no input of energy that you can detect, the Griddle persist—thrive, even, in their slow, dense, ancient way. The calciumite walls are their substrate, and they are in constant contact with it, and something passes between them. Not consumption in the way a mouth consumes—there are no mouths. More like *exchange*. The Griddle press against the mineral surface and the mineral surface presses back, and in that pressure, in that sustained, epoch-long contact, something is transferred. Calcium, perhaps. Or information. Or the simple fact of *touch*, which in a room this old and this dry and this undisturbed may be the only currency that matters. The walls have a faint give to them, an almost organic softness, and this is not erosion—this is *accommodation*. The calciumite has learned to be touched. It has been touched so thoroughly, for so long, that it has become something other than stone. It has become *skin*.

The Griddle tend the old iron bolts on the platforms with what looks like indifference but might be the deepest form of care—the care of a system that maintains its own infrastructure without knowing it is infrastructure. The bolts are rusted to a dark red so deep it is almost black, and the Griddle flow over them and around them and through the microscopic crevices of their corrosion, and the rust does not spread. It does not deepen. It is held in stasis by the constant, gentle pressure of living bodies, the way a wound is held closed by a bandage. The Griddle are not preserving the bolts. They are not aware of the bolts. But their presence—their density, their friction, their perpetual touch—has created a microenvironment in which decay has been slowed to a rate so close to zero that it might as well be stopped. The bolts are artifacts of a purpose now forgotten, and the Griddle are the custodians of that forgetting.

The large ones—the fist-sized ancients—move differently. They move *less*, for one thing, their migrations so slow that watching them is like watching a hourglass from across a room. But they also move with a quality that the smaller ones lack: *direction*. The small Griddle flow in sheets, in tides, in patterns that are collective rather than individual. The large ones move with something that looks, from certain angles, like intention. They cluster on the platforms. They arrange themselves in patterns that are not shapes—not circles, not lines, not spirals—but *repetitions*, recursive geometries that suggest a process running, a computation being performed in the medium of living bodies on mineral substrate. They are slow. They are so slow. But they are not stopped.

This is what lives in the Calcination Rooms: a fauna so dense it has become a surface, a surface so alive it has become a mind, a mind so slow it has become a room. The Griddle are not thinking. They are not conscious. They are not even, in any meaningful sense, alive by the definitions you carry with you. But they are *doing something*. They have been doing something for longer than the iron bolts have been rusting, longer than the calciumite has been smooth, longer than the light has been coming from nowhere. They are maintaining a process that was started by hands that are gone—hands that built the platforms, that set the bolts, that shaped the room into a vessel for something they needed to keep running.

And it is still running.

The room breathes. The Griddle migrate in their slow tidal patterns—floor to walls, walls to ceiling, ceiling to floor—and the density shifts, and the sound rises and falls in waves too long to perceive, and the ancient ones on the platforms rearrange themselves by fractions of a millimeter, and the calciumite walls press back against the constant touch with their faint, organic give, and the light from nowhere holds everything in its flat, directionless, 4:00 PM October glow, and the room is not a ruin.

It is a machine that has forgotten it is a machine, tended by a fauna that has forgotten it is fauna, in a space that has forgotten it is a space. The Griddle are the keepers of this forgetting. They are the process and the product, the custodians and the calcination itself—the slow, dry, mineral transformation of something into something else, in a room with no outside, by a life that has no name, in a time that has no measure.

They do not respond. They overlap. They are the loudest quiet you will ever hear, and they have been hearing it since before you were born, and they will be hearing it after the last word for what they are has been forgotten, and the room will still be running, and the light will still be coming from nowhere, and the sound will still be the sound of every surface touching every other surface, perpetually, in the dark that is not dark, in the silence that is not silent, in the dry that has never known water, in the place that has no outside.
