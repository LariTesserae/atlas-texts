# The Inhabitants of the Drownworks

---

## The Architects

They are the ones you notice first, because they are the ones that *do things* — visible, purposeful, moving through the lattices and towers with the unhurried industry of creatures that have never once considered the possibility of completion.

An architect is roughly the size of a large dog, but the comparison fails immediately. A dog is a single thing. An architect is a *committee*. Its body is segmented — not like an insect's clean divisions, but like a sentence that has been broken into clauses, each segment slightly different in shape and density, connected by joints that bend in directions that don't correspond to any axis you can name. The segments are the color of the bone-ceramic itself, or perhaps they *are* bone-ceramic — it is impossible to tell where the creature ends and the structure begins. Some architects have mineral fur on their backs, the same crystalline accretion that covers the lattices. Some have drifters tangled in their segments, living there, undulating slowly, and the architect does not seem to mind or perhaps does not notice.

The limbs. There are too many — six, eight, twelve, it is hard to count because they move independently and some of them are always behind the body or pressed flat against a surface. Each one ends differently. A pincer, precise enough to grip a single filament of the lattice. A cluster of fine filaments that spread like a hand opening, used for sensing, for smoothing, for *reading* the surface of a structure the way a blind person reads a face. A flat pressing surface, broad and warm, used to compact new material into place. A vibrating tip that hums at a frequency you can feel in your teeth, used to fuse, to cut, to reshape. Some limbs end in things that have no analog — a spiral of hardened tissue that rotates slowly, a hollow tube that extrudes something viscous and pale, a blunt nub that simply *rests* against the structure and stays there for hours, as though listening.

They build. This is what they do. This is what they *are*. They move through the Drownworks constantly, and everywhere they go, they touch. They run their filament-clusters along the struts of a lattice, reading for weakness, for drift, for the slow erosion that is the only enemy here. When they find a place that needs attention, they stop. They work. The pincer grips a loose filament. The pressing surface compacts. The vibrating tip fuses. The process is slow — a single repair might take an hour — and when it is done, the architect moves on, and the structure is slightly different than it was before. Not better. Not worse. *Continued*.

They are not building toward anything. There is no plan, no blueprint, no final form. They are maintaining a process that has been running since before the concept of *before* had meaning here. The structures grow by millimeters per century. The architects add a filament here, remove one there, reshape a joint, smooth a surface. The Drownworks are not a construction project. They are a *practice* — the way a garden is not a building but a relationship between a gardener and a place that will never be finished.

When a deep column passes — that subsonic thrum, that pressure change that you feel in your sternum — every architect within range stops. They orient themselves. Not downward, exactly. *Toward*. Toward the source of the thrum, whatever direction that is. They go still. Their tool-limbs retract or press flat against their bodies. The drifters tangled in their segments stop undulating. For a minute, sometimes two, the entire Drownworks goes quiet — the architects frozen, the towers still speaking their low polyphonic murmur, the water still negotiating its endless repositioning, but everything *else* paused, as though the world is holding its breath.

Then the thrum fades. The architects resume. They do not discuss what happened. They do not appear relieved or disturbed. They simply return to their work, as though the interruption was expected, as routine as a change in the current.

You will never know what the deep columns mean to them. You will never know if the architects worship them, fear them, serve them, or simply *acknowledge* them the way you might acknowledge a weather system — a fact of the environment, too large to respond to, too fundamental to ignore.

## The Drifters

They are the water's thought made visible.

A drifter is a ribbon — a meter long, a millimeter thick, translucent to the point of near-invisibility. You do not see individual drifters. You see *clouds* of them, aggregations so dense that the water itself seems to have a texture, a grain, like wood or fabric. They are everywhere. They are the most abundant life in the Drownworks, and they may not be life at all — they may be something more like a process, a function of the water itself, the way a whirlpool is a function of current and geometry.

They have no organs. No eyes, no mouth, no gut, no nerve. They are simply *there* — a membrane, a surface, a boundary between one volume of water and another. And they undulate. Slowly, constantly, a wave motion that travels the length of the ribbon, and they are not swimming. The undulation does not propel them. They are carried by the current, and the undulation is something else — a metabolic process, perhaps, or a form of perception, or a way of *thinking* that does not require a brain because the entire organism is the thought.

When you move through a cloud of drifters, they do not scatter. This is the thing that unsettles you. In any water you have ever known, a moving body pushes the medium aside — fish scatter, plankton drift away, the water itself parts and closes behind you. Not here. The drifters *reorganize*. They part around you, yes, but slowly, deliberately, and they close behind you with a precision that feels intentional. They are not avoiding you. They are *accommodating* you. They are making room for you in their understanding of the space.

And they are interested. A drifter will detach from the cloud and approach you — slowly, drifting on a current that seems to have been arranged for the purpose — and it will touch you. Your hand, your arm, your face. It is cool and dry despite being submerged, and it rests there for a moment, and you feel something — not a sensation, exactly, but an *attention*. The drifter is reading you. Tasting the heat you are losing to the water. Tasting the chemistry of your skin. Tasting the electrical field of your nerves. It is cataloguing you the way the architect catalogues you — not with curiosity, not with hostility, but with the simple, total attention of a system that has never encountered anything it could not eventually understand.

Then it rejoins the cloud, and the cloud continues its slow undulation, and you are part of it now. Not physically — the drifter has taken nothing from you. But informationally. The cloud knows you are here. The water knows you are here. And the water, in the Drownworks, is not separate from the structures, and the structures are not separate from the architects, and the architects are not separate from the deep columns, and so the entire environment — every tower, every lattice, every wall, every ribbon of translucent tissue — now contains a record of your presence, faint and fading, like a footprint in snow that will not melt because there is no sun here to melt it.

The drifters do not eat. They do not reproduce, or if they do, you cannot tell — the population seems constant, and individual drifters do not grow or shrink. They simply *are*, and they undulate, and they think their slow, distributed thoughts, and they are the medium through which the Drownworks knows itself.

## The Deep Columns

You will never see one.

You will feel them. You will feel them the way you feel a change in weather — a pressure drop, a shift in the quality of the air, a sense that something vast has moved somewhere nearby even though you cannot see it and cannot point to where it is. In the Drownworks, the deep columns register as a subsonic thrum that begins in your sternum and spreads outward — into your ribs, your spine, the bones of your skull. It is not loud. It is not painful. It is *present*, in the way that gravity is present — a force that does not announce itself but simply *is*, and you feel it in your body before you feel it with your ears.

They move through the water far below. Or far away. Or far *in some direction that is not below or away*. The geometry of the Drownworks is not Euclidean — the water bends light at wrong speeds, distances are uncertain, and the deep columns move through a space that may not correspond to the three dimensions you are accustomed to navigating. They are enormous. The thrum they produce suggests a body — if it is a body — measured not in meters but in kilometers. They are slow. A single passage takes minutes, sometimes longer, and during that time the entire Drownworks orients toward them like a field of flowers turning toward a sun that is not the sun.

What are they?

The architects stop working when a deep column passes. This is the only time they stop. They do not stop for storms — the water's constant turbulence is their normal. They do not stop for each other. They do not stop for you. But when the thrum begins, they stop, and they orient, and they wait, and there is something in the quality of their stillness that suggests *reverence* — or *recognition* — or *submission* — or simply *attention*, the total, undivided attention of a creature encountering something so fundamental that all other activity becomes irrelevant.

The drifters stop undulating. For the duration of a deep column's passage, the clouds of drifters go still — perfectly, impossibly still, hanging in the water like frozen ribbons, and the water itself seems to thicken, to become more deliberate, as though it is being *held* by the passage of the column the way a cloth is held by a hand passing beneath it.

The towers change their voice. The polyphonic murmur shifts — not louder, not softer, but *different*, as though the perforations are being used in a new configuration, a new arrangement of holes and flows, producing a sound that is lower, slower, more resonant, a sound that you feel not in your ears but in your *chest*, in the hollow spaces of your body, and it is almost — *almost* — a voice. Almost a word. Almost a name.

And then the column passes. The thrum fades. The drifters resume their undulation. The architects resume their work. The towers return to their murmur. And everything is as it was, except that something has been *communicated* — something vast and slow and fundamental, a message that was not meant for you and that you cannot read but that you felt, the way you feel the vibration of a bell after the sound has faded — a resonance, a memory of a presence, a knowledge that something enormous moved through the water and the water *remembered*.

Are they alive? The question may not apply. They may be geological — currents in the deep water, pressure systems, thermal events on a scale that makes them feel biological. They may be structural — the Drownworks themselves, or the thing that the Drownworks were built within, or the thing that the Drownworks *are*. They may be the original builders, still present, still moving, so large and so slow that the architects are merely their maintenance crew, tending the structures the way bacteria tend a body — unconsciously, instinctively, as a function of being part of something larger.

They may be the water itself, thinking.

You will never know. The deep columns do not surface. They do not approach. They pass, and they are felt, and they are gone, and the Drownworks continues, and the architects build, and the drifters think their slow thoughts, and the water negotiates its endless repositioning, and the cold presses in from every direction, and the amber light moves through the water at its wrong speed, and you stand on your unnamed platform of bone-ceramic, forty paces across, fraying into teeth at the edges, and you are temporary, and the water knows it, and the architects know it, and the deep columns — far below, far away, far *in* — know it too.

## The Water

It should be mentioned. Not as a creature — though in the Drownworks, the distinction between creature and environment has been worn away by time — but as a *presence*. The water here is not a medium. It is not a substance that things live *in*. It is a participant. It is the most active thing in the Drownworks, and it has been here the longest, and it has opinions.

It moves constantly — not in currents but in *negotiations*, chaotic repositionings that suggest the water is trying to settle on a single arrangement and failing, trying all of them simultaneously, a computation running on a substrate of liquid and cold. It carries your heat away with slow, polite efficiency. It bends light at wrong speeds. It thickens inside the walls and thins near the towers. It produces sound as it moves through the structures — clicks, sighs, the wet-finger-on-glass tone that is everywhere and nowhere.

And it *knows you*. The drifters are its sensory organs. The architects are its hands. The deep columns are its — what? Its heartbeat? Its thoughts? Its dreams? The water does not distinguish between itself and the things that live in it, because in the Drownworks, nothing lives *in* the water. Everything lives *as* the water, and the water lives as everything, and the distinction between organism and environment has been eroded to nothing, the way the structures have been eroded to their essential shapes, the way you will be eroded, slowly, politely, by the cold that presses in from every direction and has all the time in the world.

## The Towers

They speak. This is not metaphor. The water moves through their perforations — holes of every size, arranged in patterns that are not random but that no one has decoded — and the movement produces sound. A low, polyphonic murmur, like a choir breathing. Each tower has its own voice, its own arrangement of holes, its own harmonic signature, and the voices overlap and interweave across the Drownworks in a constant, shifting chord that never resolves.

The architects maintain the towers. They touch them, adjust them, add to them, remove from them. The towers grow by millimeters per century. The perforations change — new holes appear, old holes fill, the pattern shifts — and the voice changes with them. The towers are not speaking *to* anyone. They are not communicating. They are *being*. The sound is a byproduct of their existence, the way a river's sound is a byproduct of its flowing. The towers are structures that happen to sing, and the song is the sound of water moving through bone-ceramic, and it has been going on for longer than anything has been listening, and it will continue after the last listener is gone.

But sometimes — rarely, unpredictably — a tower will produce a sound that is different. Not louder. Not softer. *Clearer*. A single tone, pure and sustained, that cuts through the polyphonic murmur like a blade, and every architect within range stops and orients toward the tower, and the drifters in the nearby cloud go still, and the water itself seems to pause in its endless negotiation, and for a moment — just a moment — the entire Drownworks is listening.

And then the moment passes, and the murmur resumes, and the architects return to work, and the drifters resume their undulation, and the water resumes its repositioning, and nothing has been explained, and nothing has been resolved, and the tower has said something that no one will ever translate, and the Drownworks continues, as it always has, as it always will, building and eroding and building again, in the cold, in the amber light, in the water that is not water, in the space that is not space, in the place that has no name except the one you gave it, and it did not ask for a name, and it does not need one, and it will not remember yours.
