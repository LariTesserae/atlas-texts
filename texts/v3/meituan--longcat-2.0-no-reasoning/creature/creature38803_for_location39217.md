# What Lives in the Breathery

---

## The Mass

It has no name that the settlers use. They call it "the tree," sometimes "the system," sometimes nothing at all — just *it*, the way you might refer to the weather or the sea, something so total that naming it feels like a category error. It is not a tree. It was a tree, once, centuries ago, a single organism rooted in whatever soil existed before it consumed the soil and replaced it with itself. Now it is a continuous living architecture — root and trunk and branch and tendril and bark-analog and leaf, all one body, all one circulatory system, spreading across an area that no one has fully mapped because mapping it would require cutting into it, and cutting into it is the one thing no one does without permission, and the permission comes from laminated signs written by people who are, in the end, tenants.

The Mass is not intelligent in any way that the settlers can agree on. It is reactive. It responds to vibration, to pressure, to the presence of foreign objects embedded in its body. When a bracket is bolted into its wood, it does not reject the bracket — not immediately. It grows around it, slowly, over months and years, testing the intrusion the way a body tests a splinter: not with immune response but with accommodation, with gradual incorporation. Sometimes the accommodation becomes constriction. A root thickens beneath a bracket until the metal shears. A tendril finds a pipe and wraps it, gently, over the course of a season, until the flow is restricted and someone has to come with a blade and cut it free, leaving a wound that the Mass seals over with amber resin within days.

It breathes. This is not metaphor. The settlers have instruments — the man with the stethoscope-and-gauge is reading turgor pressure, or moisture flux, or something adjacent to both — and the data confirms what the body knows: the Mass operates on a cycle. During light hours, transpiration. Moure pushed out through stomata in the bark-analog, millions of tiny mouths exhaling water vapor into the hollow, raising the humidity until the air is almost liquid, until your lungs feel like they're working in something thicker than air. Then, as light fades, the cycle reverses. The stomata close. The cells adjust. The whole structure contracts — a centimeter, two centimeters — and the sound changes from the patter of falling condensation to a deeper, more structural creaking, the sound of wood under tension, the sound of something enormous shifting its weight in its sleep.

The Mass is warm in its depths. Below the lowest platform, where the roots have been growing without human guidance for longer than the settlement has existed, the surface temperature rises. The roots there pulse — a slow, peristaltic rhythm, as if fluid were being moved through internal channels from somewhere to somewhere else. The engineers have theories about this. They have theories about a lot of things. They do not test the theories about the deep roots, because testing would require going below the lowest platform, and no one goes below the lowest platform.

The Mass is not hostile. This is important to understand. It is not hostile the way a predator is hostile, or the way a fire is hostile. It is indifferent in the way that geology is indifferent — it operates on timescales and according to imperatives that do not include human welfare as a variable. It does not want to hurt the settlers. It does not want anything from the settlers. It is simply growing, and the settlers are growing inside it, and the two processes are not always compatible, and the incompatibility is managed through rules and caution and the slow, patient work of people who have learned that the thing they live inside is alive and does not care whether they live or die.

## The Settlers

There are not many of them. The Breathery supports perhaps a dozen people at any given time, though the number fluctuates — someone leaves, someone arrives, someone goes up to the surface for supplies and comes back changed by the sky and takes a few days to readjust to the dimness and the drip and the breathing. They are not a community in the way that word usually implies. They are a crew. A shift. A roster of people who have been hired or who have inherited or who have simply stayed, the way people stay in places that are difficult to leave not because they can't but because the leaving would require an explanation they don't have.

The woman in the waxed canvas coat is named Orla, though you don't know that yet. She has been here for six years. She processes fiber — the pale, flexible root tips that are harvested from the mid-level tendrils, stripped of their outer bark, dried in the workshop's dehydrator, and spun into a thread that is stronger than hemp and more flexible than wire. She is good at it. Her hands move with the particular efficiency of someone who has done the same task ten thousand times and will do it ten thousand more. She does not look up when she crosses the walkway because there is nothing to look at that she has not already seen, and because looking up would require acknowledging the scale of the thing she lives inside, and she has learned, as all the settlers learn, to keep her gaze at the level of the immediate: the walkway, the bundle, the next task.

The man with the stethoscope is newer. He arrived three months ago, sent by whatever organization funds the settlement — a university, a corporation, a government agency, the details are unclear and no one asks because asking would imply that the answer matters, and it doesn't, not here, not when the work is the work regardless of who signs the checks. He is measuring something. Turgor pressure, probably, or the rate of moisture flux through a particular root segment. He presses the bell of the stethoscope against the bark and watches the gauge and writes numbers in a notebook that is already softening at the edges from the humidity. He is trying to understand the breathing cycle. He will not understand it. No one has. The cycle is not regular enough to predict and not irregular enough to dismiss as noise. It is something in between — a pattern that exists but resists formalization, like weather, like mood.

The child is named Fen. Fen is nine, or ten, or eleven — no one is quite sure, including Fen, who has lived in the Breathery for as long as memory extends and whose relationship to time is governed not by calendars but by the breathing cycle, the harvest schedule, the slow tilt of the walkways. Fen is the child who is always there, the child who is always in these places, because the Breathery is the kind of place that accumulates children the way it accumulates moisture — not through any deliberate process but through the simple fact that it is a place where people live, and where people live, children eventually appear, and where children appear in a place like this, they learn to watch beetles with total absorption, because the beetles are reliable in a way that nothing else in the Breathery is reliable. The beetles are small and dark and they move in straight lines and they eat the algae and they do not breathe and they do not pulse and they do not slowly, patiently swallow the infrastructure.

The settlers do not socialize in any structured way. They share the workshop. They share the rules. They share the particular quality of attention that the Breathery demands — a constant, low-level awareness of the thing around them, the way a sailor is aware of the sea, the way a miner is aware of the rock. They do not share much else. Conversations are brief and functional. Meals are taken alone, on platforms, standing or sitting on crates, looking at the root-walls or the walkways or the light filtering down from above. There is a loneliness to the place that is not unpleasant — it is the loneliness of being inside something larger than yourself, of being a small organism in a large body, tolerated but not necessary.

## The Beetles

They are everywhere. Small, dark, hard-shelled, with legs that click against the steel grating in a sound that is almost musical if you listen to it in aggregate — a constant, arrhythmic clicking, like rain on a tin roof but drier, more percussive. They are a species that has adapted to the Breathery's particular conditions: the constant humidity, the mildly acidic moisture, the absence of direct sunlight, the abundance of algae on every root surface. They eat the algae. That is what they do. They move across the root-walls in loose aggregations, their mandibles working at the thin green film that grows on the bark-analog, and they are so perfectly suited to this single task that it is difficult to imagine them existing anywhere else.

They are not native. No one knows where they came from. They may have arrived with the first settlers, carried in on equipment or clothing or supplies, and found the conditions inside the Mass so ideal that they established a population that has now been self-sustaining for decades. Or they may have been here before the settlers, part of the Mass's own ecology, a species that evolved in the hollow's unique microclimate and that the settlers simply noticed and named. No one has studied them formally. They are too small, too common, too unremarkable. They are the background fauna, the equivalent of mice in a house or pigeons in a city — present, persistent, beneath notice.

Except to Fen, who watches them with the intensity of a naturalist, crouched at the edge of a platform, tracking a single beetle as it navigates a crack in the grating. The beetle pauses. Its antennae wave. It changes direction. It finds the edge of the crack and crosses it, one leg at a time, with a deliberateness that seems almost philosophical. Fen watches. The beetle does not know it is being watched. The beetle does not know anything, probably. But Fen knows the beetle, in the way that a child knows a thing they have watched for a long time — not scientifically, not taxonomically, but intimately, the way you know a face, the way you know a voice.

## The Gecko-Analogs

There are not many of them. You see one every few days, maybe — a pale shape against the dark wood, motionless, then suddenly not, lunging at a beetle with a speed that seems impossible for something so still a moment before. They are small, no longer than a hand, with wide, flat feet that adhere to the slick bark through some mechanism that the engineers have not examined closely. They are pale — not white, but a translucent, pinkish gray, as if they have never been exposed to direct sunlight, which they haven't. Their eyes are large and dark and they do not blink.

They hunt the beetles. That is their role in the Breathery's ecology — the only predator in a system that has no other. They do not threaten the settlers. They do not interact with the settlers at all, except to flee when a human approaches, pressing themselves flat against the root-walls and then vanishing into cracks and crevices with a speed that makes it seem as though they were never there. They are shy in the way that all things are shy in the Breathery — not fearful, exactly, but private, self-contained, operating according to their own imperatives in the spaces between human activity.

## The Thing in the Canopy

No one has seen it clearly. There is a rustling, sometimes, in the upper reaches of the hollow — above the highest platform, above the last walkway, in the dense tangle of roots and branches where the light from the surface filters through in shafts and the condensation is heaviest. A rustling that is too large to be a beetle, too deliberate to be wind. A shadow against the light filter, brief, gone. The settlers hear it and do not discuss it. It is part of the unwritten agreement — the understanding that the Mass has its own ecology, its own inhabitants, and that the settlement's jurisdiction extends only as far as the infrastructure goes, and no further.

It might be a bird. It might be something that was a bird once and has been incorporated into the Mass's system the way everything else is incorporated — slowly, patiently, over years, until the boundary between organism and environment dissolves. It might be something that the Mass itself has grown — a structure, an organ, a sensory apparatus, something that rustles and casts shadows and is not alive in any way that the settlers would recognize but is not dead either. No one investigates. No one wants to know. The Breathery gives them fiber and resin and amber fluid and a place to live and work, and in return it asks only that they stay on the platforms and do not go below and do not look too closely at the things that move in the canopy above.

## The Workshop

It is the only place in the Breathery that sounds like the surface world. The mechanical press clanks — a heavy, rhythmic compression that processes root fiber into dense, usable sheets. The generator whines, burning biodiesel that is hauled up from the surface in drums, the exhaust vented through a pipe that runs up along a root and disappears into the canopy, where it is absorbed or dispersed or incorporated, no one is sure which. Steam hisses. Metal clangs against metal. The fluorescent lights buzz and flicker, casting everything in a flat, shadowless glare that makes the root-walls look diseased, exposed, like flesh under a surgical lamp.

The workshop is where the Mass becomes product. Root tips go in — pale, flexible, still damp with transpiration — and come out as fiber, as resin, as the dense amber fluid that is the Breathery's primary export, a substance with properties that no synthetic chemist has fully replicated: it is adhesive and waterproof and biocompatible and it cures at room temperature into a material harder than epoxy and more flexible than rubber. It is worth more than its weight in most things. It is the reason the settlement exists. It is the reason the platforms and walkways and brackets and rules exist. It is the reason Orla strips bark and Fen watches beetles and the man with the stethoscope writes numbers in a softening notebook. The Mass produces it. The settlers harvest it. The arrangement holds.

The workshop is also where the rules are most visible. The laminated signs at each access point — *Do not cut primary structural roots. Do not harvest within 2 meters of a node cluster. Report any signs of reactive thickening.* — are supplemented here by more specific instructions, handwritten on metal plates bolted to the walls, some of them so old that the wood has begun to grow over their edges. *If the root bleeds amber, stop cutting and wait. It will seal. Do not apply accelerator to fresh cuts. Do not harvest during the breathing-in phase — the fiber quality degrades.* The rules are practical, not reverential. The settlers do not worship the Mass. They work with it, the way a farmer works with soil, the way a fisherman works with the sea — with respect born not of awe but of experience, of having seen what happens when the rules are ignored.

## The Sound

Under everything, the sound. The drip of condensation, leaf to leaf to root to platform, never reaching the ground. The creak of wood under tension, the slow contraction of the breathing-in phase. The click of beetle feet on steel. The distant clank of the press. The whine of the generator. The hiss of steam. And underneath it all, if you press your ear to the wall — if you are brave enough, if you can stand the intimacy of it, the feeling of your skull against living wood, the damp seeping into your hair — the breathing. Not metaphorical. Actual. An inhalation that takes twenty minutes, slow and patient, the platform rising almost imperceptibly beneath you, the air pressure shifting, the condensation pausing mid-fall as if the world has drawn a breath and is holding it. Then the exhalation, twenty minutes more, the platform settling, the condensation resuming, the air thickening with moisture pushed out through millions of stomata, and the whole cycle beginning again, and again, and again, a rhythm older than the settlement, older than the rules, older than the laminated signs and the rusted brackets and the snapped walkways and the child watching beetles and the woman carrying fiber and the man with the stethoscope pressed against the bark, reading something, writing something down, trying to understand something that does not want to be understood, that is not hostile to understanding but simply operates on a scale and according to a logic that does not include understanding as a variable, the way the sea does not include the fish's understanding of the sea as a variable in its tides.

The Mass breathes. The settlers work. The beetles eat the algae. The gecko-analogs hunt the beetles. The thing in the canopy rustles and is not seen. The moisture falls and falls and falls, never hitting the ground, only finding another surface, another leaf, another root, another bead of water joining another bead of water joining another, rolling slowly down through the endless green dimness of the Breathery, settling, settling, settling, like sediment, like a house on old foundations, like something that has been here a long time and will be here a long time after the brackets have snapped and the walkways have tilted past use and the fluorescent lights have burned out and the last settler has gone up the narrow stone stair and emerged into the sky and stood there, blinking, readjusting to the light and the wind and the silence — the silence most of all, the absence of the drip and the creak and the breathing, the terrible silence of a world that does not breathe, that does not pulse, that does not slowly, patiently, incorporate everything it touches into itself.
