# The Inhabitants of Aeth

---

## The Gardeners

They are not the first thing you notice. They are not the second thing. They are the thing you notice last, because they have learned to move at the speed of the place, and the place does not hurry.

There are perhaps forty of them. Perhaps sixty. Counting is difficult because they do not gather. They do not cluster. They distribute themselves across the terraces the way nutrients distribute through soil — evenly, invisibly, according to a logic that is chemical rather than social. You might walk the length of the settlement and see no one, and then find three of them in a single greenhouse, working in silence, and understand that this is not unusual. This is the density. This is the population doing what it does.

They are small. Not short — small. Compact. Their bodies have a quality of *compression*, as though the ambient moisture has pressed them slightly over the years, the way it presses everything, and they have accommodated. Their hands are broad and flat, with fingers that seem to have an extra joint, or perhaps one fewer — it is hard to tell because they move with a fluidity that makes discrete joints difficult to track. Their skin is pale, not from lack of light but from the mineral coating that settles on everything in Aeth. They are dusted with it. It gives them a faintly luminous quality in the dim greenhouse light, as though they are slowly becoming the stone they quarry.

Their eyes are large. Not dramatically so — not the exaggerated orbs of a deep-sea creature — but large enough to notice, large enough to suggest that vision in Aeth is a different task than vision elsewhere, that the dim diffuse light requires more surface area, more gathering. The irises are dark, the color of the warm pools, and they do not reflect light the way human eyes reflect light. They absorb it. When a Gardener looks at you, you feel a slight pull, the same faint suction you feel near the Pillars, and you understand that their gaze has a quality of *collection* — they are taking you in the way the water takes in mineral, the way the membrane takes in light.

They do not speak, or they do not speak in air. They communicate through touch — a hand on a shoulder, fingers pressed briefly against a forearm, a palm laid flat against the wall of a greenhouse as though reading the structure's slow cure. When two Gardeners meet on a path between terraces, they stop, and one of them places a hand on the other's chest, over the heart, and holds it there for several seconds. This is greeting. This is inquiry. This is *are you still becoming, am I still becoming, is the place still becoming*. The answer is always yes. The answer is always the only answer this place can give.

They tend the greenhouses with a precision that is not mechanical but *botanical* — they move through the dense vegetation the way tendrils move through a thicket, finding paths that are not visible from outside, pressing through gaps that close behind them. They prune, but their pruning is strange: they do not cut away dead growth so much as redirect living growth, bending a stem here, tying a tendril there, guiding the volume of vegetation into configurations that serve purposes you cannot identify. The greenhouses are not gardens in any conventional sense. They are *negotiations* — ongoing conversations between the Gardeners and the organisms they cultivate, conversations conducted in the language of growth and restraint, of encouragement and redirection.

They harvest the membrane-secretion from the warm pools. This is delicate work. The organism that produces it — the thing between mollusk and fungus — is not domesticated. It is not tame. It is *accommodated*. The Gardeners wade into the pools, which are warm as blood, and they reach beneath the surface and they *wait*. The organism responds to their presence — slowly, over minutes — by extruding a thick, glossy strand of secretion, which the Gardener draws out with a steady hand, coiling it around a wooden frame. The organism does not resist. It does not cooperate. It simply *does what it does*, and the Gardener's role is to be present for it, to receive it, to shape the receiving into something useful. The dried membrane becomes the translucent skin of the greenhouses. The organism continues in the pool, slightly diminished, and over days it replenishes. This is the relationship. This is the economy. Nothing is taken. Everything is received.

They cut stone. This is the hardest work, and the youngest Gardeners do it — or rather, the ones who are still flexible enough to spend hours kneeling at the quarry face, working the soft pale rock with copper blades before it commits to hardness. The stone resists, but gently. It is not yet sure it wants to be stone. The young Gardeners coax it. They cut blocks that are rough and imprecise, and they carry them — on their backs, in woven baskets, one at a time — up the terraces to where the greenhouses are growing, where new structures are always needed because the old ones are always hardening, always becoming too brittle, always approaching the end of their useful life even as they approach the beginning of their final form.

They do not sleep, or they do not sleep in ways you can observe. They rest. They sit in the greenhouses, in the warm mist, among the dense vegetation, and they become still — not unconscious, not inert, but *still*, the way a plant is still, the way a pool is still. Their breathing slows. Their eyes remain open, or half-open, and they watch the growth around them with an attention that does not seem effortful. They are not sleeping. They are *attending*. They are doing the thing that the place requires of its inhabitants, which is to be present for the becoming.

## The Pool-Organisms

In the warm water, beneath the surface, there are things.

The membrane-producers are the most visible — pale, soft, roughly ovoid masses that rest in the shallows like submerged cushions. They are the size of a human torso, or larger, and their surface is slick and faintly iridescent, shifting between colors that do not have names in any language you know — not quite green, not quite gold, not quite the color of a bruise but adjacent to it. They do not move, or they do not move quickly. But they *respond*. When a Gardener enters the pool, the nearest organism shifts — not physically, not in position, but in *density*, becoming slightly more opaque, slightly more present, as though it is paying attention. The secretion begins to flow. The organism is doing what it does.

There are other things in the pools. The pale shapes you see moving beneath the surface — they are larger than larvae, smaller than fish, and they have a quality of *incompletion* that mirrors everything else in Aeth. They have bodies, but the bodies are provisional. Fins that might become limbs. Segments that might become vertebrae. Eyes that are not yet committed to being eyes — dark spots on the head that might be organs of vision or might be something else, something that has not yet decided its function. They move through the warm water with a slow, purposeful undulation, and they do not avoid the Gardeners. They circle the Gardeners' legs. They press against the Gardeners' hands. They are curious, or they are something that resembles curiosity — an orientation toward the new, the warm, the living.

The Gardeners do not eat them. The Gardeners do not name them. The Gardeners regard them the way they regard the Pillars — as features of the place, as phenomena that are part of the ongoing process of becoming, as things that are not yet what they will be and may never be what they will be and are, for now, exactly what they are.

Sometimes, very rarely, one of the pale shapes will leave the water. It will drag itself onto the warm stone at the pool's edge and lie there, breathing — or doing something that resembles breathing, a slow expansion and contraction of its central segment — and the Gardeners will watch it. They do not interfere. They do not help. They watch, with their large dark absorbing eyes, and after some time — hours, sometimes a full day — the shape returns to the water, or it doesn't, and if it doesn't, it becomes part of the mineral coating, part of the slow accumulation, part of the place's patient project of building itself up.

## The Wall-Dwellers

In the soft stone of the greenhouses, there are burrowers.

They are small — the length of a finger, the width of a pencil — and they are segmented, and they are numerous. They move through the porous stone the way worms move through soil, leaving behind a network of fine tunnels that weaken the structure even as they aerate it, even as they perform some function that the Gardeners seem to value. The Gardeners do not kill them. The Gardeners do not evict them. The Gardeners *maintain* them, in the way they maintain everything — by accommodating, by redirecting, by allowing.

The wall-dwellers are pale, like everything in Aeth that lives in darkness. They have no eyes. They have mouths that are not quite mouths — openings at the front of the leading segment that may be for feeding or may be for sensing or may be for both. They eat the stone, or they eat something in the stone — the mineral content, the organic residue of the membrane-adhesive, the microscopic algae that colonize every surface in the valley. They excrete something. The Gardeners collect it. It is used in the greenhouses, mixed into the soil, fed to the plants. This is the cycle. This is the economy of the small. The wall-dwellers eat the buildings and feed the gardens and the gardens feed the Gardeners and the Gardeners maintain the buildings and the buildings feed the wall-dwellers. Nothing is wasted. Nothing is finished. Everything is in transit.

## The Thing Above

You never see it clearly.

It passes through the moisture-veil at irregular intervals — once in a day, once in a week, once in a span of time that might be a month if months existed here. You see the disturbance first: a thinning of the mist, a brief clearing, a column of slightly-less-dense air descending from above like an inverted Pillar. And then the silhouette.

It is large. That is the only thing you can say with certainty. It is large the way the valley is large — not in specific dimensions but in *presence*, in the way it occupies space, in the way the space around it reorganizes itself to accommodate its passage. The silhouette suggests wings, or it suggests fins, or it suggests something that is both and neither — broad, flat appendages that move through the thick air with a slow, powerful rhythm, not flapping but *rowing*, pulling the body forward through the moisture the way a swimmer pulls through water.

It does not land. It does not descend below the level of the greenhouse roofs. It passes overhead, and the Gardeners look up — all of them, simultaneously, wherever they are — and they watch it with their large dark eyes, and their expression is not fear and is not reverence and is not curiosity. It is *recognition*. They know this thing. They have always known this thing. It is part of the place, as the Pillars are part of the place, as the warm pools are part of the place. It is a feature of the unfinished world, a component of the becoming.

It makes no sound, or it makes a sound that is below the threshold of your hearing — you feel it in your chest, in your teeth, in the fluid of your inner ear, a vibration that is not quite a tone, not quite a hum, but something deeper, something that resonates with the same frequency as the note that the pools occasionally produce. You wonder if they are connected. You wonder if the thing above and the thing below are the same thing, or are parts of the same thing, or are two things engaged in the same slow process of deciding what they are.

The Gardeners do not speak of it. If they speak of it, they speak of it in the language of touch, in the language of palm-on-chest, and the meaning is not translatable. The meaning is: *it is here, it has always been here, it will be here when we are finished becoming, if we ever finish becoming, which we will not*.

## The Greenhouses Themselves

This is the thing you must understand, and it is the thing that is hardest to understand:

The greenhouses are not buildings that contain life. They are *organisms* that contain buildings.

The pale stone is the skeleton. The membrane is the skin. The warm mist is the breath. The dense vegetation is not decoration, not crop, not garden — it is *metabolism*. The greenhouses are alive, or they are something adjacent to alive, something that has not yet decided whether alive is the category it wants to occupy. They grow. They respire. They respond to the Gardeners' touch the way the pool-organisms respond to the Gardeners' presence — by becoming slightly more opaque, slightly more present, slightly more *there*.

The oldest greenhouses, the ones lowest on the terraces, closest to the pools, are the most alive. Their stone has hardened past the point of workability, and their membrane has thickened and darkened until it is nearly opaque, and the vegetation inside has achieved a density that is no longer botanical but *architectural* — the plants have formed walls, have formed floors, have formed chambers and corridors and spaces that serve the functions of rooms without having been designed as rooms. The Gardeners move through these oldest structures with a particular tenderness, touching the walls, pressing their palms against the plant-formed surfaces, and the walls respond — a slight shift, a slight tightening, the way a muscle responds to being touched.

The newest greenhouses, highest on the terraces, are more stone than life. Their membrane is thin and clear. Their vegetation is sparse, experimental, still deciding what it wants to be. The young Gardeners work here, tending the new growth, guiding the new stone, and the greenhouses around them are quiet, are waiting, are doing the thing that everything in Aeth does, which is to become.

And between the oldest and the newest, the greenhouses exist in every stage of the transition from built to grown, from stone to life, from structure to organism. This is the spectrum. This is the arc. This is the trajectory of every building in Aeth, from the moment it is quarried and stacked to the moment it is absorbed into the living mass of the settlement, becoming not a ruin but a *body*, not a corpse but a *component* of something larger that is still deciding what it wants to be.

---

## The Place Itself

And beneath all of this — beneath the Gardeners and the pool-organisms and the wall-dwellers and the thing above and the greenhouses that are slowly becoming alive — there is the warmth.

The warmth that comes from below. The warmth that has no source you can identify. The warmth that keeps the pools at the temperature of blood, that keeps the greenhouses at the temperature of growth, that keeps the entire valley at the temperature of *something recently alive*.

It is not volcanic. It is not geothermal in any conventional sense. It is something else. It is the warmth of a process that is still running, a reaction that is still incomplete, a formation that is still forming. The geology of Aeth is young — not young as in recently created, but young as in *not yet finished*, as in still undergoing the processes that elsewhere completed billions of years ago and settled into the cold stable silence of finished rock. Here, the rock is still warm because it is still *happening*. The mineral processes are still running. The deep structures are still assembling. The valley is not a place where things live. It is a place that is itself *living*, or becoming alive, or something that occupies the space between those two states the way the moisture occupies the space between condensation and evaporation — not one thing, not the other, but the *between*, sustained, persistent, patient beyond any human scale of patience.

The Gardeners know this. They have always known this. They do not mine the warmth. They do not exploit it. They *tend* it, the way they tend everything — by being present, by attending, by allowing the process to continue. They are not the inhabitants of Aeth. They are the *symptoms* of Aeth. They are what the place produces the way the pools produce the membrane and the greenhouses produce the mist and the warm deep geology produces the note — the single sustained tone that rises from the pools at unpredictable intervals, the sound of something deep and large and still-forming, the sound of a world that is teaching itself to be a world, and the Gardeners are part of the lesson, and the lesson is not finished, and the lesson will not be finished, and the not-finishing is the point.

---

You stand on the terrace. The moisture presses against your skin. A Gardener passes you on the path, and does not look at you, or looks at you with those large dark absorbing eyes in a way that makes you feel *collected*, makes you feel like a mineral residue settling on a surface, like a layer in the slow accumulation, like something that is being incorporated into the place's patient project of building itself up.

The Pillar in the middle distance shimmers. The thing above passes through the veil, and the mist thins, and for a moment you see the sky — if it is a sky — and it is not blue and is not black but is the color of the membrane, the color of the pool-organisms, the color of the space between green and gold and the color of a bruise, and then the mist closes and the sky is gone and the dim grey-gold light returns and the place continues becoming itself.

The air thickens.

The warmth rises.

The Gardeners tend.

The greenhouses breathe.

The pools sing, sometimes, when the deep geology remembers that it has a voice.

And nothing is finished. And nothing will be finished. And the not-finishing is not a failure but a *condition*, the fundamental condition of a place that is still teaching itself to be a place, still learning the grammar of its own existence, still writing the first sentence of a story that has no final page.

You are here now. You are part of the accumulation. The moisture is settling on you. The mineral is coating you. The warmth is entering you.

You are becoming part of Aeth.

Slowly.

The way everything here becomes.

Slowly.
