# The Khorathi

They are small. Not dramatically so — not the exaggerated smallness of illustration — but compact, dense, built close to the ground in the way of people who have spent generations in low-ceilinged spaces. The average Khorathi adult stands about five feet tall, with a broad, barrel-shaped torso and short, thick limbs. Their bones are heavy. Their muscles are dense. They move with a deliberate, unhurried economy that reads, to outsiders, as calm, but is actually an adaptation to heat: unnecessary movement in 60-degree air is a luxury the body cannot afford.

Their skin is pale — not the pallor of illness but the deep, permanent paleness of a people who have never seen direct sunlight. It has a faintly translucent quality, like the kells themselves, and in the amber light of the Khora it takes on a warm, almost golden undertone that makes them look, from a distance, as though they are lit from within. Their veins are visible at the wrists, the temples, the insides of their forearms — dark lines beneath the surface, mapping the body's hidden architecture. The Khorathi find surface-dwellers' skin *opaque* and slightly disturbing, the way a surface dweller might find it disturbing to look at someone whose veins showed.

Their eyes are large. Not enormous — not the cartoonish eyes of cave-dweller mythology — but noticeably larger than surface-average, with wide irises that are almost uniformly dark: deep brown, near-black, the better to gather what little light exists. The sclera is not white but a faint amber, tinted by the constant low-level exposure to the bioluminescent moss-light. Their pupils are quick and precise. They can read the health of a moss-strip at twenty paces by its color, can tell the temperature of a wall by the quality of light it throws. They do not see well in bright light. Direct sunlight is not merely uncomfortable but genuinely damaging — their retinas, optimized for the amber dimness of the Khora, burn quickly under the unfiltered sun. Khorathi traders who travel to the surface wear thick, smoked-glass visors carved from salt-crystal, and even then they squint and suffer.

Their hair is fine, straight, and uniformly dark — black or deep brown — and they keep it short or shaved close to the head. Long hair in the Khora's heat is miserable, and the Khorathi are practical people. Both men and women shave their heads in patterns that denote family lineage and trade affiliation: a bare crown with a single stripe of hair along the left temple for the tufa-carvers, a close crop with a shaved circle at the back of the skull for the kell-climbers, a full shave with a thin line from forehead to nape for the sevikhar. Children wear their hair unshaved until their apprenticeship, at which point the cutting is a ceremony — the *sevikh*, the "first silence," in which the child sits in a quiet cavern and listens to the kells for an entire day and night before receiving their trade-mark. The hair that is cut is not discarded. It is mixed into the tufa paste used to build the family's walls. The Khorathi quite literally live inside each other.

Their hands are their most remarkable feature. They are large for their body size — broad-palmed, thick-fingered, with wide, flat nails that are slightly translucent and grow quickly. The fingertips are calloused to a remarkable degree, hardened by constant contact with rough tufa and hot stone, and they have lost some fine sensitivity in exchange for durability. A kell-climber's fingertips are essentially leather. But the *joints* — the joints are extraordinary. The Khorathi hand has a range of motion that exceeds the surface-dweller norm, particularly in the fingers, which can splay wide and flex backward at angles that look, to an outsider, anatomically impossible. This is the physical basis for their sign language — the *khoravot*, the "stone-speech" — which requires rapid, precise finger configurations that no surface-dweller hand can replicate. The khoravot is not merely a practical adaptation to the noise. It is an art form. A fluent signer's hands move like something alive — like the kells themselves, shifting and chiming — and the Khorathi consider beautiful signing to be as expressive and emotionally complex as any spoken poetry.

Their voices, when they use them, are loud and flat and strange. The Khorathi larynx has adapted to project over the kell-sound: their voices carry a distinctive clipped, staccato quality, with consonants sharply articulated and vowels shortened, so that their speech sounds, to outsiders, like someone hammering words into stone. They do not sing in the surface sense — the Khorathi singing voice is a low, resonant hum, more felt than heard, used primarily in the khoravani moss-growing tradition. A Khorathi singing alone in a quiet cavern is one of the most unsettling sounds in the world: a single human voice, stripped of melody, reduced to pure tone, harmonizing with the kells until you cannot tell where the person ends and the stone begins.

---

They dress in layers of pressed moss-fiber — a textile the Khorathi produce by weaving dried moss-stems into a coarse, warm fabric that is naturally amber-colored and faintly luminescent. A Khorathi garment glows, very softly, in the dark. Not enough to read by — enough to see the shape of the person beside you, the outline of their shoulders, the movement of their hands. The effect in a crowded market is extraordinary: dozens of faintly glowing figures moving through the amber dark, their bodies outlined in soft light, their gestures leaving trails in the eye.

The layers are functional. The inner layer is a close-fitting tunic that wicks the constant sweat away from the skin. The outer layer is a heavier wrap, draped over both shoulders and belted at the waist, with deep pockets for tools, moss-cakes, and the small salt-crystal knives that serve as the Khorathi's universal implement — eating utensil, trade token, and, in extremis, weapon. The crystal knives are beautiful: translucent, amber-tinted, sharpened to a fine edge that dulls quickly but can be re-ground on any rough tufa surface. A Khorathi without their crystal knife is, in their cultural understanding, *naked* — not merely undressed but incomplete, missing a part of themselves.

They wear no shoes. The feet, like the hands, are broad and tough, with thick soles hardened by generations of walking on warm stone. The Khorathi can walk barefoot on surfaces that would blister a surface-dweller's feet in minutes. They can feel the thermal cycling through the floor — the subtle warming and cooling of the stone that precedes the kells' expansion and contraction — and an experienced Khorathi can predict the acoustic weather of the next several hours by the temperature of the ground beneath their bare feet.

---

The kell-climbers are the most physically specialized. They are the smallest and lightest of the Khorathi — children are often recruited young, before their bones have fully thickened — with extraordinary grip strength and a preternatural sense of balance. They climb the kells barehanded, scaling the mineral columns to harvest salt-crystal from the growing tips, to trim formations that are producing disruptive tones, to collect the precious kell-dust that accumulates in the cups and hollows where kells meet the ceiling. It is the most dangerous occupation in the Khora. Kells fracture. They ring their warning — a sharp, sudden *ping* that every Khorathi knows means *get down now* — but the warning is sometimes not enough. A falling kell, even a thin one, in a cavern where the stone floor is unforgiving, is lethal. The kell-climbers die at a rate that would be unacceptable in any surface culture. The Khorathi accept it the way a seafaring culture accepts drowning: as the cost of living in a place that provides everything. The dead climbers are not mourned with grief but with a specific, formalized sound — a low, sustained hum produced by the entire community, a note held for exactly as many breaths as the dead person lived in years. The sound is added to the Khora's acoustic texture, the climber's life translated into a single tone that joins the chorus of the kells. This is not metaphor. The Khorathi believe it literally. The dead become part of the Khora's voice.

The sevikhar are the opposite: the heaviest, the stillest, the most sedentary. They sit. They listen. They sit in the deep caverns where the sound is most complex and they listen for hours, days, weeks, their eyes closed, their breathing slowed to a rate that surface-dwellers find alarming — four breaths per minute, sometimes fewer. They are listening for the *pattern* — the underlying structure in the kell-sound that they believe constitutes the Khora's thought. They do not eat while listening. They do not sleep in the normal sense; they enter a state that is somewhere between meditation and trance, a light, floating awareness in which the sound becomes the entire world. The oldest sevikhar have sat so long that their bodies have begun to adapt: their legs atrophy, their spines curve, their skin takes on the pale, mineral quality of the tufa around them. They do not mind. They are becoming what they have always listened to. The most revered sevikhar in living memory — a woman named *Teshkora*, who sat in the deep cavern of *Veth-Sora* for eleven years without rising — was said to have developed, by the end, a resonance in her own chest cavity that harmonized with the kells. When she spoke, her voice carried undertones that were not human. When she died, they did not bury her. They left her in the cavern. Within a year, moss had grown over her body, and the kells around her had shifted their tone — subtly, but measurably — as though the cavern had incorporated her frequency into its own.

---

The children are loud. This is the great exception to the Khorathi rule of acoustic adaptation — Khorathi children scream and shout and bang and cry with the full-throated abandon of small humans everywhere, and the Khora absorbs it, adds it to the texture, makes room. The children play in the market cavern, chasing each other between the tufa tables, their bare feet slapping the warm stone, their laughter adding a bright, chaotic layer to the kell-sound. They climb on the low kells — the ones deemed safe, the ones that ring in gentle, predictable tones — and the parents watch with the calm of people who know that a child who learns to read the kell-sound early is a child who will survive. The children are taught to listen before they are taught to speak. A Khorathi toddler's first word is almost always a shout — but their first *meaningful* communication is a sign, a small hand moving in the khoravot, reaching out to shape the air into meaning.

The children eat moss-cake from the time they can chew, and they grow up with the taste of it — mineral, faintly bitter, warm — as the foundational flavor of their existence. They drink kell-beer before they are technically supposed to, sneaking cups from the communal troughs, getting warm and dizzy and giggling in the amber light. They are told stories — the *sevikhani*, the "stone-tales" — which are not spoken but *signed*, told entirely in the khoravot, performed by elders whose hands move through the air like kells swaying in a thermal current. The stories are about the Khora's deep history, about the first people who found the cavern and heard the sound and understood, immediately, that they were being spoken to. The stories are about the kells as individuals — each one named, each one with a personality, a temperament, a tone. The children learn the names of the kells the way surface children learn the names of streets. *That is Veth-Khora, the deep singer. That is Sevi-Tesh, the sharp one, the one that warns. That is Kora-Veth, the old one, the one that has been ringing since before the first people came.*

The children believe this literally. So do the adults.

---

The Khorathi do not have a concept of loneliness. They have a word — *khoraveth*, which translates roughly as "the silence between tones" — but it describes an acoustic condition, not an emotional one. It is the moment when the kells pause, briefly, between cycles, and the cavern holds its breath. It is beautiful. It is sought after. The sevikhar pursue it the way a surface mystic pursues emptiness. But it is not *loneliness*. Loneliness requires the possibility of separation, of being apart from others, and in the Khora, this is simply not a meaningful concept. The walls are thin. The sound is shared. The moss-light falls on everyone equally. You are always, always in the Khora, and the Khora is always, always full of people.

This makes the Khorathi almost incomprehensible to surface-dwellers, who find their communal intimacy claustrophobic and their lack of privacy appalling. The Khorathi, for their part, find surface-dwellers *haunted* — haunted by silence, haunted by solitude, haunted by the terrible freedom of being alone with their own thoughts. The Khorathi do not trust thoughts that happen in silence. Silence, to them, is not peaceful. It is the sound of something missing. It is the sound of the Khora *not thinking*.

---

They are not a happy people, exactly. Happiness is a surface concept, tied to light and space and the possibility of change. The Khorathi are something else — *resonant*. They are in tune with their place. They are the frequency that the Khora has been waiting for, the voice that answers the stone's slow grinding song. When a Khorathi elder dies — peacefully, in the warmth, surrounded by family, their hand signing one last word into the amber air — the feeling is not grief. It is *completion*. A note held for its full duration. A tone that has said what it came to say.

The kells ring. The moss glows. The Khorathi listen, and answer, and the cavern thinks its slow, hot, ancient thoughts, and the city breathes, and the sound goes on.
