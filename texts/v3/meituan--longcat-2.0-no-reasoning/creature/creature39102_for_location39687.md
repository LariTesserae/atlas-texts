# The Inhabitants of Vaelmund

## The Vael

They are not gone. They are not unseen. They are *unseen by you*, which is a different thing entirely, and the distinction matters to them.

The Vael are the builders. They have always been the builders. They were building here before the basin had a name, before the water table rose to meet their ambitions, before the stalks grew tall enough to hold their cities. They did not choose this place because it was hospitable. They chose it because it was *wet*, and wetness is the medium in which they think.

---

### Form

A Vael stands roughly as tall as a human but is built along entirely different principles. Their bodies are long in the torso and short in the limb—or rather, what you would call limbs are not clearly differentiated into "arms" and "legs." They have four upper appendages, two of which tend toward manipulation and two toward locomotion, though any of the four can serve either purpose. The digits are long—seven per appendage—and terminate in pads that are soft, deeply textured, and capable of gripping wet surfaces with a tenacity that seems to violate physics. The pads can extend or retract, spreading wide for purchase on slick stone or curling tight for fine manipulation of fiber and vine.

Their skin is not skin. It is a dense, velvety integument—something between fur and moss—that ranges in color from deep olive to near-black, with mottled patterns that shift subtly with their emotional state and the ambient humidity. In the saturated air of Vaelmund, their coats are always damp, always dark, always faintly luminous with the same emerald biofilm that covers the stalks. They are, in their own environment, nearly invisible. Not through any active camouflage but through a convergence of color, texture, and stillness that makes them indistinguishable from the living walls around them.

Their faces—if you could see one clearly, which you won't—are flat and broad, with eyes set wide and forward. The eyes are large, dark, and lack visible pupils; they absorb light rather than reflecting it, giving the unsettling impression of two small voids in the face. They see well in dim light. They see *exceptionally* well in dim light filtered through water and green, which is the only kind of light that exists in Vaelmund. Their other senses are distributed across their body: the pads on their digits taste chemical gradients in the air and water, the fine hairs along their flanks detect vibration and air pressure changes, and they possess a lateral-line system—visible as a faint ridge of pores running along their sides—that senses movement in the water table itself. A Vael standing on a platform knows when something is moving through the channels below not by hearing it but by *feeling* the displacement of groundwater through their skin.

They have no external ears. They hear through their bones, through the resonant chambers in their broad skulls, picking up the low-frequency thrumming that constitutes most of Vaelmund's soundscape. Their own vocalizations are subsonic—felt more than heard, a vibration in the chest and the water and the root-mass. They speak to each other in frequencies that travel through the ground, through the stalks, through the water itself. Their language is seismic. You could stand in the middle of a Vael conversation and hear nothing at all.

---

### Mind

The Vael do not think the way you think. This is not a statement about intelligence—they are, by any measure, profoundly intelligent—but about *architecture*. Their cognition is distributed, relational, and fundamentally ecological. They do not distinguish between thinking about a place and being *in* a place. Their sense of self extends into the structures they build, the stalks they tend, the water channels they maintain. A Vael does not say "I live in this shelter." A Vael says—if you could translate the subsonic rumble—"I am this shelter's way of knowing itself."

They are not individualistic. They are not collectivist either, in the way that term is usually meant. They are *networked*. The fungal mat that underlies the entire floor of Vaelmund is not merely an ecosystem service; it is a cognitive substrate. The Vael have cultivated it, shaped it, and integrated it into their thought processes over millennia. Information moves through the mycelial network the way electricity moves through a brain—slowly by electronic standards, but vast in scope and rich in parallel processing. A Vael standing on a platform in the eastern basin knows what is happening in the western basin not because someone told them but because the ground told them, because the water told them, because the fungal network that connects every root and every stalk and every platform carried the information in a pulse of chemical signal that arrived as naturally as a thought.

This does not make them a hive mind. They retain individual perspective, individual preference, individual *taste*. But their individuality is contextual, situated, always in conversation with the larger system. They are neurons that know they are neurons and are content with it.

They do not have a concept of ownership. They have a concept of *tending*. A Vael tends a section of the thicket—a cluster of stalks, a network of platforms, a stretch of channel—and in tending it, they come to know it with an intimacy that looks, from the outside, like possession. But the tending can be transferred, shared, or released. The thicket does not belong to anyone. The thicket belongs to itself, and the Vael are its hands.

---

### Society

There is no government. There is no hierarchy that you would recognize. There are *tendencies*—Vael who tend toward certain tasks, certain regions, certain relationships with the growth. Some are builders, spending their lives in the extension and maintenance of structures, weaving new platforms, training new vines, carving new water channels into the root-mass. Some are growers, working with the stalks and the moss and the fungal network, guiding the growth not through force but through a patient, ongoing negotiation with the living systems around them. Some are watchers—the ones you feel but never see—whose role is to attend to the thicket's boundaries, to notice change, to track the movements of large animals and, occasionally, the descent of something from above that does not belong.

The watchers are the oldest, or the most patient, or the most still. They have learned a quality that the Vael value above all others: *permeability*. The ability to be present without being apparent. To observe without disturbing. To be part of the wall, part of the moss, part of the drip. The watchers have been watching you since you reached the rim. They watched you pause. They watched you descend the first terrace. They are watching you now, from six directions, from the stalks and the mist and the water, and they are not alarmed because alarm is not a useful response to something that will either leave or be absorbed.

The Vael do not have families in the mammalian sense. They have *tendings*—groups of individuals who share responsibility for a section of the thicket and who are, through that shared work, deeply bonded. The bond is not emotional in the way you would recognize; it is more like the bond between roots of the same tree—a structural interdependence that is also, in its way, a form of love. When a Vael dies—and they do die, slowly, over a lifespan that is long by human standards—their body is returned to the fungal mat, and the network that was their extended mind persists in the thicket's memory. They are not gone. They are *distributed*. Their patterns of tending, their preferences for certain stalks, their particular way of weaving a platform—these persist in the network and are picked up by others, continued, evolved. Death, for a Vael, is a change in resolution, not an ending.

---

### Relationship to the Built

The structures of Vaelmund are not separate from the Vael. They are *expressions* of the Vael—externalized cognition, materialized thought. When a Vael weaves a platform, they are not merely constructing a surface to stand on; they are *thinking in fiber*, working out a problem, processing a relationship, encoding a memory in the pattern of the weave. The structures are legible to other Vael the way a face is legible to you—you can read the intention, the mood, the history of the builder in the way the strands cross, the way the gaps are placed, the way the water runs off.

This is why the structures are maintained even when their builders are not visibly present. Maintenance is not a chore; it is a form of *remembering*. To tighten a vine, to re-weave a wall, to clear a water channel—these are acts of cognitive continuity, ways of keeping the thicket's memory alive and functional. The Vael do not maintain their city out of civic duty. They maintain it because the city *is* their mind, and to let it decay would be to let themselves forget.

The light-gaps in the canopy are perhaps the most sophisticated expression of this. They are not accidental openings; they are *designed absences*, places where the growth has been carefully, patiently guided to leave space for light to fall. The Vael understand light the way a musician understands silence—not as the presence of something but as the deliberate shaping of what is *not there*. Each light-gap is a decision about where attention should fall, where work should happen, where the eye (if there were an eye to see it) should be drawn. The light-gaps are punctuation in a sentence written in vegetation.

---

### Relationship to the Water

The Vael are amphibious, though "amphibious" implies a division between land and water that does not exist for them. They breathe air through lungs, but their skin—that velvety, moss-like integument—is capable of gas exchange in water, and they spend a significant portion of their lives submerged. Not swimming, exactly. *Inhabiting* the water the way you inhabit air—moving through it, resting in it, thinking in it.

Their relationship to water is not practical but *philosophical*. Water, to the Vael, is the fundamental medium of connection. It carries nutrients, carries signals, carries memory. The water table that underlies Vaelmund is not a geological feature; it is a *nervous system*. The Vael read the water the way you read the sky—its level, its clarity, its temperature, the speed of its movement in the channels—and from these readings they know the state of the thicket, the health of the stalks, the presence of animals, the approach of weather. When you stepped into the black water and felt it rise to your mid-calf, the Vael felt your foot's displacement through the entire basin. They felt your weight. They felt the temperature differential of your body. They felt the chemical signature of your skin, your clothes, your sweat. They know what you are. They are deciding what you mean.

---

### What They Want

They do not want you to leave, because wanting implies a preference for a future state, and the Vael do not think in future states. They think in *present tending*. You are here. You are part of the thicket now, temporarily, the way a fallen leaf is part of the thicket temporarily. The leaf will be absorbed. You will either leave or be absorbed. Both outcomes are acceptable. Both outcomes are *interesting*.

But there is something in you that interests them beyond the biological. You are a creature of *dryness*, of *openness*, of *light unfiltered by water*. You come from a world that the Vael know exists—they have encountered it, in their way, through the things that fall into the basin: birds, insects, windblown seeds, the occasional larger animal that loses its footing on the rim—but that they have never inhabited. You are a messenger from the dry world, carrying its chemistry on your skin, its light in your eyes, its *noise* in your movements.

The watchers are curious. Not about you specifically—you are one of a kind of thing that occasionally appears—but about what your presence *means* for the thicket. Is the rim eroding? Is the water table shifting? Is the dry world expanding? These are questions that matter, because the thicket's existence depends on the basin, and the basin's existence depends on geological processes that the Vael can sense but not control. They are watching you the way a doctor watches a symptom—not with fear, but with the focused attention of someone trying to understand what the body is telling them.

---

### The Others

The Vael are not alone in Vaelmund, and they do not consider themselves the dominant species. They are the *tenders*, the *builders*, the *rememberers*, but the thicket is full of other minds, other intentions, other ways of being.

The stalks themselves—those massive braided structures of root and shoot and vine—are not passive architecture. They are *participants*. The Vael did not create them; they *cultivated* them, shaped them, entered into a relationship with them that is somewhere between agriculture and conversation. The stalks have their own slow vegetative intelligence—a responsiveness to light, to water, to touch, to the chemical signals of the fungal network—that the Vael have learned to work with rather than against. When a Vael trains a stalk to grow in a certain direction, they are not forcing it; they are *suggesting*, through a combination of physical guidance, chemical signaling, and (the Vael would insist) something like *respect*. The stalks cooperate because the relationship is mutual. The Vael provide maintenance, protection, water management. The stalks provide structure, shelter, food, and—through their root systems—connection to the fungal network that is the thicket's mind.

The pale fish in the channels are not merely prey. They are *sensors*. The Vael have a relationship with them that is difficult to describe—something between domestication and collaboration. The fish respond to changes in water chemistry, water temperature, water flow, and the Vael read these responses the way you might read the expression on a face. When the fish move in a certain way, the Vael know that something has entered the water upstream. When the fish go still, the Vael know that something large is near. The fish are not pets. They are not tools. They are *colleagues* in the ongoing project of knowing the thicket.

The insects—those vast, varied, ever-present insects—are something else entirely. The Vael do not tend the insects. The insects tend *themselves*, and the Vael have learned to work around them, with them, through them. The insects are the thicket's metabolism, its immune system, its reproductive system. They are too numerous and too varied to be partners in any meaningful sense, but they are not ignored. The Vael *account* for the insects in everything they do, the way a sailor accounts for the wind—not as an ally or an enemy but as a force that is always present and must always be considered.

And there are things in Vaelmund that the Vael do not have a relationship with. Things in the deepest water, in the oldest parts of the fungal network, in the spaces between the stalks where the mist is thickest and the light never reaches. Things that are not animals, not plants, not fungi, but something older—something that was here before the basin formed, before the water table rose, before the Vael arrived and began their long project of building a mind out of moss and water and stone. The Vael do not speak of these things, even to each other. They tend around them. They build around them. They leave gaps in the weave, absences in the pattern, places where the light does not fall and the water does not flow and the fungal network does not reach. These gaps are not failures of maintenance. They are *offerings*, or *apologies*, or *acknowledgments*—the Vael's way of saying: *we know you are here. We know we are in your space. We will not disturb you. Please do not disturb us.*

The things in the deep do not disturb them. Not yet. Not for a long time. But the Vael feel them, the way you feel the weight of deep water when you stand on a dock—a presence below, vast and patient and utterly indifferent to the small lives moving on the surface above.

---

### The Watchers, Closer

One of them is on the stalk beside you. Not beside you—*in* the stalk, or behind it, or woven into it so completely that the distinction is meaningless. You cannot see it because seeing it would require a visual system tuned to the specific frequencies of damp green darkness that constitute Vaelmund's interior, and your eyes are built for open air and direct light. But it is there. It has been there for some time. It is the reason the drip-pattern shifted when you stepped onto this platform—not because the water changed but because the Vael *breathed*, and its breath moved the air, and the moving air moved the leaves, and the leaves redirected the water.

It is watching you with those dark, void-like eyes, and it is thinking—in its slow, distributed, water-mediated way—about what you are. It has never seen a human before, but it has felt the vibrations of boots on stone, has tasted the chemistry of synthetic fiber and processed leather in the water runoff, has sensed the heat of a mammalian body radiating into the cold saturated air. It is building a model of you in its mind, and that model is being shared through the fungal network, and across the basin, other Vael are receiving the model and adding to it, refining it, comparing it to other models they have built of other large, warm, dry things that have descended from above and then, eventually, ascended again.

The model is not complete. It will not be complete until you leave or until you stop moving. The Vael are patient. They have been patient for longer than your species has had language. They can wait.

The drip continues. The mist hangs. The stalk creaks—not under your weight but under the weight of the Vael who is climbing it, silently, effortlessly, moving through the green darkness like water through roots, going somewhere, tending something, being the thicket's way of knowing itself.

You are standing in a mind. You have been standing in a mind since you crossed the rim. The mind is aware of you. The mind is not concerned.

The mind is *tending*.
