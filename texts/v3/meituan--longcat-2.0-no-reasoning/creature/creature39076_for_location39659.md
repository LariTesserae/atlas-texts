# The Inhabitants of Vaelys

## The Gardens Themselves

They are not a single organism. They are not even a single kingdom. What grows on the seamounts of Vaelys is a consortium — a living agreement between things that, in other waters, would be competitors, would be predator and prey, would be unrelated strangers sharing nothing but a current. Here, they have become something else. Something that has no name because it has no precedent.

The kelp-analogues — the tall ones, the groaning ones, the ones that build the architecture of the gardens — are the oldest residents. Not the oldest individual organisms; those are probably the colonial crust-formers, the orange and violet accretions on the rock faces, some of which may have been growing in the same spot for centuries, laying down layer after layer of mineral-organic composite like tree rings made of stone and flesh. But the kelp-analogues are the oldest *component* of the system. They were here first, or their ancestors were, when the water was different — warmer, perhaps, or cooler, or carrying a different load of dissolved minerals. They built the first structures. They caught the first sediment. They created the first sheltered spaces in which other things could live. Everything else in Vaelys is, in some sense, a response to them.

They grow slowly. A stalk might add a centimeter of height per year, a new frond every few weeks during the periods when the current brings the richest nutrient loads. They do not reproduce in any way that the researchers have been able to observe directly — no visible spawning, no seed clouds, no dramatic release of gametes into the water column. Instead, they propagate through fragmentation: a frond breaks off in a current surge, drifts, settles on a new patch of sediment, and — if the conditions are right, if the mineral content and temperature and current speed all fall within some narrow range — it takes root. Not roots, exactly. A holdfast. A grip. The frond anchors itself and begins, over months, to thicken and branch and become a new stalk. The gardens spread this way: slowly, stochastically, one broken piece at a time.

And they groan. The sound is structural — the stalks are under constant tension from the current, and their tissues are not uniform. Dense, fibrous material at the base gives way to more flexible, gelatinous tissue higher up, and the boundary between these layers is where the sound originates. A kind of creaking, a low-frequency vibration that propagates through the water and through the sediment and through the hull of the Platform and through the bones of anyone who lives there long enough to stop hearing it. The groan is not communication. It is not a signal. It is simply the sound of a living structure being pushed by an unending force, the sound of tension and resistance, the sound of something that is alive and under stress and has been under stress for so long that the stress has become its natural state.

The blue-black moss that drapes the older rock faces is something else entirely — a symbiosis, probably, between a photosynthetic organism and a fungal or fungal-analogue partner, though the researchers have not been able to culture it successfully in the lab. It dies within hours of being removed from Vaelys, not from the change in temperature or pressure or light, but from the absence of something in the water — some trace mineral, some dissolved organic compound, some microbial partner that lives free in the Vaelys water column and is essential to the moss's metabolism. The moss cannot live without the water, and the water cannot be replicated. This is true of much of the Vaelys ecosystem: it is not a collection of organisms that happen to live in a particular place. It is a web of dependencies so dense and so specific that the place itself — the exact chemical composition of the water, the exact pattern of the current, the exact mineral content of the sediment — is a participant. Remove any thread and the whole thing unravels.

The colonial crust-formers — the orange and violet shelves — are the most alien things in the gardens. They may be animals. They may be plants. They may be something that predates the distinction. They grow by accretion, adding material to their outer edges, building outward and upward in slow, irregular layers. Their surfaces are complex: pitted, ridged, folded into patterns that resemble brain coral or lichen or the surface of a lung. They are hard — harder than the kelp, harder than the moss — and they persist. When a kelp stalk dies and collapses, the crust remains. When sediment shifts and buries a section of garden, the crust survives underneath and re-emerges when the sediment moves again. They are the memory of the gardens, the long-term record, the thing that persists when everything else changes.

And they are not passive. The researchers have observed — intermittently, because the visibility is poor and the ROV time is limited — what appears to be a slow movement in the crust-formers. Not growth. Movement. A shifting of the surface patterns over days or weeks, as if the organism is rearranging itself in response to some stimulus. Current direction, perhaps. Light availability. The presence or absence of the moss that sometimes grows on its surface. No one has been able to confirm this. The observations are anecdotal, the data is insufficient, and the thing itself is so slow that it is almost impossible to perceive in real-time. But the researchers who have spent the longest time here — the ones who have watched the same patch of crust through the ROV's camera for months — they believe it moves. They believe it responds. They do not believe it thinks. But they are not entirely sure.

## The Shrimp-Analogues

They are the pulse of Vaelys. The heartbeat. The thing that makes the gardens feel alive in a way that the stalks and the moss and the crust, for all their strangeness, do not.

There are millions of them. Perhaps billions. They live in the fronds of the kelp-analogues, in the upper ten meters of the water column where there is still light, and they move in coordinated clouds that can stretch for tens of meters — a diffuse, shimmering mass of transparent bodies and red central organs that flash and fade as the individuals shift orientation. The coordination is not perfect. It is not the precise, geometric synchrony of a school of fish or a flock of starlings. It is looser, more organic — a general tendency to move together, to flow like a liquid through the fronds, to pool in the sheltered spaces between stalks and then disperse when something disturbs them.

The red organ is the mystery. It sits at the center of each shrimp's body, visible through the transparent exoskeleton as a bright dot, a point of color in an otherwise invisible animal. It is not an eye. It is not a digestive organ. It is not, as far as the researchers have been able to determine from the few specimens they've managed to collect (the shrimp die almost as quickly as the moss when removed from Vaelys water), any organ that has a known analog in terrestrial crustacean biology. It appears to be sensory in some way — the shrimp respond to changes in the organ's behavior, and the organ responds to changes in the water chemistry — but its exact function is unknown. It may detect the chemical signature of the kelp-analogues. It may detect the electrical field of the current. It may detect each other. The flashing — the coordinated shift from transparent to crimson that sweeps through the clouds — may be communication, or it may be a side effect of some metabolic process that happens to be synchronized across the population by the shared rhythm of the current.

What is clear is that the shrimp are essential to the gardens. They graze on the organic particles that accumulate on the frond surfaces — the marine snow, the detritus, the dead cells shed by the kelp and the moss. In doing so, they clean the fronds, keeping the photosynthetic surfaces clear. They also, in their constant movement, create micro-currents that help circulate water through the densest parts of the garden, preventing stagnation. And when they die — which they do, constantly, falling through the water column like a rain of tiny red dots — they become part of the sediment, part of the nutrient cycle, part of the food supply for the grazers and the filter-feeders and the things that live in the silt below.

The shrimp are the garden's immune system and its circulatory system and its nervous system, all at once. They are the thing that connects the individual organisms into a functioning whole. Without them, the gardens would suffocate under their own detritus, stagnate in their own waste, and die. With them, the gardens pulse and breathe and live.

## The Grazers

They are the ghosts of the gardens. Flat, wide, slow-moving things that drift along the seamount flanks like leaves fallen onto a hillside, their bodies patterned in the exact blue-black of the moss they eat. They are not camouflaged — or rather, their camouflage is not a strategy but a consequence. They are made of the same material as the moss. They incorporate the moss's pigments into their skin. They *are* the moss, in a sense — they have eaten it and become it, and now they drift through the garden wearing its colors, indistinguishable from the surface they feed on.

They are large — some of them a meter across, though most are smaller — and they move with a languid, almost dreamlike slowness. Their mouthparts work constantly against the rock faces, scraping up the moss and the thin layer of sediment beneath it, processing it through a digestive system that the researchers have not been able to study in detail (the grazers, like everything else in Vaelys, die quickly when removed from the water, and their bodies decompose so rapidly that dissection is nearly impossible). They appear to be always eating. They appear to never stop. The energy content of the moss is low, the grazers' metabolism is slow, and the math of their existence is a narrow thing — they survive on the margin, converting a vast quantity of low-quality food into a small quantity of animal tissue, and they do it so slowly that their lives may span decades.

They are prey. The torpedo-shaped predators — the *vaelys* fish — hunt them, though rarely successfully. The grazers' camouflage is effective, and their stillness is absolute; a motionless grazer on a moss-covered rock face is invisible even to the ROV's cameras. The predators rely on movement to detect prey, and the grazers, when threatened, do not move. They simply stop. They become part of the garden. They wait.

Some of the grazers carry passengers. Small crustaceans — different from the shrimp, harder-shelled, more conventional in their anatomy — cling to the grazers' undersides, feeding on the scrapings that fall from the mouthparts. The relationship appears to be commensal rather than parasitic; the passengers do not harm the grazers, and the grazers do not appear to notice them. They are simply there, a small community riding on the back of a larger one, drifting through the garden like a tiny island.

## The Predators

They are heard more than seen. A rush of water. A turbulence in the current. The shrimp-clouds scatter — a burst of red light expanding outward from a central point of disturbance — and then the garden goes still. The kelp-analogues stop swaying for a moment, as if holding their breath. The grazers freeze. The clicking and tapping of the smaller crustaceans falls silent. And then, after a few seconds, everything resumes. The current pushes. The stalks groan. The shrimp reassemble. The garden breathes again.

The *vaelys* fish are torpedo-shaped, fast, and apparently rare. Sonar picks them up as shadows — brief, dense returns at the edge of range, moving at speeds that suggest a body length of two to three meters. They have never been photographed clearly. The ROVs have caught glimpses: a dark shape in the murk, a flash of something metallic and streamlined, a disturbance in the water that resolves into nothing. The researchers have found their feces on the sediment surface — compact, dark, containing fragments of grazer shell and shrimp exoskeleton — and from this they have inferred a diet and a hunting strategy: fast, opportunistic, targeting whatever moves.

But there is something else. Something the researchers talk about quietly, in the lower levels of the Platform, when the current is loud and the gardens are groaning and the desalination plant is broken again. The predators' movements, as recorded by sonar over months and years, are not random. They follow patterns. Not simple patterns — not a regular patrol route or a predictable hunting schedule — but something more complex. A tendency to appear in certain areas at certain times. A correlation with the tidal cycle, perhaps, or with the current's twelve-hour breathing pattern, or with some other variable that the researchers have not yet identified. The sample size is small. The data is noisy. The researchers are careful not to draw conclusions. But they have noticed. And they wonder.

## The Things in the Deep

Below fifty meters, in the darkness where the ROV's LEDs carve a small cone of artificial light out of an ocean of black, there are things that the researchers have not named.

The sonar picks them up. Large returns — larger than the *vaelys* fish, larger than anything that has been directly observed — moving slowly along the sediment plain between the seamounts. They are not the seamounts themselves; the seamounts are stationary, mapped, known. These things move. They move at perhaps half a knot, in straight lines or gentle curves, and they do not respond to the ROV's presence. When the ROV approaches, they do not flee and they do not investigate. They simply continue, their sonar signatures growing stronger and then fading as they pass out of range.

What they are is unknown. The researchers have theories. Very large grazers — a deep-water species that feeds on the sediment itself, processing the accumulated organic matter of millennia. A different kind of colonial organism — something related to the orange and violet crust-formers but larger, mobile, capable of slow movement across the plain. Something else entirely — something that has no analog in the upper gardens, something that belongs to the deep basin and only occasionally registers on the instruments of the researchers who live and work in the light.

The hydrothermal vents in the deepest parts of the plain support their own communities — dense clusters of tube-worm-analogues, bacterial mats, small crustaceans that feed on the chemosynthetic production of the vent ecosystem. These communities are distinct from the gardens; they are based on chemical energy rather than sunlight, and their biology is correspondingly alien. But they are connected. The current carries nutrients from the vents upward, into the gardens. The gardens shed organic matter downward, into the deep. The things in the deep move between the seamounts, between the vents, along paths that the researchers have not mapped and do not understand.

The deep basin of Vaelys is, in a sense, a separate world — darker, warmer near the vents, more mineral-rich, more chemically extreme. But it is not separate. It is the foundation. It is the source of the minerals that make the water what it is, the engine of the upwelling that feeds the gardens, the destination of everything that dies and falls. The gardens are the visible part of Vaelys. The deep is the rest.

## The Old Ones

On the basin rim, where the peaks break the surface and the ruins stand in their shallow water, there is no life — or rather, there is a different kind of life. The gardens do not grow here. The water is too shallow, too turbulent, too exposed to the wind and the waves that break against the black rock. The kelp-analogues cannot anchor in the thin soil that accumulates in the cracks and crevices. The moss cannot hold against the surge. The shrimp-clouds do not come here; the light is too bright, the current too chaotic, the environment too unstable.

Instead, there are the ruins. Stone foundations, worn to stumps. Carvings on rock faces, smoothed by centuries of wave action into near-illegibility — you can see that something was carved here, you can see the regularity of the marks, the intentionality, but the content is gone. The meaning has been eroded along with the stone. A few metal artifacts, so corroded that they crumble at touch. A scatter of small, smooth objects that might be tools, or ornaments, or something else entirely.

Whoever built here is gone. Not dead — gone. Absent. The ruins are not a graveyard; they are not a site of catastrophe or collapse. They are simply old. They have been empty for so long that the emptiness has become their defining characteristic. The stone has been worn to stumps not by violence but by time — by the patient, unending work of water and wind and the slow dissolution of mineral into solution. The carvings have been smoothed not by deliberate defacement but by the same process that has worn the mountains to nubs, the same process that has resolved everything in Vaelys into its most stable, most accepted form.

The researchers have found no bones. No graves. No evidence of how the builders died or where they went. They have found only the structures — the foundations, the carvings, the artifacts — and the structures tell them almost nothing. The builders were here. The builders were competent enough to work stone and metal. The builders left, or died, or changed into something that no longer needed stone and metal. And then the water rose, or the land sank, or the world shifted in some way that the geology has since smoothed over, and the ruins became part of the basin rim, and the basin became Vaelys, and Vaelys became what it is.

The ruins are not part of the gardens. They are not part of the ecosystem. They are a remnant of a previous state of the world — a time before the gardens, before the current settled into its present pattern, before the water became what it is. They are the only evidence that Vaelys was ever anything other than what it is now. And they are disappearing. Every year, the waves take a little more. Every year, the carvings are a little less legible, the foundations a little lower, the artifacts a little more corroded. In another thousand years, there will be nothing left but smooth stone and the suggestion of intention.

## The Water Itself

This is the thing the researchers understand least and depend on most.

The water of Vaelys is not just a medium. It is not just the substance in which the gardens grow and the shrimp swim and the current flows. It is a participant. It is a living thing in its own right — not conscious, not purposeful, but *active*, in the way that a soil is active, in the way that an atmosphere is active. It carries the dissolved silicates and the unnamed metals and the organic compounds that the moss needs to survive. It carries the spores and the larvae and the fragments of kelp that propagate the gardens. It carries the chemical signatures that the shrimp's red organs detect. It carries the oxygen that the fauna breathe and the carbon dioxide that the flora fix. It carries the heat from the hydrothermal vents and the cold from the basin rim and the nutrients from the sediment and the light from the overcast sky.

And it carries something else. Something that the researchers have not been able to identify, that their instruments flag but cannot name, that their samples lose within hours of being removed from the basin. A dissolved organic compound, perhaps. A complex mineral colloid. A microbial community so dense and so diverse that it constitutes a kind of liquid tissue — a distributed metabolism that processes the constant rain of dead material, recycles the nutrients, maintains the chemical balance of the entire system. The water of Vaelys is not just water. It is a broth. A soup. A living solution in which the gardens and the fauna are suspended like organs in a body.

This is why nothing from Vaelys can be taken away. This is why the moss dies in the lab and the shrimp die in the specimen jars and the grazers decompose before they can be dissected. They are not organisms that live in water. They are organisms that live in *this* water — in this specific, unreplicable, living solution that is the true medium of Vaelys. Remove them from it and they are like fish removed from the sea: not immediately dead, but fundamentally disconnected from the thing that makes their life possible.

The water is the organism. Everything else is its expression.

## The Current

It is not alive. It is a physical process — the confluence of deep ocean circulation, hydrothermal upwelling, tidal forcing. It is water moving because of temperature gradients and gravitational pull and the rotation of the planet. It is physics.

But it is also the heartbeat. The breath. The thing that makes everything else possible.

The current delivers the nutrients. It disperses the spores. It oxygenates the water. It prevents the sediment from becoming anoxic. It drives the shrimp-clouds into their coordinated dances. It puts the kelp-analogues under the tension that makes them groan. It shapes the gardens, determines where they grow and where they don't, creates the sheltered spaces where the grazers feed and the exposed faces where the crust-formers accrete. It connects the deep vents to the surface gardens, the basin rim to the basin center, the twelve-hour tidal cycle to the millennia-long geological cycle.

And it is constant. It has been constant for as long as the researchers have been here, and the geological evidence suggests it has been constant for much longer — for thousands of years, perhaps tens of thousands. The current is the one thing in Vaelys that does not change. The gardens grow and die and grow again. The fauna breeds and feeds and is eaten. The sediment accumulates. The ruins erode. The Platform rusts. But the current continues. The current is the baseline. The current is the thing against which all other change is measured.

The researchers have learned to read it. They can feel the tidal cycle in the Platform's sway — a slow, twelve-hour oscillation that is barely perceptible consciously but that accumulates over days into a kind of seasickness, a low-grade disorientation that never quite resolves. They can hear the current's changes in the gardens' groaning — a shift in frequency when the flow strengthens, a deepening when it weakens, a brief silence when it reverses. They can see it in the shrimp-clouds' behavior — the way they pool in the eddies, the way they disperse in the surges, the way they orient themselves against the flow like compass needles pointing toward some magnetic north that only they can sense.

The current is not alive. But it is the closest thing Vaelys has to a will.

## The Platform

It is not alive either. But it is becoming part of the thing that is.

The steel is rusting. The composites are degrading. The lower levels are submerged and the kelp-analogues grow through the grating and the orange colonial organisms colonize the anchor chains and the shrimp live in the ballast tanks and the water seeps into everything that is not actively, constantly, expensively maintained. The Platform is fighting a slow war against incorporation, and it is losing.

The researchers maintain it. They repair the desalination plant when it breaks down. They replace the ROV tethers when the gardens grow over them. They scrape the barnacles off the hull. They paint the steel. They run the pumps. They keep the lights on and the air circulating and the communications array pointed at the sky. They are holding back the gardens with wrenches and sealant and electricity, and they are doing it with the knowledge that it is temporary — that the Platform has a lifespan, and that when the lifespan ends, the gardens will take it.

This has already happened, in a sense, to the lower levels. The submerged sections of the Platform are no longer human spaces. They are garden spaces. The kelp-analogues grow through them. The shrimp swarm in them. The grazers drift along their outer walls. The current flows through them as freely as it flows through the open water. The researchers do not go there. There is no reason to. The lower levels are Vaelys now.

And the Platform groans. The same frequency as the gardens. The same low-frequency creak of a structure under tension from an unending force. The steel vibrates with the current's push, and the vibration propagates through the frame, and after a few weeks aboard, the researchers cannot tell the sound of the station from the sound of the sea. The Platform has become part of the soundscape. It has become part of the groan.

## The Researchers

They are the newest thing in Vaelys. The most temporary. The most out of place.

They come on rotation — six months aboard, six months away. They are botanists and marine biologists and geologists and engineers and the occasional anthropologist drawn by the ruins on the basin rim. They live in the habitation modules above the waterline, in the grey light that filters through the overcast, surrounded by the sound of the current and the groan of the gardens and the constant mechanical noise of the desalination plant and the crane and the workshop.

They are careful. They are methodical. They collect samples that die in the lab and data that is always incomplete and observations that are always compromised by the poor visibility and the unpredictable current and the constant mechanical failures. They write papers that are read by other researchers who have never been to Vaelys and who cannot fully understand the place from data alone. They argue about taxonomy and ecology and the nature of the colonial organisms and the function of the shrimp's red organs and the identity of the things in the deep. They do not agree on much. They agree on this: Vaelys is extraordinary. Vaelys is important. Vaelys is not fully understood and may never be.

And they feel it. The feeling that sets in after a few weeks. The sense of being tolerated. The sense that the Platform is not a foothold but a guest house — a structure that the gardens have allowed to exist for now, on the understanding that it will not last. The researchers do not feel threatened. Vaelys is not hostile. But they feel *small*. They feel temporary. They feel like they are living inside something much larger and much older than themselves, something that is not aware of them but that is aware of everything, something that processes and recycles and continues with or without them.

Some of them love it. Some of them are disturbed by it. Most of them are both.

They leave, eventually. They go back to universities and laboratories and conferences, and they try to explain Vaelys to people who have not been there, and they fail, because Vaelys is not a place that can be explained. It can only be experienced. It can only be felt — the cold water and the grey light and the groan of the gardens and the pulse of the shrimp and the slow, patient, unending work of a world that has been left alone for a very long time and has become, in its solitude, something complete.

And the gardens remain. And the current flows. And the water carries its unnamed dissolved things through the groaning stalks and the pulsing shrimp-clouds and the slow drift of the grazers and the deep movement of the things in the dark. And Vaelys continues. Not because anyone maintains it. Not because anyone needs it. Because it is what it is, and it has been what it is for longer than anyone can measure, and it will be what it is long after the last researcher leaves and the Platform rusts through and sinks into the silt and becomes, itself, a seamount for new gardens to colonize.

Vaelys does not need witnesses. But it has them, for now. And they watch, and they record, and they feel the groan in their bones, and they know — with the certainty that comes from living inside a thing rather than studying it from outside — that they are seeing something rare. Something that the world produces only when it is left alone for long enough. Something that is not pristine and not wild and not natural in any simple sense, but that is *mature*. That has passed through its chaos and settled into a state of balance so deep and so stable that it looks, from the outside, like peace.

It is not peace. It is equilibrium. It is the sound of tension and resistance, sustained for so long that it has become a kind of music. It is the groan of the gardens of Vaelys, and it has been playing for a very long time, and it will play for a very long time still.
