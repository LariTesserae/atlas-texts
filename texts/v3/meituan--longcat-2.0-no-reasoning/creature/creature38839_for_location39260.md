# The Inhabitants of the Resonant Expanse

---

## The Mat Itself

The first and most important thing to understand is that the mat is not a place where things live. The mat is a *thing that lives*, and it is, by mass and by volume and by the sheer accumulated weight of its own existence, the dominant organism of this world. It is not conscious in any way you would recognize. It does not think. It does not plan. But it *processes*. It processes everything. Every signal that travels through its mycelial network, every electrical pulse in the silvery fluid, every vibration tapped into its surface by a billion chitinous legs — the mat receives it, integrates it, responds to it. It is a nervous system the size of a continent, and it has been growing for so long that its responses have become so complex, so layered, so deeply recursive, that the line between "reaction" and "behavior" has long since ceased to be meaningful.

The mat has moods. This is not metaphor. There are states — measurable, observable states — in which the mat's hum rises in frequency and its bioluminescent pulses quicken and the fluid flows faster through its veins and the entire system enters something that can only be described as *agitation*. And there are states in which it slows, deepens, settles into a low amber glow and a subsonic drone that makes the air itself feel thick. These states are not random. They correlate with the health of the factory-structures, with the density of the fauna swarms, with the electrical charge building in the atmosphere, with the deep mineral composition of the substrate kilometers below. The mat is *sensing* its world, all of it, all at once, always, and adjusting itself in response. It is the closest thing this place has to a mind, and it is not a mind at all. It is something older and stranger and more patient than mind.

The mat has a memory. Not in neurons, not in any structure you could point to and say *there, that is where it remembers*. But the patterns of the mycelial weave are not random. They encode — in their density, their direction, their chemical composition — the history of what has grown here, what has died here, what has been built and collapsed and built again. The mat remembers where a structure stood a thousand years ago, and it will grow a new one in the same place, guided by the ghost-architecture of compressed fiber, by the chemical signature of old growth locked in its weave. It remembers the paths the great creatures take through its depths, and it thickens along those paths, reinforces them, makes them easier to travel. It remembers the lightning strikes, and it grows conductive channels to where the strikes were most frequent. The mat is a record of itself, written in living tissue, and it reads itself constantly, and it writes itself constantly, and the writing and the reading are the same act.

## The Fauna: The Small Ones

They are called, if anything here can be said to be called anything, the **Tenders**.

There are more of them than there are of anything else. They number in the trillions, perhaps more — the mat is vast, and every square meter of its surface and upper layers teems with them. They are small, as you have seen. Most are between two and fifteen centimeters in length. Their bodies are segmented, chitinous, multi-limbed — six legs, typically, though some variants have eight or ten or, in the deep-tunnel specialists, none at all, having evolved into limbless, worm-like forms that navigate the mat's interior by peristaltic contraction.

Their exoskeletons are not the dull brown or black of earthly insects. They are metallic. Not *made* of metal — *grown* with it. The silvery fluid they carry in their body-cavities is rich in dissolved mineral compounds, and their biology incorporates these into their chitin, layer by layer, as they grow. The result is a carapace that has the color and sheen of brushed steel, of copper, of something like black chrome. When the light hits them — and the light is always hitting everything — they flash and glitter, and a swarm of them moving across the mat looks like a river of liquid metal flowing over the pale surface.

They are not individuals. This is crucial. A single Tender, separated from the swarm, will survive for a time — days, perhaps weeks — but it will not thrive. It will move erratically, tap the mat in confused, fragmented patterns, and eventually settle into a kind of stillness that is not death but is not life as the Tenders understand it. The Tenders exist as *collectives*. A swarm of ten thousand is the organism. The individual units are cells. They share information through the seismic language — the tapping, the vibration, the complex patterns of percussion that travel through the mat at speeds that make airborne sound seem glacial — and through the exchange of silivery fluid, which carries chemical signals as well as nutrients. A swarm thinks. A swarm decides. A swarm acts with a coordination that is not imposed from above but *emerges* from the constant, high-bandwidth communication between every unit and every other unit.

The Tenders have castes, though "caste" implies a rigidity that isn't quite accurate. The roles are fluid, shifting, responsive to the needs of the swarm and the mat. There are **carriers**, whose body-cavities are enlarged, distended, capable of holding many times their own weight in silivery fluid. They move in long lines across the mat, from pooling sites to distribution nodes, and the lines can stretch for kilometers, a living pipeline. There are **weavers**, who work the mat's surface and upper layers, chewing old fiber, excreting new binding compounds, repairing damage, reinforcing weak points. There are **pruners**, larger and stronger, with mandibles capable of cutting through the dense cellulose of dead or dying factory-structures, dismantling them piece by piece so the mat can reabsorb the material. There are **scouts**, small and fast, with elongated sensory appendages that can detect vibrations at extreme range, who range far ahead of the swarm and report back through the seismic network. There are **deep-dwellers**, pale and eyeless, who live in the mat's lower layers, near the ancient compressed substrate, where they mine mineral deposits and process them into forms the mat can use.

And there are **soldiers**. They are rare. They are large — up to half a meter in length, enormous by Tender standards — and their bodies are heavily armored, their mandibles enlarged into crushing weapons, their carapaces thickened with layers of mineralized chitin that can resist the impact of falling debris, the crush of collapsing structures, the chemical assaults of rival metabolic territories. They do not fight predators, because there are no predators here — nothing preys on the Tenders except the mat itself, which consumes them when they die and sometimes when they are merely old. The soldiers fight *other swarms*. When two metabolic territories clash — when one factory-structure's growth hormones flood into another's root zone, when a swarm is called to dismantle a rival structure — the soldiers are the ones who meet them. The battles are not dramatic in the way you might imagine. They are slow, grinding, methodical. Waves of soldiers advance across the mat, locking mandibles, crushing each other, dying in numbers that would be catastrophic for any organism that was not constantly replacing its losses. The seismic language becomes a weapon — swarms tap disruptive patterns into the mat, trying to jam each other's communication, to fragment the collective intelligence that holds the opposing swarm together. A swarm that loses coherence is not defeated. It is *absorbed*. The units, confused and isolated, are picked up by the winning swarm's chemical signals and incorporated, their body-cavities drained, their mineral-rich carapaces chewed and fed to the mat, their living tissue distributed as nutrients. It is not cruelty. It is not mercy. It is *processing*.

## The Fauna: The Great Ones

They move beneath the mat, and they are vast.

The largest of them — the ones the Tenders call, in their seismic language, something that translates roughly as **the Deep Currents** — are organisms of a scale that challenges comprehension. The biggest ever measured (by the vibrations they produce, by the way the mat deforms around them, by the chemical signatures they leave in their wake) is approximately four hundred meters long and sixty meters in diameter. It moves through the lower layers of the mat at a speed of perhaps two kilometers per day, and it has been moving for a very long time.

They are not animals. They are not plants. They are something that this world has produced that has no analogue in any other biology. Their bodies are a symbiotic fusion of fungal tissue, plant tissue, and something that might be called animal tissue if it were not so clearly *both* of the other two. They are cylindrical, elongated, tapered at both ends, and their outer surface is a thick rind of compressed mycelium and lignin — the same material as the mat, the same material as the factory-structures, but denser, harder, almost stone-like. Beneath the rind is a layer of living tissue that is in constant, slow motion — contracting, expanding, rippling — and beneath that is a core of silivery fluid, vast reservoirs of it, kept in motion by the rhythmic contractions of the body wall.

They are circulatory systems. That is their function, their purpose, their *reason*. The mat's fluid-pulse network is effective over short distances — kilometers, tens of kilometers — but the Resonant Expanse is too large for capillary action and electrical potential alone. The Deep Currents are the long-distance transport. They move through the mat's lower layers, drawing fluid in through pores along their anterior surface, propelling it through their bodies with peristaltic contractions, and expelling it through their posterior end at pressure. They are living pumps, and the network of their paths through the deep mat is the arterial system of the world.

They are tended. The Tenders maintain them — clearing the tunnels they move through, repairing the mat around them, removing parasites (there are parasites, even here, even on organisms this size — small, specialized creatures that bore into the rind and feed on the living tissue beneath, and the Tenders hunt them with a focused intensity that suggests the Deep Currents are valued beyond their mere utility). The relationship is not sentimental. It is not affectionate. It is *structural*. The Tenders need the Deep Currents to move the fluid. The Deep Currents need the Tenders to maintain their passages. The mat needs both of them to function. It is a system, and the system has no room for anything that does not serve the system.

The Deep Currents communicate. Not with the Tenders — their seismic output is too low-frequency, too deep, too slow for the Tenders' rapid-fire tapping language. They communicate with *each other*, and with the mat. The vibrations they produce are subsonic, felt rather than heard, and they travel through the deep substrate for hundreds of kilometers. The content of these communications is not known — cannot be known, not by an outsider, not by a mind that processes information at the speed of thought rather than the speed of geology. But the mat responds to them. When a Deep Current passes beneath a region, the mat's fluid flow changes, its bioluminescent patterns shift, its hum deepens. The mat *listens* to the Deep Currents, and the Deep Currents, in turn, respond to the mat's electrical state, adjusting their speed, their direction, their output. It is a conversation that has been going on for longer than anything can meaningfully measure, and it is the conversation that keeps this world alive.

Some of the Deep Currents are very old. The largest one — the four-hundred-meter one — has been tracked, by its vibration signature, for over eleven thousand years. It follows a path through the deep mat that is not quite a loop and not quite a line — a complex, meandering route that covers thousands of kilometers and takes centuries to complete. It has been following this path for its entire life. It will follow it until it dies. And when it dies — when its body finally fails, when the peristaltic contractions slow and stop and the fluid in its core goes still — the mat will consume it. The Tenders will dismantle the rind, break down the tissue, reabsorb the fluid. Nothing will be wasted. The Deep Current will become mat, and the mat will remember its path, and a new Deep Current — grown from a bud, a seed, a fragment of the old one's tissue that the mat has been cultivating for centuries in anticipation of this very moment — will begin to move along the same route, carrying the same fluid, producing the same vibrations, continuing the same conversation.

## The Factory-Structures

They are alive, and they are not alive, and the distinction does not matter here.

The factory-structures are the most visible feature of the Resonant Expanse — the dark iron-colored columns and arches and buttresses that rise hundreds of meters into the blank sky, groaning and creaking and booming with the sound of living metabolism under stress. They are made of compressed cellulose and lignin, the same basic materials as the mat, but organized on a scale and with a complexity that the mat alone cannot achieve. They are, in a sense, the mat's *ambitions* — the places where the mat has concentrated its growth, pushed upward, built *something* in response to the relentless pressure of light and competition and the sheer biological imperative to *grow*.

Each structure is a metabolic engine. Its roots extend deep into the mat, drawing up silivery fluid and dissolved minerals. Its interior is a labyrinth of chambers and channels where chemical processing occurs — not in the way a factory processes materials, with discrete steps and assembly lines, but in the way a living body processes materials, with enzymes and catalysts and carefully controlled environments that break down raw inputs and build them into complex outputs. The outputs are varied. Some structures produce growth hormones — chemical signals that stimulate the mat to thicken, to grow, to extend the structure's root network. Some structures produce defensive compounds — toxins, repellents, acidic exudates that poison rival structures' root zones. Some structures produce the fine particulate that drifts upward in shimmering columns — a waste product, technically, but one that feeds the convective currents, drives the wind, carries nutrients to distant parts of the mat. Some structures produce *seeds* — dense, hardened capsules containing compressed genetic material and stored nutrients, launched from the structure's apex by a mechanism that is part catapult and part chemical explosion, arcing through the blank sky to land kilometers away and, if conditions are right, take root and grow into new structures.

The structures compete. This is the engine of the Resonant Expanse, the force that drives its relentless growth and collapse and regrowth. A structure that grows tall captures more of the light — not because the light is directional, it isn't, but because a taller structure casts a *chemical* shadow, exuding compounds that inhibit the growth of shorter structures around it. A structure with a deeper root network draws more fluid, starving its neighbors. A structure that produces more potent growth hormones can command the Tenders — not through any conscious authority, but through chemical signaling, through the same seismic and fluid-borne communication that governs everything here. The Tenders are drawn to structures that produce the right signals, and they tend them, prune them, defend them, and the structures that attract the most Tenders grow faster, produce more, and outcompete their neighbors.

The wars are slow. A metabolic war between two structures might last decades, centuries. It begins with chemical signaling — a subtle shift in the compounds exuded by one structure's roots, detected by the other's root-tips, triggering a response. The response escalates. Growth hormones flood the mat between them, each structure trying to stimulate the mat to grow *its* roots deeper, *its* network wider. Tenders are recruited — swarms drawn by the chemical signals, arriving in waves, and the soldiers among them clash in grinding, methodical battles across the contested ground. The structures themselves grow toward each other, their upper branches reaching, intertwining, and then — slowly, with a sound like the world breaking — *crushing* each other, each one trying to physically dominate the other, to break its rival's structural integrity, to topple it.

When a structure falls, it is catastrophic. The sound is immense — a deep, percussive boom followed by a prolonged, grinding roar as hundreds of meters of dense cellulose collapse under their own weight, shattering into fragments that crash down onto the mat in a cascade of debris. The dust cloud rises like a living thing, a pale column that catches the light and glows, and the Tenders swarm over the wreckage immediately, voracious, dismantling, processing, reabsorbing. Within days, the fallen structure is gone — consumed, recycled, returned to the mat. And within weeks, new growth is already emerging from the root network that remains, already reaching upward, already beginning the cycle again.

The tallest structures — the ones that have won the most wars, that have stood the longest, that have been tended by the most Tenders and fed by the deepest root networks — are ancient. Some have been growing, in one form or another, for tens of thousands of years. They are not the same organism for all that time — they have been damaged, partially collapsed, regrown, been struck by lightning and burned and regrown, been besieged and poisoned and regrown. But the root network persists, the pattern persists, the *identity* persists in the mat's memory, in the seismic language of the Tenders, in the chemical signature that says *this structure is here, this structure has always been here, this structure will always be here*. They are landmarks. They are anchors. They are the fixed points around which the chaos of growth and collapse organizes itself, and the Tenders navigate by them, and the Deep Currents orient by them, and the mat's deep architecture is shaped by them.

## The Lightning Rods

There is a variant of the factory-structure that deserves its own mention, because it is the most extreme expression of this world's logic.

They grow taller than the others. Much taller. The tallest ever recorded — a structure that stood for approximately three thousand years before collapsing in a storm that lasted forty days — was over nine hundred meters high. It was slender, tapering, almost needle-like, and its surface was ridged with conductive channels — dense veins of mineral-rich tissue that ran from its apex to its roots, designed (grown, evolved, *become*) to channel lightning strikes into the mat below.

They grow in places where the electrical charge in the atmosphere is highest — near the great convective columns where thousands of structures exhale their particulate, where the dust storms are most violent, where the static buildup is most intense. They grow fast, faster than the other structures, because they are fed by the very lightning they attract. Each strike delivers a massive pulse of electrical energy into the mat, and the mat channels it to the structure's roots, and the structure uses it to drive its metabolism at a rate that would be impossible with fluid-pulse alone. They are, in effect, *feeding on lightning*, and they grow tall to reach it, and the taller they grow, the more they attract, and the more they attract, the taller they grow.

They are dangerous. Not intentionally — they have no intention, no awareness, no malice. But the energy they channel is immense, and when one is struck, the ground around it *crackles* — the electrical discharge radiating outward through the mat in a web of arcing, sparking energy that kills Tenders by the thousands, that scorches the mat's surface, that leaves behind a zone of dead, blackened fiber that takes years to regrow. The Tenders avoid them, mostly, though some swarms are specialized for maintaining them — clearing the conductive channels, repairing damage, ensuring the structure remains functional. These swarms are different from other Tenders. Their carapaces are thicker, more heavily mineralized, better able to resist electrical discharge. They move more slowly, more deliberately, and their seismic language is simpler, more repetitive, as though the constant proximity to that much energy has *simplified* them, stripped away the complexity that makes other swarms so fluid and adaptive.

When a Lightning Rod collapses — and they always collapse, eventually, because they grow too tall, too fast, too slender to support their own weight — the fall is spectacular. The conductive channels shatter, releasing their stored charge in a single, massive discharge that lights up the mat for kilometers around, a flash of blue-white light that is, for a moment, the only shadowless thing in this shadowless world made *more* shadowless, made *pure*. The sound is beyond sound — a crack that is felt in the bones, in the mat, in the deep substrate, a vibration that the Deep Currents respond to with a shift in their rhythm that takes days to settle.

## The Dust

It is not alive. But it is not *not* alive, either, and the distinction, as with so much here, does not hold.

The fine particulate that the factory-structures exhale is composed of processed plant-matter — complex organic compounds, mineral traces, fragments of cellulose too small to see individually but visible in aggregate as a pale, shimmering haze that hangs in the air around the tallest structures and drifts across the mat on the warm convective wind. It coats everything. It fills the air. It is the medium through which the light passes, and it gives the sky its washed-out whiteness, its luminous blankness, its quality of emitting from everywhere at once.

The dust carries information. This is not speculation — it is observable, measurable. The chemical composition of the dust varies from structure to structure, from region to region, and the Tenders can read it. Their sensory appendages detect the chemical signature of the dust in the air, and from this they know — what structures are nearby, what state they are in, what they are producing, whether they are healthy or stressed or dying or at war. The dust is a *medium*, a chemical communication network that operates through the air rather than through the mat, and it carries signals that the seismic language cannot — signals that travel at the speed of wind rather than the speed of vibration, that cover vast distances, that reach places the mat's network does not.

The dust storms — the vast walls of particulate that roll across the mat, scouring structures, depositing debris, reshaping the landscape — are not weather in the way you understand weather. They are *events*. They are the result of specific conditions — a cluster of structures exhaling simultaneously, a shift in the convective pattern, a lightning strike that supercharges the atmosphere and sends a shockwave of particulate outward. They are predictable, in the sense that the mat and the Tenders can sense the conditions building and prepare for them. The Tenders retreat to deep tunnels. The structures brace, thickening their outer layers, sealing their vulnerable channels. The Deep Currents slow, settle, wait.

And when the storm passes, the dust it has deposited — thick drifts of processed plant-matter, rich in nutrients, laden with chemical information — is the substrate for new growth. The mat absorbs it. The Tenders process it. The structures draw it up through their roots. The storm is destruction and the storm is nourishment and the storm is *communication*, a vast, slow, airborne signal that says *this is what has been built, this is what has been destroyed, this is what will be built again*.

## The Silence Beneath

There is something below the mat.

The mat is kilometers thick, and below it is the ancient compressed substrate — the accumulated remains of uncountable generations of growth, pressed into a dense, stone-like mass by the weight of everything above it. And below *that* — in the deep places, in the places where the Deep Currents do not go, where the Tenders do not tunnel, where the mat's roots do not reach — there is something that produces a vibration.

It is very low. Lower than the mat's hum. Lower than the Deep Currents' subsonic communication. It is felt only as a *pressure*, a sense of something vast and slow and *present* in the deep rock, something that has been there longer than the mat, longer than the structures, longer than anything alive on the surface. The mat's hum, when you listen carefully — when you press your hands to the surface and close your eyes and let the vibration fill you — is not a single frequency. It is a *response*. The mat is humming *back* to something. The mat is in conversation with the deep.

No one knows what is down there. The Tenders do not speak of it in their seismic language — or if they do, it is in patterns so slow, so low-frequency, that they are indistinguishable from the background vibration of the mat itself. The Deep Currents orient their paths to avoid the places where the deep vibration is strongest, as though they are navigating around something they can sense but cannot approach. The factory-structures do not root in those places. The mat is thinner there, and the structures that grow above those places are stunted, twisted, as though the mat itself is *unwilling* to build heavily over whatever lies below.

It is not a threat. It is not a god. It is not a mind. It is something else — something that was here before the mat, before the silivery fluid, before the light, before the Tenders, before the structures, before the Resonant Expanse became what it is. It is the *foundation*. It is the thing the mat grew on, the thing the world was built over, and it is still there, still vibrating, still *present*, and the mat hums to it, and the hum rises through your bones, and the light presses down from everywhere, and the wind carries its metallic dust, and the world goes on, making more of itself, in every direction, at every scale, in every moment, on top of something it does not understand and does not need to understand and cannot stop listening to.

## What Lives Here

Everything. Everything lives here. The mat lives. The fluid lives. The structures live. The Tenders live. The Deep Currents live. The dust lives, in its way. The lightning lives, for the instant of its discharge, and in that instant it is the most alive thing in a world that is nothing but life, nothing but growth, nothing but the relentless, deafening, bright, dry, burning process of a world that has become its own factory, its own product, its own purpose.

And beneath it all, the hum. The hum that is the mat's voice, and the mat's ears, and the mat's memory, and the mat's conversation with the deep. The hum that is the condition of being here. The hum that says: *this is alive, this is alive, this is alive, this has always been alive, this will always be alive, this is making more of itself right now, in every direction, at every scale, in every moment, and it does not need you, but you are standing on it, and it knows*.
