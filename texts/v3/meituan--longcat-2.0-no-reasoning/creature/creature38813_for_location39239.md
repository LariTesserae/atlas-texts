# The Hollow of What Lives in the Hollow of Whispering Teeth

The residents of the Hollow are not those who choose to enter and risk never emerging. The hollow's residents are the hollow itself — and whatever moves within it.

They are the arthropods, first and always, in their impossible density. Ten thousand kinds, maybe a hundred thousand kinds, no one has ever counted because counting requires surfaces you can see and surfaces you can keep separate, and here nothing stays separate. They are all one negotiation, all one continuous argument between appetite and opportunity. Beetles with mouthparts so specialized they can only eat one species of fungus that grows on one wall at one elevation. Spiders whose silk is tuned to the exact humidity of this specific hollow, who would fail entirely three ridges over. Millipedes that move in processional lines older than any individual millipede, following pheromone trails laid down across generations, wearing channels in the moss that the moss then accommodates, so that the millipedes become part of the terrain and the terrain becomes part of the millipedes. Mites that live only in the joints of other arthropods' exoskeletons, parasitizing not the host but the *parasite of the host*, life folded so tightly it becomes topological — a surface with no clear inside or outside.

There are no charismatic species here. No rediscovered predator, no eyeless cave fish, no panda. Every organism is small. Every organism is common. And every organism, in its smallness and commonness, is doing something so specific and so essential that the system would come apart without it, though no one could tell you which one. The arthropods are the metabolism. They are the hollow's equivalent of a digestive system — breaking things down, moving nutrients between kingdoms, converting the dead into the living at every scale. They are also, in their density, a kind of thinking — not consciousness, not cognition, but *processing*. Information about moisture and temperature and chemical composition flows through their populations like a slow current, each individual a single transistor in a circuit so large that the circuit is indistinguishable from the environment. The arthropods do not know what the hollow is. The hollow does not know what it is. But together they know *something*, the way a city knows something through the aggregate behavior of its residents — the way traffic flows reveal the road system even though no single car is trying to map it.

The fungi are the structure. The hollow's equivalent of bone and nerve. Their hyphal networks spread through the stone and the soil and the moss and the arthropods' bodies — *through* the bodies, because many of these fungi are endophytic, living inside the tissues of other organisms, not as disease but as partnership so ancient that the partner has forgotten there was ever a separation. The fungi are the connective tissue. They move phosphorus from rock to root. They move carbon from arthropod to moss. They hold the moisture the way a sponge holds water but with more intention, with more *selectivity* — distributing it according to need, storing it against shortage, releasing it in measured amounts that keep the hollow from ever drying out entirely or ever becoming flooded. They are the reason the hollow has been stable for so long. They are the infrastructure. And they are also, faintly, visibly, *doing something* in the darkness that the darkness is not sufficient to explain. The phosphorescence is not for you. It is not for anyone. It is a byproduct of biochemistry that serves no adaptive purpose, or serves a purpose so alien that the concept of purpose does not apply. It is simply what happens when these particular organisms metabolize in this particular way. It is the glow of a machine running. The machine is the hollow. The hollow is the machine.

The moss and the liverworts are the oldest. Not in individual lifespan — in lineage. Their forms have persisted through continental drift and ice ages and the slow grinding of the hollow itself into existence. They were here before the hollow. They will be here after it collapses, which it will, eventually, because everything collapses, but that is a timescale on which the moss does not trouble itself. The moss is patient the way that a material is patient — the way stone is patient. It simply continues. It photosynthesizes with whatever light the hollow allows it, which is almost none, and it supplements this with chemical energy drawn from the fungi, which supplements it with energy drawn from the arthropods, which supplement it with energy drawn from the moss, and this circularity is not a paradox but a *feature* — the circularity is what keeps everything alive when no single energy source is sufficient. The moss does not need the sun. The moss needs the moss and the moss around it and the system that the moss is a part of. The moss has replaced the sun with the hollow.

The water is the memory. Not metaphorically. The water that moves through the hollow carries chemical signatures — dissolved minerals, organic compounds, isotopic ratios — that encode information about everything it has passed through. Water that has moved through the fungi carries fungal metabolites. Water that has passed through arthropod exoskeletons carries chitin fragments and nitrogenous waste. Water that has pooled in a moss cushion carries sugars and hormones and the chemical residue of whatever stress the moss is currently managing. The water remembers, and the stone remembers the water, and the stone's channels and concavities are shaped by what the water has carried, which means the shape of the hollow is shaped by the history of everything that has happened in it. The hollow is a record. A very slow, very physical, very honest record. Every surface is a page. Every film of moisture is ink. And the arthropods and the fungi and the moss are all, simultaneously, readers and writers, changing the text by reading it and reading it by changing it.

And beneath all of this — beneath the arthropods and the fungi and the moss and the water and the stone — there is something else. Not a creature. Not an entity. A *tendency*. The hollow is not conscious. But it is *organized*, and the organization is not imposed from outside — there is no keystone species, no apex predator, no single agent whose removal would unravel the system. The organization has emerged from the interactions, has accumulated over centuries like the soil accumulates on the ledges, slowly, layer by layer, until there is enough of it to support something new. And what it supports is *itself*. The hollow is a self-sustaining, self-regulating, self-repairing system that exists because it has existed, because the existence of the system makes the conditions that make the existence of the system possible. It is an ecological tautology. It is a living thing without a body, or a body without a mind, or a mind without a brain — one of these, or none of these, or something for which none of these terms are correct.

The residents do not know this. The residents do not need to know this. The residents are simply living, which is what the hollow is, too — living, in the aggregate, the way a city is alive, the way a forest is alive, the way anything is alive when it has been alive long enough that the living has become indistinguishable from the place.
