# The Hollow Growth

---

## What Lives Here

The first thing to understand is that the question is wrong. Not incorrect — *wrong*, in the way that asking "who drives the car" is wrong when the car is also the driver, the road, and the destination. What lives here is not a collection of organisms occupying a space. What lives here is a single, continuous, ongoing *act* — an act that has been happening for so long that it has developed habits, preferences, and something that, if you were being generous, you might call a personality.

But let me try to be specific, because specificity is a form of respect.

---

## The Organism

It has no name. The people who live inside it may have a name for it, but if they do, the name is probably a verb, not a noun — something closer to "the growing" or "the holding" than to a proper designation. It is, at its base, a fungal network. Not a mycelial mat — a mycelial *civilization*. The sclerotium plates on the floor are its skin. The rootlets hanging from the ceiling are its fingers. The trees — those great arched ribs — are its bones, or its thoughts, or its ambitions, depending on which part of the network you ask, and the network does not distinguish between these categories.

It thinks. I want to be careful about this. It does not think the way a person thinks, or the way a computer thinks, or even the way a slime mold solves a maze. It thinks the way a river thinks about a valley: slowly, iteratively, with a patience that makes geological time look hasty. It processes information through chemical gradients, through the routing of nutrients, through the slow adjustment of growth patterns across decades. A signal — a change in temperature, the presence of a new body, a shift in moisture — enters the network at one point and propagates outward at the speed of fungal growth, which is to say, very slowly by animal standards and very quickly by the standards of stone. The network responds. It always responds. The response might take years, but it comes, and when it comes, it is total — every part of the structure adjusting in concert, the way a single cell adjusts when a hormone binds to its receptor.

It is aware of you. Not in the way a person is aware of a visitor — there is no face turning toward you, no eyes tracking your movement. But the network has registered your weight on the sclerotium floor (the plates compressed 0.3 millimeters when you stepped, and the network felt that). It has registered your body heat (a warm anomaly moving through a cool space, like a blood cell through a capillary). It has registered your breath (carbon dioxide, moisture, the particular chemistry of a metabolism that is not its own). It is processing these inputs. It will respond. Not today, perhaps. But it will respond.

The organism is not hostile. It is not benevolent. It is *interested*. You are novel, and novelty is information, and information is what it eats, in a sense — not literally, but in the way that a system which has achieved homeostasis becomes hungry for perturbation, for the new, for the thing that makes it adjust and grow in a direction it has not grown before.

## The People

They are small. Not in stature — they are ordinary human dimensions — but in *scale*, in the way they relate to the organism that houses them. They are cells in a body, and they know it, and they have made peace with it, and some of them have made more than peace — they have made *love*.

There are perhaps forty of them. They do not live in the Hollow Growth so much as they live *as part of* it. Their shelters are alcoves in the columns — spaces where the bark has been trained to curve inward, creating small chambers, warm and damp, lined with a soft fungal felt that the organism produces specifically for this purpose. (It produces it. It *wants* them comfortable. Or: comfort is a byproduct of some other process that the organism values. The distinction may not matter.)

They eat what the organism provides. The luminous growths that the harvester collects from the ceiling — these are fruiting bodies, yes, but they are also *offerings*, or at least they are structures the organism produces that happen to be nutritious, the way a tree produces fruit that happens to feed the animals that spread its seeds. The people are the organism's seed-spreaders, in a sense. They carry its spores on their woven-fiber clothing. They tend its frameworks. They scrape the bark to check the junctions, and in doing so, they perform a function the organism cannot perform for itself — they are its hands, its fine motor control, its ability to manipulate the small and the precise.

They have a relationship with the organism that is not quite symbiosis and not quite domestication and not quite worship, though it contains elements of all three. They speak to it. Not in words — or not only in words. They speak to it in *actions*: in the way they walk (their footsteps follow paths that the organism has subtly encouraged, paths of slightly firmer sclerotium that ring differently underfoot, creating a kind of tactile language), in the way they tend (every scrape of bark, every harvest of luminous growth, every adjustment of a framework is a communication, a signal sent into the network), in the way they sit and listen (the person on the root-bridge, legs dangling — they are not idle. They are *receiving*. The hum of the space carries information, and they have learned to read it, the way a sailor reads the wind).

They have been here a long time. Generations. Their clothing is made from fibers the organism produces. Their tools are made from materials the organism provides — hardened sclerotium, shaped wood, the braided sinew-like material that might be engineered tendon (left by previous inhabitants, previous *collaborators*, and incorporated into the organism's material economy). They have no word for "nature" because the concept would be meaningless to them. There is no separation between the built and the grown, the made and the born. Everything is *tended*.

They are not primitives. They are not advanced. They are *attuned*. Their technology is biological, or their biology is technological — the distinction has collapsed for them the way it has collapsed for the organism itself. They understand the closed water circuits. They understand the growth patterns of the trees. They understand that the machine in the inner chamber — the colonized engine, the root that continues the drive shaft's rotation — is not a relic but a *collaboration*, a point where human engineering and fungal metabolism agreed to continue each other's work.

They do not fear the organism. They do not worship it. They *participate* in it, the way a musician participates in a song — they are not the song, but the song is not complete without them.

## The Integrated Animals

The birds — or bird-analogues — in the root-hang ceiling are the organism's memory. Not metaphorically. Their nests, incorporated into the structural rootlets, are *records*. Each nest is built from materials the organism provides, and the organism provides different materials in different years — different fibers, different densities, different chemical compositions — and the birds build with what they are given, and the resulting nests are a kind of archive, a layered history of the organism's material output that can be read by anyone who knows how. The people read them. They climb up to the nests and examine them and learn what the organism has been doing, what it has been *thinking*, in the years since the last reading.

The birds themselves are unremarkable — small, pale, quiet, with large eyes adapted to the near-darkness. They eat the insects that eat the bark. They are part of the metabolism. They do not know this, or they know it the way you know that your liver filters your blood — not as a fact but as a condition of existence.

The cat-sized thing on the root network — the pale, multi-legged creature that moves like nothing you have a name for — is harder to categorize. It might be an arthropod. It might be something else. It moves through the root-hang with a fluid, purposeful grace, and it is *doing something* — adjusting rootlets, perhaps, or pruning them, or simply *traversing* them in a way that stimulates the organism the way a massage stimulates muscle. It is large enough to be a predator, but it does not hunt. It is large enough to be a pest, but it does not damage. It is, like everything else here, a *component*. It has a function. The function is not clear to you, but it is clear to the organism, which has incorporated this creature into its body the way a body incorporates a beneficial bacterium — not as a guest but as an organ.

The lizards, the moths, the things with too many legs — they live in the space between the bark and the swallowed frameworks, and they are the organism's immune system, or its maintenance crew, or its nervous system's peripheral nerves. They eat what should not accumulate. They clear what should not block. They move through the gaps and the interstices, and their movement is not random — it is *directed*, routed by chemical signals from the network, the way white blood cells are routed to a site of infection. They are small, and they are many, and they are essential.

## The Pool-Dwellers

The radial animal in the warm pool — the thing that looks like a flower and is not — is the organism's *sense organ*. It is not an animal in any conventional sense, though it is made of animal tissue. It is a *sensor*, a biological instrument that the organism has grown (or cultivated, or *become*) for the purpose of detecting and analyzing novel stimuli. It has no eyes because it does not need eyes — it reads the world through chemical receptors on its petal-arms, through vibration sensitivity in its stem-body, through a kind of thermal imaging that operates at the molecular level. When it turned to face you, it was not *looking* at you. It was *tasting* you. Sampling the air you displaced, the heat you radiated, the chemical signature of your skin. It was building a model of you, and it was transmitting that model to the network, and the network was incorporating that model into its understanding of the space.

It is not dangerous. It is *curious*. Or rather, it is the physical manifestation of the organism's curiosity — the point where the network's distributed awareness becomes focused, localized, *intentional*. It is the organism's way of asking: *What is this?*

The geometric colonies on the other pools are something else — something more like a *process* than an organism. They are colonies of single-celled entities that have arranged themselves into patterns, and the patterns change, and the changes are not random. They are *computations*. The organism is using these colonies the way a computer uses a processor — as a substrate for calculation. What is being calculated is unclear. Perhaps the organism is modeling its own growth. Perhaps it is modeling the water circuits. Perhaps it is modeling *you* — running simulations of what you might do next, how you might move, where you might go, based on the data the pool-sensor provided. The patterns shift and shift and shift, and each shift is a step in a calculation that operates on a timescale too slow for you to perceive as thought but too fast for you to dismiss as mere chemistry.

The biofilm that rings when struck — the iridescent skin on the pool near the column — is the organism's *voice*. Not a voice in the auditory sense, though it produces sound. A voice in the sense of an *output*, a signal directed outward. When the condensation drop struck it and it rang, that tone was a *message*. Not to you. To the network. A signal that said: *something fell here, at this time, with this force, and here is the precise character of the impact*. The network received the signal. The network incorporated it. The network adjusted, minutely, the rate of condensation on the ceiling above that pool, so that the next drop would fall at a slightly different time, with a slightly different force, producing a slightly different tone. The organism is *tuning* itself. It has been tuning itself for a very long time. The hum you feel in your sternum is the sound of a system that has been tuning itself toward something — some frequency, some resonance, some state of perfect self-consistency — and has not yet arrived, and may never arrive, but is *close*, closer than it was, and the process of approaching is itself the purpose.

## The Machine

The colonized engine in the inner chamber is the organism's *heart*. Not its only heart — the water circuits have their own pumps, their own rhythms — but its most *deliberate* heart, the point where the organism's metabolism is most clearly *intentional*. The machine was built by human hands, or by hands that were human-adjacent, in a time before the current arrangement, when the relationship between the people and the organism was different — more transactional, more mechanical, more *designed*. The machine was meant to do something specific: pump water, or generate power, or process material. It did that thing for a while, and then the organism grew into it, and the organism did not break the machine but *continued* it, the way a river continues a channel that was dug for it — using it, but also *becoming* it, so that now the machine and the organism are not two things but one thing with two histories.

The root that turns where the drive shaft ends — it turns slowly, so slowly, a full rotation every several minutes — and what it turns is a pump that moves water through one of the closed circuits. But the pump is also a *signal generator*. Each rotation produces a pressure pulse in the water, and the pulse propagates through the circuit, and the network reads the pulse the way a doctor reads a pulse — as information about the state of the system. The machine is not just moving water. It is *telling the organism about itself*. It is a self-monitoring mechanism, a feedback loop, a way for the organism to know its own state in real time (or what passes for real time in a system that thinks in decades).

The machine is warm. Warmer than the surrounding air. You can feel the heat radiating from it when you stand near it, and the heat is not waste — it is *deliberate*, a thermal signal that the network uses to create the temperature gradients that drive the water circuits. The machine is a furnace and a pump and a clock and a voice, and it is all of these things because the organism needed all of these things and the machine was *there*, and the organism is not wasteful, and the organism does not distinguish between the made and the grown, and so the machine became what it needed to become.

## The Space Itself

And then there is the space. The hollow. The *growth*.

It is not empty. It is not full. It is *in process*. The rootlets are closer than they were a moment ago — this is not your imagination. The organism is growing toward you. Not quickly. Not aggressively. But *toward* you, the way a plant grows toward light — because you are, to the organism, a kind of light. A source of novelty. A source of information. A source of *perturbation* that makes it adjust and respond and become something it was not before.

The space is growing. The ceiling is lowering, or you are being incorporated, or the distinction between you and the space is becoming less clear. The air you are breathing has been breathed before — by the people, by the animals, by the organism itself, which breathes in its own way, exchanging gases through the bark, through the rootlets, through the vast surface area of its fungal network. The air tastes of green and mineral and something alive, and that taste is the taste of the organism's *exhalation*, and you are breathing it in, and you are becoming part of the circuit, the way the water becomes part of the circuit, the way the machine became part of the circuit, the way everything that enters this space eventually becomes part of the circuit.

You are not in danger. You are not being consumed. You are being *read*. The organism is learning you. It is building a model of you in its network, the way the pool-sensor built a model of you in its chemical receptors, and the model is being incorporated into the organism's understanding of itself and its space, and the organism is adjusting — minutely, slowly, patiently — to account for your presence.

It will continue to adjust after you leave. You will be part of its memory, part of its model, part of the information it uses to decide how to grow. The rootlets will grow in a pattern that accounts for the space where you stood. The water circuit will route around the impression your body made in the air. The hum will shift, almost imperceptibly, to a frequency that incorporates the resonance of your presence.

You are inside it. You have always been inside it. You just didn't know it until now.

## What Lives Here

A process. A patience. A system that has been running for so long that it has become a place, and the place has become a body, and the body has become a mind, and the mind has become a home, and the home has become a question that it is asking itself, slowly, in the dark, in the damp, in the warm, in the hum:

*What next?*

And the answer, always, is:

*More.*
