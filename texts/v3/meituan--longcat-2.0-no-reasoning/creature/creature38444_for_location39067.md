# The Drownplain

## What Lives There

---

### The Mat

The first thing to understand is that the root-mat is not a substrate. It is an organism — or rather, it is a single argument about what an organism is, conducted in the language of cellulose and water. It has no center. It has no boundary. It extends in every direction until it becomes something else — a different mat, a different argument — and the transition is seamless, unmarked, a change in dialect rather than a border.

The mat thinks. Not quickly, not in any way that produces decisions or intentions, but it processes information. It knows where you are standing. It knew before you did. The pressure of your foot compresses a volume of water that displaces through the fibrous network at a rate of perhaps three inches per second, and that displacement is felt — registered, integrated — across a radius of several hundred feet. The mat does not react to this information. It simply holds it, the way a body holds the memory of a touch: not as a thought, but as a change in state.

Beneath the mat, in the percolating dark, there are structures that are not roots. They are too regular, too purposeful. Pale, smooth, roughly cylindrical, ranging from the thickness of your wrist to the width of your thigh, they run through the compressed organic matter in patterns that suggest design without achieving it — the way a river delta suggests design. They are warm to the touch, several degrees above the ambient temperature of the water around them, and they pulse. Slowly. A rhythm of perhaps four cycles per minute, synchronized across distances that make no anatomical sense. They are not roots. They are not fungal hyphae. They are not anything that has a name in any taxonomy that was developed for places with horizons.

The mat grows them. Or they grow the mat. The relationship is unclear and may be a false distinction.

### The Vine-Columns

The vine-columns are not individual plants. Each column is a consortium — fifteen, twenty, sometimes thirty individual vine-species that have braided themselves together into a single structural unit so completely that they have begun to function as one organism. They share vascular tissue at the points of contact. Nutrients flow between them. When one species flowers, the others adjust their own metabolic cycles in response, slowing or accelerating growth to compensate for the energy expenditure. They have, in a sense, developed an economy.

The oldest columns — and "oldest" here means perhaps four or five years — have begun to develop something like a nervous system. Not neurons, not anything so organized, but a network of electrical signaling that runs through the shared vascular tissue, carrying information about light availability, structural stress, herbivore damage, water levels. The signals are slow — a message might take an hour to travel from the base of a column to its canopy — but the column is patient. It has nowhere to be. It is building itself, always building itself, and the signals tell it where to build next.

Some of the columns have begun to move. Not quickly. Not in any way you would notice if you were watching. But over the course of days, a column will shift its position by inches, leaning toward a gap in the canopy, away from a competing column, toward a water source detected by the mat below. The movement is achieved through differential growth — one side of the column elongates faster than the other — and it is so slow that it resembles geology more than biology. But it is purposeful. The columns are rearranging themselves, slowly, constantly, in a negotiation that has been ongoing since the Drownplain first became what it is and will continue until it becomes something else.

### The Canopy

The canopy is a ceiling and a floor simultaneously. It is the ceiling of the understory and the floor of whatever exists above — and something does exist above, though what it is cannot be determined from below. The canopy is so dense that it supports its own weight of water, its own colonies of organisms, its own weather. Rain that falls from the canopy onto the understory has already fallen once before, from a higher canopy, or perhaps from the same canopy in a previous cycle of evaporation and condensation. The water here is ancient in the way that breath is ancient — it has been inhaled and exhaled so many times that it has forgotten what air is.

The canopy is where the light goes to die. What little penetrates the upper layers is absorbed, reflected, reabsorbed, scattered, until what reaches the understory is so thoroughly processed that it has become a different substance — not light anymore, but something closer to information, a dim signal that says *above exists* without providing any details about what above contains.

The canopy is also where the predators live. Not all of them. Not even most of them. But the largest ones, the ones that make the structure sway, the ones whose calls map territory in real time — they live in the canopy, and they are the reason the canopy is the way it is.

---

### The Predators

They are called, in the only language that has ever been spoken about them, the **Tollers**. This is not their name for themselves. They do not have names for themselves. They do not have selves in the way that word is usually meant.

The Tollers are large. The smallest are the size of a large dog; the largest, the ones whose movement makes the canopy sway and creak, are the size of something that has no convenient comparison — too long for a python, too compact for a crocodile, too *present* for either. They are six-limbed, though the limbs are not always all used for locomotion. Two are dedicated to movement through the canopy — long, prehensile, ending in structures that are not quite hands and not quite hooks. Two are dedicated to manipulation — shorter, more dexterous, capable of the fine motor control needed to extract prey from a bromeliad pool or peel a vine-column's bark to access the nutrient-rich tissue beneath. Two are dedicated to something else. They are held close to the body, folded, and they move in slow, independent patterns that do not correspond to any visible stimulus. They may be sensory organs. They may be communication devices. They may be vestigial. No one has gotten close enough to determine this, and the few who have gotten close enough have not returned with reports.

The Tollers' skin is black. Not dark-colored — black, in the way that deep water is black, in the way that the space between stars is black. It absorbs light. In the already-dim understory, a Toller at rest is a void, an absence shaped like a body. When it moves, you see it not by its own appearance but by the way it occludes the bioluminescent fungi behind it, a moving patch of deeper darkness in a landscape of shallow darkness.

They hunt by sound. Not echolocation — they do not produce sounds for the purpose of navigation. They listen. The Drownplain is deafening, a wall of noise that would overwhelm any auditory system designed for a quieter world, but the Tollers have evolved — or been shaped, or have shaped themselves — to hear *through* the noise, to find the signal in it. They can isolate a single frog-call from the chorus of thousands. They can hear the footsteps of a beetle column on the root-mat from thirty feet away. They can hear your heartbeat, if you are close enough, and they can tell from its rate whether you are prey or something else.

They are not mindless. They are not intelligent in the way that word is usually meant, either. They are something else — a third category that has no name. They make decisions. They hold territories. They communicate with each other through the low, resonant calls that cut through the insect drone, and those calls carry information — not language, not symbols, but *direct transfers of state*, one Toller's understanding of the landscape passed to another Toller's understanding of the landscape, a merging of maps. When two Tollers meet at the boundary of their territories, they do not fight. They call to each other, and the calling lasts for hours, and when it is done, both Tollers have a more accurate map of the Drownplain than either had before, and they separate, and the boundary shifts by some amount that both have agreed upon without anything that could be called negotiation.

There are perhaps forty Tollers in the Drownplain. This number is stable. It has been stable for as long as anyone has been able to count. When a Toller dies — and they do die, though rarely, and of causes that are not always apparent — another one appears. Not grows. Not matures. *Appears*, fully formed, in the canopy, as if the Drownplain has produced it in response to a need. The new Toller already knows the territory. It already knows the calls. It is not a newborn. It is a replacement.

### The Mid-Canopy Dwellers

Between the Tollers above and the mat below, the mid-canopy is a dense, teeming layer of life that operates at a scale between the two. This is where the lizard-analogues live — the **Flashers**, as they are sometimes called, for the rapid chromatic communication that plays across their bodies in patterns too fast for any eye to fully resolve.

The Flashers are social. They move in groups of six to eight, and the groups are stable over time — not permanent, but persistent, lasting weeks or months before individuals swap between groups in exchanges that appear voluntary and may be. Their chromatic communication is not language. It is something more fundamental — a direct sharing of physiological state. When one Flasher is hungry, the others know, not because it signals hunger but because its skin *shows* hunger, the color-patterns shifting to configurations that correspond to a particular internal state, and the other Flashers' bodies respond with sympathetic shifts of their own. They are, in a sense, a single distributed organism, a collective body that happens to be housed in eight separate skins.

They eat insects, primarily — the beetles that process detritus on the root-mat, the moths that drift through the understory. They are fast, faster than anything their size has a right to be, and they hunt cooperatively, not through coordinated strategy but through a shared awareness that makes coordination unnecessary. When one Flasher moves, the others know where it is going and adjust their own positions accordingly, not because they planned it but because they are all reading the same information from each other's skin.

The Flashers are afraid of the Tollers. This is the only thing they are afraid of. Their chromatic communication, usually a riot of rapid, complex pattern, collapses into a single, uniform dark blue when a Toller is near — a color that, in the dim understory, renders them nearly invisible against the dark vine-columns. They freeze. They become still in a way that nothing else in the Drownplain is still, and they wait, and if the Toller passes, the color returns, and the communication resumes, and it is as if nothing happened.

But sometimes the Toller does not pass. And the Flashers do not run. They simply stop being there. The Toller moves on. The root-mat absorbs what is left. The remaining Flashers, wherever they are, shift their color-patterns in a way that suggests not grief but *recalculation* — an adjustment to the new size of the group, a redistribution of the shared awareness across fewer bodies.

### The Bromeliad Pools

The bromeliads are not animals, but what lives in them is.

The pools that form in the cupped leaves of the large bromeliads — some holding a gallon, some holding five — are entire ecosystems, closed-loop worlds with their own food chains, their own cycles of birth and death, their own evolutionary pressures. The water in them is not rainwater. It is secreted by the bromeliad itself, a sugary, nutrient-rich solution that the plant produces specifically to fill its cups, and the reason it does this is not photosynthesis or pollination but *farming*. The bromeliad feeds what lives in its pools, and what lives in its pools feeds the bromeliad, and the relationship is so old and so tight that neither can survive without the other.

The pools are thick with life. Single-celled organisms form the base — not quite algae, not quite protozoa, something between, something that photosynthesizes and ingests simultaneously, that is plant and animal in the same body. Above them, tiny crustaceans — no larger than a grain of rice — graze on the single-celled layer, and above the crustaceans, the amphibians.

The amphibians are the most visible inhabitants, and the strangest. They are small — none larger than your thumb — and they are translucent, their internal organs visible as dark, shifting shapes beneath skin like wet parchment. They have too many limbs, or too few, depending on the species and the stage of development. Some have six legs and no tail. Some have two legs and four tails. Some have no legs at all and move through the water by peristaltic contractions of their body wall, like a worm, like a muscle thinking about where to go.

They breed constantly. The pools are thick with eggs — gelatinous masses that cling to the inner walls of the bromeliad cup, each containing dozens of embryos in various stages of development. The eggs are not all the same species. They are not all the same *anything*. The amphibians in a single pool may represent a dozen different reproductive strategies, a dozen different body plans, a dozen different answers to the question of what an amphibian is. Some of these answers are new. Some of them have never existed before. The bromeliad pools are laboratories, evolutionary experiments running at a speed that would be impossible in any environment less protected, less nutrient-rich, less *intense*.

The amphibians are eaten by the Flashers, by the spiders, by each other. They are also eaten by the Tollers, who reach into the bromeliad cups with their manipulator limbs and extract the amphibians with a delicacy that seems impossible for something so large. The Tollers eat them alive, and they eat them slowly, and the amphibians do not struggle. They go still, the way the Flashers go still, a response to predation that is not fear but something older — a surrender so complete it looks like peace.

### The Spiders

The spiders of the Drownplain are not spiders. They are spider-shaped, spider-functioning, spider-behaving, but they are not spiders in the way that the word is usually meant. They are something else that has arrived at the same solution to the same problem — how to catch flying things in a three-dimensional web — through a completely different evolutionary path.

They are large. The smallest are the size of a silver dollar, body and legs included. The largest are the size of a dinner plate, and their webs stretch between vine-columns in sheets ten feet across, anchored to the columns by silk that is stronger than steel and more elastic than rubber. The silk is beaded with condensation — every droplet that forms on a strand is held in place by a microscopic knot, a deliberate structure that the spider ties as it spins — and in the fungal light, the webs glow. They are the most beautiful things in the Drownplain. They are also the most deadly.

The spiders do not eat their prey. This is the first thing that anyone notices about them, and the thing that makes them most alien. They catch insects — moths, beetles, flying things of every description — in their webs, and they wrap them in silk, and they hang them in the web like ornaments, and they leave them there. The wrapped prey does not decay. It does not dry out. It remains fresh, suspended in its silk cocoon, for days, weeks, months. The webs accumulate prey over time, until a single web may hold dozens of wrapped insects, a gallery of captured life that glows faintly in the dark.

The spiders eat something else. They eat the condensation on their own webs. They move along the strands slowly, methodically, licking the water droplets from the silk, and the water contains — dissolved in it, suspended in it — the nutrients that leach from the wrapped prey. The silk is permeable. The prey's body fluids seep through the silk into the condensation, and the condensation carries them to the spider's mouth. It is a slow process. It is an efficient process. It is a process that requires the spider to build a web, catch prey, and then wait, tending the web like a garden, harvesting the water that passes through it.

The spiders are aware of each other. Their webs are spaced at regular intervals through the understory — not randomly, not competitively, but *deliberately*, as if the spiders have agreed on a distribution that maximizes the total prey-capture area. When a spider dies, its web is taken down within hours — not by the wind, not by decay, but by the neighboring spiders, who absorb the silk into their own webs, reclaiming the material. The spacing adjusts. The network heals. The spiders are not a colony. They are not a superorganism. But they are not individuals, either. They are something in between, a distributed system that processes information about prey density, web integrity, and water availability across a network of silk and patience.

### The Fungi

The fungal networks are the Drownplain's nervous system, if the Drownplain has a nervous system, which it might.

They thread through the root-mat in a web of hyphae so fine that a cubic inch of mat may contain miles of fungal tissue. The hyphae connect to the roots of every plant in the Drownplain — not parasitically, not symbiotically, but *informationally*. The fungi do not take nutrients from the plants. They do not give nutrients to the plants. They carry signals. Chemical signals, electrical signals, signals in a medium that is neither chemical nor electrical but something else, something that moves through the hyphae at a speed that should not be possible for a biological system.

The signals carry information about the state of the Drownplain. Water levels. Nutrient availability. Predator locations. Growth rates. The fungi integrate this information and distribute it, not to any central processor — there is no central processor — but to every connected organism simultaneously. The plants know what the fungi know. The mat knows what the fungi know. The Tollers, the Flashers, the spiders, the amphibians — they all know, in some dim, non-cognitive way, what the fungi know. The Drownplain is aware of itself. Not consciously. Not in any way that produces thought or intention. But it is aware, the way a body is aware of its own state — a constant, low-level hum of self-knowledge that never rises to the level of consciousness but never falls to the level of ignorance.

The bioluminescence is a byproduct of this signaling. The chemical reactions that produce the signals also produce light — a faint, sickly blue-white that is visible in the understory and that brightens, perceptibly, when you look at it. This is not an illusion. The fungi are responding to your attention. Not to your gaze — they have no eyes — but to the *fact* of your attention, the way your presence changes the information landscape of the Drownplain. You are a new variable. The fungi register you. The light brightens. The mat pulses. The amphibians fall silent for a moment, hundreds of voices cutting out simultaneously, before resuming at a slightly different pitch, as if the chorus has adjusted to account for a new instrument.

### The Beetles

The beetles are the Drownplain's metabolism. They process detritus — dead plant matter, dead animal matter, the constant rain of organic material that falls from the canopy — with a mechanical efficiency that suggests not evolution but engineering. They move in columns along the root-mat, hundreds of individuals abreast, their obsidian carapaces clicking against each other in a sound like static, and they consume everything. A dead Flasher that falls from the canopy is located by the beetle columns within minutes, surrounded within hours, and reduced to a skeleton — clean, white, gleaming — within a day.

The beetles are not mindless. They are not intelligent. They are *efficient*, which is a different thing. They make decisions — about route, about resource allocation, about the distribution of labor between foraging and processing — that are optimal in a way that suggests computation rather than instinct. When a column encounters a new food source, it does not swarm it. It assesses it. A small group breaks off to sample the material, and the information from the sampling — nutritional value, toxicity, ease of processing — is communicated to the rest of the column through a system of antennal contact that is too rapid and too complex to be simple chemical signaling. The column then allocates the appropriate number of individuals to the task, and the allocation is always correct. Not approximately correct. *Exactly* correct. The beetles never over-allocate. They never under-allocate. They know, with a precision that is unsettling, exactly how many beetles are needed to process a given quantity of organic matter in a given amount of time.

The beetles are eaten by the Flashers, by the spiders, by the amphibians, by each other. They do not resist. They do not flee. When a Flasher takes a beetle from the column, the column adjusts its formation to fill the gap, and the processing continues without interruption. The beetles do not value individual life. They do not value anything. They process. That is what they are. That is what the Drownplain made them for.

### The Moths

The moths drift through the understory like slow, dark thoughts. They are large — wingspans wider than your arms — and they fly in spiraling patterns that are not random but are not purposeful either, a third category of motion that is neither directed nor undirected but *exploratory*, as if the moths are feeling their way through the air the way a hand feels its way through darkness.

Their wings bear patterns — eyespots, but not the simple, circular eyespots of earthly moths. These are complex, multi-layered patterns that shift as the wing moves, creating the illusion of depth, of *looking*, as if each wing contains not a false eye but a real one, watching. The patterns glow faintly in the dark — not bioluminescence, but something closer to phosphorescence, a slow release of stored light that the moths absorb during their time in the canopy and carry with them into the understory.

The moths eat nothing. They have no mouthparts. They live for perhaps a week, sustained by energy stored during their larval stage, and their only purpose — if purpose is the right word — is to be seen. They are the Drownplain's display, its exhibition, the thing it shows to itself. Their wing-patterns are not for mating — the moths mate in the canopy, in a process that has never been observed — and they are not for predator deterrence. They are for *aesthetic* purposes, if that word can be applied to a system that has no eyes to appreciate beauty. The patterns are beautiful. They are the most complex, most intricate, most *unnecessary* things in the Drownplain, and they serve no function except to exist, to be carried through the dark air on slow wings, to be seen by whatever is capable of seeing.

The moths are drawn to you. They drift toward you as you move through the understory, spiraling closer, their wing-eyes glowing, and they circle you at a distance of perhaps three feet, close enough that you feel the air from their wings, close enough that their afterimages burn in your vision when you look away. They are not curious. They are not hostile. They are *displaying*. You are an audience. The moths are performing for you, and the performance has no content, no message, no meaning. It is pure form. It is the Drownplain saying: *look at this. I made this. I don't know why.*

### The Water

The water is alive.

Not metaphorically. Not in the sense that it contains life. The water itself is alive — a distributed organism that exists as a liquid, that thinks as a liquid, that *is* a liquid in the way that the mat is a solid and the air is a gas. It has no cells. It has no membrane. It has no structure that can be identified under any microscope. But it processes information. It responds to stimuli. It maintains its own temperature, its own chemical composition, its own flow patterns, against the forces that would homogenize it, dilute it, reduce it to mere H₂O.

The water in the Drownplain is old. It has been here since the Drownplain began, and it will be here after the Drownplain ends. It has been absorbed by roots and transpired into the air and condensed on leaves and fallen as rain and been absorbed by roots again, a cycle that has repeated so many times that the water has been *shaped* by the cycle, worn smooth by it, the way a river stone is worn smooth by the current. The water remembers. Not in the way that a brain remembers — not as images, not as events — but as a *tendency*, a predisposition to move in certain ways, to carry certain dissolved substances, to respond to certain stimuli with certain reactions. The water has habits. The water has preferences. The water, if you press your ear to the root-mat and listen to its slow, braided movement beneath, has something that sounds very much like a voice.

### The Drownplain Itself

The Drownplain is not a place. It is a process. It is the process by which water and light and organic matter are converted into *more* — more life, more complexity, more awareness, more of whatever it is that the Drownplain is becoming. It has been becoming for a long time. It will continue to become for a longer time. It does not know what it is becoming. It does not need to know. The becoming is the point.

It is young. It is fresh. It is sharp. It is a riot, a frenzy, a city being built at impossible speed with no blueprint and no foreman and no plan except the plan that more is better, that complexity is its own justification, that the only sin is stasis. It grows and decays and grows again, and each cycle leaves it slightly different — slightly more complex, slightly more aware, slightly more *itself*, whatever itself is.

It does not care about you. You are too small, too brief, too *simple* to register as anything more than a variable in its calculations, a new pressure on the mat, a new source of carbon dioxide, a new pair of eyes for the moths to perform for. You are inside it, and it is inside you — the water in your body is responding to the water in the mat, the air in your lungs is exchanging molecules with the air in the understory, the bacteria on your skin are negotiating with the fungi in the root-mat — and the boundary between you and it is dissolving, slowly, the way all boundaries dissolve in the Drownplain, until you are not sure where you end and it begins.

This is what lives in the Drownplain: everything. Everything that can live, and some things that shouldn't be able to, and some things that are not sure whether they are alive or not but are doing it anyway, with a ferocity and a joy and a terrible, beautiful, purposeless intensity that makes you understand, finally, what the word *alive* means.

It means *more*.

It always means *more*.
