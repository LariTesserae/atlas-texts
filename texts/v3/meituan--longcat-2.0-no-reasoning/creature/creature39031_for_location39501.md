# The Drowned Workshop

---

They have no name for themselves. They have no name for anything. Naming requires a distance from the thing named, and they have never been distant from anything — not from the walls they work, not from each other, not from the water that holds them. They are continuous with their environment in the way that a fingernail is continuous with a finger: distinct, but not separate.

They are small. Smaller than you expected, even after you were told. The length of a human forearm, yes, but *dense* — compact in a way that suggests their bones are heavy, their muscles thick and close to the body. They are not delicate. They are not fragile. They are built like things that have been pressed together for a very long time and have learned to enjoy the pressure.

Their skin is pale — not white, not gray, but the color of something that has never needed color. A translucent, faintly luminous cream, like the flesh of a cave fish, like the underside of a leaf held against the sun. In the bioluminescent patches, you can see *into* them — not clearly, not anatomically, but enough to know that their interiors are simple. A dark core. A few branching structures. Nothing that looks like what you would expect an organ to look like. They are not complex inside. They are complex in *number*, in *duration*, in *accumulation*.

They have six limbs. Or eight. It is difficult to count because they are always in motion — not rapid motion, but a slow, constant repositioning, like the arms of a sea star testing the terrain. Each limb ends in a cluster of small, hard digits — not quite fingers, not quite claws, but something between: dense, precise, capable of holding a tool or gripping a wall or passing an object to another creature with a fluidity that suggests this is what the limbs were *for*. Not locomotion. *Manipulation*. They move through the passages by gripping and releasing, gripping and releasing, a slow peristalsis along every surface, and their limbs are always doing two things at once — holding on, and working.

Their faces — if they have faces — are turned away from you. They do not have faces in the way you understand faces. There is a front end, yes, and it has sensory apparatus: small dark spots that might be eyes, or might be something else entirely, organs that detect pressure changes or chemical gradients or the faint electrical signatures of living stone. There is no expression. There is no *front* in the way that a face implies a front — a direction of attention, a orientation toward the world. They attend to everything equally. They face all directions at once.

---

They were not always here.

This is something you understand not from evidence but from the quality of their work — from the way the oldest passages, the deepest ones, the ones closest to the column, have a slightly different character than the newer ones. The oldest work is *tentative*. The chisel marks are shallower, more uncertain, as though the creatures were still learning the stone, still discovering what it would yield. The passages are narrower, more irregular. There are dead ends — places where they carved in the wrong direction and had to stop, had to turn around, had to begin again.

They came from somewhere else. From above, perhaps — from the water column, from some other structure, from a place that became uninhabitable or insufficient. They found this stone — this ancient, already-old stone — and they began to work it. Not because they had a plan. Not because they had a vision of what the structure would become. They began to work it because *working is what they do*. It is not a choice. It is not a culture. It is closer to metabolism — as involuntary and as necessary as breathing. They work the way a river works a canyon: not with intent, but with *persistence*, and persistence, over sufficient time, becomes indistinguishable from intent.

---

The tools are held in a specific way. You watch one creature work a wall for what might be an hour or might be a decade — time has become unreliable here — and you notice that the tool is not gripped so much as *incorporated*. The digits close around it and do not fully release. The tool becomes an extension of the limb, and the limb becomes an extension of the wall, and the wall becomes an extension of the creature, and the boundaries between them blur in a way that is not metaphorical. When the tool is passed to the next creature, there is a brief moment of adjustment — a recalibration, a mutual accommodation — and then the new holder continues the work without pause, without hesitation, as though the tool has simply changed hands in a single continuous motion that began before you arrived and will continue after you leave.

The tools wear down. You can see this. The volcanic glass chips become smaller, smoother, less effective, and eventually they are too small to use and are set aside — pressed into a crevice in the wall, or dropped to the floor, or simply released into the current. New tools are made, but not quickly. Making a tool seems to require a specific kind of attention that is different from the attention of working — a focused, almost meditative state that the creatures enter rarely and briefly. Most of the time, they use what exists. They use what has been used before. They use the tools their ancestors held, and their ancestors' ancestors, and the wear patterns on the oldest tools are so deep and so smooth that they have become *sculptures* — small, dense objects that fit the digits of these creatures with a precision that no single generation could have achieved.

---

They do not eat. Or rather, they do not eat in any way you can observe. There is no hunting, no gathering, no consumption. They are surrounded by mineral deposits — the same bioluminescent patches that provide the faint light — and you begin to suspect that they derive sustenance from these, slowly, continuously, the way a plant derives sustenance from light. They press against the patches. They scrape them. They cultivate them. And something passes from the mineral into the creature — not energy, exactly, but *material*, the raw stuff of persistence, the thing that allows a small body to continue its small work for a span of time that would be impossible for anything that needed to eat.

This is why they are warm. Not from metabolism in the way you understand metabolism — not from the burning of fuel — but from the slow, continuous conversion of mineral into motion, of stone into scrape, of the inanimate into the animate. They are warm because they are *processes*, not objects. They are warm because they are happening.

---

The column is the center. Not the geometric center — the structure is too irregular, too organic, too accumulated for geometry — but the *conceptual* center, the place toward which all the passages tend and from which all the passages radiate. The creatures treat it differently than they treat the walls. They work the walls with a kind of absent-minded diligence — the diligence of breathing, of blinking, of any automatic function. But when they work the column, there is a *quality* to their attention that is different. Not more intense — they are incapable of intensity — but more *deliberate*. Each mark on the column is placed with a precision that the wall-work does not require. Each mark is the same depth, the same length, the same angle. Each mark is a unit of something — not time, not space, not quantity, but *continuity*. Each mark says: *the work has not stopped. The work will not stop. The work is all there is.*

The oldest marks are at the base of the column, and they are almost invisible — worn smooth by the touch of millions of limbs passing over them, obscured by the accumulation of newer marks layered on top like sediment. The column is not a record in the way that a book is a record. It is a record in the way that a tree trunk is a record — a physical accumulation of time made solid, a thing that grows not upward but *inward*, each new layer compressing the old, each new mark adding to the density of what was already there.

You press your hand against the column. It is warm — warmer than the walls, warmer than the water. The warmth of concentrated time. The warmth of millions of small acts of attention, compressed into stone.

---

They do not communicate. Or rather, they do not communicate in any way that involves the transfer of information from one individual to another. There is no language, no gesture, no signal. When one creature passes a tool to another, it does not *indicate* that it is passing a tool. It simply passes it, and the other simply receives it, and the work continues. They are not individuals in the way that you are an individual. They are *nodes* in a process — points of temporary concentration in a flow of activity that has no beginning and no end. A creature is born — or buds, or divides, or simply *appears*, you cannot tell which — and it begins to work, and it works until it stops, and when it stops it is absorbed back into the walls, into the mineral deposits, into the warm water, and its material becomes the material of the structure, and the structure continues.

They do not die. They *complete*.

---

You are leaving now. You have been here for a long time — longer than you intended, longer than you realized. The water is thick with them, and they part around you with the same slow patience they have shown since you arrived, and you move through the passages toward whatever surface exists above, and the passages narrow, and the ceiling lowers, and the walls press against you, and you realize that the structure is not just a place they live in.

It is a place they *are*.

The walls are not stone that has been worked by creatures. The walls are creatures that have become stone. The slow accumulation of mineral, the centuries of scraping and polishing, the metabolic heat, the bioluminescent cultivation — it has all converged into something that is neither alive nor dead but *both*, a living architecture, a structure that is also an organism, an organism that is also a structure. The creatures are not *in* the workshop. The creatures *are* the workshop. And the workshop is not a place where work is done. The workshop *is* the work — continuous, unending, patient beyond the scale of anything that has ever been patient before.

Behind you, a creature adds a mark to the column.

The mark is identical to every other mark.

The mark says: *we were here.*

The mark says: *we are still here.*

The mark says: *the work continues.*

The mark says nothing else.

It does not need to.
