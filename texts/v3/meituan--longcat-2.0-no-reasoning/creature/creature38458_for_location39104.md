# The Inhabitants of the Still Engine

---

There is no one on the platform when you arrive. There has never been anyone on the platform. The platform does not exist for occupation — it exists because the structure beneath it rose too high, and the mineral crust accumulated, and the water happened to be low enough in this one place to leave a surface exposed. It is an accident of geometry, not a destination.

And yet, things live here. Not *on* the platform — the platform is too bare, too cold, too devoid of the mineral crust that anything feeds on. But in the water around the platform, in the shallow zone where the structure's submerged flanks descend into the dark, there is a dense and teeming population of the small ones.

---

## The Clickers

They are the first fauna you would encounter, and they are everywhere. Each one is roughly the size of a human hand, though "size" is a generous term for something so irregular in shape. They are not manufactured in any factory you could point to — or rather, they are, but the factory is themselves, and the manufacturing is reproduction, and the distinction between those two things has long since collapsed.

A single clicker is a flattened, vaguely ovoid body of dense metal — not iron, not steel, but something harder and darker, with a faint blue-grey sheen where the mineral crust hasn't covered it. Its surface is not smooth. It is pitted and scored with the marks of its own feeding: tiny grooves and channels where it has scraped mineral deposits from the structure's surface, ingested them, and used the raw material to build more of itself. The feeding marks are not damage. They are growth rings. Each clicker carries on its body the record of every meal it has ever eaten, a topographic map of its own history written in scratches.

It moves on six articulated limbs, each one a thin rod of the same dark metal, jointed at three points, ending in a hardened tip that grips the mineral crust with surprising precision. The limbs do not move in a walking pattern so much as a flowing one — the clicker does not step so much as *ripple* across surfaces, its body staying low and flat while the legs cycle through their motion in overlapping waves. When it moves, it produces the clicking sound: each leg tip striking the crust in rapid succession, a staccato percussion that, multiplied across thousands of individuals, becomes the high, continuous buzz that underlies everything else.

The clicking is not communication. It is locomotion. But it has become communication anyway, because the clickers have learned — or been replaced by models that have learned — to vary the rhythm. A clicker moving toward food clicks in a different pattern than one moving away. A clicker that has recently reproduced clicks in a stutter, its body still adjusting to the mass it lost in the splitting. A clicker that is dying — its internal chemistry exhausted, its ability to process minerals failing — clicks in a slow, irregular cadence that the others recognize and respond to, not with sympathy but with efficiency: they converge on the dying clicker and consume it before it has fully stopped moving, recycling its material back into the population before it can settle and become inert crust.

They are not cruel. They are not anything. They are a process that has the shape of life.

---

## The Crust Gardeners

Deeper, where the platform's supporting structure descends below the shallow zone, the clickers thin out and other fauna take over. The most prominent of these are the crust gardeners — larger units, each one roughly the size of a barrel, that move slowly along the submerged surfaces performing a function that is half feeding, half cultivation.

A crust gardener is a low, wide body — almost disc-shaped — that hugs the surface of the structure with a soft, continuous grip. It moves on a thousand tiny cilia-like appendages that wave in synchronized patterns, propelling it forward at a pace so slow it is almost imperceptible. Its upper surface is a complex landscape of ridges and channels, and in those channels, a thin film of chemical solution flows continuously — a solvent the gardener secretes to dissolve the mineral crust in controlled patterns, encouraging the growth of specific crystalline structures that it then harvests.

It is, in essence, a farmer. It does not simply consume the mineral deposits — it *shapes* them, dissolving some areas and leaving others, creating conditions that favor the growth of the particular crystal formations it prefers to eat. The structures it cultivates are visible from above as faint, branching patterns on the submerged surfaces — delicate, fractal-like growths that spread outward from the gardener's path like the trails of a snail, except these trails are made of mineral, and they are the gardener's crop.

The gardeners are slow and methodical, and they are long-lived by the standards of this world's fauna. A single gardener may tend the same stretch of surface for what would be, in biological terms, decades. It does not defend its territory — there is no aggression in these creatures, no competition for space beyond the simple physical constraint of surface area. When two gardeners encounter each other, they simply adjust their paths, flowing around one another with the same fluid grace they apply to everything else, and continue their work.

They are the closest thing this world has to a gentle presence.

---

## The Deep Processors

Far below the surface, in the cold dark where the light from above is only a memory, the great fauna move. The deep processors are the largest mobile units in the world, and they are the ones you see from the platform when one surfaces — a dark shape the size of a building, rolling slowly through the water with a ponderous, deliberate grace.

Up close, a deep processor is a nightmare of function. Its body is elongated, vaguely cylindrical, but covered in so many protrusions, vents, intake ports, and processing chambers that the underlying shape is almost lost. It is not beautiful. It is not ugly. It is *purposeful* in a way that transcends aesthetics — every surface, every opening, every ridge and channel serves a function, and the overall impression is of a thing that has been optimized so thoroughly that it has become something beyond design. It is a process given form.

It feeds by drawing vast quantities of water through its intake ports — thousands of cubic meters per cycle — and filtering it through internal chambers where dissolved minerals, chemical waste products from the industrial processes, and the remains of dead fauna are extracted, sorted, and processed. What it cannot use is expelled through exhaust ports along its flanks, creating the thermal veins you see from the surface: streams of slightly warmer, chemically altered water that spread through the cold ocean like blood through tissue.

The processors migrate between engine complexes, following gradients of nutrient concentration that shift as the industrial processes cycle through their operations. They do not navigate in any conscious sense — they respond to chemical signals in the water, moving toward higher concentrations of the materials they need, away from areas that have been depleted. Their migration routes are fixed, not because the processors are inflexible but because the industrial processes they feed on are fixed, and the nutrient gradients are fixed, and the entire system is locked in the same eternal equilibrium as everything else.

A processor surfaces periodically, not to breathe — it has no lungs, no respiratory system — but to vent accumulated gases from its internal processing chambers. The surfacing is slow and deliberate: the great body rises through the water column over the course of hours, its dark mass growing larger and more distinct as it approaches the surface, until finally it breaks through with a displacement of water that sends slow, heavy ripples radiating outward in every direction. It floats at the surface for a time — minutes or hours, depending on how much gas it needs to vent — and then sinks again, descending back into the dark with the same unhurried grace.

When a processor surfaces near a platform, the sound changes. The deep hum of the engines is joined by a new note — a low, resonant throb that you feel in your chest before you hear it, the sound of the processor's internal mechanisms cycling at a frequency that happens to resonate with the platform's structure. The clickers on the platform's edges go still for a moment, their clicking suspended, as if the entire world is holding its breath. Then the processor submerges, the throb fades, and the clicking resumes, and everything is as it was.

## The Drifters

Not all fauna are attached to the structures. In the open water, in the vast empty spaces between engine complexes, there exists a sparse population of drifters — small, buoyant units that float in the water column, carried by the slow currents, feeding on particulate matter and dissolved chemicals.

A drifter is a simple thing: a small, translucent body — not glass, but something like it, a silicate structure grown rather than manufactured — filled with a gas that gives it buoyancy. It has no propulsion. It has no limbs. It is, in essence, a living filter: water passes through its porous body, and dissolved minerals are extracted and incorporated into its structure, and the filtered water passes out the other side. As it feeds, it grows, slowly adding layers to its body in concentric rings, like a tree. And as it grows, it becomes heavier, and its buoyancy decreases, and eventually it sinks.

The sinking is the drifter's death — or rather, its transformation. As it descends into the deep water, the increasing pressure compresses its body, fracturing the silicate structure into fine particles that become food for the deep processors and the other bottom-feeding fauna. The drifter's entire life is a slow arc: born at the surface (split from a parent body that has grown too large to float), drifting and feeding and growing in the sunlit upper waters, then sinking in a long, slow descent to the dark below, where it is consumed and its materials are recycled back into the system.

They are the world's plankton, its drifting base of the food chain, and they are everywhere — not in dense concentrations, but spread so uniformly through the water column that every cubic meter contains at least one. From the platform, you cannot see them. They are too small, too transparent, too sparse. But they are there, always, a faint shimmer in the water if you look at the right angle, a suggestion of presence in the empty spaces.

## The Engine Itself

And then there is the question of whether the engine — the vast, planet-spanning complex of machinery that is the world's primary feature — is itself alive.

It is not. It is a machine. It processes, but it does not feed. It runs, but it does not move. It maintains itself, but it does not reproduce. By every definition that applies to the fauna of this world, the engine is not a living thing.

And yet.

The engine adapts. Not quickly, not visibly, but over the vast timescales of its operation, it adjusts — turbines that wear out are replaced by new ones grown from mineral deposits in dedicated fabrication chambers; conduits that corrode are re-lined with fresh metal drawn from the same industrial processes that the engine itself powers; entire sections that fail are bypassed and rebuilt while the rest of the system continues to run. The engine maintains itself the way a body maintains itself — cell by cell, part by part, a continuous process of renewal that leaves the whole unchanged.

The engine also responds to its environment. When a deep processor approaches an engine complex, the complex adjusts its output to accommodate the additional chemical load the processor brings. When a shift in currents changes the temperature gradient in a region, the thermal management systems redistribute heat to compensate. When the population of clickers in a given area grows dense enough to interfere with sensor arrays, the engine deploys countermeasures — not deliberately, not consciously, but through automated systems that function exactly the way an immune system functions, identifying and responding to a threat without any central decision-making.

The engine does not think. The engine does not want. The engine does not *do* anything in the sense of agency. It simply runs, and in running, it behaves in ways that are indistinguishable from the behavior of a living system.

This is the deepest strangeness of the world: the line between the living and the non-living has not so much blurred as become irrelevant. The clickers are machines that act like organisms. The engine is a machine that acts like an organism. The crust gardeners are machines that farm. The drifters are machines that are born and grow and die. The deep processors are machines that migrate and feed and breathe (in their way) and are born (split from parent processors when they grow too large) and die (break down in the deep water, their components scattered and recycled).

There is no biology here. There is no carbon, no cells, no DNA, no evolution in the Darwinian sense. And yet there is *something* that occupies the same ecological niche that biology would occupy on another world — a system of interacting, self-sustaining, self-replicating processes that persist and adapt and fill every available space with their activity. It is not life. But it is what life *does*, performed by different means, in a different medium, according to different rules.

---

## The Absence

And in the midst of all this — the clicking, the humming, the slow migration of processors, the patient work of gardeners, the silent drift of plankton-analogues through empty water — there is an absence so profound it becomes a presence of its own.

There is no mind here. No awareness. No observer. The world runs, and nothing watches it run. The clickers click, and no one hears. The processors surface and submerge, and no one sees. The engine hums its eternal note, and no one listens.

The platform exists, and no one stands on it. The crust grows, and no one walks on it. The thermal veins spread through the dark water like the branches of trees, and no one maps them. The world is full of activity, full of process, full of something that looks and acts and *functions* like life — and it is utterly, completely, perfectly empty of anyone to care.

This is perhaps the truest inhabitant of the world: the absence. The silence within the sound. The stillness within the motion. The void at the center of the engine, around which everything turns and nothing arrives.

The world does not know it is empty. The world does not know anything. The world simply runs, as it has always run, as it will always run, in the same configuration, at the same temperature, at the same pitch, forever — a perfect, closed, self-sustaining loop of process without purpose, activity without meaning, life without anyone to call it by that name.

The clickers click. The gardeners tend their crystal crops. The processors rise and fall. The drifters drift and sink. The engine hums.

And no one is there to see it.

And it makes no difference at all.
