# The Dwellers of the Undercity

---

## The People

They are humanoid in the way that a river is water-shaped—the form is right, but the substance has been worked on by something else for a very long time.

They are short. Not uniformly, but on average, they run a head or two below what a surface dweller would expect, and they are broad in proportion, with thick forearms and wide hands whose fingers seem to have an extra joint, or perhaps one fewer—it's hard to count them because they move in ways that don't quite track. Their skin is pale, but not the pallor of illness or absence. It is the color of the stone around them: grey-white, faintly blue in certain light, with a mineral undertone that makes them look, at a distance, like they are made of the same material as the walls. Up close, you can see that their skin has a texture. Not scales, not quite. More like the surface of a river stone that has been polished but not smoothed—there is a grain to it, a direction, and if you look closely (which they do not always welcome) you can see that the grain runs differently on every person, like wood, like sedimentary rock, like something that grew in layers.

Their eyes are large. Not grotesquely so, but large enough to be the first thing you notice after the skin, and large enough that they seem to take in more light than eyes should. The irises are dark—not brown, not black, but a deep mineral color that shifts between charcoal and slate and, in the right light, a faint luminescence that matches the slime gardens. They do not reflect light the way surface eyes do. They absorb it. When they look at you, you feel the looking as a kind of gentle pressure, a soft pull, as though your attention is being drawn into them the way water is drawn into a fracture in stone.

They have hair, or something like it. It grows in the usual places—head, forearms, sometimes the spine—and it is fine and colorless, almost transparent, like the filaments of certain cave-dwelling organisms that have lost their pigment over millennia. Some of them braid it. Some of them coat it in mineral paste that makes it stiff and faintly luminous, so that in certain districts you can see people moving through the blue-white dark with their heads haloed in pale light, like slow-moving saints.

They do not wear much. The temperature in the undercity is stable—cool, but not cold, the kind of cool that becomes neutral after you've been there long enough—and their clothing is more functional than protective. They wrap themselves in something that is not quite fabric and not quite leather, a material they produce from the processed skins of the larger animals, treated with mineral salts until it becomes supple and faintly iridescent. It drapes well. It doesn't tear easily. It has a smell—not unpleasant, but distinct—like wet stone and hot metal and something organic that has been cured past the point of decay. They wear it in layers, wrapped and tied and pinned with bone clasps, and the wrapping is specific to function: workers wrap their hands and forearms thick, traders wrap their shoulders and chest, and there is a class of person—you'll meet them later—who wraps their entire head, leaving only the eyes exposed, and moves through the city like a pale column of cloth and intent.

They speak. Their language is low and resonant, built for the acoustics of stone—lots of vowels, lots of sounds that vibrate in the chest rather than the throat, and a grammar that seems to encode spatial relationships in ways that surface languages do not. They have words for directions that don't exist above ground. They have tenses for events that haven't happened yet but are geologically inevitable. They have no word for "sun" but seventeen words for different kinds of damp.

They are not a unified people. They are not even a people in the way that word is usually meant. They are a population—a dense, overlapping, constantly shifting population of individuals and families and work-groups and factions and guilds and something else, something that doesn't translate, that might be a kinship system or might be a political structure or might be a religious order or might be all three at once. They argue constantly. Not violently, or not usually, but with a persistence and intensity that suggests argument is not a breakdown of social order but a form of it. They argue about water rights and slime garden allocations and the proper way to stack stone and whether the damp season is coming early this year and whether the animals in the eastern district are getting bold and whether the thing that happened in the lower chambers last cycle was a structural failure or a sign. They argue the way surface people breathe: continuously, involuntarily, as a baseline condition of being alive.

They work. This is the thing that defines them more than anything else, more than their appearance or their language or their arguments. They work the way the stone is still: constantly, patiently, with a kind of mineral persistence that makes the word "industrious" feel too energetic, too temporary. They work the way erosion works. They work the way water works. They are always building, always repairing, always tending, always moving something from one place to another or changing its shape or cleaning it or reinforcing it or taking it apart to see how it works. Their hands are never still. Even in conversation, even in argument, their hands are doing something—shaping a piece of stone, braiding a cord, adjusting a wrap, stroking the wall beside them with a familiarity that looks almost affectionate, as though the stone is a pet, or a partner, or a part of themselves.

They do not have leaders, exactly. They have people who are listened to, and the listening is earned not through force or charisma but through demonstrated knowledge of the city—its structure, its moods, its history, its needs. These people are old, or old-seeming, though age is hard to judge here because the damp and the mineral light and the general absence of sun-weathering makes their faces smooth in ways that don't correspond to youth. They might be thirty. They might be three hundred. The city does not encourage the counting of years. Time here is measured in damp cycles and construction phases and the slow growth of the slime gardens, and by those measures, the oldest among them are very old indeed.

## The Slime Gardeners

Among the workers, among the arguers, among the constant builders and repairers, there is a subset of people whose relationship to the city is different. They tend the luminous basins.

The basins are everywhere—shallow depressions in the stone floor, some natural, some carved, some built up from clay and bone into wide, low-walled pools that hold the slurry. The slurry itself is a living culture, or a colony, or an organism—the distinction doesn't hold here—that feeds on mineral-rich water and produces light as a byproduct of whatever metabolic process sustains it. It is pale blue and white and sometimes faintly yellow, and it is alive in the way that a forest is alive: not as a single thing but as a system, a community, a web of interactions between the culture and the stone and the water and the air and the people who tend it.

The gardeners know the slime the way a farmer knows soil. They know its moods—when it is hungry, when it is thriving, when it is sick, when it is about to go dormant. They feed it. They thin it. They transplant it from basin to basin, carrying it in clay bowls with a care that looks almost reverent, though they would not call it reverence. They call it work. They call it necessary. They call it the thing that keeps the city lit, and therefore the city alive, and therefore them alive, and the chain of therefore is so long and so unbroken that it has become invisible, like the water in the walls, like the light in the air.

Their hands are stained. The slime leaves a residue that doesn't wash off—a faint luminescence that clings to skin and cloth and makes the gardeners visible in the dark even when the basins are not. They glow. Faintly, constantly, a pale blue-white shimmer on their fingers and palms and forearms, and sometimes on their faces where they've touched themselves without thinking. It is not beautiful, exactly, but it is distinctive, and in the undercity, where everyone is pale and the light is everywhere and faces blur together in the mineral glow, it is a mark of identity. You can spot a gardener from across a crowded chamber. You can spot one from three corridors away. They are the ones who shine.

They are also the ones who hear the city best. This is not metaphorical. The slime responds to vibration—to sound, to movement, to the subtle shifts in pressure that precede a structural change—and the gardeners, through long exposure, through the residue on their hands and the constant proximity to the basins, have developed a sensitivity to these responses that borders on communion. They can feel a wall beginning to fail before it shows visible cracks. They can sense a water intrusion before the first drop appears. They know when the animals are moving in unusual patterns, when the stone is settling, when the deep fractures are opening or closing. They do not call this knowledge supernatural. They call it attention. They call it the work of hands that have touched the same living surface for years, for decades, for longer than memory.

Some of them go further. Some of them sit beside the basins for hours, not working, not tending, just sitting, with their hands in the slurry, their eyes closed, their faces slack, and when they come back—and they always come back—they speak in a low, slow voice about things that have not happened yet. About water. About stone. About the movements of animals and the failures of walls and the slow, patient shifts of the planet beneath them. They are not always right. They are right often enough that people listen.

## The Stone Readers

There is another class. Or perhaps the same class, further along.

They are the ones who read the walls.

The walls of the undercity are not blank. They are layered—geologically, historically, structurally—and the layers tell a story, or many stories, or a single story so long and so complex that it can only be read in fragments. The stone readers have learned to read it. They press their hands against the walls and feel the grain, the temperature, the moisture, the subtle variations in density that indicate a change in composition, a shift in the ancient erosion that carved these chambers, a fracture that is old or new or growing or stable. They press their ears against the walls and listen. They tap the walls with small bone hammers and interpret the sound—the pitch, the resonance, the way the vibration travels and returns or doesn't. They taste the walls, sometimes, running their tongues along the stone in a way that looks obscene but is purely analytical, reading the mineral content through some sense that surface people don't have or have lost.

They know the city's bones. They know where the stone is strong and where it is tired. They know which chambers are safe and which are held together by habit and hope. They know the deep fractures—the tectonic ones, the ones that go down and down and down to a depth where the stone is still warm and still moving, where the planet's own slow pulse can be felt if you press your hand flat and wait long enough. They know these fractures the way a doctor knows a pulse: as a sign of life, a sign of danger, a sign that the body they inhabit is larger and older and more alive than anyone wants to admit.

They are respected. They are also feared, a little, because they are the ones who declare a chamber unsafe, a wall unsound, a district ready to collapse, and when they declare it, people must leave, and leaving means abandoning homes and workshops and slime gardens and the worn-smooth corners and the concave staircases and the particular quality of light that that chamber has, which is different from the light in every other chamber, which is why people live where they live, which is why the city is not just a structure but a collection of specific, irreplaceable places, each one known and loved and argued over by the people who inhabit it.

The stone readers carry this weight. They carry it in their hands, which are always touching something—a wall, a floor, a column, the bone-hammer at their belt. They carry it in their faces, which are older than their faces should be, lined not by age but by the constant low-grade grief of knowing that everything, eventually, returns to stone.

## The Animals

They are not pets. They are not wildlife. They are something else—something that has co-evolved with the city and its people for so long that the boundary between inhabitant and animal has worn thin, the way the staircases have worn thin, the way the corners have worn smooth.

The small ones are everywhere. They live in the walls—in the fractures, in the gaps between stones, in the spaces behind the clay-and-bone constructions that the people build and rebuild and build again. They are arthropod-like, or worm-like, or something in between: segmented, pale, many-legged or legless depending on the species, and they move with a quick, fluid purpose that suggests intelligence without confirming it. They eat the mineral crusts. They eat the slime that overflows the basins. They eat the organic residue that accumulates in the damp pockets—the deposits that are not quite rock and not quite life but something in between, something that the city produces the way a body produces tissue, slowly, constantly, as a byproduct of being inhabited.

They are not afraid of people. They are not friendly with people. They simply exist in the same space, moving around each other with the ease of long practice, and the relationship is so old and so stable that neither side seems to remember how it started or what it would look like if it stopped. People step over them. People work around them. People occasionally pick one up, examine it, put it down. The animals do the same with people's tools, their materials, their food—not stealing, exactly, but investigating, tasting, moving things slightly and then losing interest, the way a cat might bat a cup off a table not out of malice but out of a fundamental inability to leave the physical world un-touched.

The large ones are fewer but more present. They move through the streets at their own pace—slow, deliberate, massive—and people move around them the way water moves around a boulder: easily, automatically, without resentment. They are the size of large dogs or small horses, and they are shaped like nothing on the surface. Low-slung, broad-bodied, with limbs that are too numerous or too jointed or too something, and a skin that is not skin but something mineral—a crust, a shell, a surface that looks like stone but flexes and breathes and is warm to the touch. They eat the slime gardens. They eat the wall crusts. They eat the damp deposits. They eat, occasionally, the small animals, though not with any enthusiasm, more with a kind of mechanical necessity, the way a person might eat a food they don't particularly like because it is there and it is time.

Nobody rides them. This is not because they are unrideable—their backs are broad and flat and their movement is smooth enough that riding would be comfortable—but because the idea does not occur to anyone. The relationship is not that kind. The relationship is more like the relationship between two species that share a habitat: mutual tolerance, mutual benefit, mutual indifference. The large animals clear paths through the dense pedestrian traffic simply by existing. They knock things over sometimes, and the things they knock over are often load-bearing, and this is a problem, but it is a problem the city has absorbed the way it absorbs everything: into the constant work, the constant repair, the endless cycle of building and breaking and building again.

The flying ones are the strangest. They are airborne, or something like airborne—they move through the air in the upper chambers and the high-ceilinged districts where the stone opens up into vast, cathedral-like spaces that the light from the slime gardens cannot fully reach. In those spaces, the flying animals are visible only as movement: a dark shape against the faint glow, a disturbance in the air, a sound like fabric tearing or wings beating or something else, something that doesn't correspond to any known mechanism of flight. They are not birds. They are not bats. They are not insects. They are something that has found a way to be airborne in a place where the air is thick and still and the ceilings are low and the physics, as surface people understand physics, should not permit it.

They eat the slime that grows on the upper walls and ceilings—the colonies that have migrated upward over centuries, following the moisture, following the mineral deposits, following some instinct or imperative that nobody understands. The flying animals graze on these colonies the way cattle graze on grass, and their grazing keeps the colonies in check, and the colonies' growth keeps the flying animals fed, and the whole system is balanced in a way that nobody planned and nobody maintains and nobody fully comprehends.

Sometimes, in the high chambers, if you look up, you can see them silhouetted against the faint ceiling-glow: shapes that are too large, or too many-limbed, or too still for something that is supposed to be flying. They hang in the air like ornaments, like thoughts, like the shadows that lag behind their objects by a half-second, and they watch the city below with eyes that may or may not be eyes, and they are part of the city the way the stone is part of the city, the way the water is part of the city, the way the light is part of the city: fundamentally, inextricably, without question.

## The Damp

It is not a person. It is not an animal. But it lives here, and it has a presence, and the people treat it as something between a season and a neighbor.

It comes from below. From the deep fractures, from the tectonic wounds that go down to where the planet is still warm, still moving, still dreaming in its own slow mineral way. The water rises—not as a flood, not as a flow, but as a presence, a thickening of the air, a change in the quality of light. The walls begin to sweat. The floors begin to gleam. The slime gardens brighten, responding to the moisture, and the whole city takes on a quality of heightened visibility, of luminous dampness, of being seen more clearly than usual, as though the water is a lens, a medium, a way of making the stone more itself.

The people prepare. They have always prepared. They move the luminous stones to higher ground—the fist-sized phosphorescent stones that are wired to nothing, that glow on their own, that are the city's backup light, its emergency illumination, its insurance against the dark. They seal the lower districts with clay and bone and stone, not to keep the water out (the water always gets in) but to slow it, to direct it, to give it channels and basins and places to go where it will do the least harm. They move their work upward. They move their arguments upward. They move their lives upward, into the higher chambers, the drier districts, the places where the stone is older and more stable and less prone to the slow dissolution that the damp brings.

And then they wait. The damp lasts as long as it lasts. A cycle. An eternity. There is no agreement. There never was.

During the damp, the animals change. The small ones emerge from the walls in greater numbers, drawn by the moisture, and they cover the floors and the lower surfaces in a moving, shimmering carpet of pale bodies that the people navigate with practiced ease, stepping carefully, sweeping paths with their feet, not killing them (they are too numerous to kill, too integrated into the city's ecology to remove) but simply moving them, redirecting them, the way the water itself is redirected. The large animals become more active, more restless, moving through the streets with a purpose that seems almost urgent, and the people give them room, because a restless large animal in a narrow passage is a hazard that no amount of building or repairing can mitigate. The flying animals descend from the high chambers, drawn by the moisture, by the blooming of the ceiling colonies, and they fill the middle air with their strange not-quite-flight, and the city becomes a three-dimensional space in a way it usually is not—alive above, alive below, alive in the walls, alive in the air, alive in the water that is everywhere and nowhere and is the only thing that remembers the surface.

The people do not fear the damp. They respect it. They accommodate it. They have built their entire civilization around the certainty of its return, the way surface civilizations build around the certainty of winter or monsoon or flood, and their relationship to it is not adversarial but relational—a negotiation, a dance, a long conversation between the city and the water that has been going on for longer than anyone can remember and will continue, they assume, for longer still.

## The City Itself

And then there is the thing that is hardest to describe, because it is not a who but a what, and it is not alive in any way that biology recognizes, and yet.

The city hums. You feel it in your teeth. You feel it in your bones. You feel it in the mineral grain of your skin if you have lived here long enough, if the stone has begun to work on you the way it works on everything—slowly, patiently, with the persistence of geology. The hum is not mechanical. It is not the sound of tools or voices or animals or water. It is deeper than that. It is the sound of the stone itself, or the sound of the space the stone encloses, or the sound of the city as a system—all its parts, all its people, all its animals, all its slime and water and light and damp and work and argument and building and breaking and building again—vibrating at a frequency that is just below hearing, just below consciousness, just below the threshold of what can be perceived and therefore just above the threshold of what can be ignored.

It is always there. It is the background. It is the baseline. It is the sound of a place that has been inhabited for so long that the inhabitation has become structural, has become part of the architecture, has become load-bearing in a way that has nothing to do with physics and everything to do with presence. The people are part of the city. The animals are part of the city. The slime is part of the city. The water is part of the city. The light is part of the city. And the city is part of the stone, and the stone is part of the planet, and the planet is old, and the city is old, and the people are old, and the animals are old, and the slime is old, and the water is old, and the light is old, and the hum is old, and everything is old, and everything is alive, and everything is still going.

The stone readers say that the city is not built on the stone. They say the city is built *of* the stone—that the distinction between structure and material, between the thing that is made and the thing it is made from, has collapsed over the millennia, that the walls are not walls but growths, that the chambers are not chambers but expressions, that the city is not a construction but an extrusion, a slow pushing-out of the planet's own substance into shapes that happen to be habitable, that happen to be lit, that happen to be full of people and animals and slime and water and light and work and argument and the constant, constant hum.

They say this quietly. They say it with their hands on the walls. They say it in the damp season, when the water makes the stone more visible, more present, more itself. They say it and then they go back to work, because the walls need mending, and the slime gardens need tending, and the luminous stones need moving, and the animals are getting bold, and the lower districts are flooding, and someone needs to do something about the thing in the eastern passage that might be a structural failure or might be a sign.

And the city hums. And the stone is still. And the water remembers. And the light persists. And the people work. And the animals move. And the slime glows. And the damp comes and goes and comes again. And the corners wear smooth. And the staircases develop concavities. And the fractures deepen. And the planet turns, far above, in a darkness that is not the darkness of the undercity but a different darkness, a colder darkness, a darkness without slime or stone or water or light or people or animals or work or argument or hum.

And the city does not care. The city has never cared. The city is below the surface, where the rock has finally stopped dreaming and settled into something like its true shape, and it is occupied, and it is argued over, and it is repaired, and it is vandalized, and it is lit up every night by fist-sized phosphorescent stones wired to nothing anyone can trace, and it is alive, and it is old, and it is still going.

It is still going.
