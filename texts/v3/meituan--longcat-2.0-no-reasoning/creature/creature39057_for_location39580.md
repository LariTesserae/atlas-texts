# The Inhabitants of the Hanging Green

---

## The Rooted

They do not call themselves anything you could pronounce easily. The word they use — if it is a word, if it is not a gesture, a posture, a particular way of standing on the living floor that communicates belonging — translates roughly to *the ones who stayed down* or *the ones who grew here* or simply *the rooted.* Language in the Hanging Green is not entirely sonic. Much of it is chemical, tactile, positional. A conversation between two Rooted standing on a walkway might involve speech-sounds — low, breathy, the consonants formed without much lip movement, as if water-intensive articulation is a luxury they've learned to do without — but it will also involve the angle of their bodies relative to the nearest root-column, the direction their skin-facing turns, the slow exchange of volatile compounds from their chlorophyll-rich dermis that you, with your water-heavy nose, cannot detect.

They are tall. Not unnaturally so — you would not call them giants — but tall the way trees are tall, the way things grow when they have vertical space and no particular reason to stop. Two meters is common. Two and a half is not unusual. Their thinness is proportional, not emaciated; they are built for this place, for climbing the helical ramps and crossing the vine-bridges and reaching into the overhead lattice to adjust a light fixture or harvest a fruit. Their limbs are long. Their joints have an extra degree of freedom — not an extra joint, exactly, but a looseness, a rotational capacity in the wrist and shoulder that suggests adaptation to a three-dimensional environment where "up" and "down" are directions of travel, not fixed orientations.

Their skin is the color of birch bark in certain light, of sage leaves in others. The green undertone is real: a layer of photosynthetic cells in the upper dermis, symbiotic algae or something like algae, housed in transparent follicles that give the skin a faintly granular texture up close. It is not enough photosynthesis to sustain them — they eat, they metabolize, they are animals in every meaningful sense — but it supplements. A Rooted standing under a light fixture for an hour gains something. A calorie, a vitamin, a trace mineral synthesized by the symbionts and passed through the follicle walls into the bloodstream — or whatever the sap-analog is that moves through their circulatory system. They are, in a small but real way, partly plant. Not metaphorically. Not spiritually. Cellularly.

Their faces are human-ish. Two eyes, a nose, a mouth. The proportions are slightly off by your standards — the eyes a touch too large, the nose a touch too flat, the mouth a touch too wide — but these are differences of degree, not of kind. They are not aliens. They are not, in any taxonomic sense you could defend, a separate species. They are what happens to humans — or to whatever the ancestors of humans were — when you put them in a dry, lighted, plant-dominated underground environment for a very long time and let selection and cultivation do their work. Centuries. Maybe longer. The Hanging Green is old. The Rooted are old. They have been growing here as long as the city has been growing around them, and the distinction between the two processes — the evolution of the people and the evolution of the place — has become unclear.

They wear wraps of dried leaf-fiber, as you noticed. The wraps are practical — protection against the rough root-surfaces, insulation against the cool air, pockets and folds for carrying tools and food — but they are also communicative. The pattern of wrapping, the type of fiber, the way it is dyed (with plant pigments: ochre from mineral deposits, green from chlorophyll extract, a deep brown from tannin-rich bark) indicates something about the wearer. Status, maybe. Trade. Family line. You cannot read it. A Rooted could. A Rooted passing you on a walkway is reading you, too — reading your water-heavy scent, your pale non-photosynthetic skin, your clumsy two-dimensional gait, the way you grip the vine handrail with both hands on a bridge they would cross barefoot and carrying a load.

They are not hostile. They are not welcoming. They are *attentive.* You are a novelty. You are a thing that has come from above, from the wet place, from the world they sealed themselves away from. Some of them are curious. Some are wary. Most are simply watching, waiting to see what you do, what you want, whether you are a problem that needs to be solved.

## The Tenders

Among the Rooted, there is a subset — not a caste, not a class, something more like a vocation or a calling — known (roughly) as the Tenders. They are the ones who work with the city itself. Who prune and train and graft. Who speak to the plants.

Not speak, exactly. But communicate.

The Tenders have a relationship with the vegetation of the Hanging Green that goes beyond agriculture or horticulture into something you don't have a word for. They touch the root-walls and the root-walls respond — not quickly, not dramatically, but measurably. A Tender places both hands on a root-column and stands still for ten minutes, and the column's growth pattern shifts. A new branch emerges where the Tender's left hand was. A root-tip redirects toward the Tender's right. It is not telepathy. It is not magic. It is a chemical dialogue — the Tender's skin exudes compounds that the plant's tissues detect and respond to, growth hormones and signaling molecules that say *grow here, grow this way, thicken this wall, hollow this stem, make a channel for air, make a shelf for sitting.* The Tenders have cultivated this ability the way a musician cultivates an ear: through practice, through sensitivity, through generations of selection for those who could hear what the plants were saying and answer in a language the plants could understand.

You see a Tender at work in the third chamber — the one that is still becoming. She is young, or young-looking, her skin more green than brown, her wraps dyed a pale, chlorophyll-heavy shade that marks her trade. She is standing in the center of the chamber with her arms outstretched, palms flat against two emerging root-columns simultaneously. Her eyes are closed. Her breathing is slow. Around her, the roots are growing — visibly, perceptibly, the pale tips extending upward and outward, branching, seeking each other, beginning to form the lattice that will become the ceiling. She is not making them grow. They would grow anyway. She is *directing* the growth, shaping it, nudging it toward a configuration that will serve. In a year, this chamber will be a workshop. In five years, a residence. In ten, it will be indistinguishable from the old city, and no one will remember that it was once an empty space with a young woman standing in the middle of it, telling the roots what to become.

The Tenders are respected. Not revered — the Rooted don't seem to do reverence — but respected in the way you respect someone whose work is essential and difficult and that you cannot do yourself. They are the architects, the urban planners, the civil engineers of the Hanging Green. Without them, the city would grow wild, would become the unplanned bulb-and-branch chaos of the oldest chambers. With them, it grows *intentionally* — still organic, still alive, but shaped toward purpose.

## The Smelters

In the second chamber, among the presses and lathes and kilns, you find the Smelters. They are broader than the average Rooted, thicker in the shoulder and arm, their skin darker — less green, more brown, as if the photosynthetic symbionts have been partially displaced by something else, some mineral-accumulating cell type that gives their dermis a faintly metallic sheen. They work with heat and metal, and their bodies have adapted to it. They sweat — or excrete, or secrete; it is not water, it is a lipid-based coolant that evaporates slowly and leaves a faint oily residue on the skin. They are hot to the touch. Standing near a Smelter standing near a kiln is like standing near a furnace with a person-shaped buffer.

The Smelters mine nothing. There are no mines in the Hanging Green. The metal they work — that dark, slightly reddish alloy — comes from the surface, brought down in sacks by traders or scavengers or some supply chain you haven't mapped. It comes as ore, as scrap, as raw mineral wealth from the dry plain above. The Smelters process it. They smelt it in their small kilns, alloy it with carbon from the plant matter (the Hanging Green has carbon in abundance — it is made of carbon, it is swimming in carbon, every root and leaf and bracket fungus is a carbon store), and forge it into tools. The dark metal is hard, slightly brittle, takes an edge well. It is not steel. It is not iron. It is something the Smelters have developed through trial and error over generations, a material science rooted in the specific conditions of this place — the available ores, the available carbon, the available heat, the absence of water for quenching. They quench in oil. They quench in resin. They quench in air, slowly, letting the metal cool in the still, dry atmosphere of the chamber until it reaches a hardness that satisfies them.

A Smelter named — approximately — Keth is working the forge when you pass. Keth is a gender the Rooted have a word for that doesn't translate; something between "he" and "she" and "the one who works with heat." Keth is hammering a blade — a short, broad blade, a tool for cutting root-fiber or shaping wood — on an anvil that is a root-burl the size of a dining table, its surface flat and dense and ringing with each strike. The hammer is a Smelter-made tool: wooden handle grown on a jamb, metal head forged in this very kiln. Keth's strikes are precise, rhythmic, each one producing a spray of sparks that die quickly in the dry air — no embers, no sustained burning, just brief bright points of light that wink out like fireflies. The blade glows dull red. Keth flips it. Strikes. Flips. Strikes. The sound is the oldest sound in any industrial civilization: metal on metal, shaped by muscle and heat and will.

## The Children

The children of the Hanging Green are small and green and quick. They move through the three-dimensional space of the city with a facility that the adults have lost or never had — climbing the root-columns like squirrels, swinging from the vine-bridges, dropping from the overhead lattice to the walkways below with a casualness that makes your stomach lurk. They are learning the city the way children anywhere learn their environment: by touching it, testing it, pushing against its boundaries and seeing what pushes back.

They go to school. Or something like school. In a mid-level chamber, you find a dozen children sitting on root-knots in a semicircle around an adult — a Tender, older, her skin so green it is almost blue-green, the color of deep algae. She is teaching them to listen to the walls. Each child has a hand on the root-floor, eyes closed, face concentrated. The Tender is speaking — low, slow, her words punctuated by long silences. She is teaching them to feel the sap-movement in the vascular bundles, to detect the slow pulse of the city's hydraulic system, to read in that pulse information about the health of the structure, the needs of the plants, the intentions of the growth. It is a basic skill for the Rooted — not everyone becomes a Tender, but everyone learns to listen. The city is alive, and you live inside it, and you need to know what it is saying.

The children are better at it than the adults, usually. Their skin is more sensitive, their symbionts more active, their minds less cluttered with the assumptions that come from years of interpreting the city's signals through habit rather than attention. A child can feel a root-column beginning to fail — a blockage in the vascular bundle, a structural weakness forming — before any instrument (if the Rooted have instruments; they might, but they might not need them) would detect it. The children are the city's immune system, in a sense. Its early warning network. Its most sensitive interface with itself.

## The Dry Walkers

Not all the Rooted stay below. Some go up.

The Dry Walkers are the ones who cross the membrane-seal and emerge onto the surface — the flat, dry, colorless plain that is the world above the Hanging Green. They are traders, scouts, scavengers, diplomats (if the word applies). They carry water — sealed, precious, carried in bladders made from the internal organs of the larger fauna — and they walk the surface world, trading with whatever or whoever lives there. You don't know what lives on the surface. The description doesn't say. But the Dry Walkers go there and come back, and they bring things: ore, scrap metal, seeds, news, strangers.

They are the most different from the other Rooted. Their skin is less green — the photosynthetic symbionts are less useful on the surface, where the light is harsh and the UV is unfiltered and the dryness is absolute. Their skin has adapted in a different direction: thicker, more leathery, with a reflective quality that bounces back the worst of the radiation. They are darker than the chamber-dwellers, their skin a deep brown-black, their wraps heavier and more covering. They wear goggles — industrial, salvaged or manufactured, with dark lenses that protect against the glare. They look, when they descend back through the membrane and into the cool green dimness of the Hanging Green, like astronauts returning from a hostile planet. Which, in a sense, they are.

A Dry Walker is coming down as you are going up. You pass on the helical ramp — a brief, close encounter, your bodies nearly touching as you squeeze past each other on the worn root-surface. The Dry Walker smells of the surface: hot dust, ozone, something metallic and sharp. Their goggles are pushed up on their forehead. Their eyes, behind the goggles, are different from the chamber-dwellers' — smaller, more recessed, with a nictitating membrane that flickers across the cornea as they blink. They look at you. You look at them. Neither of you speaks. The moment passes. They descend. You ascend. The city breathes around you both.

## The Mycelial Mind

This is the hardest thing to describe, and the most important.

The Hanging Green is not just a city made of plants. It is, in some sense that the Rooted understand intuitively and that you can only approximate, a single organism. The root systems that form the walls and floors and columns and bridges are all connected — not metaphorically, not poetically, but physically, cellularly, through a network of mycorrhizal fungi that permeates every cubic meter of the structure. The fungi are the city's nervous system. They carry chemical signals from root to root, from chamber to chamber, from the deepest level to the Vestibule at the top. They carry information. They carry, perhaps, something like thought.

The Rooted do not worship this. They do not personify it. They do not call it a god or a spirit or a consciousness. But they *relate* to it. They speak of the city as a living thing — not as a figure of speech, but as a literal description. When a Tender places her hands on a root-column and directs its growth, she is not commanding the plant. She is *requesting* something of the mycelial network, which processes the request, which evaluates it against the needs of the whole organism, which responds. Sometimes the response is yes. Sometimes it is no. Sometimes it is *not yet* — the network is slow, its timescale geological, its decisions made over days and weeks and months rather than seconds. The Tenders have learned to be patient. The Rooted have learned to be patient. Everyone who lives here has learned to be patient, because the city thinks slowly and you cannot rush it.

Is it intelligent? You don't know. It processes information. It makes decisions. It maintains homeostasis — regulating temperature, humidity, gas exchange, structural integrity across a vertical extent of hundreds of meters and a population of thousands. It responds to damage. It adapts to change. It grows toward purpose. These are things that intelligent systems do. Whether there is something it is *like* to be the Hanging Green — whether there is an experience, a subjectivity, a point of view — you cannot say. The Rooted cannot say. They live inside it, they tend it, they listen to it, and they do not know if it knows they are there.

But they act as if it does. And perhaps that is enough.

## The Fauna

The animals of the Hanging Green are not incidental. They are not pests or pets or wildlife in the way you understand those categories. They are *components* — functional elements of the city-organism, as necessary as the root-columns or the light fixtures or the Tenders themselves.

The small chitinous insects — the six-legged, wingless crawlers — are the city's maintenance crew. They clean. They remove dead plant matter, carrying it in tiny loads to decomposition chambers where it is broken down and recycled into nutrients. They groom the root-surfaces, chewing away fungal growths that are not the beneficial mycorrhizae but competing species that would damage the structure. They tend the light fixtures, crawling into the housings and removing debris, polishing the lenses with their smooth carapaces, repairing — actually *repairing*, with secretions that function as solder and insulation — damaged wiring. They are not trained. They are not domesticated. They are *specialized* — evolved, or engineered, or cultivated over centuries to perform these functions as naturally as a bee tends a hive. The Hanging Green is their hive. They are its immune system, its janitorial staff, its repair service.

The rabbit-creatures — the grazers — are mobile nutrient cyclers. They eat plant matter that the city produces in excess: the moss, the shelf fungi, the leaves that are pruned during training and shaping. They digest it, process it, excrete it in a form that is more readily absorbed by the root systems. They are, in effect, external digestive organs of the city-organism, performing a step in the nutrient cycle that the plants cannot perform themselves. Their droppings — small, dry, nearly odorless pellets — are collected by the Rooted and applied to root-columns that need feeding, or composted in the decomposition chambers, or used as fuel in the kilns (they burn slowly, with a hot, clean flame, leaving almost no ash). Nothing is wasted. Everything cycles.

The dog-sized creature with the photosynthetic fur — you saw it on the high walkway, its root-tip nose questing the air — is something else. It is a sensor. A mobile sensory platform. The root-tip on its nose is a genuine root, connected to the mycelial network through a symbiotic interface at the base of its skull. The creature roams the city, and as it roams, it *tastes* the air, the surfaces, the chemical environment, and it feeds that information back to the network. It is the city's way of knowing what is happening in places the fixed root-system cannot reach: the upper chambers near the membrane-seal, the narrow gaps between structural members, the spaces where the insects are too small to carry useful data. The creature is not intelligent in the way a dog is intelligent. It is more like a blood cell — a mobile component of a larger body, performing a function, carrying information, responding to signals it does not understand.

There are others. You glimpse them in the shadows, in the gaps, in the overhead lattice. A thing like a snake, but segmented, that moves through the hollow stems of the root-columns like a peristaltic pulse, clearing blockages. A thing like a spider, but with too many eyes, that sits motionless in the corners of the market chamber for hours, watching, its purpose unclear — surveillance, maybe, or simply observation, the city watching its inhabitants the way a body monitors its own internal state. A thing you cannot see at all, only hear: a deep, subsonic vibration that you feel in your sternum rather than your ears, emanating from somewhere far below, from the deepest chambers, from the root of the root of the root. It might be geological. It might be biological. It might be the city-organism's equivalent of a heartbeat, slow and vast and patient, the pulse of something so large and so old that your presence here is less than a fleeting stimulus, a momentary irritation, a brief and soon-forgotten sensation in the skin of a being that does not think in moments but in centuries.

## The City Itself

And finally — the inhabitant that is not a person, not an animal, not a creature in any sense you are comfortable with, but that is, in the end, the reason all the others are here.

The Hanging Green.

It is alive. It grows. It maintains itself. It responds to its environment. It adapts. It learns — slowly, chemically, in ways that look like instinct but might be something more. It has goals, or something like goals: it grows toward light. It grows toward stability. It grows toward complexity. It resists damage. It repairs itself. It incorporates foreign objects — the light fixtures, the machines, the tools, the people — into its structure, not as parasites or symbionts but as *organs.* The Rooted are its hands. The Tenders are its will. The insects are its immune system. The grazers are its digestive tract. The Dry Walkers are its sensory organs, reaching out into the world above, bringing back information and resources. The machines are its metabolism — the industrial processes that transform raw material into useful form, that generate heat and light and the conditions necessary for life.

It is not conscious. Probably. Almost certainly. But it is not *not* conscious, either. It occupies a space on the spectrum of being that your language, your philosophy, your science does not have a category for. It is a living city. A city that is alive. A thing that is both organism and environment, both structure and inhabitant, both the place and the thing that lives in the place.

It was cultivated. Someone, centuries ago, planted the first root, sealed the first membrane, hung the first light. Someone made the decision to build a city that would grow itself, that would be alive, that would be *home* in a way that no building made of dead materials could ever be. That someone is long gone. The city remains. The city grows. The city tends its tenders and feeds its feeders and houses its housed and pulses, slowly, in the dark, in the dry, in the green, in the deep.

It is below the surface. It is almost without water. It is almost all plant. It is cool, and quiet, and strange.

And it is alive, and it is building itself, and it is home.
