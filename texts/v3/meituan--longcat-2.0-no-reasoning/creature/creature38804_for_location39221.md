# The Lithoderm Highlands

---

## The Crust Dwellers

They are not what you would call animals, though they move, and they are not what you would call plants, though they are rooted — or something like rooted — and they are not what you would call fungi, though they spread in networks through the mineral substrate in ways that are deeply, fundamentally mycelial. They are something else. Something that has no analogue on wet worlds, no comfortable taxonomic box to be placed in. The closest word might be *lithovore*, but even that implies a simplicity of relationship with stone that does not capture what they actually are.

They are large. The ones you have not yet seen — the ones that left the track in the dimpled depression, the dark shape on the distant ridge — are the size of large boulders, or small vehicles, or something in between. Their bodies are low and broad, flattened against the crust in a way that suggests they have been pressed down by the weight of the thin atmosphere, or by the weight of time itself. They move on many legs — not the jointed, articulated legs of arthropods, but something more like the tube feet of echinoderms, or the pseudopods of amoebae, or the root tips of plants: soft, muscular, mineral-encrusted appendages that extend and contract in slow, hydraulic waves. The gait is deliberate. The gait is *geological*. A single step might take seconds. A crossing of a hundred meters might take hours. They do not hurry. There is nothing here that requires hurry.

Their bodies are armored — not with chitin, not with bone, but with mineral. They incorporate the crust itself into their tissues, building layers of calcium and silica and something else, something that does not appear in any periodic table you know, into plates and scales and ridges that cover their dorsal surfaces like the armor of ancient, vanished creatures. The armor is the color of the crust — pale, chalky, bone-white — and it is weathered in the same way the crust is weathered, dimpled and ridged and pocked, so that a lithovore at rest is indistinguishable from a boulder, from a ridge, from a piece of the landscape itself. This is not camouflage in the active sense. It is something deeper: a convergence of form and substrate so complete that the boundary between organism and environment has become, for all practical purposes, meaningless.

They eat the crust. Or they eat something in the crust. Or they eat the *process* of the crust — the slow chemical reactions that occur at the interface between the mineral surface and the atmosphere, the oxidation and reduction and dissolution and precipitation that constitute the planet's geochemistry. Their ventral surfaces — the soft, unarmored undersides that press against the pale mineral — are covered in a dense mat of the same hair-like filaments that cover the lithophyte patches, and these filaments are not for show. They are chemical factories, enzymatic engines that catalyze reactions the crust would not perform on its own, breaking down mineral bonds and extracting energy from the rearrangement. The lithovores are, in a sense, *accelerating* the weathering of the crust — speeding up processes that would otherwise take millennia, harvesting the energy released by the acceleration, and leaving behind a residue of altered mineral that is softer, more porous, more *alive* than what was there before.

This is why the lithophyte patches grow where they do. The lithovores move across the crust in slow, meandering paths — paths that are not random but follow chemical gradients, following the richest mineral deposits, the most energetic reactions — and behind them, in the altered substrate they leave in their wake, the lithophytes take root. The lithophytes are the successors, the colonizers, the organisms that move in after the lithovores have done their work and made the crust *fertile* — fertile not in the way soil is fertile on wet worlds, but in the way that a freshly exposed mineral surface is fertile: rich in reactive chemistry, full of energy waiting to be tapped. The lithophytes harvest that energy with their filament-hairs, and in doing so they stabilize the altered crust, binding it, consolidating it, preparing it for the next cycle. And the lithovores move on, following the chemical frontier, always seeking the unaltered crust, the fresh mineral, the unweathered stone.

It is a cycle. It is an ecosystem. It is slow beyond comprehension — a single lithovore might take centuries to cross a single crustal plate, and the lithophyte patches that follow it might take millennia to fully colonize the trail it leaves behind — but it is *real*, and it is *functional*, and it has been running for longer than the crust itself has existed in its current form.

## The Deep Ones

The fissures are not empty.

The fissures are the most alive places on this world — more alive than the lithophyte patches, more alive than the lithovore trails, more alive than anything on the surface. The surface is where the energy is harvested, but the fissures are where it is *processed*, where it is *concentrated*, where the deep chemistry of the planet meets the surface chemistry of the atmosphere and the two engage in a slow, patient exchange that has been ongoing since the crust first cracked open and revealed the warmth beneath.

The organisms that live in the fissures are not visible from the surface. They live on the walls, in the crevices, in the narrow ledges and shelves of mineral that line the blue-black depths. They are small — most of them no larger than your hand, some no larger than your fingernail — and they are *dense*, packed together in communities of staggering complexity, layer upon layer, species upon species, a vertical ecology that descends into the darkness for distances you cannot estimate. They are chemotrophs, all of them, feeding on the thermal gradient, on the chemical exhalations that rise from the planet's interior through the fissures like breath through open mouths. They are the base of a food chain that has no analog on wet worlds — a food chain based not on sunlight and photosynthesis but on heat and chemistry, on the slow, steady flow of energy from the planet's core to its surface.

Some of them are filamentous — thin, thread-like organisms that hang from the fissure walls in dense curtains, swaying in the faint updraft of warm air that rises from below. Some are encrusting — flat, spreading bodies that coat the mineral surfaces in layers of color, the ochre and rust and bruised violet you saw from above, each color a different species, each species a different chemical strategy for extracting energy from the gradient. Some are motile — small, crawling things that move along the fissure walls with the same deliberate, hydraulic slowness as the lithovores above, grazing on the encrusting communities, preying on each other, participating in a food web that is as complex and as dynamic as any coral reef, but operating on timescales that make the reef look frantic.

And some of them are *loud*.

The sound you heard — the low, resonant vibration that seemed to come from the ground itself, from the crust, from the depths below — was not geological. It was biological. It was the sound of the fissure communities, the collective vibration of millions of tiny organisms moving and feeding and communicating in ways that produce, as a byproduct, a deep, sustained tone that resonates through the crust like a bell. The tone is not constant. It varies — in pitch, in intensity, in duration — and the variations are not random. They are *information*. The fissure communities are talking to each other, across the vast distances of the crustal plates, through the medium of the mineral itself, using the crust as a resonating chamber, a communication network, a *nervous system* that connects every fissure on the planet into a single, slow, planetary conversation.

You cannot understand the conversation. You do not have the ears for it, or the time, or the patience. But it is happening. It has been happening for longer than you can imagine. And it is *about* something — about the state of the crust, about the movement of the lithovores, about the health of the lithophyte patches, about the thermal output of the planet's interior, about the slow, patient, ongoing business of being alive on a world that has never known rain.

## The Sky Dwellers

There is life in the air.

Not much. The atmosphere is too thin, too dry, too poor in the resources that life requires to support anything large or complex. But there is *something* — something that drifts in the high, amber air, something that is not quite a microbe and not quite a spore and not quite a seed, something that occupies the ecological niche that dust occupies on wet worlds but is *alive* in ways that dust is not. They are small — microscopic, most of them — and they are *everywhere*, suspended in the thin air in concentrations that are low by the standards of wet-world atmospheres but high by the standards of this one. They are the aerial plankton of this world, the base of an atmospheric food chain that has no visible upper trophic levels, the drifting, living dust that gives the amber sky its faint, uniform luminosity.

They are not photosynthetic — there is no point, when the light comes from everywhere and nowhere, when there is no directionality to exploit, no advantage to be gained from orienting toward a source. They are *radiotrophic*, or something like it — they harvest energy directly from the ambient radiation, from the photons that fill the amber sky, using pigments that are not chlorophyll but something analogous, something that absorbs across a broad spectrum and converts the energy into chemical form with an efficiency that is low but sufficient. They do not grow. They do not reproduce, in any sense you would recognize. They simply *persist*, drifting in the air, harvesting light, maintaining themselves in a state of minimal metabolic activity that can continue indefinitely — for years, for centuries, for as long as the light shines and the air moves and the world turns beneath the amber sky.

And sometimes, when the dust events come — when the thermal currents lift fine mineral particles from the crust and the air becomes briefly opaque — they *feed*. Not on the dust itself, but on the chemistry of the dust, on the reactive mineral surfaces that are exposed by the lifting, on the energy that is released when fresh mineral meets atmosphere. The dust events are, for the sky dwellers, what rain is to life on wet worlds: a pulse of nutrients, a surge of activity, a brief, intense period of growth and reproduction and metabolic frenzy that ends when the dust settles and the clarity returns and the world goes back to its slow, patient, amber-lit equilibrium.

## The Oldest Ones

There is something beneath the crust.

Not in the fissures — the fissures are surface features, relatively young, relatively shallow. Beneath the crust. In the warm, dense, mineral-rich layer that lies between the surface and the core, the layer that the crust floats on like a scum on old milk, the layer that is the planet's mantle and its memory and its *mind*, if it has a mind, which it might.

The lithovores know about it. The lithovores, in their slow, deliberate crossings of the crustal plates, are not just following chemical gradients. They are *listening*. Their ventral filaments, the ones that press against the crust and catalyze the reactions that feed them, are also sensors — chemical sensors, thermal sensors, *seismic* sensors — and they are tuned to the deep vibrations that rise from below, the slow, planetary pulses that are the heartbeat of the world. The lithovores respond to these pulses. They change direction. They speed up, or slow down, or stop entirely and press themselves flat against the crust and *listen*, their whole bodies becoming antennae, receivers, instruments for detecting signals that originate far below the surface, in the warm, dense, living rock of the deep interior.

What is down there is not alive in any sense you would recognize. It is not an organism. It is not a community. It is not an ecosystem. It is a *process* — a self-sustaining, self-regulating, self-*aware* process that has been running since the planet formed, a process that involves the slow circulation of mantle material, the gradual differentiation of chemical species, the patient accumulation and release of thermal energy, and something else, something that is not chemistry or physics but *information* — the storage and processing of data in the structure of the mineral itself, in the arrangement of crystals, in the distribution of isotopes, in the pattern of magnetic fields frozen into ancient rock.

The planet is thinking. Not quickly. Not in any way that produces thoughts you would recognize as thoughts. But it is *processing*. It is *aware*. It knows about the lithovores on its surface, and the lithophyte patches, and the fissure communities, and the sky dwellers, and the thin, dry atmosphere, and the amber light, and the slow, patient erosion of its crust. It knows about all of these things because they are *part of it*, because they are expressions of the same deep process that drives the mantle circulation and the thermal output and the chemical differentiation, because the boundary between the planet and its life is as blurred and as meaningless as the boundary between the lithovore and the crust it walks on.

The sound you heard — the bell-tone, the deep resonance — was not just the fissure communities talking. It was the planet *humming*. It was the sound of the deep process, the slow, planetary awareness, expressing itself through the medium of the crust and the fissures and the lithovores and the lithophytes and the sky dwellers and the thin, amber air. It was the sound of a world that is alive — not in the way a single organism is alive, but in the way a *system* is alive, a system that has achieved a stable state of self-regulation and self-awareness that has persisted for longer than any individual component, any single organism, any one species or community or ecosystem has existed.

The lithovores hear it. The lithovores respond to it. The lithovores are, in a sense, the planet's *hands* — its agents on the surface, its instruments for manipulating the crust, for accelerating the weathering, for creating the conditions that allow the lithophytes to grow and the fissure communities to thrive and the sky dwellers to drift in the amber air. The lithovores are not separate from the planet. They are *of* the planet, as the planet is of them, and the cycle they participate in — the slow, patient, geological cycle of crust alteration and colonization and succession and alteration again — is not just an ecosystem. It is a *thought*. A very slow thought. A thought that has been thinking itself for billions of years and is not finished yet.

## The Silence

And in the silence — the deep, mineral silence of the Lithoderm Highlands, the silence that is not empty but *full*, full of the subliminal hum of lithophyte filaments and the deeper hum of the fissure communities and the deepest hum of the planet itself — in that silence, you understand something.

You understand that this world is not a place where life has *adapted* to harsh conditions. It is a place where life has *become* the conditions. The lithovores are not surviving on the crust. They *are* the crust, in the process of becoming something else. The lithophytes are not growing on the mineral surface. They *are* the mineral surface, in the process of becoming alive. The fissure communities are not living in the cracks. They *are* the cracks, in the process of becoming conscious. The sky dwellers are not drifting in the atmosphere. They *are* the atmosphere, in the process of becoming aware.

And the planet — the vast, pale, cracked, ancient planet beneath your feet, the planet that has never known rain, the planet that glows amber and hums with a sound like a bell, like stone, like a living thing that has forgotten it is alive — the planet is not a place.

It is a *being*.

And it knows you are here.
