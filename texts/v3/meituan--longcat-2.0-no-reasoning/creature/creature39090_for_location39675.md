# The People of Korvathane

They are not a people who were shaped by their city. They are a people who were *selected* by it, the way water selects its path through stone — slowly, by the removal of everything that could not stay.

---

## The Body

A Korvathani person, seen for the first time, registers as slightly wrong in a way you cannot immediately name. They are not deformed. They are not ill. They are *adapted*, and adaptation, when you see it clearly, always looks like a small violence done to the expected form.

They are short. Not uniformly — there is variation — but the average height is perhaps fifteen centimeters below what you would expect for a population with adequate nutrition. Their frames are dense. Not heavy, exactly, but *compact*, as if the body has learned to pack itself more efficiently into less space. Limbs are proportionally shorter than torsos. Necks are thick. Hands are broad and flat, with fingers that seem to have an extra joint — they don't, but the tendons move in a way that creates that impression, a fluid, multi-segmented articulation that makes their hands look like they are always slightly in the process of becoming something else.

Their skin is pale. Not the pallor of illness or confinement, but a genuine lack of melanin that has been selected for over generations — there is so little direct sunlight in the underground levels and the haze-softened streets that pigmentation offers no advantage, and something about the mineral-heavy water and air has made darker skin prone to a particular kind of dermatitis that is common in newcomers but absent in the native-born. The pale skin is not smooth. It is stippled with tiny mineral deposits — calcium, iron — that form a faint, permanent freckling, a constellation of rust-colored and white dots that is most visible on the hands and forearms. They call this *stone-bloom* and consider it beautiful. It is beautiful, in the way that a patina is beautiful — evidence of time and contact and the slow exchange between a body and its environment.

Their eyes are large. This is the feature that unsettles newcomers most. Not grotesquely so — not the exaggerated, luminous eyes of a deep-sea creature — but large enough to be noticeable, with irises that are uniformly dark, a brown so deep it reads as black in anything less than direct light. The pupils are wide even in bright conditions. They see well in the perpetual twilight of the lower levels. They see *too* well, sometimes — they can detect movement in conditions that would leave you reaching for a lamp, and they find your inability to do the same faintly amusing, the way a person who grew up in a quiet place finds a city-dweller's tolerance for noise amusing.

Their hearing is changed. This is the deepest adaptation, and the one they are least aware of. The bellows-sound has been present for so many generations that their auditory systems have literally restructured around it. The low-frequency oscillation that you feel as a pressure in your chest, they do not hear at all — it has been filtered out at the neurological level, the way you filter out the sound of your own heartbeat. What they *do* hear is the upper harmonics, the clatter of the tear-fangs, the whistle of steam, the grinding mid-range — and they hear these things with extraordinary precision. They can identify a pump station by its sound signature from three levels away. They can tell you which kiln is firing by the pitch of its resonance. They can hear a water channel reversing its flow before any instrument registers the change. Their hearing is not better than yours in absolute terms. It is *different* — tuned to a frequency band that their city occupies and that yours does not.

They speak quietly. This is not a cultural preference; it is a physiological necessity. Their own voices, in the frequency range they use for speech, would be painful to sustain at volume in the resonant architecture of the city. So they have developed a mode of communication that is low, close, and precise — a speaking style that requires you to be near the speaker and to attend carefully. Conversations in Korvathane are intimate by default. You cannot shout across a street here. You cannot call down a corridor. You must go to the person, stand near them, and speak into the narrow space between your mouth and their ear. This has shaped everything about their social life.

## The Mind

They are patient in a way that looks, from the outside, like indifference. It is not indifference. It is a temporal orientation that has been calibrated to the pace of their city. They think in long cycles. A Korvathani person making a decision does not weigh options the way you do — quickly, with a preference for resolution — but slowly, with a preference for *correctness*, where correctness is defined not as optimality but as *fit*: does this decision fit the rhythm of the place? Does it align with the cycle? Will it still be right when the pressure shifts?

This makes them seem slow to outsiders. It makes them seem passive. They are neither. They are operating on a timescale that you cannot perceive without living here, the way you cannot perceive the movement of an hour hand without watching it for a long time. They are making constant adjustments — to the sound, to the water, to the subtle shifts in the mineral patterns on the walls, to the behavior of the moths and the slugs and the rats — and these adjustments are so fine and so continuous that they look like stillness.

They have a relationship with the city that is not quite symbiosis and not quite parasitism but something older than either word. They maintain the city. The city maintains them. The boundary between the two is not clear. A Korvathani engineer repairing a pump is not performing a task on a machine; she is performing a function within an organism, the way a white blood cell performs a function within a body. She does not think of it this way. She would find the metaphor strange. But her hands move with the unconscious precision of a process that has been internalized so deeply it has become reflex.

They dream of water. This is documented. Sleep studies conducted by a visiting researcher — one of the few outsiders who stayed long enough to conduct formal research — found that Korvathani dream content is overwhelmingly aquatic. Not drowning. Not fear. Just *water* — the sensation of being surrounded by it, held by it, moving through it. The researcher noted that the dreams had a rhythmic quality, a rise and fall that matched the bellows-cycle. The subjects, when asked, said they did not find this unusual. Several said they did not dream at all. The researcher believed the instruments.

## The Social Form

Korvathane has no government in the way you would recognize one. It has a *maintenance hierarchy* — a system of roles and responsibilities organized around the care of the city's infrastructure. The most important people in Korvathane are not politicians or merchants. They are the pump-station operators, the kiln-tenders, the channel-walkers who patrol the drainage system and clear blockages, the resonance-listeners who monitor the sound and track its changes.

The resonance-listeners are the closest thing the city has to priests. They are a small group — perhaps thirty individuals at any given time — who have trained their hearing to a degree that goes beyond even the native adaptation. They can hear things in the sound that others cannot: micro-variations in the bellows-cycle, harmonic shifts that precede pressure changes in the deep rock, the faint, almost subliminal tones that the tear-fangs produce when the wind moves through them at specific angles. They keep records. They maintain a continuous log of the city's sound that stretches back, in various forms, for centuries. They can tell you what the sound was doing on any given day in the last two hundred years. They can tell you when it changed. They can tell you what happened in the city when it changed.

They do not make predictions. This is important. They do not say *the sound will shift and therefore X will happen*. They say *the sound has shifted before, and when it did, X happened*. They are historians, not prophets. But the distinction is lost on most residents, who treat the resonance-listeners with a deference that is not quite reverence and not quite fear but something in between — the way you might treat a person who can read the weather in a way you cannot, whose knowledge you depend on but do not fully understand.

Families in Korvathane are organized around maintenance roles. A pump-operator's children are likely to become pump-operators. A channel-walker's children are likely to become channel-walkers. This is not enforced. It is simply how knowledge transfers — a child who grows up in a pump station, who hears the machinery before they hear language, who learns the rhythm of the valves before they learn to walk, will find it natural to operate a pump station the way a child who grows up speaking a language finds it natural to speak. The roles are not inherited. They are *absorbed*.

There is a role that has no name in the language of Korvathane but that outsiders have called *the rememberers*. These are individuals — usually elderly, usually women, though not exclusively — who maintain the city's non-technical knowledge. They know the history of every structure. They know which alcove was carved by which family, which kiln was built in response to which crisis, which street was raised and which was lowered and why. They know the names of people who have been dead for a hundred years. They know the stories that are not written down — the ones about the doubling, about the water that flows the wrong way, about the mineral patterns that look like writing. They are not consulted formally. They are consulted constantly, in the way that you consult a memory — automatically, without deciding to, because the knowledge is there and it is needed.

## The Outsiders

There are perhaps two hundred people living in Korvathane who are not native-born. They are a distinct population, visible at a glance — taller, darker-skinned, louder, with eyes that are still adjusting to the light levels and ears that have not yet learned to filter the bellows-sound. They are not mistreated. They are not welcomed. They are *tolerated*, in the way that a body tolerates a graft — with the understanding that integration will take time and may not succeed.

Some of them are engineers, sent by distant municipalities to study Korvathane's water-management systems, which are centuries ahead of anything else in the known world. Some are traders, dealing in the resonance-ceramics that the kilns still produce. Some are researchers — geologists, biologists, acousticians — drawn by the strangeness of the place. Some are refugees from other cities, people who left places that were too loud or too dry or too bright and who heard, from travelers, that there was a city built into a wet rock face where the sound was constant and the pace was slow and no one asked you to be anything other than what you could become.

The ones who stay are the ones who learn to hear. Not the sound — the sound is always there, and you never truly stop hearing it, no matter how long you live here — but the *information* in the sound. The ones who stay are the ones who, after months or years of living with the bellows-breath, begin to notice when it changes. Who begin to feel the pressure shift in their chest before the instruments register it. Who begin to wake, briefly, at the top of the cycle, when the inhalation peaks and the city holds its breath for a half-second before exhaling. These people are not becoming Korvathani. That is not possible. But they are becoming *compatible*, and compatibility, in Korvathane, is enough.

The ones who leave do so quickly. They arrive, they hear the sound, they feel the pressure, and within days or weeks they are on the high road again, climbing back toward the escarpment edge, and they do not look back. They do not write about the place. They do not recommend it. When asked, they say it was *interesting* or *unique* or *not for me*, and they change the subject. They do not say what they felt, which was that the city was *aware of them* — that the sound changed when they entered a room, that the water in the channels near their lodging flowed differently, that the moths on the walls oriented toward them in a way that moths should not orient toward anything. They do not say this because it sounds insane, and because they are not sure it was true, and because the not-sureness is worse than either alternative.

## The Children

Korvathani children are quiet. This is the first thing that strikes outsiders, and the thing that disturbs them most. The children do not shout. They do not run. They do not play in the way that children in other places play — loudly, expansively, with the unselfconscious physicality of beings who have not yet learned that their bodies take up space. Korvathani children move carefully. They walk close to walls. They speak in the same low, close register as adults. They attend to the sound.

They are taught, from a very young age, to listen. Not to music — there is music in Korvathane, but it is functional, tied to the rhythms of the pumps and the kilns, and it is not taught to children as art. What they are taught to listen to is the city itself. A Korvathani child of five can tell you which direction is down-level by the sound. A child of eight can tell you whether a pump is running correctly by its vibration. A child of ten can hear the tear-fangs ringing and tell you which way the wind is blowing and how fast. These are not remarkable abilities in Korvathane. They are baseline competencies, the equivalent of learning to read in a literate society.

The children play in the lower levels, in the spaces between the pump stations, in the cisterns when the water is low. Their games are quiet and repetitive and involve a great deal of waiting. They play games that require patience — sitting still for long periods, tracking the movement of slugs through drainage channels, watching the mineral patterns on the walls for changes that may not come for hours. They play games that involve listening — identifying sounds, locating their sources, predicting their changes. They do not play games that involve winning. The concept of winning, in the competitive sense, is foreign to them. Their games have outcomes — a pattern is identified, a sound is located, a slug is tracked to its destination — but the outcome is collective, shared, a thing that happened to the group rather than a thing that one child achieved over another.

They are not unhappy. This is important to state, because an outsider watching them would assume they were. The quietness, the stillness, the absence of what you would recognize as childhood exuberance — it looks like deprivation. It is not. It is a different model of childhood, one calibrated to a place where exuberance is not useful and stillness is. The children of Korvathane are not suppressed. They are *attuned*. They are learning, from the beginning, the skill that defines life here: the ability to be present in a place that is always changing on a timescale you cannot perceive, and to find that presence not oppressive but *sufficient*.

## The Old

The elderly in Korvathane do not retire. There is no concept of retirement. A pump-operator who can no longer climb the stairs to the pump station becomes a resonance-listener. A resonance-listener who can no longer hear the upper harmonics becomes a rememberer. A rememberer who can no longer speak clearly becomes a presence — a person who sits in a particular room or alcove and whose mere existence in that place is a form of knowledge, a living connection to the history of that specific spot.

The very old — those in their final years — gravitate toward the deepest levels. This is not institutionalized. No one sends them there. They go. They descend, slowly, over months or years, moving to lower and lower levels, closer to the water, closer to the sound at its source. They say the sound is clearer down there. They say it is easier to hear. They say they can hear things in it that they could not hear above — not words, not messages, but *patterns*, deep structures in the oscillation that are obscured by the noise of the upper levels.

Some of them report, in their final weeks, that the doubling has become more frequent. That they spend more time in the other place — the higher place, the lighter place, the place that is the same shape but not the same place. They describe it calmly, without fear or wonder, the way you might describe a change in the weather. *I was there again today*, they say. *It was the same. It is always the same.*

They die in the lower levels. This is where Korvathani people die — not in hospitals, not in beds, but in the deep rooms near the pump stations, lying on stone that weeps, breathing air that is thick with mineral vapor, listening to the sound that has been the background of their entire lives. Their families sit with them. They speak quietly, close, into the narrow space between mouth and ear. They do not say goodbye. They say *the cycle is shifting*, which is the Korvathani way of saying that a life is ending, and which means, literally, that the bellows-breath is changing, and which means, in the deepest sense, that the city is breathing and the breath is moving through and the person is becoming part of the breath.

Their bodies are returned to the stone. There are alcoves in the deepest levels — natural cavities, expanded by carving, lined with the same iron-oxide accretion as the tear-fangs — where the dead are placed. The mineral deposits begin immediately. Within a year, the body is coated. Within five years, it is encased. Within a generation, it is indistinguishable from the stone around it. The Korvathani do not find this morbid. They find it *accurate*. The body came from the stone — from the mineral-heavy water, from the clay, from the calcium and iron that built the bones — and to the stone it returns. The cycle completes. The breath exhales.

## What They Know

They know that the city is slowing. The bellows-cycle, which their grandparents counted at thirty seconds, now takes forty-five. The resonance-listeners have tracked this. The rememberers have confirmed it against older records. The deep rock is losing pressure. The hydrothermal system that powers the pumps, that drives the sound, that keeps the water at its cruelly shallow depth — it is diminishing. Not quickly. Not in a way that threatens the city in any single generation. But in a way that, projected forward, means something.

They do not discuss this openly. It is not a secret. It is simply a fact that exists at the same depth as the water table — always present, always pressing, always just below the surface of daily life. They maintain the pumps. They clear the channels. They tend the kilns. They listen to the sound. They do what they have always done, because what they have always done is what the city requires, and the city requires it now as it has always required it, and the fact that the requirement may not be permanent does not change the fact that it is current.

They know something else, too. The ones who have lived here longest, who have descended to the deepest levels, who have sat in the alcoves where the dead are becoming stone — they know that the doubling is not a phenomenon. It is a *place*. The other place, the higher place, the lighter place, the place that is the same shape but not the same place — it is real. It exists. It is Korvathane, or it is what Korvathane is becoming, or it is what Korvathane has always been underneath the stone and the water and the sound. They do not have a theory about this. They have an experience of it, repeated over years, consistent across individuals, and the experience is this: the city is not only a place. It is a *process*. And the process is moving in a direction. And the direction is *up*.

They do not say this to outsiders. They do not say it to each other. They say it to the stone, in the way that they say everything to the stone — quietly, close, into the narrow space between breath and surface, where the mineral deposits grow in patterns that might be writing, where the water flows and pauses and flows again, where the sound rises from the deep rock like a breath that has been held for centuries and is only now, slowly, being released.
