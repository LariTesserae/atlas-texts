They do not have a name for themselves. They do not have names at all. They have the floor, and the floor has them, and this is the entirety of the relationship.

Each one is a body the size of a closed hand, low and flat, armored in segments that overlap like the scales of something that decided to be a coin. Their color is the color of the stone — wet black, dark gray, the occasional brown where mineral content shifts — and this is not camouflage because there is nothing here that hunts by sight. It is convergence. They are made of the same mineral logic as the floor, the same slow precipitation of dissolved stone and organic residue, the same patient chemistry. Whether they are creatures that learned to be geological or geology that learned to be creature is a distinction the place does not recognize.

They have legs. Many legs. Tiny, jointed, numerous — they move in waves, each segment's limbs firing in sequence so that the body ripples forward in a motion that is more peristaltic than walking. They are slow by the standards of surface animals, fast by the standards of rock. Over a day, one of them might travel the length of its own body a hundred times. Over a year, it might cross a valley. Over a century, it might circle the base of a column and find that the column has changed shape beneath it, worn by the passage of its own kind into something smoother, more curved, more suited to their movement, which wore it, which made it more suited, which wore it further.

This is what they do. This is what they are.

They eat by pressing their undersurfaces against the stone and secreting — slowly, over hours — a film of something that is not quite acid and not quite enzyme, a solvent that works on geological time, dissolving the thinnest possible layer of mineral and absorbing it. The amount each individual takes is negligible. The amount the colony takes, across millions of bodies working in continuous contact with the floor for longer than the stone has had its current shape, is the reason the floor looks the way it does. The channels. The basins. The smooth undulations. The architecture that resembles arches and columns and vaulted spaces. They made this. Not intentionally — there is no intention here, or if there is, it operates on a scale that makes the word meaningless — but persistently, collectively, the way water makes a canyon, the way roots crack a foundation. They are the erosion. They are the weather. They are the slow force that has replaced whatever other forces might have shaped this place.

And the stone feeds them, and they multiply, and their bodies, when they cease, become part of the floor again — their mineral content reabsorbed, their organic residue incorporated into the dark wet surface, so that the boundary between organism and substrate is not a line but a gradient, a zone of transition where it is impossible to say where one ends and the other begins.

They do not have a social structure. They do not have a hierarchy. They have density.

When they are crowded, they move differently than when they are sparse. In the valleys, where water pools and the stone is softest and the feeding is best, they pile atop one another in layers — three deep, five deep, a living sediment — and the ones on top feed on the secretions of the ones below, or feed on the ones below directly, or simply exist in a state of contact so continuous that the distinction between feeding and touching dissolves. They press against each other with a gentle, constant pressure, each body adjusting to the bodies around it, finding the configuration of least resistance, the arrangement that allows the most legs to reach the stone, the most surface area to contact water, the most efficient distribution of weight across the bodies below.

This is the sound. This is the roar.

It is the sound of millions of tiny legs on stone. Of segments sliding against segments. Of mouthparts working at a frequency too low to hear individually but collectively producing a hiss that fills the air like weather. Of bodies shifting, resettling, the constant micro-adjustments of a crowd that never stops moving because stopping means drying out, means losing contact with the water film, means losing contact with the stone, means ceasing to feed, means ceasing.

They do not sleep. They do not rest. They slow, perhaps, in ways that might be analogous to rest — periods where the rippling motion of their legs becomes shallower, where their secretions thin, where they press more firmly against their neighbors and less against the stone — but they do not stop. The sound does not stop. It has not stopped.

The waves that move through them — the pulses of higher activity, the coordinated surges that flow uphill and pool in valleys and shift the pitch of the roar — these are not decisions. They are physics. A density gradient here produces a pressure wave there. A pool of water expanding in a basin draws organisms toward it, and their movement displaces other organisms, and the displacement propagates, and what looks like coordination is simply the mechanics of bodies in contact responding to the same stimuli with the same limited repertoire of responses. They move toward water. They move toward density. They move away from dryness, from emptiness, from the places where the stone is too hard or too high or too far from the seepage. These are not choices. They are the shape of the organism expressing itself in motion, the way a drop of water expresses the shape of gravity.

And yet.

The light.

When one of them goes reflective — when its surface, for a fraction of a second, ceases to absorb the ambient gray glow and instead returns it, flaring outward in a bright brief instant — the ones around it do the same. And the ones around them. And the ones around them. A wave of brightness rolls across the visible landscape, thousands of bodies flaring in sequence, a cascade of reflection that moves faster than any individual organism could move, that propagates through the colony like a signal, like a pulse, like a thought moving through a brain made of stone and water and millions of small dark bodies pressed together in the low dark damp.

It means something. Or it doesn't. The distinction may not apply.

What is observable is this: the flares happen more often near water. They happen more often in dense regions. They happen in patterns that are not quite periodic but not quite random — clusters, gaps, clusters, a rhythm that suggests something being communicated, or something being computed, or something being felt by a sensorium so distributed that no single organism contains it and the colony as a whole is the only unit large enough to register what it is registering.

They do not notice you because you are not water. You are not stone. You are not density. You are a warm dry thing standing in a place that is cool and wet and dense beyond comprehension, and you produce no gradient they are built to respond to. Your footsteps vibrate the stone in ways that ripple outward and are absorbed. Your body heat creates a tiny zone of slightly warmer air that the moisture condenses around, and the organisms nearest you adjust — pressing slightly away from the warmth, toward the water that beads on your skin, extending toward the mineral content of your sweat — but this is not recognition. This is chemistry. This is the organism doing what it does: moving toward water, moving toward soluble minerals, moving toward the conditions that allow it to continue doing what it does.

You are, to them, a feature of the landscape. A warm, damp, mineral-rich feature that appeared recently and may not persist. They will erode you the way they erode everything — slowly, collectively, without malice or awareness, the way the floor has been eroded, the way the arches and columns have been shaped, the way the channels have been carved that now carry water to the pools where they gather in their greatest density and the sound is deepest and the thrumming is something you feel in your chest.

They have been here since before the floor had its current shape. They will be here after it has a different shape. They are the process by which the floor becomes itself, the living erosion, the biological weather, the organism that is also the environment, the creature that is also the stone, the sound that is also the silence underneath it, the continuous granular hiss of ten million small bodies doing the only thing they know how to do, which is to press against the world and dissolve it and absorb it and become it and press against it again, in the low dark damp, on the resonant floor, in the sound that never stops.
