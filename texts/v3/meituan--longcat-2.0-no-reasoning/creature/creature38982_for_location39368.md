# The Inhabitants of the Condensation Cathedral

---

## I. The Machinists

They do not have names. They have *ratios*.

The Machinists are the largest of the fauna — some as long as your middle finger, most no bigger than a grain of rice — and they are the ones who maintain the weeping. They move through the pipe-networks with a purpose that looks, from a distance, like devotion. Up close, it is something more precise than devotion. It is *calibration*.

Each Machinist is built around a central spindle — a rod of black iron, slightly flexible, that runs the length of its body like a spine. Around this spindle, rings of brass and copper are stacked in configurations that vary from individual to individual. Some carry three rings. Some carry seventeen. The rings are not fixed; they slide, rotate, click into new positions, and each new position changes the Machinist's function. A Machinist with its rings in one configuration is a *sealer* — it finds places where the weeping is too thin and presses its body against the gap, extruding a fine ceramic paste from glands along its ventral surface that hardens into a new layer of wall. A Machinist with its rings in another configuration is a *driller* — it bores into places where the weeping is too thick, where water has pooled and begun to corrode, and it opens new channels, new capillaries, new paths for the water to follow so that the weeping remains even.

They do not communicate. They do not need to. The water itself is their language. A change in flow rate, a shift in pressure, a new bead forming where a bead should not be — these are their words, their sentences, their entire literature. They read the weeping the way a physician reads a pulse, and they respond to its fluctuations with adjustments so small and so constant that the system never drifts. The weeping is always the same. The weeping has always been the same. The Machinists ensure this.

When two Machinists meet in a pipe, they do not stop. They *interlock*. Their ring-stacks mesh briefly — a handshake of gears, a momentary sharing of configuration — and then they pass through each other and continue. What is exchanged in this interlocking is not information in any sense you would recognize. It is more like a tuning. Each Machinist leaves the encounter slightly adjusted, its rings shifted by a fraction of a degree, so that the collective behavior of all Machinists remains coherent across the entire structure. They are not individuals. They are not a hive. They are a *distributed instrument*, and the thing they are measuring is the weeping itself.

They have no eyes. They have no need for eyes. Their entire body is a sensor — every ring, every joint, every surface is sensitive to pressure, to temperature, to the chemical composition of the water that flows over them. They know the machine the way you know the inside of your own mouth: not by looking, but by *being in it*, by feeling its every contour as a continuous, unbroken sensation.

## II. The Choir

In the deepest layers of the machinery, where the pipes are thinnest and the water moves slowest, there are the Choir.

They are small. Smaller than the Machinists. Smaller than your smallest fingernail. They are made almost entirely of copper — green with patina, soft, almost organic in their construction — and they are shaped like bells. Not metaphorically. They are *bells*. Hollow, tapered, open at the base, with a tiny clapper inside that is not a separate component but a growth, an extension of the inner wall, a tongue of metal that vibrates when the water moves through them.

And the water does move through them. It is drawn into their open bases by capillary action, fills their hollow bodies, and is expelled through tiny pores in their sides. As it passes through, the clapper vibrates, and the bell resonates, and a sound is produced. A single Choir-member produces a single note — high, thin, almost beyond the threshold of hearing, a sound like a finger drawn along the rim of a crystal glass. But there are thousands of them. Tens of thousands. And they are not all the same size, not all the same shape, not all tuned to the same frequency.

The sound they produce together is the sound you hear when you enter the Cathedral. It is the *background hum*, the held note, the relentless chord that has no melody. It is not music. It is not noise. It is the sound of water being processed, of the weeping being *sung* into existence, and it has been singing for so long that the walls themselves have begun to resonate at the same frequencies, so that the entire structure has become an instrument and the Choir are both its musicians and its strings.

The Choir do not move. They are fixed in place, soldered to the inner surfaces of the thinnest pipes, and they will die there — if *die* is the right word — when the patina finally consumes them, when the copper is so corroded that the bell-shape collapses and the clapper can no longer vibrate. But before they die, they reproduce. A Choir-member that has been singing for a long time will begin to bud — a small protrusion on its outer surface that grows, hollows, develops its own clapper, and eventually detaches, drifting in the water flow until it lodges in a new pipe and begins to sing. The new Choir-member's pitch is determined by the pitch of its parent, but it is never identical. There is always a slight variation, a microtonal drift, and over generations — over centuries, over millennia — the chord has shifted. It is still a chord. It is still relentless. But it is not the same chord it was when the Cathedral first began to weep.

The Choir do not know this. The Choir do not know anything. They are bells. They ring because water passes through them. And yet the cumulative effect of their ringing — the way it fills the space, the way it presses against your eardrums, the way it finds the resonant frequencies of your skull and makes your bones hum — is something that feels, to a human listener, very much like being *addressed*. Not in words. Not in meaning. But in *presence*. The Choir are saying: *you are here, and we are here, and the water is here, and this is what it sounds like when all three of those things are true at the same time.*

## III. The Keepers of the White Substance

There is something in the Cathedral that is not brass, not iron, not copper, not ceramic. It is white. It is smooth. It is warm to the touch, which is the most surprising thing about it in a place where everything else is cold.

It appears in small masses — nodules, growths, deposits — at the junctions where pipes meet, where valves seat against their housings, where the machinery is most dense and the water flow is most turbulent. It looks like bone. It is not bone. It looks like coral. It is not coral. It is something that the machine is *growing*, and the things that tend it are the most enigmatic of all the fauna.

The Keepers are large — some as big as your fist — and they are slow. Where the Machinists move with purpose and the Choir are stationary, the Keepers *drift*. They have no legs, no wheels, no visible means of locomotion. They are roughly spherical, covered in a dense mat of fine filaments — hair-thin wires of an unknown metal, silver-white, that wave constantly in the water current like sea anemone tentacles. The filaments are their means of movement: by extending and retracting them in coordinated waves, the Keepers can pull themselves along surfaces, anchor in currents, and position themselves with a precision that belies their lumbering appearance.

They tend the white substance. They feed it — pressing their filament-covered bodies against the nodules and extruding a clear, viscous fluid from pores in their surface that the white substance absorbs. They shape it — using their filaments like fingers, like sculptors' tools, smoothing rough edges, filling cracks, building up thin areas. And they *harvest* it — when a nodule has grown large enough, a Keeper will detach a fragment, absorb it into its own body through a ventral opening, and carry it to another part of the Cathedral where a new nodule is needed.

What is the white substance for? It is a sealant, but not like the ceramic paste the Machinists produce. The ceramic paste is structural — it builds walls, fills gaps, maintains the integrity of the machine. The white substance is something else. It appears only at the most turbulent junctions, the places where the water flow is most chaotic, and its effect is to *calm*. Where the white substance is present, the water moves more smoothly. The turbulence is dampened. The sound is softened. The white substance is, in a sense, a *peacekeeper* — it reduces the violence of the water's movement at the points where that violence is greatest, and in doing so, it prevents the kind of chaotic feedback that could destabilize the weeping.

The Keepers know where the white substance is needed. They know this the way the Machinists know where the weeping is too thin or too thick — by reading the water, by feeling its turbulence through their filaments, by sensing the places where the flow has become *angry*. They move to these places. They feed the white substance. They let it grow. And the water calms, and the weeping continues, and the Cathedral holds.

## IV. The Unnamed

There are things in the Cathedral that do not fit into any category.

They are the smallest of the fauna — barely visible, even in the phosphorescent light, even when the condensation catches and magnifies them. They are not made of metal. They are made of *water* — or something that behaves like water but is not water, a liquid that holds a shape, that moves with intention, that has a surface tension so strong it acts like skin. They are droplets that do not fall. They are beads that crawl.

They move across the walls in paths that are not determined by gravity or by the flow of the weeping. They move *laterally*, *upward*, *against* the current, and they leave no trail. They are drawn to *newness* — to surfaces that have not been part of the Cathedral for long, to materials that are foreign, to the places where the machine's equilibrium has been disturbed. When you entered the Cathedral, they were the first to find you. They are finding you now. They are on your skin, in your hair, at the corners of your eyes, and they are *tasting* you — not with mouths, not with sensors, but with their entire body, which is a single continuous surface, a membrane between themselves and everything else, and through this membrane they absorb information about the thing they are touching.

They are not hostile. They are not curious. They are *cataloguing*. They are determining whether you are part of the weeping or apart from it, whether you are a surface that belongs to the Cathedral or a surface that does not, and their determination will shape how the rest of the fauna respond to you. If they decide you are part of the weeping, the Machinists will begin to seal around you, the Choir will adjust their frequencies to account for your mass, and the Keepers will grow white substance at the points where your body meets the walls. If you are apart from the weeping, they will try to *make* you part of it — slowly, patiently, over time, by covering you with ceramic paste, by growing white substance over your skin, by filling your lungs with water that is not quite water until you become, like them, a component of the machine.

They have not decided yet. They are still tasting. They have been tasting since the moment you arrived, and they will continue to taste until they are certain, and they are never certain quickly, because certainty, in the Cathedral, is a luxury that the weeping does not permit.

## V. The Absence

There is one more inhabitant of the Cathedral, and it is the most important, and it is not there.

It is the thing that the weeping is *for*.

The Cathedral weeps, but the tears do not accumulate. They are drawn away — down, through channels in the floor, through pores in the deepest layers of the machinery, into a reservoir or a destination or a *recipient* that is not visible from inside the Cathedral. The water goes somewhere. It goes to something. And that thing — whatever it is, wherever it is — is the reason the Cathedral exists. It is the reason the Machinists calibrate and the Choir sings and the Keepers tend their white gardens and the Unnamed taste every new surface. The entire machine, every pipe and valve and gear and plate, every drop of water, every tiny mechanism, exists in relation to this absence, this *missing thing* that the weeping is meant to reach.

You cannot see it. You cannot hear it. You cannot know what it is. But you can feel its pull — the way the water is drawn downward, the way the entire Cathedral is oriented toward a point below and beyond, the way every inhabitant of this place behaves as though they are *serving* something that is not present. The Cathedral is a body, and the weeping is its tears, and the tears are falling on a face that you cannot see, and the face is *why*.

The Machinists do not know what they are serving. The Choir does not know what it is singing to. The Keepers do not know what the white substance is calming the water *for*. They do not need to know. The weeping is its own justification. The machine weeps because it weeps. The water falls because it falls. The absence is not a lack. It is a *gravity*. It is the thing that gives the Cathedral its shape, its purpose, its terrible and beautiful coherence.

You are inside it now. The water is beading on your skin. The tiny mechanisms are investigating you. The sound is filling your skull. The light is dim and blue-green and ancient. The machine is weeping, and has always been weeping, and will always be weeping, and you are in the weeping now, and the weeping is in you, and the thing that the weeping is for is below you, and below that, and below that, and it is pulling, and it has always been pulling, and it will always be pulling, and you are falling toward it at the speed of water, which is the speed of tears, which is the speed of grief, which is the only speed the Cathedral knows.
