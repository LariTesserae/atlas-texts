The Korami are not the only ones who live here.

There is something in the stone itself — not the Korami, not the moss-beards, not the insects struggling through the thick air. Something that was here before the Korami arrived, and will be here after they are gone, and does not know the difference.

It lives in the transitional zone — that middle layer where mineral and organic blur together, where the rock is neither fully stone nor fully alive but something in between. The Korami know it is there. They have a word for it that translates, roughly, as "the patience," or "the waiting," or "the thing that is still deciding." They do not worship it. They do not fear it. They simply build around it, the way you build around a tree that is too large to move.

It is not conscious, not in any way you would recognize. It does not think. It does not plan. But it *responds*. When the Korami carve into the soft stone, it shifts — slowly, over weeks, over months — filling in the spaces they have opened, not aggressively, not defensively, but the way a wound closes. The Korami have learned to work with this. They carve their chambers and then wait, watching the walls, and when the stone begins to close, they carve again. It is a conversation, of sorts. The Korami speak with their bone blades, and the stone answers with its slow, mineral patience.

You can feel it if you press your hand against the wall in the right place — not the warmth of the semi-cured stone, but something beneath that, something deeper and slower, a vibration so low it is almost not a vibration at all but a *tendency*, a direction, a pull. It is the feeling of something growing, but growing so slowly that a human lifetime is not enough to perceive the change. The Korami can feel it. They have lived with it for so long that it is part of their bodies, part of the way they move through their city with that quiet, unhurried purpose. They are not slow because they are primitive. They are slow because they are listening to the stone, and the stone takes a long time to say anything.

There are others, too — smaller things, stranger things. In the deepest chambers, where the weeping is heaviest and the light never reaches, there are colonies of something that is not quite mold and not quite lichen and not quite animal. They grow in flat, overlapping sheets on the chamber walls, and they are warm — warmer than the stone, warmer than the air — and they pulse. Not visibly. You cannot see it. But if you place your palm against them, you can feel it: a slow, rhythmic expansion and contraction, like breathing, like a heartbeat, but so slow that each pulse takes minutes. The Korami use these chambers for healing. They bring their injured and their sick and lay them against the warm, pulsing walls, and the sick recover. The Korami do not know why. They do not need to know why. The cliff provides.

And in the gardens, at the very top, where the wind moves across the open terraces and the blue lichen grows in vivid, electric patches, there is something that watches. Not the Korami. Not the insects. Something in the lichen itself, or something that lives *as* the lichen, or something that the lichen is the visible expression of — a vast, slow, distributed awareness that extends across the entire upper face of the cliff, threaded through the moss-beards and the flowering plants and the weeping stone, connected by filaments too fine to see, too fine to feel, but present, always present, a network of something that is not quite thought and not quite life but occupies the space between.

It does not watch you the way a person watches you. It does not have eyes. It does not have attention, not in the focused, directed sense. But it *registers* you. Your warmth. Your weight on the stone. The carbon dioxide of your breath. The moisture of your skin. You are a new variable in its environment, and it adjusts — not quickly, not dramatically, but in the way that a garden adjusts to a new plant, making room, shifting resources, incorporating the foreign thing into its slow, patient, ongoing process of becoming.

The Korami know about this, too. They call it "the garden's mind," and they tend the gardens not just for food and dye and medicine, but because the gardens are the place where the cliff is most awake. When they stand in the wind with their eyes closed and their faces upturned, they are not just listening to the wind. They are listening to the cliff thinking its slow, wet, mineral thoughts, and they are part of those thoughts, and the thoughts are part of them, and the distinction between the Korami and the stone they live in is not as clear as you might think.

This is what lives in Veth-Koram: the Korami, yes, small and quiet and deliberate, with their large dark eyes and their bone blades and their moss-fiber clothing. But also the stone itself, unfinished and patient, closing the spaces the Korami open, opening new spaces of its own. The warm, pulsing things in the deep chambers, healing what is broken. The vast, slow awareness in the gardens, registering everything, adjusting to everything, thinking thoughts that take centuries to complete. The water, always the water, weeping from every surface, connecting everything, carrying mineral and heat and information through the porous stone in a continuous, percussive flow that is the closest thing this place has to a heartbeat.

And the moss-beards, hanging thirty meters from every ledge, swaying in the updrafts, half plant and half fungus and half something else entirely — three halves, an impossibility, a thing that does not add up, a thing that should not exist but does, thriving in the wet, directionless light, feeding the Korami with their fibers, feeding the gardens with their spores, feeding the stone with their slow, patient roots that pry into the semi-cured mineral and pull it apart and rebuild it, molecule by molecule, into something new.

The Korami are not the inhabitants of Veth-Koram. They are one of its organs. The stone is not the structure of Veth-Koram. It is one of its tissues. The water is not the environment of Veth-Koram. It is its blood. And the thing that lives here — the real thing, the deep thing, the thing that was here first and will be here last — is not any of these individually but all of them together, a single vast organism that is part city, part cliff, part garden, part creature, still becoming, still deciding, still unfinished, still patient, still waiting for whatever it is waiting for with the absolute, mineral, water-heavy patience of something that has all the time in the world and knows it.
