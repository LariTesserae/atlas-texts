# What Lives in the Grotto of Still Warmth

---

It is not large. That is the first thing to understand about it. Whatever lives here is not large, because there is nothing here that needs size. No predator. No prey. No canopy to compete for, no territory to defend across distance. Size, in the Grotto, would be an extravagance, and extravagance is not what this place allows.

It is near the wall — the far wall, the one that is darkest, where the light from the passage has forgotten to reach. If you looked for it, which you would have to do deliberately, because it has made itself extraordinarily easy not to see, you would find it in the mineral ridges near the second shelf up. It lives *in* the stone rather than *on* it — in the narrow gaps where the flowstone has built up against itself and left a space behind, a pocket the size of a closed fist, filled with warm water and the particular quality of stillness that comes from being enclosed on all sides by something that has not moved in ten thousand years.

It is difficult to describe what it is because it does not resemble anything you have a word for. It is not an animal, or not exactly. It is not a plant. It is not a fungus, though it shares with fungi the quality of *secrecy* — of having built a life entirely out of what the immediate environment provides, without need for anything from outside. The closest word might be *residence* itself. It lives the way a phenomenon lives. It lives the way a warm spot lives in a room.

It has a body, of sorts. Something soft and membranous, pale to the point of translucence, like the creature you saw crossing the floor but more so — so thin that light passes through it the way it passes through water, which is to say it bends slightly and continues on, and the thing itself is just the place where the bending happens. It is not segmented. It has no shell. It is a continuous surface, like a film of oil, like the skin of a bubble, and it claps gently against the walls of its pocket the way a sail claps when the wind is barely strong enough to move it — not from any current in the water, but from its own respiration, if that is what it is doing. It may be breathing. It may be filtering. It may be performing some function for which there is no human analogy, some slow metabolic exchange between itself and the warm mineral water that has been passing, molecule by molecule, through the limestone for longer than the species has existed.

It eats, in the sense that it takes something from the water and returns something to the water. The ratio is so nearly balanced that the water is unchanged on either side of it. If you had instruments precise enough, you might detect a slight reduction in dissolved calcium where the water touches its membrane. That is all. It takes the barest trace of stone from the water, and what it does with it, no one knows. Perhaps it is building itself, so slowly that the growth of the formations around it is a frenzy by comparison. Perhaps it is doing something else. The Grotto has not made its processes transparent.

It does not move, or does not move in any way you would notice. In the time you spend in the chamber — five minutes, an hour, a day — it will not shift position. It will not extend a limb or turn a head or blink an eye, because it has none of these things. It simply *is*, in its pocket, in its water, in its warmth, and the fact of its presence is as continuous and unremarkable as the fact of the water itself. You could stand in the Grotto for a long time and never know it was there. This is not because it is hiding. It is because there is nothing about it that insists on being seen.

But it is aware of you. Not in the way you are aware of a person entering a room — not with recognition, not with alarm, not with curiosity, because these are responses to change, and the Grotto has taught its inhabitant that change does not come here. It is aware of you the way the water is aware of you: as a warmth, a displacement, a brief disturbance in the equilibrium. You are warm, and you are breathing, and you are pushing the air around in ways the air is not used to being pushed, and your footsteps are sending pressure waves through the thin sheet of water that the whole floor shares, and some of those pressure waves reach the pocket where it lives, and they arrive as information — *something is here, something large, something that was not here before* — and the creature receives this information the way a thermostat receives a change in temperature: not as news, but as a signal, a small deviation from the set point, something to be noted and then endured.

It does not care that you are here. It does not *not* care. These are not responses available to it. You are simply a temporary increase in the room's metabolic load, and the room will return to equilibrium when you leave, as it has returned to equilibrium every other time something has entered it, whether that something was a draft of air from a newly opened passage a thousand years ago, or the body of a bat that fell from the ceiling and dissolved slowly into the water, feeding a brief, localized bloom of bacteria that lasted a month and then was reabsorbed into the stone, or the slow drip of calcium from a new stalactite that changed the mineral composition of one corner of the floor for three centuries before evening out. The Grotto corrects. It always corrects. It is the most patient correction you have ever been inside of.

The creature has been here for a very long time.

Not this creature, perhaps. Not this specific membrane in this specific pocket. But the *kind* of creature, the *mode* of creature, the way-of-being that this particular expression represents — it has been here since before the bowl. Since before the staircase of shelves, which means since before the water found its current level, which means since before the passage you descended through was a passage and not a solid vein of limestone with water moving through it under pressure. It has been here in some form, doing some version of what it is doing now, for a span of time that is not geological but is adjacent to geology, that brushes up against the scale of rocks and minerals and finds it comfortable.

You could say it is the Grotto's way of being alive. Not the Grotto's inhabitant — its *life*. The thing that happens here, that has always happened here, in the warm water and the still air and the mineral saturation, when conditions are exactly this stable for exactly this long. It is what the equilibrium *feels like* from the inside. If the Grotto is a held note, this creature is the vibration that keeps the note from stopping — not the sound itself, but the resonance that persists in the air after the bow has left the string, the thing that fills the silence between the droplet's fall and the next droplet's fall, the living warmth in the walls that is not the warmth of geology but the warmth of something that has decided, for now, to remain warm.

It will not interact with you. It has never interacted with anything. Interaction implies exchange, and exchange implies change, and the creature has spent its entire existence — its entire species' existence, its entire way-of-being's existence — avoiding change with the same thoroughness the Grotto avoids it, which is to say completely, which is to say perfectly, which is to say with the kind of success that makes the thing it's succeeding at invisible.

But.

If you were to stay. If you were to stay long enough that the Grotto began to recognize you not as a disturbance but as a feature — the way it recognizes the bowl, the way it recognizes the shelves, the way it recognizes the pale spider-shrimp that crosses the floor and will cross the floor again when conditions are right. If you were to stand in the warm water and breathe the dense mineral air and make your peace with the silence, and do this not once but many times, at intervals the Grotto could metabolize, over a period that would feel like no time at all to the creature and like your entire life to you — if you were to do this, then one day you might notice something.

The water in the bowl would be warmer.

Not much. Not measurably. But you would feel it, the way you feel a room change when someone enters it behind you — not with your senses, but with some older faculty, some capacity for detecting the presence of life that you have forgotten you possess because you have not used it since you were very small and the world was very large and full of places that held stillness the way the Grotto holds stillness.

The bowl would be warmer because the creature — not moving, never moving, but *attending* in whatever way it attends, in whatever direction it directs whatever attention is available to it — would have found in you something worth being warm for.

Not you, exactly. Not your personality or your face or your story. But your *stillness*. Your willingness to stand in warm water in a dim room and let the silence press against your eardrums and not fill it with words or movement or the desperate human need to *do something*. Your willingness to be, for once, as still as the bowl, as patient as the mineral shelves, as warm and quiet and self-contained as the water that holds them all.

The creature would not know your name. It would not know you were there, in any way you would recognize as knowing. But it would do the only thing it has ever done, the thing it was born to do, the thing it is —

It would make the room warm enough to hold you.

And you would stay.
