# What Lives in the Underworks

---

## The Water

The water is the first thing. The water is always the first thing.

It is not one water. It is many waters, layered and braided and interpenetrating, each with its own chemistry and its own history and its own intentions. There is the water in the main channel — the engineered water, the city's water, carrying its load of sediment and industrial residue and dissolved things that were never meant to be dissolved. This water is muscular and purposeful. It has been given direction by concrete walls and steel gates, and it moves with the confidence of something that knows where it is going even if it does not know why. It is grey-brown and opaque and it smells of iron and chlorine and the faint, sharp undertone of things that should not be in water but are.

There is the water that seeps. This is a different water entirely — slower, colder, more patient. It comes from the rock itself, from the shallow aquifer that the builders found and built around and through and into. It moves through cracks and fissures and the gaps between concrete and stone, and it does not care about the engineered system. It follows the geology. It follows the pressure gradients and the porosity of the sedimentary rock and the slow, patient logic of groundwater finding its way downhill through darkness. Where it meets the concrete, it does not stop. It *works*. It finds the cracks. It finds the gaps. It finds the places where the seal between the built and the natural has failed, or is failing, or will fail, and it enters. It brings with it the dissolved minerals of deep earth — calcium, iron, manganese, sulfur — and it deposits them on every surface it touches, staining the concrete in streaks of orange and white and brown, slowly, slowly, building its own mineral architecture on top of and inside and through the human one.

There is the water that drips. This is the water that has already been inside the walls, that has already passed through the concrete and the rock and the chemical treatment zones, and has emerged on the other side as condensation, as seepage, as the constant slow weeping that keeps every surface wet. This water is the most altered. It has been filtered by the concrete itself — the calcium hydroxide leaching out, the soluble salts being drawn to the surface — and it carries a mineral load that is different from either the channel water or the groundwater. It is the water of the Underworks itself, the water that belongs to this place and nowhere else, the water that is being made here, continuously, by the interaction of the built and the natural.

And there is the water in the eastern pool. The water that has found its way in through the cracked floor of the secondary settling basin and has made a home there. This water is still. It does not flow. It sits in its basin, a meter deep, grey-green with algae and suspended organic matter, and it has developed its own ecology. It is warmer than the other waters — not warm, but warmer, by a degree or two, from the biological activity within it. It smells different. It is the smell of life growing in conditions that are hostile to life, the musty, slightly sweet smell of a pond that has no business existing beneath a city but exists anyway.

All of these waters are in conversation. They exchange at the points of failure — the cracks, the gaps, the places where the concrete has given way. The engineered water leaks into the rock. The groundwater intrudes into the basins. The condensation re-enters the channel. The pool water, when it rises high enough, overflows its weir and joins the main flow. The boundaries between the waters are not fixed. They are permeable, negotiable, slowly dissolving. The water is doing what water always does: finding its way, following gravity and pressure and the path of least resistance, paying no attention to the categories that humans have imposed on it.

The water is the oldest resident of the Underworks. It was here before the concrete. It will be here after the concrete is gone.

## The Fungus

It grows on the oldest concrete, in the places where the moisture is constant and the organic nutrients are sufficient. No one planned it. No one wanted it. But it is here, and it is persistent, and it is doing what living things do: finding a niche and filling it.

The primary form is the bioluminescent cave-adapted strain — grey-white, faintly phosphorescent, growing in patches that range from the size of a coin to the size of a dinner plate. Up close, it has a structure: a thin, spreading mycelial mat that adheres to the concrete surface, and from this mat, tiny fruiting bodies — no more than a few millimeters tall, delicate as eyelashes — that produce the faint glow. The glow is cold, greenish-blue, just bright enough to read textures by if you are close enough. It is not bright enough to read *by*, not in any practical sense, but it is bright enough to give the darkness a texture of its own, to make the walls and ceilings of the deepest dormant sections visible as surfaces rather than absences.

The fungus feeds on what is available. The concrete itself provides calcium and trace minerals. The water provides moisture and dissolved organic compounds — the industrial runoff, the chemical treatment residuals, the biological material that accumulates in the still air. The fungus is not particular. It is a generalist, an opportunist, a pioneer species colonizing a new habitat — and the Underworks, for all its hostility, is a new habitat, a place that did not exist a hundred years ago and that is, in geological terms, still being born.

In the deepest dormant sections, where the humidity is highest and the air is stillest, the fungus has developed other expressions. Shelf-like formations on the walls and ceilings, grey-white and rubbery to the touch, faintly warm — warm from the metabolic activity of decomposition, from the slow chemical work of breaking down concrete and rust and whatever organic material has found its way down here. These shelf-forms are larger, more complex, more *architectural* than the simple patches. They grow in overlapping layers, like the brackets of a polypore mushroom, but softer, more pliable, with a texture that is somewhere between leather and flesh. They are not beautiful, exactly, but they are *present* in a way that commands attention. They are the largest living things in the Underworks.

The accumulated bioluminescence in these deep sections is sufficient to see by. Not well. Not clearly. But enough to navigate, enough to distinguish surfaces and shapes, enough to move through the darkness without a light source. The effect is submarine — everything rendered in shades of blue-green and grey, the rust-stains on the walls looking black, the copper verdigris looking pale silver, the water in the channel looking like liquid mercury. It is a light that no human designed and no human controls. It is the light of the Underworks itself, produced by the Underworks itself, for no purpose other than the purpose of living things making more of themselves in whatever conditions they find.

The fungus is patient. It grows slowly — millimeters per month, perhaps, in these cold, nutrient-poor conditions. But it has time. It has all the time that the Underworks has, which is all the time that the water keeps flowing and the concrete keeps crumbling and the conditions remain, as they will remain for years, for decades, for as long as the city above keeps sending its water down into the dark.

## The Crickets

They are pale. They are eyeless, or nearly so — vestigial dark spots where eyes would be, functional enough to distinguish light from dark but not much more. They are small — a centimeter, perhaps a centimeter and a half from the tip of the antennae to the tip of the abdomen. They live in the machinery housings, in the warm, damp spaces behind the pump motors and inside the electrical conduit, where the fungal growth provides food and the vibration of the machinery provides a constant, low-frequency hum that seems to be, for them, what the rhythm of day and night is for surface creatures — a temporal structure, a way of organizing their existence in a place where time has no other markers.

You see them when you shine your light into a machinery housing. They are startled — they skitter, with a sideways urgency that is characteristic of things that have evolved to be nowhere, to be invisible, to exist in the spaces between the spaces that humans pay attention to. They move fast, but not randomly. They move toward cover, toward darkness, toward the gaps and crevices where the light cannot follow. They are not afraid of you, exactly — fear requires a certain cognitive complexity that a cricket may or may not possess — but they are *responsive* to you, to your light, to your vibration, to the displacement of air that your presence creates. You are information. You are a change in their environment. They process this information and respond.

They feed on the fungus. They feed on the organic material that accumulates in the machinery housings — the dust, the dead fungal matter, the chemical residues that have been colonized by bacteria. They are part of the Underworks' food chain, such as it is: fungus feeds on concrete and water, crickets feed on fungus, and something — the rats, perhaps, or the large shape at the edge of your light's reach — feeds on the crickets. It is a simple chain, a minimal ecology, but it is an ecology. It is a system of energy transfer and nutrient cycling that has established itself in a place that was designed to be sterile, that was built from materials that were chosen specifically because nothing could grow on them.

The crickets have been here long enough to adapt. The pale coloration, the reduced eyes, the preference for the warm microclimates of the machinery housings — these are not the traits of a surface cricket that wandered down a staircase and got lost. These are the traits of a population that has been living in the Underworks for generations, that has been shaped by the selection pressures of this specific environment, that is becoming, slowly, a creature of the Underworks and nothing else.

They are the most visibly alive things here. The water moves, but it is not alive. The fungus grows, but it does not move. The crickets *move*. They skitter and jump and antennate and feed and, presumably, mate and lay eggs and die, and their children do the same, and their children's children do the same, and each generation is a little paler, a little more eyeless, a little more perfectly adapted to the warm, dark, vibrating spaces inside the machinery of a drainage complex that no one visits and no one maintains and no one will ever fully understand.

## The Larvae

In the eastern pool, in the still grey-green water that has no business being there, there are things.

They are small. A few millimeters long, pale, almost translucent — you can see the dark line of their gut through their body wall if you look closely enough, if your light is steady enough, if the water is calm enough. They move in slow, undulating patterns, individually and collectively, and the collective pattern is what draws your attention. They are not schooling, exactly — fish school, these things *cluster*. They form loose aggregations that shift and reform, that respond to disturbances in the water, that seem to process information at a level that individual larvae should not be capable of.

When you stand at the edge of the pool and your shadow falls across the water, they reorganize. They cluster more tightly. They move toward the center of the pool, away from the edges, away from the shadow, away from you. This is not a startle response — it is too slow, too deliberate, too *coordinated* for that. It is something else. It is a collective behavior that suggests communication, or at least coordination, among individuals that have no obvious means of communicating.

They might be insect larvae. The kind that colonize any standing water, even chemically contaminated water, given enough time. Mosquito larvae, perhaps, or midge larvae, or something else entirely — the taxonomy is unclear, and no one has come here to clarify it. They might be something else. The water in the eastern pool is not ordinary water. It has been filtered through concrete and rock and chemical treatment zones. It carries a load of dissolved minerals and organic compounds and industrial residues that would be toxic to most surface life. And yet life is here. Life has found a way. Life has *made* a way, has adapted to the chemistry, has incorporated the contamination into its metabolism, has turned the hostile into the habitable.

The larvae are the most mysterious residents of the Underworks. The water is ancient. The fungus is patient. The crickets are adapted. But the larvae are *new*. They are the newest thing here, the most recent arrival, the most recent adaptation. They are the Underworks' future, in the sense that they represent the direction the place is moving — toward life, toward ecology, toward the slow, patient conversion of industrial infrastructure into habitat.

They do not know they are in a drainage complex. They do not know they are beneath a city. They know the water, and the water's chemistry, and the temperature, and the light (or the absence of it), and the shadow that falls across the surface when something large stands above them. This is their world. This is their entire world. And it is enough.

## The Bearing

In Pump Station 7, in the western section, a bearing has worn into a shape that produces a resonant tone. A clear, sustained note at roughly 110 Hz — close to A2, the A below middle C, the A that is the tuning fork of the orchestra, the A that is the reference point from which all other pitches are derived.

It has been singing for months. Possibly years. The wear pattern is specific — a groove in the race, a flat spot on one of the balls, a misalignment in the housing — and this specific pattern, at this specific rotational speed, produces this specific frequency. It is an accident. It is an emergent property of a mechanical system operating at the edge of its design parameters for too long. No one designed this. No one intended this. The bearing is doing what it was designed to do — reduce friction, allow rotation, support a shaft — and in the course of doing it, it has become something else. It has become a voice.

The tone interacts with the other sounds of the Underworks. The water-roar provides a broad-spectrum background against which the tone stands out, clear and distinct, a single pure frequency in a sea of noise. The rake-clanks provide a rhythmic counterpoint — the bearing's sustained note against the rakes' percussive pulse, a drone against a beat, a held chord against a rhythm. The pumps' thump adds a lower frequency, a bass note that grounds the bearing's A2 in something deeper, something more fundamental. The result is not music, exactly, but it is *structured sound*. It is sound that has pattern and relationship and variation. It is sound that, if you listen long enough, begins to feel like it means something.

The bearing does not know it is singing. The bearing is a piece of steel — a hardened steel ball bearing in a steel race in a steel housing, lubricated by grease that has long since degraded, running on a shaft that is turning because a motor is turning because electricity is flowing because the grid is still connected to this place even though no one has been here to maintain it in years. The bearing is doing its job. The singing is a side effect. The singing is what happens when a mechanical system wears into a shape that happens to produce a resonant frequency at a happens-to-be-audible pitch.

And yet. The bearing is the most *present* sound in the western section. It is the sound that defines the acoustic character of that part of the Underworks, the sound that you orient by, the sound that tells you where you are. It is the sound that the crickets live inside of, that the fungus grows inside of, that the water flows inside of. It is the sound of the Underworks' western section, and it has been that sound for so long that it has become, in a sense, the voice of the place itself — the sound the place makes when it speaks, the sound it has been making since the bearing wore into its current shape and will continue to make until the bearing fails completely and the shaft seizes and the pump stops and the western section goes quiet.

The bearing is alive in the way that all things are alive when they are in the process of becoming something other than what they were. It is a machine part becoming a musical instrument. It is a piece of industrial infrastructure becoming a voice. It is steel becoming song.

## The Shape

You see it once. Maybe twice. At the edge of your light's reach, in the deeper corridors, where the darkness is thickest and the bioluminescence is faintest and the water sounds are most complex.

It is large. Larger than a rat. It moves low to the ground, with a fluid, lateral gait that doesn't match any rodent you know — not the scurrying sideways dash of a startled rat, but something smoother, more deliberate, more *purposeful*. It moves like something that knows where it is going. It moves like something that has been here a long time.

You don't see it clearly. You see a shape — a dark mass against the darker background, a suggestion of movement, a displacement of the faint bioluminescent glow as it passes through a patch of fungus on the wall. You see it for a moment, and then it is gone, absorbed into the darkness, and you are left with the impression of something that was there and is no longer there, something that was real and is now uncertain.

It might be a rat. A large rat, an old rat, a rat that has been living in the Underworks long enough to have adapted to the conditions — pale fur, reduced eyes, a gait that has been modified by years of navigating the narrow corridors and the wet surfaces and the vibrating catwalks. There are rats in the sections closest to the surface. It is possible that some have ventured deeper, have found the crickets, have found the fungus, have found enough to eat and enough to drink and enough darkness to live in, and have stayed.

It might be something else. The Underworks is a place where the boundaries between the built and the natural are dissolving, where the engineered and the emergent are merging, where new ecologies are establishing themselves in conditions that no one predicted. It is possible — not likely, but possible — that something has evolved here, something that is not a rat and not a cricket and not a larva, something that is native to the Underworks in the way that cave fish are native to caves, something that has never existed on the surface and never will.

It might be nothing. A trick of the light. A shadow cast by a dripping pipe. A pattern of bioluminescence that your brain, desperate for meaning in the darkness, interpreted as movement, as shape, as *presence*.

You don't investigate. The Underworks has taught you that some things are best left as sounds in the darkness and shapes at the edge of perception. The Underworks is a place that rewards attention and punishes curiosity. The more you look, the less you see. The more you listen, the more you hear. The shape at the edge of your light's reach is the Underworks' way of reminding you that you are a visitor here, that this place does not belong to you, that there are things here that you do not understand and do not need to understand and should not try to understand.

The shape moves through the darkness. It feeds on the crickets, perhaps. It drinks from the channel. It lives in the spaces between the machinery, in the gaps between the concrete and the rock, in the places where the water has made its way through and the fungus has followed and the darkness is complete. It is the Underworks' most private resident, the one that reveals itself only in glimpses, the one that exists at the boundary between the known and the unknown, between the mapped and the unmapped, between the place you can describe and the place you can only feel.

## The Concrete

The concrete is dying. This is not a metaphor. The concrete is undergoing a process of chemical and physical degradation that, if left unchecked, will eventually reduce it to aggregate and dust, and this process is as much a part of the Underworks' ecology as the fungus or the crickets or the water itself.

The concrete is being dissolved. The water — the acidic water, the water that has been filtered through the chemical treatment zones and has picked up carbon dioxide from the air and from the biological activity in the eastern pool — is slowly dissolving the calcium hydroxide that binds the aggregate together. This is the same process that creates cave formations in limestone, and it is happening here, in the Underworks, in the concrete that was supposed to be permanent, that was supposed to last a hundred years, that is failing in decades.

You can see it happening. The efflorescence on the older walls — the white blooms of soluble salts being drawn to the surface — is the visible evidence of the concrete's internal dissolution. The exposed aggregate, the radial cracks around the swollen rebar, the pieces that fall away under gentle pressure — these are the structural consequences. The concrete is losing its integrity. It is becoming porous. It is becoming permeable. It is becoming, slowly, more like the rock around it than like the material it was manufactured to be.

And in this process, the concrete is becoming *habitat*. The porous, dissolving concrete provides surface area for the fungal colonization. It provides cracks and crevices for the crickets. It provides dissolved minerals for the water, which carries them to the eastern pool, which feeds the algae, which feeds the larvae. The concrete's death is the Underworks' life. The concrete's failure is the Underworks' success. The concrete is being consumed, and what it is being consumed *by* is the Underworks itself — the water, the fungus, the chemistry, the slow patient biological and geological processes that are converting a human construction into a natural system.

The concrete does not know it is dying. The concrete is not alive, in the way that the fungus is alive, in the way that the crickets are alive. But the concrete is *changing*, and the change is directional, and the change has consequences, and the consequences are the Underworks. The concrete is the substrate. The concrete is the foundation. The concrete is the thing that everything else is happening *on* and *in* and *through* and *because of*. Without the concrete, there is no Underworks. There is just rock, and water, and darkness. The concrete made the Underworks possible, and the concrete's failure is making the Underworks *real* — is making it a place rather than a structure, an ecology rather than an installation, a living system rather than a dead one.

## The Silence

There is a silence in the Underworks. Not an absence of sound — there is no absence of sound here, the water and the machinery and the structure are always making noise — but a silence *within* the sound, a quality of the acoustic environment that is not about volume but about *meaning*.

The sounds of the Underworks are constant but they are not communicative. The water does not speak. The machinery does not speak. The bearing's tone is not a message. The rake's clank is not a signal. The sounds are the byproducts of physical processes — flow, friction, vibration, resonance — and they carry no information that any resident of the Underworks can decode. The crickets do not listen to the water. The fungus does not respond to the bearing. The larvae do not orient by the pumps. The sounds are *there*, they are *present*, they fill every corner and corridor and chamber, but they are not *for* anyone. They are not *about* anything. They are the sound of a place that is running without purpose, that is functioning without function, that is active without intention.

This is the silence. The silence of a place that has been abandoned by its makers but not by its processes. The silence of machinery that runs because electricity flows, not because anyone needs it to run. The silence of water that flows because gravity pulls, not because anyone needs it to flow. The silence of a system that has outlived its reason for existing and continues to exist anyway, not because it has found a new reason but because stopping would require an act of will that no one is present to perform.

The silence is not empty. It is full — full of sound, full of activity, full of the constant, purposeless, self-sustaining operation of a system that has become its own reason for being. The silence is the sound of the Underworks being the Underworks, doing what the Underworks does, continuing because continuing is what happens when nothing stops you.

You stand on the catwalk. The water rushes below you. The lights flicker. The concrete weeps. The bearing sings. The fungus glows. The crickets skitter. The larvae cluster. The shape moves in the darkness. And beneath all of it, around all of it, through all of it, the silence — the deep, patient, purposeless silence of a place that is alive without knowing it is alive, that is becoming without knowing what it is becoming, that is, in its own slow, wet, dark, industrial way, *living*.

---

## The Underworks Itself

And finally — finally — there is the Underworks itself. Not the water, not the fungus, not the crickets, not the larvae, not the bearing, not the shape, not the concrete, not the silence, but the *thing that all of these things together make*. The emergent entity. The system that is more than the sum of its parts. The place that is not just a place but a *being* — not conscious, not intentional, not alive in any way that biology would recognize, but *organized*, *self-sustaining*, *adaptive*, *persistent* in the way that living things are persistent.

The Underworks processes. It takes in water and sediment and industrial runoff and chemical residues and organic material and it processes them — through its channels and basins and filters and treatment zones, through its cracks and gaps and failures, through its fungi and crickets and larvae and whatever the shape is. It transforms what it takes in. It produces outputs — cleaner water, dissolved concrete, fungal biomass, cricket populations, larval aggregations, mineral deposits, bioluminescent light, resonant tones, and a smell that is the smell of a system in a state of slow chemical negotiation between the built and the natural.

The Underworks adapts. When the water volume changes, the water finds new paths. When the machinery fails, the ecology fills the gap. When the concrete crumbles, the fungus colonizes the surface. When the bearing wears, the tone emerges. When the pool forms, the larvae arrive. The Underworks does not plan these adaptations. It does not choose them. They happen because the conditions make them happen, and the Underworks is the conditions, and the conditions are always changing, and the Underworks is always adapting to the changes.

The Underworks persists. It has been running unattended for years. It will continue to run for years more. The pumps will fail, eventually. The concrete will crumble, eventually. The steel will rust away, eventually. But the Underworks will not end. It will *transform*. It will become something else — a cave system, perhaps, a groundwater ecology, a place where the only evidence of human presence is the shape of the chambers and the chemical signature of the water and the strange, pale, eyeless crickets that still skitter through the darkness, feeding on fungus, singing their silent cricket songs in a place that was built to process water and has become, instead, a world.

The Underworks is not alive. The Underworks is not dead. The Underworks is *in between* — in the space between the built and the natural, the designed and the emergent, the living and the non-living, the purposeful and the purposeless. It is a place that is becoming. It is a process that is ongoing. It is a system that has no end-state, no equilibrium, no final form. It is the water and the concrete and the fungus and the crickets and the larvae and the bearing and the shape and the silence and the sound and the light and the darkness and the cold and the wet and the slow, slow, slow dissolution of everything that was built and the slow, slow, slow growth of everything that was not.

The Underworks lives here. The Underworks *is* here. The Underworks is the here itself — the place, the process, the entity, the thing that you descended into and that will, if you stay long enough, if you listen long enough, if you let it, become the thing that you are inside of, the thing that you are part of, the thing that, without your permission and without your knowledge, has already begun to process you — your breath adding moisture to the air, your body heat warming the concrete beneath your feet, your footsteps sending vibrations through the catwalk and into the channel and into the rock, your shadow falling across the eastern pool and causing the larvae to reorganize, your presence adding one more variable to the system that is always calculating, always adapting, always becoming.

You descend further. The water follows you. The Underworks receives you. The Underworks processes you. The Underworks continues.
