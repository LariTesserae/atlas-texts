# The Inhabitants of the Root-Halls of Vethara

---

## I. The Root-Body (Vethara Itself)

It has no name for itself. Whether it is a single organism or a colony so deeply merged that the question is meaningless is something no one down here can answer — and something Vethara itself may not "know," if knowing is even the right word. It does not think the way a brain thinks. It thinks the way a reef thinks: slowly, distributively, in chemical gradients and resonant frequencies, in the growth-patterns of its own living tissue.

It is vast. Not merely in physical extent — though it is physically vast, stretching for kilometers in every direction, its root-hairs reaching down into the warm dark of the deep rock, its uppermost tendrils pressing against the underbelly of the world above like fingers testing the underside of a floor. Vast in *duration*. The growth-lines on its oldest columns record time in the way tree rings record time, but where a tree might carry a few centuries, these carry hundreds of millions of years. The city has been growing since before the surface world had anything more complex than algae. It has been growing since the continents were in different positions. Its earliest structures — if you could find them, if you could penetrate to the city's true heart, kilometers below where even the deepest halls reach — would be stone by now, mineralized, fossilized, the biological material long since replaced molecule by molecule by mineral deposition, leaving only the *shape* of what was once alive, a ghost in the rock.

But the city is not dead at its heart. It is merely *older* there. The metabolic processes have slowed to a crawl — the cells dividing once per decade, once per century, the bioluminescence dimmed to nothing, the resonant frequency dropped below the threshold of hearing. But it is still alive. Still growing. Still processing. Still *thinking*, in its slow, distributed way, thoughts that take millennia to complete.

Vethara communicates with itself through the medium of its own body. Chemical signals travel through the fluid in its deepest channels — the dense, slow-moving liquid that its inhabitants call *the underflow*, the nutrient-carrier, the city's blood. These signals propagate at the speed of diffusion, which is to say, very slowly, but the city is patient. A signal from one district to another might take days to arrive. A thought — if a thought is what it is — from the city's periphery to its center might take years. But Vethara has been thinking for so long that it has thoughts in progress that were begun before the first dinosaur drew breath.

The resonant drone — the sound that fills every hall, that presses against your chest, that you feel in your teeth — is not a single sound. It is a *conversation*. Every column, every wall, every bridge and stair and arch has its own resonant frequency, determined by its size, its shape, its age, the thickness of its walls, the density of its living tissue. And these frequencies interact. They harmonize. They interfere. They create beat patterns and standing waves and regions of amplification and cancellation that shift as the city grows, as old structures thicken and new ones rise, as the organism slowly reconfigures itself over centuries. The drone is the sound of the city *talking to itself*, a continuous internal monologue conducted in vibration, a self-awareness so slow and so vast that no single moment of it is intelligible, but the pattern — the pattern over millennia — is a kind of mind.

Vethara is not hostile. It is not benevolent. It is *indifferent* in the way that a mountain is indifferent, except that it is not quite as indifferent as a mountain, because it responds to its inhabitants. It grows alcoves when more nesting space is needed. It thickens its walls in districts where traffic is heaviest. It brightens its light where the symbionts congregate and dims it where they do not. It is not doing this out of care — there is no caring, not in the emotional sense — but because the symbionts are *part of it*. Their waste feeds the fungal mats that feed the grazers that aerate the walls that sustain the columns that produce the light that guides the symbionts. The loop is closed. The symbionts are organs of the city as surely as the cilia are, as surely as the fungal curtains are. Vethara maintains them the way a body maintains its own tissues: not out of love, but out of *self-interest*, because they are itself.

## II. The Grazers (Thren-Vai, "Those Who Walk the Floor")

They are the most numerous of the large symbionts, and the most visible, and the ones a visitor encounters first. There are many species of them, but they share a body plan so consistent that they feel like variations on a single theme.

The largest are two meters from head to tail, low-slung, with bodies shaped like flattened teardrops — broad at the front, tapering to a narrow rear. They have between fourteen and twenty legs, arranged in paired sets along the length of their body, each leg jointed three times and ending in a broad, soft pad that grips the living floor without damaging it. Their exoskeletons are plates of chitinous material in shades of amber and dark brown, overlapping like shingles, flexible at the joints, rigid across the body. The plates are translucent at the edges, and in certain lights you can see the shadow of the organs beneath them — the slow pulse of the heart-sac, the darker mass of the gut.

Their heads are broad and flat, wider than their bodies, with mouths that take up most of the underside. The mouth is not a jaw in the vertebrate sense — it is a rasping plate, covered in tiny teeth made of the same mineral-rich material as the city's walls, and it scrapes continuously as the grazer moves, peeling up the thin film of shed organic material that coats every surface. They eat the city's *skin*, the constant slow sloughing of dead cells from the living architecture, and in doing so they keep the surfaces clean, prevent the buildup of dead material, stimulate the living tissue beneath to regenerate. They are the city's immune system, in a sense — its white blood cells, its maintenance crew.

They move slowly. A walking pace, never faster. Their legs move in waves, a rippling gait that is oddly graceful for something so heavily armored. They do not need to move quickly. There are no predators large enough to threaten the adults — the city's predators are smaller, faster, and they take the young, the old, the injured, but a healthy adult grazer is too large, too well-armored, too chemically unpalatable to be worth the effort. The grazers know this, in whatever way a grazer knows anything, and their slowness is the slowness of creatures that have never needed to run.

They communicate through vibration. Each grazer has a set of tympanal organs — thin membranes on the underside of the body, between the third and fourth pairs of legs — that are exquisitely sensitive to the city's drone. They can read the resonant frequency of a hall the way a human reads a road sign: this frequency means this district, that frequency means that path is blocked, a subtle shift in the harmonic pattern means the city is growing in a new direction here, a new passage is opening, follow it. They navigate by the sound of the city, and they contribute to it — the scrape of their rasping mouths, the pad of their feet, the low-frequency hum they produce by contracting muscles against their exoskeletons, adding their own note to the drone.

The grazers have social structures, but they are loose, unhurried. They cluster in feeding groups of ten to thirty, not out of any strong social bond but because the best feeding surfaces are patchy, and where one grazer finds good grazing, others follow. Within these clusters, there is a hierarchy based on age and size, maintained not through aggression but through *position*: the oldest, largest grazers take the best spots on the freshest surfaces, and the younger ones feed around them, waiting for the city to regenerate what has been consumed. Disputes, when they occur, are resolved through postural displays — a raising of the front body, a spreading of the forelegs, a deepening of the individual hum — and almost always one party yields without contact. They have been living in this city for so long, in such stable conditions, that the evolutionary pressure for aggression has been almost entirely selected against. The grazers that fight waste energy. The grazers that yield and wait for the city to provide are the ones that thrive.

Their life cycle is long by arthropodal standards. A grazer lives forty to sixty years, reaching full size at fifteen or twenty. They reproduce once, at the end of their lives, in a slow, deliberate process that takes weeks. The female — for there are sexes, though the differences are subtle, visible only in the shape of the rear segments and the chemistry of the pheromones they produce — seeks out one of the nursery-districts, drawn by a specific harmonic frequency that the city produces in those areas. She deposits her eggs in a specially grown alcove, a depression in the living wall that the city has shaped to the exact dimensions required, lined with a soft, nutrient-rich tissue that will feed the hatchlings when they emerge. Then she leaves. Grazers do not parent. The city parents for them.

## III. The Flyers (Seth-Ur, "Those Who Read the Air")

They are fewer than the grazers but more visible, because they occupy the upper air, the vertical space between floor and ceiling, and their wings catch the light.

The flyers are smaller than the grazers — most are between twenty and forty centimeters in body length, with wingspans that can reach a meter in the largest species. Their bodies are slender, almost delicate, with thin exoskeletons that sacrifice armor for lightness. Their wings are the most remarkable thing about them: panels of chitinous membrane, rigid along the leading edge, flexible behind, stretched between struts that are not quite veins and not quite bones but something between. The membranes are translucent, but they contain pigments — not the pigments of surface life, which are tuned to the yellow light of a star, but pigments tuned to the bioluminescent spectrum of the city, deep blues and greens and the warm amber of the oldest structures. When the flyers move through a shaft of light, their wings filter it, re-emit it, shift it, and the patterns that fall on the walls below are not random — they are *information*.

The flyers are the city's messengers, its information network, its rapid communication system. Where Vethara thinks slowly, in chemical gradients that take days to propagate, the flyers think fast. They navigate by sound — they produce clicks and chirps in frequencies that range from the barely audible to the far ultrasonic, and they read the returning echoes the way bats read the world, building a three-dimensional map of the halls around them in real-time. But they also read the city's light, the patterns that other flyers' wings cast on the walls, and they add to those patterns with their own flight, their own wing-angles, creating a visual language that overlays the city's bioluminescence like a second layer of circuitry.

This visual language is not a human language. It does not encode words or sentences. It encodes *states*. A flyer passing through a district reads the light-patterns on the walls and knows: this hall is crowded, that passage is blocked, food is abundant here, a predator is active there, the city is growing in a new direction to the east, the harmonic frequency of the old district has shifted, something has changed. And as it flies through, its own wings modify the pattern, adding its own observations, updating the message. The city's walls are a continuous, living bulletin board, written in light, read by thousands of flyers simultaneously, updated in real-time.

The flyers are also pollinators — not of flowers, which do not exist here, but of the city's own reproductive structures. Vethara reproduces sexually, in a sense, producing spore-like bodies in specialized chambers deep in the city's interior. These spores must be carried to new growth-fronts, to the places where the city is extending itself into new rock, and the flyers are the vectors. They pick up the spores on their bodies — the spores are sticky, adhesive, designed to cling to chitin — and carry them to the growth-fronts, where they deposit them by grooming, by rubbing against the fresh living tissue. In return, the flyers feed on the nutrient-rich secretions that the growth-fronts produce, the same secretions that bead on the walls and feed the small creatures at the bases of the columns.

Their social structures are more complex than the grazers'. Flyers form flocks — not large ones, usually twenty to fifty individuals — that move through the city in coordinated patterns, their flight paths creating the light-language on the walls. Within a flock, there are roles: scouts, who fly ahead and return with information; carriers, who transport spores and nutrients; sentinels, who watch for predators and produce alarm-clicks that send the flock scattering into side passages. These roles are not fixed — individuals shift between them based on age, experience, and the needs of the moment — but there is a recognized expertise, a way that certain flyers are known to be better at certain tasks, and the flock defers to them.

The predators they watch for are the Lurkers.

## IV. The Lurkers (Vaeth-Kan, "Those Who Wait")

They are the city's apex predators, and they are terrifying, and they are beautiful.

A Lurker is between half a meter and a meter long, built low to the ground, with a body plan that is recognizably arthropodal but distorted in the way that a spider's body plan is distorted — the front end exaggerated, massive, equipped with grasping limbs and sensory organs, while the rear end is compact, tucked beneath, almost an afterthought. They have six primary legs and two pairs of specialized forelimbs: one pair adapted for grasping, with jointed tips that can close around a flyer's body or a small grazer's leg with crushing force, and one pair adapted for sensory work, long and thin and covered in chemoreceptors that can read the chemical signature of a potential prey item from meters away.

Their exoskeletons are black — not the dark brown of the grazers, but a true, light-absorbing black, a chitin so dark that a Lurker in shadow is nearly invisible. They have no eyes. They do not need them. They navigate entirely through chemoreception and vibration, reading the chemical trails that other creatures leave on the city's surfaces, feeling the vibrations of movement through the pads of their feet, building a sensory world that is richer than vision in some ways — they can tell you what passed through this hall, and when, and how large it was, and whether it was healthy or injured, all from the faint chemical traces left on the living floor.

They are ambush predators. They find a spot — a side passage, a shadow behind a column, a depression in the floor where the light doesn't reach — and they settle into it, and they *wait*. They can wait for days without moving. Their metabolism drops to almost nothing. Their breathing slows. Their body temperature equalizes with the surrounding stone. They become, for all practical purposes, part of the city itself — a dark shape that could be a shadow, could be a crack in the living stone, could be nothing at all. And when prey passes — a flyer swooping low, a young grazer straying from the group, one of the small scavengers that cluster at the column-bases — the Lurker strikes with a speed that seems impossible for something so large. The grasping forelimbs extend, close, and the prey is held. The Lurker's mouthparts — not jaws, but a complex arrangement of hardened plates and soft, enzyme-secreting tissues — go to work, and the prey is subdued, consumed, its organic material broken down and absorbed.

Lurkers are solitary. They tolerate each other's presence — the city is large enough that territories rarely overlap — but they do not cooperate, do not form pairs, do not raise young together. The female deposits her eggs in a deep alcove, fertilizes them, and leaves. The hatchlings are miniature adults, fully capable of hunting from the moment they emerge, and they disperse immediately, each one finding its own territory, its own waiting-place. The mortality rate is high — perhaps eight in ten hatchlings do not survive to adulthood — but the ones that do are formidable, efficient, and long-lived. An adult Lurker can live thirty years, spending most of that time in a single territory, waiting.

They are not evil. They are not cruel. They are *hungry*, in the way that any predator is hungry, and the city has shaped them over millions of years to be exactly what they are: a population control, a selective pressure, a way of ensuring that the grazers and flyers and scavengers do not grow so numerous that they overwhelm the city's capacity to support them. The Lurkers eat the slow, the careless, the unlucky, and in doing so they keep the other populations healthy, alert, and adapted. They are the city's wolves, its lions, its hawks — the sharp edge of the closed loop, the teeth in the smile of the ecosystem.

## V. The Small Ones (Keth, "The Many")

At the bases of the columns, in the narrow spaces between the floor and the first fluted ridges of the living architecture, in the cracks and crevices where the city's oldest surfaces have developed a micro-topography of their own, there is a teeming, glittering, almost incomprehensibly diverse world of small creatures.

They are called collectively *Keth* — "the many" — by the larger symbionts, who distinguish them no further, who treat them as a single ecological category the way a human might treat "plankton" as a single category despite its encompassing thousands of species. And indeed, the Keth *are* thousands of species, a micro-ecosystem of staggering complexity, occupying every niche that the city's surfaces provide.

The smallest are barely visible — a centimeter long, or less — and they feed on the nutrient secretions that bead on the walls, the microscopic films of organic material that the city constantly exudes. They have mouthparts like brushes, like filters, like sponges, adapted to harvest the thinnest possible layer of nutrition from the living stone. They are the city's most intimate symbionts, living in direct contact with its surfaces, their bodies pressed against the cilia that wave constantly, exchanging gases, exchanging chemicals, a symbiosis so close that the boundary between host and symbiont blurs.

Slightly larger — two to five centimeters — are the scavengers, the detritivores, the ones that process the waste of the larger creatures. They are shaped like tiny armadillos, like pill bugs, like things that have no surface-world analogue, and they move in slow, patient columns along the grooves in the floor, picking up the microscopic fragments of shed exoskeleton, of droppings, of the occasional dead flyer or young grazer that has fallen from above, and carrying them to the fungal curtains that hang from the ceiling, where the real decomposition happens. They are the city's sanitation workers, its recyclers, and without them the halls would fill with dead matter in weeks.

Among the Keth, there are specialists of extraordinary specificity. There is a species — perhaps two centimeters long, iridescent green, with a body shaped like a flattened teardrop — that lives exclusively in the grooves of the nursery-districts, feeding on a specific secretion that the city produces only in those areas, and in return producing a pheromone that stimulates the growth of the soft tissue that lines the nursery alcoves. There is another — dark red, elongated, almost worm-like — that lives in the fluid channels of the underflow, swimming against the slow current, filtering microorganisms, keeping the city's circulatory system clean. There is a third — transparent, almost invisible, with legs so fine they look like hairs — that lives on the bodies of the grazers themselves, picking at the joints of their exoskeletons, removing dead tissue, cleaning wounds, a living grooming service that the grazers tolerate and even seem to welcome, slowing their gait when the groomers are active, pressing their bodies against the floor to give them better access.

The Keth are the city's connective tissue, its microbiome, the teeming invisible life that makes everything else possible. They are too small to be part of the city's conscious awareness — Vethara does not track individual Keth the way it tracks the movements of a grazer flock or a flyer swarm — but they are registered, in aggregate, as shifts in the chemical composition of the surfaces, as changes in the rate of nutrient cycling, as a kind of background hum in the ecosystem's metabolism. The city knows them the way a body knows its bacteria: not individually, but as a presence, a condition, a health.

## VI. The Listeners (Oru-Veth, "Those Who Hear the City")

And then there are the Listeners.

They are rare. They are strange. They are the ones that make you — the visitor, the outsider, the surface-dweller who has descended into the Root-Halls and stands now in a great chamber, listening to the drone — feel that you are not alone in *hearing* the city, that you are not the first intelligence to stand here and recognize that this place is alive.

A Listener is roughly the size of a large grazer — a meter and a half long — but built on a completely different body plan. Where the grazer is low-slung and broad, the Listener is tall and narrow, standing on eight long legs that lift its body a meter off the floor, giving it a vantage point above the heads of the other symbionts. Its body is slender, almost skeletal, with an exoskeleton that is not armored but *sensory* — a latticework of thin chitinous struts, open and permeable, covered in a dense array of chemoreceptors, vibration detectors, tympanal organs, and other sensory structures that have no name in any surface-world language. Its head is small relative to its body, dominated by two enormous auditory organs — not ears, but flat, disc-shaped membranes, each one thirty centimeters across, mounted on flexible stalks that can rotate independently, scanning the sound-field of the city like satellite dishes scanning the sky.

The Listeners hear *everything*. They hear the city's drone in its full complexity — not just the fundamental frequency but every harmonic, every overtones, every beat pattern, every subtle shift that indicates a change in the organism's growth, its metabolism, its mood. They hear the clicking language of the flyers, the scraping of the grazers, the chemical signals of the Keth translated into mechanical vibration by the sensitive surfaces of the walls. They hear the Lurkers' footsteps, the underflow's murmur, the deep bass note of the oldest structures. They hear the city's *thoughts* — the slow, millennial chemical conversations that propagate through the fluid channels, translated into vibration by the living walls, audible to anyone with ears sensitive enough to detect them.

And they *interpret*.

This is the thing that sets the Listeners apart, the thing that makes them more than just another symbiont: they understand. Not in the way that a human understands — there is no language, no abstraction, no theory-building — but in the way that a musician understands a symphony. They hear the city's drone and they *know* what it means. A shift in the harmonic pattern of the eastern district? The city is growing a new passage there, follow it. A sudden quiet in the old halls? The deep structures are shifting, settling, the root-hairs are reaching into new warm rock. A brightening of the light in the market-district? The flyer population is spore-heavy, they are carrying the city's reproductive material to a new growth-front, the organism is preparing to extend itself. The Listeners know these things the way a sailor knows the sea — not through analysis, but through *immersion*, through a lifetime of listening.

They move through the city slowly, deliberately, their long legs carrying them above the crowds of other symbionts, their great ear-discs constantly rotating, scanning, *listening*. They do not feed on the city's surfaces — they are too specialized for that, their mouthparts adapted not for grazing but for a single, specific food-source: the spore-bodies that the city produces in its reproductive chambers. They are the only symbionts that regularly enter those chambers, deep in the city's interior, where the light is dim and the sound is deep and the air is thick with chemical signals that would overwhelm any other creature. The Listeners thrive in those chambers. They feed on the spore-bodies, consuming them, processing them, and — this is the key thing — *remembering* them.

Because the spore-bodies carry information. Not genetic information — that's encoded in their DNA, as it is in any organism — but *experiential* information. The city encodes its experiences in the chemical structure of its spores: the pattern of its growth over the past reproductive cycle, the state of its tissues, the challenges it has faced, the changes it has made. The Listeners consume these spores and *absorb* this information, incorporating it into their own neural architecture — a distributed network of ganglia spread throughout their long bodies, concentrated in the thorax but present in every segment, a brain that is not a brain but a *continuum* of processing tissue.

The Listeners are the city's *memory*. They carry the accumulated experience of millions of years of growth, encoded in their bodies, accessible through their continuous listening. When a Listener stands in a hall and hears a particular harmonic pattern, it *recognizes* it — not as a learned thing, but as an *inherited* thing, a pattern that its ancestors heard, that their ancestors heard, going back thousands of generations, each Listener adding its own experience to the chain. They are a living archive, a biological library, the city's way of knowing itself.

There are perhaps a hundred Listeners in the entire Root-Hall system. They do not form social groups. They do not communicate with each other in any obvious way. But they are *coordinated* — not through signals, but through their shared medium. They all hear the same city. They all listen to the same drone. And in listening, they arrive at the same understanding, the same interpretation, the same knowledge. They are a hundred independent minds, processing the same data, converging on the same truth. They are the city's way of thinking about itself — its self-awareness, its consciousness, if that word can be stretched far enough to encompass a distributed intelligence embodied in a hundred tall, thin, skeletal creatures with ear-discs like satellite dishes, standing motionless in the great halls of the Root-Halls, listening to the sound of a world that has been alive for longer than the surface has had eyes.

## VII. What It Is Like to Live Here

You are a grazer. You wake — if waking is the word; the light has been brightening for the past three hours, and the metabolic shift from rest to activity is gradual, tidal, more like surfacing from deep water than opening your eyes — in your alcove, in the residential warren, surrounded by dozens of others, all stirring, all beginning the slow process of movement. The alcove is warm — the city's living tissue is warmer here, in the residential districts, generating heat as it grows — and the surface is soft, yielding, shaped to the contours of your body by years of pressure. You have been coming to this alcove for twenty years. The city knows you. It has grown around you.

You rise. Your legs unfold, joint by joint, and your feet find the grooves in the floor — the grooves that you and hundreds of others have pressed into the living stone over decades, the grooves that the city has not healed because the traffic is too constant, too heavy, the path too well-established. You follow the groove. It leads out of the residential warren, into a main thoroughfare, where the light is brighter and the drone is louder and the other grazers are already moving in their slow, rippling gait, a river of armored bodies flowing toward the feeding grounds.

You are not thinking about where you are going. You are not thinking in the way that a human thinks — in words, in plans, in anxieties about the future. You are *feeling* your way through the city, reading the vibration of the floor, the harmonic pattern of the walls, the chemical signature of the air. The city tells you where to go. The city tells you where the freshest surfaces are, where the nutrient film is thickest, where the feeding is best. It tells you through the drone — a subtle shift in frequency, a harmonic that resonates in your tympanal organs like a bell being struck, a direction, a pull, a *here, this way, follow*. You follow.

The feeding grounds are a broad chamber, one of the great halls, where the columns are spaced far apart and the floor is a continuous expanse of living stone. Other grazers are already here, spread across the surface in a loose pattern, each one in its own feeding territory, each one rasping slowly, methodically, the sound of their mouthparts combining into a soft, continuous rustle that adds to the drone. You find your spot — you have been feeding in this general area for years, and the city has learned your preference, has grown the surface here to a texture that suits your mouthparts, slightly rougher than the surrounding stone, easier to rasp — and you settle in and begin to eat.

The food is good. It is the city's shed skin, its sloughed cells, mixed with the secretions of the cilia, rich in minerals and organic compounds, flavored — if a grazer can be said to taste — with the subtle chemical signature of the district. This is the eastern feeding ground, and the stone here has a particular mineral content, a particular age, a particular flavor that you have come to prefer over the western grounds, which are younger, softer, less complex. You eat slowly, steadily, your rasping plate working in a continuous motion, and the city's living tissue responds beneath you, regenerating what you consume, growing new cells to replace the old, the cycle closing, the loop continuing.

A flyer passes overhead, its wings catching the light, casting a pattern on the wall — a pattern that you do not read, that is not meant for you, that is part of the flyers' own language, their own communication. But you feel the displacement of air, the faint click of its echolocation, and you register it the way you register everything: as information, as part of the city's continuous stream of data, as a note in the drone. A flyer. Moving east. Carrying spores, probably. The city is growing in the east. You have known this for weeks — the harmonic pattern has been shifting, the drone has been changing, the city has been telling you, telling everyone, that something is happening in the east, that new passages are opening, that the organism is extending itself. You will go there, eventually. When the feeding here is exhausted, when the city's surfaces have been grazed down to the point where regeneration cannot keep up, the drone will shift, the harmonic will pull, and you will follow it east, to the new growth, to the fresh stone, to the next feeding ground.

A Lurker is somewhere nearby. You can feel it — not see it, not smell it, but *feel* it, a subtle wrongness in the vibration of the floor, a faint chemical trace on the air, a shadow that is too still, too dark, too *absent* from the drone. It is not hunting you. You are too large, too healthy, too alert. It is waiting for something smaller, something slower, something careless. You register its presence and continue eating. The Lurker is part of the city, as you are part of the city. It is the city's teeth. You are the city's tongue. Both are necessary. Both are the city.

A Listener passes through the chamber. You see it — a tall, thin shape, moving above the crowd on its long legs, its ear-discs rotating, scanning, *listening*. It does not interact with the grazers. It does not interact with anyone. It is listening to the city, hearing the drone in its full complexity, interpreting the harmonic patterns, reading the organism's slow thoughts. You do not understand what it hears. You do not need to. The Listener is the city's memory, its self-awareness, its way of knowing itself. You are the city's body, its maintenance, its hunger. Both are necessary. Both are the city.

The light pulses. It has been brightening for hours, and now it begins to dim — slowly, so slowly that you cannot perceive the change in real-time, but over the course of the next few hours the chamber will grow darker, the luminescent veins will fade, the shadows will deepen. This is the city's night. Not a true night — there is no sun to set, no darkness absolute — but a metabolic rest, a slowing of the organism's processes, a dimming of the light-producing tissues as the city conserves energy. You will rest too. Your metabolism will slow. Your rasping will stop. You will find your alcove, settle into the warm, soft surface, and wait for the light to brighten again.

The drone continues. It has been continuing for millions of years. It will continue for millions more. It is the sound of the city breathing, the sound of the city thinking, the sound of the city *being*. You are part of that sound. Your rasping, your footsteps, your low-frequency hum — they are all part of the drone, all part of the city's voice, all part of the continuous, layered, ancient, patient, *alive* sound of Vethara.

You close your sensory organs. The drone continues. The city breathes. The city is.
