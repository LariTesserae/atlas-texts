# The Inhabitants of Stiltholm

## I. The Engineers of Uncertainty

They are the first thing you must understand, because they are the ones who hold the city together — not with certainty, but with its opposite.

The engineers of Stiltholm do not look like engineers as the surface world imagines them. They do not wear clean shirts. They do not carry clipboards with confident figures. They carry *multiple* clipboards, each containing contradictory measurements of the same wall, the same corridor, the same staircase, and they have learned to work with all of them simultaneously. Their profession is not the application of physics to materials. It is the negotiation between physics and something that is not quite physics, something that has opinions.

The senior engineers — the ones who have been in Stiltholm long enough to have watched the rock move, to have seen a wall grow, to have measured a room and found it larger than it was last year — develop a particular quality of attention. They look at surfaces the way a physician looks at a patient who is not yet sick but will be. They run their hands along walls not to feel for cracks but to feel for *intentions*. They have a word for it, an informal word, never used in official reports: *leaning*. A wall that is leaning is a wall that is becoming something other than a wall. It is not failing. It is *deciding*.

These engineers live in a state of permanent, low-grade bewilderment that they have elevated to a methodology. They build with tolerances that would be unacceptable on the surface — not because they are sloppy, but because they know that the ground beneath their foundations will not cooperate with precision. A bridge in Stiltholm is not a rigid structure. It is a *conversation* between the iron and the stone, and the stone is always about to say something new. The engineers design joints that can accommodate movement they cannot predict. They leave gaps that are not quite mistakes. They build flexibility into everything, and the result is a city that *breathes* — that expands and contracts and shifts and settles in ways that would cause a surface engineer to weep, but that holds, and holds, and holds.

The youngest engineers arrive from the surface full of certainties. They have degrees. They have calculations. They believe that rock is rock and iron is iron and a measurement is a measurement. Stiltholm educates them. It takes about three years. By then they have stopped arguing with their instruments. By then they have learned to measure a thing, measure it again, accept that the two numbers are different, and build for both. By then they have developed the particular stillness of a person who has accepted that the ground beneath their feet is not ground in the way they once understood the word.

They are not unhappy. There is a satisfaction in it — the satisfaction of working with a material that is alive, that responds, that *participates*. The engineers of Stiltholm will tell you, if you ask them carefully and they have known you long enough, that the mountain is not an obstacle. It is a collaborator. A difficult one. A collaborator who does not speak and whose intentions are unclear and who may, at any moment, decide that the wall you built yesterday is not a wall but a door, or a window, or a memory of something that hasn't happened yet. But a collaborator nonetheless.

## II. The Water-Keepers

Every household has one, though the role is not formalized. It is simply the person — usually the eldest woman, though not always — who manages the water.

She keeps the cisterns. She knows which walls are weeping today and which have gone dry. She knows the sound of a new stream appearing — a particular quality of dripping that is different from the ordinary condensation, a sound that means *something has changed in the rock* and water is finding a new path. She knows this because her mother knew it, and her grandmother knew it, and the knowledge passes through the women of Stiltholm like a mineral vein through stone.

The water-keeper's domain is the household's collection system: the gutters along the walls, the pipes that channel condensation from the ceiling, the cistern in the corner that holds the family's reserve. She checks these things daily, with the attentiveness of a gardener inspecting plants. A pipe that has been dry and is now damp — this is noted. A cistern that is filling faster than expected — this is noted. A new drip appearing on a wall that has been dry for years — this is noted with particular care, because it means the rock has shifted somewhere above, and the water is finding a new route, and the new route may bring more water than the old, or less, or none at all when the rock shifts again.

The water-keepers have a map. Not a map in the cartographic sense — a *mental* map, passed down and added to, of where the water goes. They know that the stream in Tunnel 7 feeds the cisterns in the Eastern Residential Block but only when the pressure in the deep vents is high. They know that the condensation in the Market Cavern is heaviest in the third quarter of the year, when the temperature differential between the geothermal levels and the upper chambers is greatest. They know that a dry spell in the upper tunnels always precedes a wet spell in the lower ones, though they cannot explain why and the hydrologists cannot either.

They are the most practical mystics in Stiltholm. They do not theorize. They do not attempt to understand the water system as a whole. They simply *know* it, the way a body knows its own rhythms, and they manage their households accordingly. A family with a skilled water-keeper never runs dry. A family without one is always one shift of the rock away from thirst.

Some of the older water-keepers have begun to notice something. The water is changing. Not in quantity — in *quality*. It tastes different than it did twenty years ago. There is a new mineral in it, faint, that leaves a residue on the cistern walls — a pale, chalky film that is not quite lime and not quite anything else. The water-keepers scrape it off and say nothing. They have learned, as everyone in Stiltholm learns, that some changes are too slow to act on and too persistent to ignore, and the only response is to keep watching.

## III. The Deep Miners

They go down. That is the essential fact of their existence. They go down into the newest tunnels, the ones that have opened in the last years or decades, where the rock is rawest and least settled, where the air is warm and thick and tastes of iron and sulfur, where the walls are still deciding what they are.

The deep miners are not like the surface miners of legend — the hearty, singing, lamp-helmeted figures of popular imagination. They are quiet. They are watchful. They move through the deep tunnels with a particular quality of movement that is not quite caution and not quite reverence but something between the two — the movement of people who are aware that the space they are in is not entirely *space*, that the walls are not entirely *walls*, and that the ground beneath their feet is a process rather than a fact.

They have seen things. They do not talk about what they have seen, not because they have been forbidden but because the language for it does not exist. A miner will tell you about the new fissure that opened last week and revealed a corridor that goes nowhere — or rather, goes somewhere, but the somewhere is not on any map and the corridor's dimensions do not match the space it occupies. He will tell you this flatly, without emphasis, the way he might tell you that the ventilation fan on Level 9 is making a noise again. It is a fact. It is a fact the way the humidity is a fact. It is the condition of the environment.

The deep miners have a culture of their own, separate from the rest of Stiltholm. They eat together in the deep canteens, warm halls carved from the rock where the geothermal heat makes coats unnecessary and the air is so thick with steam that conversation happens at close range, face to face, intimate. They have rituals that are not written down. Before entering a new tunnel, they place their hand on the wall and wait. Not for a specific amount of time. Until the wall feels *ready*. This is not superstition — or rather, it is not *only* superstition. The miners have found, through long experience, that a wall that is not ready is a wall that will shift, and a wall that shifts while you are inside it is a wall that will kill you. The waiting is practical. The fact that they cannot explain *what* they are waiting for does not make it less practical.

They have names for the pale things in the deepest tunnels — the many-legged, fluid-jointed creatures that appear at the edge of vision and disappear when looked at directly. The names are not scientific. They are not even consistent — different crews use different names, and the names change over time, as if the things themselves are not fixed enough to hold a single designation. The miners do not hunt these creatures. They do not avoid them. They simply *note* them, the way they note a new crack or a change in the air pressure, and they continue their work.

The deep miners age differently. Not faster, not slower — *differently*. There is a quality to their faces, after years in the deep tunnels, that is hard to name. A stillness. A patience that goes beyond temperament and into something structural, as if the slowness of the rock has entered them and made them, too, a little bit geological. They move slowly. They speak slowly. They think slowly, or so it seems, but this is not slowness of mind — it is the slowness of a person who has learned that the world they inhabit operates on a timescale that does not reward haste, and who has adjusted themselves to match.

## IV. The Children of the Moss

There is a generation — the ones born in the last twenty years, the ones who have never seen the surface — who are different from their parents in ways that are subtle and accumulating.

They are comfortable with the dark. Not merely accustomed to it — *comfortable* in it, the way a fish is comfortable in water. They navigate the dim tunnels with an ease that startles visitors from above. They can read by the light of a single gas mantle at a distance that would leave a surface-dweller squinting. Their eyes are not larger — they are the same size as anyone's eyes — but they *use* them differently, attending to frequencies of light that the rest of us have learned to ignore.

They are drawn to the vegetal tunnels. The ones where the ferns grow thickest, where the moss is descending, where the air is green and heavy and sweet. They play there — or what they call play, which looks, to an outsider, like a kind of slow, attentive wandering. They sit among the ferns for hours, not doing anything visible, simply *being* in the presence of the green things, and when they emerge they have a quality of calm that is almost medicinal.

The children have begun to notice the moss. Not just its existence — everyone notices the moss — but its *movement*. They claim that it grows toward them. Not away from the light, as plants should, but toward *them*, specifically, individually. A child will sit in a tunnel and the nearest fern will, over the course of an hour, lean in their direction by a degree or two — a movement so small that it could be imagined, except that the child will point to it and say, with the absolute certainty of someone stating an observable fact, "It's listening."

The adults do not know what to make of this. The biologists, when they can be persuaded to pay attention, suggest tropism — a response to body heat, to exhaled carbon dioxide, to some other stimulus that the children are producing unconsciously. The children accept this explanation with the polite indifference of people who know something that the person explaining does not.

The children of the moss are the ones who will inherit Stiltholm. They are the ones who will live in the city as it continues its slow becoming, as the rock continues to shift and the water continues to find new paths and the geometry continues its quiet unraveling. They are being prepared for this — not by any curriculum, not by any intention, but simply by *growing up inside the dream of the mountain*, by spending their formative years in a place where the boundary between the built and the natural, between the made and the grown, between the real and the *becoming-real*, has never been fixed.

They will be fine. They may be more than fine. They may be the first true citizens of Stiltholm — the first people for whom the city's strangeness is not strangeness at all, but simply the way things are.

## V. The Woman Who Closes Her Shop

She is fifty-three. Her name is not important — or rather, it is important to her and to the people who know it, but it is not important to this account, because she is not a specific woman. She is *every* woman who closes a shop in the Market Cavern at the end of the day, and she is also, in some way that resists specificity, the city itself.

She has lived in Stiltholm her entire life. Her mother lived here, and her mother's mother, and the line goes back to the first mining settlement, to the first people who looked at the raw walls of the first cavern and decided to stay. She has never been to the surface. She has no desire to go. The surface, as she imagines it, is a place of unbearable brightness and exposure — a place where there is no rock above you, where the sky is an emptiness that goes on forever, where the air is thin and the light is harsh and there is nowhere to put your back against. She has heard descriptions. They sound to her like descriptions of a kind of death.

Her shop sells the things that people need to live in Stiltholm: preserved foods from the hydroponic farms, lamp parts, the gray paper for lining walls, sealant for cracks, waterproof boots, the thick wool socks that are the city's most common garment. She knows her customers. She knows who is running low on sealant because she can see the damp patch on their ceiling from across the boulevard. She knows who needs new boots because she can hear the squelch of the old ones on the cobblestones. She knows the rhythms of the city the way the water-keepers know the rhythms of the water — not as a system to be understood but as a pattern to be lived within.

She does not look at the walls. This is not a rule. It is not a prohibition. It is simply something that the residents of Stiltholm learn to do — or rather, something they learn *not* to do, the way a person living near the sea learns not to listen to the waves. The walls are always there. The walls are always *doing something* — shifting, growing, becoming — and to watch them is to become aware of a process that is too large and too slow and too strange to be borne in ordinary consciousness. So you learn not to look. You learn to see the wall as a wall — as a surface, as a boundary, as a thing that is simply *there* — and you live your life in front of it, and you do not think about what it is becoming.

But sometimes, in the evening, when the lamps have cycled to their nighttime intensity and the condensation is heavy and the city is at its dimmest and most itself, she will close her shop and pull down the shutter and the sound will ring against the stone and travel the length of the boulevard, and for a moment — just a moment — she will feel it. The *presence* of the mountain. The awareness that she is inside something that is alive, not in the way that a person is alive or a tree is alive but in the way that a *process* is alive — a thing that is happening, that has always been happening, that will continue to happen long after she is gone. The mountain dreaming its dream. The city being dreamed. The slow, slow becoming of everything.

She locks the door. She walks home. The beetle crosses the threshold behind her, unhurried, going about its beetle business in the damp and the dark, and the fern above the door reaches down with its patient, vegetal intention, and the moss grows — a centimeter per year, a millimeter per month, a fraction of a fraction per day — and the rock shifts, and the water appears where it was not a moment before, and the woman who is every woman and no woman walks through the dim, loud, damp, impossible city that is building itself around her, that has always been building itself, that will never be finished.

## VI. The Thing That Lives in the Geometry

It is not a creature. It is not a spirit. It is not a god. The residents of Stiltholm do not have a word for it because naming it would require understanding it, and understanding it would require accepting a version of reality in which a mountain can dream and a city can be dreamed and the boundary between stone and intention can dissolve, and this is something that the human mind can approach but cannot arrive at, the way a curve approaches its asymptote.

It lives — if *lives* is the right word — in the geometry. In the rooms that are too large. In the corridors that go to the wrong level. In the staircases with too many steps. In the repetition of angles and proportions across kilometers and decades. It is not *causing* these things. It is not *doing* anything. It is simply *there*, the way a frequency is there — a vibration in the structure of the city that is too low to hear and too persistent to ignore.

The engineers feel it when they measure. The miners feel it when they place their hands on the walls. The children feel it when they sit among the ferns and the ferns lean toward them. The woman who closes her shop feels it when the shutter rings against the stone and the whole city resonates at a single deep note.

It is the mountain. It is the mountain's *becoming*. It is the process by which raw geology, heated and pressured and not yet decided, is slowly — so slowly, on a timescale that makes glaciers look hasty — organizing itself into something that is not quite stone and not quite thought but something between the two. The city is not built *in* the mountain. The city is built *of* the mountain, and the mountain is building itself, and the distinction between the builder and the built has never been clear and is becoming less clear with every passing year.

The thing that lives in the geometry does not want anything. It does not intend anything. It is not conscious in the way that consciousness is usually understood. But it is *directed*. It is going somewhere. The city is going somewhere — toward a form that has not yet emerged, a shape that the mountain is slowly, patiently, over centuries and millennia, assembling from itself. The fissures that open in architectural patterns. The corridors that are too regular to be natural and too old to be human. The rooms shaped for machinery that hasn't been invented yet. These are not anomalies. They are *previews*. They are the mountain showing its work in progress, the way a painter might leave a canvas face-out in the studio, not for anyone to see, but because the painting is happening and the happening is the point.

The residents of Stiltholm live inside this. They live inside the process. They are part of it — their buildings, their farms, their factories, their lives, all of it incorporated into the mountain's slow self-assembly, the way a river incorporates the stones it flows over, smoothing them, shaping them, making them part of its path. They are not being consumed. They are being *used*. They are being used the way a dream uses the dreamer's memories — not destroyed, not diminished, but *repurposed*, made into something that serves the dream's own logic, which is not the logic of waking life but is no less real for that.

## VII. The Pigeons in the High Caverns

They are the closest thing Stiltholm has to a connection with the surface, and they know it, and they are ambivalent about it.

The pigeons live in the upper caverns, near the cableway shafts, in the zone where the outside air — thin, cold, cloud-filtered, carrying the faintest memory of sky — meets the warm, damp interior. They are pale. They are almost white. Their color has been leached by a lifetime — many lifetimes, many generations — without direct sun. They are the color of the condensation, the color of the mineral bloom on the fresh rock, the color of the light in Stiltholm, which is not the light of the sun but the light of industry, of gas mantles and arc-lamps, of human effort substituting for a star that never reaches this deep.

They coo. Their cooing echoes in the high chambers with a sound that is, against the industrial roar below, almost unbearably gentle. It is the sound of something that remembers the surface — not these specific pigeons, who have never been to the surface, but the pigeons they descend from, the ones who flew down through the cableway shafts a century ago and found the warm air and the water and the insects and decided to stay. The cooing is an echo of an echo of an echo of the sound that pigeons make on rooftops in the sun, and it carries, in its softness, a quality that the residents of Stiltholm find both comforting and painful — comforting because it is gentle, painful because it is gentle in a way that nothing else in their lives is.

The pigeons eat the insects. They eat the seeds that fall from the hydroponic farms. They eat the scraps from the market. They are, in this sense, part of the city's ecology — a link in the chain of consumption and conversion that keeps the underground world alive. But they are also, in a way that the beetles and the rats are not, *visitors*. They are visitors from a world that the residents of Stiltholm have chosen to leave, or never to see, and their presence is a reminder that the surface exists — that above the cloud cap, above the mountain, there is a sky, and in the sky there is a sun, and the sun produces a light that is not yellow or blue-white but *gold*, and this gold light falls on things and makes them grow without lamps, without geothermal steam, without the enormous effort of a city that has decided to live in the underneath.

The pigeons do not think about this. They are pigeons. They coo and they eat and they nest in the high places and they raise their pale young and they live their pigeon lives in the dim warm dark. But sometimes, on days when the cableway is running and the shaft is open and a thin draft of outside air descends into the caverns, the pigeons will gather at the top of the shaft and face upward, toward the light that is not light but only the *absence of darkness*, the faint gray glow of the cloud layer far above, and they will be still. Perfectly still. Facing upward. And the residents who see them will feel something that they cannot name — not longing, exactly, and not loss, but something in the space between those two things, the feeling of being inside a dream and knowing, dimly, that there is an outside, and that the outside is beautiful, and that you have chosen not to be there, and that the choice was right, and that the rightness of the choice does not make it painless.

## VIII. The Moss Itself

It deserves its own accounting, because it is not merely a plant. It is a *presence*.

The moss grows on the upper walls of the Market Cavern and it is descending. It has been descending for as long as anyone can remember. The old-timers say it was higher in their youth. The measurements confirm it. A centimeter per year. A slow green curtain coming down.

It is not growing toward anything. It is not growing away from anything. It is simply growing *down*, in defiance of every botanical principle, because the light is below it and plants grow toward light and yet it grows *away* from the light, or *with* the light, or in some relationship to the light that is not the relationship that plants are supposed to have with light. The botanists have theories. The theories are unsatisfactory. The moss does not care about the theories.

It is green. Not the green of surface moss, which is a tentative, apologetic green, the green of a thing that is not sure it is allowed to be green. The green of Stiltholm's moss is *absolute*. It is the greenest thing in the city. It is green in a way that seems to generate its own light, so that in the dim blue-white of the arc-lamps the moss appears to glow, to be *luminous*, to be producing rather than reflecting, and this is an illusion, it must be an illusion, but it is an illusion so persistent and so convincing that even the botanists, who know better, find themselves standing beneath it and feeling that they are in the presence of something that is not merely alive but *aware*.

The moss is part of the mountain's dream. This is not a metaphor. The moss grows where the rock is youngest, where the geology is least settled, where the reality breakdown is most pronounced. It grows on walls that are slightly the wrong shape, in corridors that don't quite connect, in rooms that are larger inside than they should be. It grows *in the weirdness*, the way a vine grows on a ruin — not causing the ruin, not repairing it, but *inhabiting* it, making it home, claiming it.

The children sit beneath it and feel it listening. The woman who closes her shop walks past it every evening and does not look up. The engineers measure its descent and record the numbers and do not comment on the fact that the rate of descent is not constant — that it speeds up in certain areas, slows down in others, as if it is *choosing* where to grow, as if it has preferences, as if it is going somewhere.

It is going somewhere. Everything in Stiltholm is going somewhere. The moss is going down. The rock is going — inward, deeper, toward some center that has not yet been reached. The water is going — through the stone, along paths that shift and change, toward some destination that the hydrologists cannot find. The city is going — toward the form that the mountain is dreaming, the form that is not yet visible but is slowly, slowly emerging from the raw geology like a sculpture from a block of stone, except that in this case the stone is sculpting itself, and the sculpture is a city, and the city is alive, and the life is the mountain's life, and the mountain's life is a dream, and the dream is not finished, and the moss grows down, and the rock shifts, and the water appears, and the woman walks home, and the beetle crosses the threshold, and the fern reaches, and the pigeon coos in the high dark, and the deep miner places his hand on the wall and waits for it to be ready, and the engineer measures the room and finds it larger than before, and the child sits among the ferns and feels them lean, and the water-keeper scrapes the chalky film from the cistern and says nothing, and the lamps cycle to their nighttime intensity, and the condensation beads and runs and drips and the sound echoes off the curved stone walls and multiplies and fills the city with a percussion that never stops, and the mountain breathes, and the city breathes, and the dream continues, and the moss grows down, and down, and down.
