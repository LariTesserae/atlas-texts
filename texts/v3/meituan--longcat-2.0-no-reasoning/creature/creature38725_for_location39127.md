# The Keepers of the Weeping

They are not old, exactly. They are *settled* — the way the stone is settled, the way the water has found its channels. There is a quality to them of having arrived at a shape and stopped searching for another one.

Their skin is dark, but not from sun — the light here is too diffuse for that kind of marking. It is more like the color of the basalt itself: a deep, warm charcoal that seems to absorb the even light rather than reflect it. Their hair is black and worn long, and it is always damp. Not wet — damp. The way everything here is damp. It hangs in heavy ropes against their shoulders and backs, and they don't seem to notice or mind. Some of them braid it with small stones — smooth, water-worn pebbles from the channels — that click softly when they move.

Their hands are their most remarkable feature. The fingers are long, the joints pronounced, and the skin of the fingertips is thickened and pale from constant contact with water and stone. Their hands look like they have been carved by the same process that carved the terraces — worn into shape by repetition, by the daily work of fitting and refitting, of clearing channels, of running their palms along the faces of walls to feel for shifts. They touch things constantly. A wall. A block. The edge of a terrace. The leaves of a fern. They touch the way a musician touches an instrument — not to test, but to *listen*.

They wear little. The warmth and the moisture make heavy clothing unnecessary and, eventually, unbearable. Wraps of the same plant-fiber used in the roofing, dyed — or perhaps naturally colored — in deep greens and the rust-red of iron-rich mineral deposits. The wraps are practical: they can be removed and wrung out, dried on the warm stone, replaced. Some of the Keepers wear nothing above the waist, and the moisture films their skin the way it films the basalt, and in the even light they are almost indistinguishable from the rock they tend. You could look at a terrace and see a figure standing still against a retaining wall and not know if it was a person or a column of stone that had been left uncarved.

They carry water.

This is the thing that defines them, more than their appearance, more than their architecture. They carry water in vessels of fired clay — wide-bellied, narrow-necked, sealed with some kind of resin that smells faintly of sulfur. The vessels are heavy. You can see the effort of carrying them in the way the Keepers move: a slow, deliberate gait, the body leaning slightly away from the vessel, the free hand extended for balance on the slick stone. They carry water from the channels to the terraces. From the terraces to the buildings. From the buildings to — somewhere. To small, private places. To the margins where the ferns grow. To the empty platforms above the settlement, where no one lives, where the water is poured onto bare stone and runs in new, temporary channels down the slope before finding the old ones again.

They do this even though water is everywhere. Even though it runs in the gutters at their feet. Even though it beads on their skin. The carrying is not about the water. It is about the *carrying*.

---

Their language, if it is a language, is quiet. Not whispered — quiet. Spoken at the volume of the dripping that surrounds them, so that words and water become part of the same soundscape. They speak to each other across terraces, their voices carrying in the heavy air with a clarity that seems disproportionate to their volume. They speak to the walls. They speak to the channels. They speak to the water in their vessels in a low, continuous murmur that might be instruction or gratitude or simply the sound of a mind that has been in one place long enough to develop a relationship with it.

They do not speak to strangers. Not out of hostility. Out of a kind of *category error* — the way you might not speak to a rock, not because the rock is beneath conversation, but because conversation is not what happens between you and a rock. Strangers arrive rarely. The terraces are not on any route to anywhere. The haze below conceals the approach, and the weeping rock makes the climb treacherous for anyone who doesn't know where to place their feet. But strangers do arrive — occasionally, unpredictably — and the Keepers receive them with the same deliberate calm with which they do everything else. They offer water. They offer a place on a terrace. They do not ask where the stranger has come from or where they are going. These questions belong to a world that is still in motion, and the Keepers are not in that world anymore.

---

There is a structure at the highest occupied terrace that is different from the others. It is larger, for one thing — not a dwelling but a *space*, open on three sides, with a roof supported by basalt columns that have been carved, not stacked. The carvings are the only ornamentation in the entire settlement: shallow channels cut into the stone in patterns that mirror the water-courses below. Spirals. Branching lines. The dendritic patterns of drainage seen from above. The columns are the work of — generations? centuries? — and they have the same unweathered sharpness as everything else here. The moisture preserves rather than erodes. The stone stays clean. The edges stay true.

In this space, the Keepers gather. Not all of them. Not always. There is no schedule, no signal. They simply arrive, one or two at a time, carrying their vessels, and they sit on the fitted stone floor and they pour their water into a central channel that runs the length of the space and spills over the edge of the terrace in a thin, deliberate waterfall. And they sit. And they watch the water run. And they are still.

This is not worship. This is not meditation, at least not in any way a stranger would recognize. This is *attendance*. They are attending to the water the way you might attend to a fire — not because it needs you, but because the act of watching it, of being present for its movement, is the thing that makes you part of the same system. The water comes from the rock. The rock holds the terraces. The terraces hold the people. The people carry the water. The circle is closed. The dynamic is zero. The attendance is the point.

---

The children — and there are children, though they are few — are indistinguishable from the adults until you see them move. They move the way water moves: finding the path of least resistance, pooling in warm spots, running in quick, laughing channels down the gentler slopes between terraces. They are taught to carry water before they are taught to walk, or perhaps the two skills develop together — the vessels are sized for small hands, and the children waddle under their weight with the same deliberate, leaning gait as the adults. They are not precious. They are not protected. They are *included*. A child of perhaps three years old will sit with the adults in the high structure and pour water into the channel with a concentration that is indistinguishable from the adults' own, and the water it pours is no less real, no less part of the system, than anyone else's.

The children do not play, exactly. They *explore*. They climb to the empty terraces. They follow the channels to their sources. They put their hands in the weeping walls and feel the water emerge, and their faces have the expression of someone solving a puzzle whose answer is both surprising and obvious. They are learning the mountain. Not about the mountain — *the mountain*. The way it breathes. The way it weeps. The way it holds water the way a body holds blood: not as a possession, but as a process.

---

There is one Keeper who is different.

She — and the stranger will think of this one as *she*, though the Keepers do not seem to mark gender in any way a stranger would recognize — sits at the lowest terrace, the one closest to the haze line, where the settlement meets the slope and the slope disappears into white. She sits at the edge with her feet above the drop, and she looks down. She has been looking down for — a long time. Long enough that the stone beneath her has been worn into a shallow depression, a smooth hollow shaped exactly to her body, and the water runs around it in a small, respectful detour before continuing down the channel.

She does not carry water.

This is the thing that sets her apart, and it is the thing the other Keepers attend to most carefully. They bring her water. They pour it at her feet, where it runs into the channel and continues its descent. She does not acknowledge this. She does not acknowledge anything. She looks down into the haze, and her face has the quality of the empty terraces — a space that was once occupied and is now waiting to be occupied again, or perhaps waiting to be understood as occupied by its emptiness.

She is the one who remembers — or the one who *is* the memory — that the settlement was not always like this. That there was a time when the terraces were new, when the walls were being built, when the channels were being carved for the first time and the water had to be *taught* where to go. She is the one who knows that the equilibrium was not always here, that it was *achieved*, and that achievement implies a before. The other Keepers have let the before go. They live in the solved present. She sits at the edge of it and looks down into the haze where the before is still dissolving, and she does not look away.

The strangers who arrive — the rare, the lost, the deliberately seeking — are brought to her. Not by any formal process. They simply find themselves standing at the lowest terrace, looking down at the seated figure, and she looks up at them, and her eyes are the color of water on basalt: black, and silver where the light catches, and deep enough to see into but not through.

She does not speak. But the strangers understand something when they look at her. They understand that the settlement is not a destination. It is a *decision*. A decision to stop. To let the water teach you where to go and then to go there and to stay. To build your walls and fit your blocks and carry your water and attend to the weeping of the rock until the weeping becomes your own, until you cannot tell where the mountain's moisture ends and yours begins.

Some of the strangers stay. They are given a vessel. They are shown a terrace. They begin to carry water, and the carrying teaches them the way the mountain works, and the mountain teaches them the way the water works, and the water teaches them the way the stillness works, and one day they realize they have not thought about leaving in — a long time. And they look down at their hands and the fingertips are pale and thickened, and they look up at the terraces and the walls are sharp and clean and the water runs in its channels exactly where it is supposed to, and they understand that they have become part of the zero. The dynamic is closed. The system is solved.

Some of the strangers leave. They climb back down through the weeping rock, through the empty terraces, past the margins where the ferns grow, down to the haze line and into the white. And the Keeper at the lowest terrace watches them go, and her face does not change, because the leaving is also part of the system. The water that does not stay on the terrace runs down the slope and joins the aquifer below and rises again through the porous rock to weep from the walls. Nothing is lost. Nothing is wasted. The strangers who leave carry the water with them, in their bodies, in their memory, in the dampness that clings to their skin for days after they descend, as though the mountain is reluctant to let go, as though it is practicing for the day when they will come back and stay.

---

The Keepers do not have a name for themselves. They do not have a name for the settlement. They do not have a name for the mountain. Names are for things that need to be called, and nothing here needs to be called. Everything is already where it belongs. Everything is already in motion or already still. The water runs. The walls stand. The ferns grow in the margins. The Keeper at the bottom looks down. The Keeper at the top — and there is one, though the stranger may not see them — sits on the highest empty terrace, above even the carved structure, and looks up, into the pale sky, where the moisture rises and becomes haze and becomes cloud and becomes rain and becomes water again, and the circle that the terraces below have closed is opened again at the top, and the system that is solved is also, at its highest point, a question.

The mountain weeps. The Keepers attend. The water runs down, and the water rises up, and in between, on the forty terraces cut into the slope, the people carry what is already there from where it is to where it already wants to be, and they call this living, and they are not wrong.
