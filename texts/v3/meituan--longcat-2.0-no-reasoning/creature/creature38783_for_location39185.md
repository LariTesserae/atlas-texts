# The Drownings

## What Lives Here

---

### The Keepers of the Agreement

They do not call themselves anything you would recognize. Their word for themselves, if they have one, sounds like water moving over stone — a soft, continuous sound with no hard edges, no stops. You might write it as a breath. You might not write it at all.

They are small. Not in the way that implies weakness, but in the way that implies *fit*. They have lived in this place so long that the place has shaped them the way it shapes everything — slowly, completely, without argument. Their skin is pale, not from lack of sun but from the constant moisture, which has leached something from them over generations, some pigment, some boundary between themselves and the air. In the fog, they are difficult to see. They appear as suggestions. A movement at the edge of vision. A hand reaching into a pool and withdrawing something silver.

Their feet are broad. This is the first thing you would notice if you looked closely, which they would not prevent but would not invite. The toes are wide-spread, the soles thick and soft, adapted not for gripping but for *reading* — for feeling the subtle differences in the damp ground that tell them where water is moving, where a root has shifted, where the stone beneath has begun to yield. They walk without sound. Not because they are trying to be quiet, but because their feet and the ground have reached the same understanding.

Their hands are always wet. This is not a condition; it is a state of being. They do not dry their hands. There is no cultural practice of drying. The concept would be as foreign to them as the concept of empty air. Their fingers are long and the nails are dark — not dirty, but stained by the tannins in the water, the same dark resin that mortars their floors. Their palms have a texture like the skin of a frog: smooth, cool, slightly yielding.

They do not wear much. What they wear is woven from the broad-leafed fern — not the dead fern, but the living fiber, stripped and retted in the channels until it is soft as cloth and the color of wet bark. It clings to them. It does not flap or rustle. It moves with their bodies like a second skin, and like a second skin, it is always damp. They do not seem to notice. They do not seem to notice the cold, either, though the air would make you shiver within minutes. Their bodies have made their own agreement with the temperature. They are cool to the touch. Their blood moves slowly. Their metabolisms have slowed to match the pace of the water, which is the pace of everything here.

They are not many. Perhaps forty. Perhaps sixty. They do not count themselves, and the fog makes enumeration difficult. They live in the structures at the edges of the central clearing, in the buildings that climb two or three levels where the root-architecture allows. They sleep on platforms raised above the damp floors, on beds of dried fern that must be replaced often — though "often" is a word that means something different here, where time moves like the water, which is to say continuously and without urgency.

They eat what the water provides. Small fish from the pools — pale, sightless things that have lived in the dark water so long they have forgotten the concept of light. Frogs, though not all frogs; there are frogs they eat and frogs they do not, and the distinction is not one you would be able to discern. Insects, dried and ground into a paste that they carry in small containers made from the hollowed segments of the great pale roots. The duckweed itself, harvested from the pools and eaten fresh, a green so intense it stains their lips and teeth.

They do not cook, exactly. They *warm*. They place food on stones that have been sitting near the chimneys, stones that are never hot but are always slightly warmer than the air, and they wait. The food does not cook. It *relaxes*. It becomes more itself. A fish placed on a warm stone does not become a cooked fish; it becomes a *warmer* fish, a fish that has been persuaded to release its hold on itself. They eat it with their fingers. They eat slowly. They eat without conversation, though they are not silent — they make small sounds while eating, sounds of acknowledgment, sounds that are not words but are not *not* words either.

### What They Do

They maintain the Agreement.

This is not a phrase they would use. They would not use any phrase. But if you watched them for long enough — days, weeks, long enough for the fog to become your sky and the water to become your blood — you would see that everything they do is in service of a single, continuous act: the maintenance of the balance between the water and the structures, between the roots and the stone, between the living and the built.

They tend the channels. This is the work that takes most of their time. The channels must flow. If a channel is blocked — by a fallen leaf, by a shift in the earth, by the slow accumulation of duckweed — they clear it. They do this with their hands. They kneel at the edge of the channel and reach in and feel for the obstruction and remove it, and the water resumes its flow, and they move to the next channel. They do this every day. They have done this every day for as long as anyone can remember, which is not very long, because they do not remember in the way you remember. They remember in the way the water remembers: by continuing.

They tend the roots. The great pale roots that form the load-bearing structures of the settlement are alive, and like all living things, they grow, and like all growing things, they change, and like all changing things, they must be guided. The Keepers walk among the roots with their hands extended, touching, feeling, and where a root has begun to grow in a direction that threatens a wall or a floor or a channel, they do not cut it. They *redirect* it. They tie it with fern-fiber to a stone or a post, and they wait, and the root, over weeks or months, accepts the new direction and grows accordingly. This is not domination. This is negotiation. The roots are not servants. They are partners. They hold up the buildings; the buildings give the roots somewhere to go. It is an arrangement that predates the Keepers and will outlast them.

They tend the pools. The pools at the center of the clearing are the heart of the settlement — not symbolically, but literally. They are where the water collects before it continues its journey down the slope to the valley below. They are where the fish live, where the frogs breed, where the duckweed grows. The Keepers monitor the pools with a attention that borders on the devotional. They watch the color of the water. They watch the density of the duckweed. They watch the behavior of the frogs. If something changes — if the water darkens, if the duckweed thins, if the frogs become restless — they respond. They do not know why they respond. They respond the way you pull your hand from a flame: because the knowledge is in the body, not the mind.

They tend each other. This is the work that is hardest to see because it looks like nothing. An older Keeper — and they do age, though slowly, their bodies gradually becoming more like the stone they live among, harder, slower, more still — will sit beside a younger Keeper while they work, and they will not speak, and they will not touch, but they will *be there*, and the younger Keeper will work differently in their presence: more carefully, more attentively, as though the older Keeper's attention is a kind of light that makes the work more visible. This is how knowledge passes here. Not through instruction. Through proximity. Through the slow osmosis of example.

### What They Believe

They do not believe in the way you believe. They do not have beliefs. They have *orientations*.

They are oriented toward the water. This is not worship. It is not reverence. It is something more fundamental, something that precedes the distinction between the sacred and the secular. The water is not a god. The water is not a symbol. The water is the *medium* in which they exist, the way air is the medium in which you exist. They do not worship the water any more than you worship the air. But they are *aware* of it in a way you are not aware of air. They feel it moving. They feel it changing. They feel it in their bodies, in their bones, in the slow pulse of their cooled blood.

They are oriented toward the frogs. This is harder to explain. The frogs are everywhere, and the Keepers move among them without disturbance, and the frogs move among the Keepers without fear, and this mutual indifference is not indifference at all but something deeper — a *recognition*. The frogs were here before the Keepers. The frogs will be here after. The frogs are the settlement's memory, its continuity, its proof that the Agreement holds. When a Keeper dies — and they do die, though rarely, their bodies lasting long in the cool damp air — the frogs gather. They do not gather in a way that looks intentional. They simply *are there*, more of them than usual, on the walls and the floors and the roof of the structure where the body lies, and they are still, and they are silent, and this is the closest thing the Keepers have to a funeral rite.

They are oriented toward the fog. The fog is their sky. The fog is their ceiling. The fog is the boundary of their world, and they do not seek to see beyond it. They do not wonder what lies beyond it. The fog is not a limitation; it is a *definition*. It tells them where they are. It tells them the size of their world. And the size of their world is the size of a fog bank, which is to say: the size of what you can see when you cannot see far, which is to say: the size of the present moment, which is to say: enough.

They do not have a concept of the future. They have a concept of *continuation*, which is different. The future is a place you go. Continuation is a thing you do. They continue. The water continues. The roots continue. The frogs continue. The fog continues. This is not optimism. This is not fatalism. This is simply the observation of a system that has reached equilibrium, a system in which change has not stopped but has become so slow and so small that it is indistinguishable from stillness.

They do not have a concept of the past, either. They have a concept of *depth*. The past is a place behind you. Depth is a thing beneath you. The settlement has depth — geological depth, temporal depth, the depth of water in the pools, the depth of roots in the stone. When an older Keeper sits beside a younger Keeper, the younger Keeper feels this depth. They feel the weight of all the days that have passed in this place, all the hands that have cleared the channels, all the feet that have walked the root-bridges, all the bodies that have cooled and slowed and finally stopped. This weight is not oppressive. It is *grounding*. It is the depth that keeps them from being blown away.

### The Children

There are children. You would not notice them at first because they are the same color as everything else — the color of wet bark, of dark water, of the space between the roots. They are small and still and they watch you with eyes that are very dark and very calm.

They learn by being. They do not go to school. There is no school. There is no curriculum. There is the settlement, and the settlement teaches. A child learns to walk on the root-bridges by walking on the root-bridges, falling into the water, climbing out, walking again. A child learns to clear a channel by watching an older Keeper clear a channel, and then by standing in the channel themselves, feeling the water move around their legs, feeling the obstruction with their small hands, pulling it free. A child learns the frogs by sitting among the frogs, being still among the frogs, becoming, over time, as unremarkable to the frogs as the walls and the water and the fog.

They play, but their play looks like work, and their work looks like play, and the distinction between the two is one they do not make. A child sitting in a pool, moving duckweed from one place to another with their hands, is playing. A child sitting in a pool, moving duckweed from one place to another with their hands, is also learning the behavior of the duckweed, which is the behavior of the water, which is the behavior of the settlement itself. There is no separation. There is no time off. There is only the continuous, saturated present, in which everything is instruction and everything is practice and everything is life.

They are not precious. This is not a place that has the luxury of preciousness. The water is too present, the fog too thick, the world too small and too complete to allow for the kind of focused attention that preciousness requires. The children are loved — you can see it in the way the adults move around them, the way they adjust their paths to avoid stepping where a child is standing, the way they pause in their work to watch a child navigate a difficult root-bridge — but the love is quiet and diffuse, like the light, like the moisture, like everything else here. It does not announce itself. It simply saturates.

### The Old Ones

At the edges of the settlement, in the structures farthest from the central clearing, there are Keepers who have stopped working. They sit. They do not move. They are not sick. They are not dying. They are *settling*, in the geological sense — the same sense in which the settlement itself has settled. They have reached the conclusion of their motion and now simply persist.

Their bodies have changed. The constant moisture has done something to them over the decades — not decay, but *transformation*. Their skin has taken on the texture of the stone, rough and cool and faintly damp. Their hair has gone the color of the pale roots, a white so pure it seems to glow in the fog. Their eyes have gone the color of the deep water in the pools, a dark that is not black but *deep* — a color that suggests layers, that suggests something beneath the surface that you cannot see and they will not show you.

They do not speak. They may not be able to speak. Their voices, if they ever used them, have been worn away by the moisture, the way the water wears the stone, slowly and completely. But they *communicate*. They communicate by being still in a particular place, by orienting their bodies in a particular direction, by opening or closing their hands. The other Keepers read these signals the way they read the water — continuously, unconsciously, as a form of perception rather than interpretation.

They are the settlement's memory. Not in the sense that they remember events — they do not tell stories, they do not recount histories — but in the sense that they *embody* the duration of the settlement. They are the proof that the Agreement has held. They are the evidence that a body can live in this place for a long time and not be destroyed by it but be *completed* by it, the way a stone is completed by the water that wears it, the way a root is completed by the stone it penetrates.

Sometimes, on the rarest of occasions, when the fog lifts — not fully, but enough to see the slope descending into the valley, enough to see the water's path as a silver thread winding down through the green — one of the Old Ones will raise a hand and point. Not at the valley. Not at the water. At something you cannot see, something beyond the fog, something that may not be there at all. And the other Keepers will stop their work and look, and for a moment the entire settlement will be still — the water still, the frogs still, the birds still, the beetles still, everything still — and then the fog will close again, and the work will resume, and the moment will pass, and the settlement will continue.

No one knows what the Old Ones point at. No one asks. The question would be meaningless here, in a place where the fog is the sky and the water is the world and the only direction that matters is *down*, the way the water goes, the way the roots go, the way time goes in a place where time has finished becoming and simply *is*.

### The Visitors

You are not the first. You will not be the last.

They come rarely — once in a generation, perhaps, or once in several generations, the intervals are impossible to judge in a place without seasons. They come from beyond the fog, from the dry places, from the places where the air is thin and the light is hard and the shadows are sharp. They come with their boots and their questions and their need to *understand*, and the Keepers do not prevent them and do not welcome them and do not acknowledge them, because acknowledgment would require a category for "visitor," and the Keepers do not have this category, because in a place where everything is part of the system, the concept of an outsider is not a concept that can be sustained.

The visitors stay for a while. They watch. They take notes, if they have brought the tools for noting. They ask questions that the Keepers do not answer, not out of hostility but out of a genuine inability to comprehend what is being asked. "Who built this place?" the visitors ask, and the Keepers look at them with their dark, deep eyes, because the question assumes a distinction between the builder and the built that does not exist here. "How long have you been here?" the visitors ask, and the Keepers look at them, because the question assumes a distinction between the present and the past that does not exist here. "What do you believe?" the visitors ask, and the Keepers look at them, because the question assumes a distinction between belief and being that does not exist here.

The visitors leave. They always leave. The settlement does not hold them. Not because it rejects them, but because it does not *recognize* them. They are not part of the system. They are not part of the Agreement. They are like the fog that lifts — present for a moment, then gone, and the settlement closes behind them like water closing behind a stone, seamless, complete, unchanged.

The Keepers do not watch them go. The frogs do not watch them go. The birds do not fall silent at their arrival or resume at their departure. The water does not change its flow. The roots do not shift. The fog does not thin.

The settlement continues.

### The Agreement Itself

This is what lives here, more than anything. More than the Keepers, more than the frogs, more than the roots or the water or the fog. The Agreement lives here.

It is not a document. It is not a covenant. It is not a set of rules or a code of conduct or a shared understanding. It is a *state of being*. It is the state that is reached when water and stone and root and flesh have been in each other's company for so long that they have forgotten where one ends and the other begins. It is the state of saturation — not the saturation of the air with moisture, but the saturation of the world with *relationship*, every surface touching every other surface, every life touching every life, every moment touching every moment, until the whole place is so full of connection that there is no room for anything else. No room for fear. No room for ambition. No room for change. No room for you.

The Agreement is what keeps the settlement from falling apart. Not the roots, though the roots help. Not the stone, though the stone helps. Not the resinous mortar or the fern thatch or the oiled membranes over the windows. The Agreement is what holds these things together, the way gravity holds a planet together — invisibly, fundamentally, as a condition of existence rather than a force applied to it.

And the Agreement is what you feel, standing here, in the fog, with the frog on your shoulder and the water at your feet and the sound of the birds filling the air like a liquid. You feel it as a pressure. Not a physical pressure — a *metaphysical* pressure. The pressure of a world that is so complete, so saturated, so *full*, that there is no space in it for you. You are standing in it, but you are not *of* it. You are present, but you are not included. You are seen — the frog sees you, the Keepers see you, the fog sees you — but you are not *recognized*, because recognition would require a place for you in the system, and there is no place for you in the system, because the system is full.

Every niche filled. Every surface occupied. Every moment full of small urgent motion.

You are standing in it. It has not acknowledged you. It does not need to.

The frog on your shoulder blinks. The water in the channel beside you moves. The fog shifts and settles and shifts again. Somewhere, a beetle clicks. Somewhere, a bird sings its glass-rim song. Somewhere, an Old One sits in the grey light, still as stone, deep as water, old as the roots that hold the world together.

The settlement continues.

It has always continued.

It will always continue.

This is the Agreement. This is what lives here. Not the people, not the frogs, not the water, not the fog. The *continuation itself*. The persistence. The saturated, complete, unbroken persistence of a world that has finished becoming and now simply, quietly, relentlessly *is*.
