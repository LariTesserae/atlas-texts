# The Gorge of Loosened Teeth

## What Lives There

---

### The Water That Remembers Its Own Shape

It is not one thing. It is not even many things. It is a *tendency*—a disposition of water to organize itself in ways that water should not, in places where the geometry of the gorge forces it into configurations that are too complex, too persistent, too *intentional* to be mere hydraulics.

They do not have names. They do not have bodies. They are patterns—standing structures in the flow, nested curves that hold their form for seconds, sometimes minutes, before dissolving and reforming in a new configuration that is similar but never identical. They exist only in the narrow places, where the gorge walls press close and the water is forced through constrictions that compress it into something denser than water wants to be. In those places, the water *thinks*. Not in the way a brain thinks—not with neurons or synapses or the electrochemical chatter of a mind—but with the same quality of *recursive self-reference* that thought has. The water folds back on itself. The water holds a shape and then modifies the shape based on the shape it held. The water is doing something that, if you saw it happening in a brain, you would call *cognition*.

They are old. Not as old as stone—nothing is as old as stone—but old in the way that a habit is old, in the way that a pattern repeated enough times becomes something more than a pattern. They have been forming and dissolving in the narrows for as long as the water has been flowing, which is as long as the gorge has existed, which is—long enough that they have developed *preferences*. They favor certain configurations. They return to certain geometries. They have, if not memory, then something adjacent to memory—a tendency to repeat, a tendency to elaborate, a tendency to build on what came before in ways that suggest not randomness but *accumulation*.

They are aware of you. Not in the way that a person is aware of another person—not with recognition, not with curiosity, not with the social machinery of attention. They are aware of you the way that a river is aware of a stone placed in its path: they adjust. When you stand in the narrows and watch them, the patterns change. They become more complex. They develop new folds, new recursions, new geometries that spiral inward toward a center that is not quite where you are standing but is *related* to where you are standing in a way that makes your skin prickle. They are not trying to communicate. They are not trying to threaten. They are *responding*—the way any system responds to a perturbation in its environment—but the response is so elaborate, so specific, so *elaborate in its specificity*, that it feels like something more.

They do not leave the water. They cannot. They are the water, in the same way that a whirlpool is water—not a thing *in* the water but a thing *made of* water, a configuration that exists only as long as the flow sustains it. If the gorge dried up, they would not die, because they were never alive. They would simply *stop*, the way a song stops when the singer falls silent, and the water would be just water again, and the narrows would be just a narrow place in a dry gorge, and nothing would be lost because nothing was ever there to begin with.

But the gorge does not dry up. The water does not stop. And so they continue—forming, dissolving, reforming, elaborating, accumulating—in the narrow places, in the constrictions, in the spots where the gorge walls press close and the water is forced to be more than it wants to be.

---

### The One That Walks Against the Current

It is not often seen. It is not often *there*. It moves through the gorge the way a thought moves through a mind—quickly, without leaving a trace, gone before you can be sure it was ever present.

It is dark. Not black—dark in the way that deep water is dark, a darkness that has *depth* to it, that suggests layers and layers of something dense and opaque extending downward beyond what you can see. It is low-profile, close to the surface of the water, moving with a slow, deliberate motion that is not the motion of swimming and not the motion of walking but something between the two—a kind of *pressing forward* that displaces the water around it without disturbing it, that moves through the flow without creating ripples or eddies or any of the signs that something solid has passed.

It moves against the current. Always against the current. It moves from the southern end of the gorge toward the north, upstream, against the flow of all the water that is making its way downward over the sharp rocks and through the narrows, and it moves with a purpose that is not visible but is *felt*—a sense of direction, of intention, of something going somewhere for a reason that is its own.

It is large. Not enormous—not the size of a building or a hill—but large in the way that a large animal is large: big enough that its presence changes the space around it, big enough that the water behaves differently in its vicinity, big enough that when it passes through a section of the gorge the sound changes—one of the many small voices in the chorus shifts its note, almost imperceptibly, as though the gorge itself is adjusting to accommodate something that does not quite fit.

It is not made of flesh. It is not made of stone. It is made of something that is *between*—something that has the density of stone and the fluidity of water and the opacity of both, something that does not reflect light so much as *absorb* it, pulling the dim, bluish light of the gorge into itself and giving nothing back. When it moves through the water, the water does not part around it so much as *forget* it—the flow continues as though the space it occupies is empty, and only the absence of the sound that should be there, the missing note in the chorus, reveals that something is present.

It does not interact with the patterns in the water. It does not interact with the structures in the walls. It does not interact with anything. It simply moves, slowly, deliberately, against the current, from south to north, and then it is gone, and the gorge is as it was, and the only evidence that it was ever there is the faint, lingering sense that the sound has changed—that one voice in the chorus is singing a slightly different note than it was before, and will continue to sing that different note for a long time, perhaps forever, as though the gorge has been *marked* by the passage of something it cannot quite process.

It comes from somewhere. It goes somewhere. The gorge is not its home—the gorge is its *path*, the way a road is a path for a traveler, and it walks that path the way a pilgrim walks a pilgrimage: with purpose, with patience, with the quiet certainty of something that knows where it is going even if it does not know why.

### The Structures Themselves

They are not alive. They are stone—the same stone as the cliff face, the same wet, grey, recently-fractured stone that makes up the walls of the gorge. But they are stone that has been *shaped*, and the shaping has given them a quality that raw stone does not have: a quality of *waiting*.

The flat slabs propped against the walls at angles, creating shallow overhangs—they are not shelters. Nothing lives under them. Nothing has ever lived under them, as far as you can tell. But they are *positioned* in a way that suggests they were meant to shelter something, and the something is not there, and the absence of the something is not an accident but a *condition*—a state of the structures that is as deliberate as their construction. They are waiting for something to shelter. They have been waiting for a long time. They will continue to wait.

The rectangular openings, perhaps a meter tall, cut into the rock face at heights that suggest they were meant to be reached by standing on something that is no longer there—they are not empty. They are *vacant*. There is a difference. An empty room has never been occupied. A vacant room has been occupied and is no longer, and the absence of the occupant is present in the room the way a scent is present—faint, lingering, impossible to confirm but impossible to deny. The openings are vacant. Something stood in them once. Something looked out through them at the gorge, at the water, at the narrows where the patterns form and dissolve. Something stood in them and *watched*, and now they are empty, and the watching has stopped, but the openings remain, and they face the gorge with the patient, unblinking attention of something that is still, in some sense, looking.

The carved channel in the deepest structure—the one that directs the thin stream of water from the back wall to the gorge floor with such precision—it is not a functional thing. The water would reach the floor without it. The channel does not serve a purpose in the way that a gutter serves a purpose. It serves a purpose in the way that a *ritual* serves a purpose: it makes the water's descent *meaningful*, it gives the water's path a *shape* that is not necessary but is *right*, and the water follows it exactly, not because it has to but because the channel is there, and the channel is *correct*, and the water recognizes the correctness the way a body recognizes a familiar gesture—without thought, without hesitation, with the deep, unreflective certainty of something that knows what it is doing even if it does not know why.

The structures are being absorbed. The gorge is forming around them, or through them, or in spite of them, and the fresh fractures in the rock are slowly, grain by grain, reclaiming the shaped stone, erasing the angles, softening the edges, returning the structures to the undifferentiated mass of the cliff face. It will take a long time. The structures are old, and old things resist dissolution the way old habits resist change—not by fighting but by *persisting*, by continuing to be what they are even as the world around them becomes something else. But the gorge is patient. The gorge has time. And the structures, one day, will be gone, and the cliff face will be smooth, and there will be no evidence that anything was ever carved here, and the water will still flow, and the patterns will still form in the narrows, and the one that walks against the current will still pass through on its slow, deliberate journey from south to north, and nothing will have changed, and everything will have changed, and the gorge will continue its long, slow act of becoming whatever it is becoming.

### The Gorge Itself

This is the thing that is hardest to say, because it is the thing that is hardest to believe, and it is this:

The gorge is not a place where things live. The gorge is a thing that lives.

Not in the way that an animal lives—not with breath, not with blood, not with the metabolic fire of a body converting fuel into motion. The gorge lives in the way that a *process* lives: it is a thing that is happening, that has been happening, that will continue to happen, and the happening is not random but *directed*, not chaotic but *organized*, not meaningless but *purposeful in a way that does not require a purpose*.

The water runs down the walls in ten thousand parallel lines, and the lines are not random—they follow the grain of the rock, yes, but they also follow something else, something that is not visible in the rock but is *expressed* through the rock, the way a melody is expressed through the vibration of a string. The water braids into rivulets, and the rivulets fall, and the falling water strikes the sharp rocks on the gorge floor, and the sound it makes is not the sound of water hitting rock—it is the sound of *ten thousand small events happening simultaneously*, each one producing its own tiny note, and all of them combining into a dense, layered, almost choral sound that is not music but is *adjacent* to music, that has the structure of music without the intention of music, that is the sound of a place that is *making itself* and the making is audible and the making has been going on for a long time and the making will continue for a long time and the making is the gorge's way of *being alive*.

The rock is settling. The fractures are fresh. The walls are weeping. The floor is a chaos of angular slabs and sharp-edged rubble that has not yet been rounded by the slow abrasion of time. The gorge is *young*—young in the way that a wound is young, raw-edged and not yet scabbed—and its youth is not a temporary state but a *permanent condition*, the way a river is always young at its source, always fresh, always new, always in the act of arriving at itself for the first time.

The gorge is aware. Not in the way that the patterns in the water are aware—not with the recursive self-reference of cognition, not with the elaborate responsiveness of something that thinks. The gorge is aware in the way that a body is aware of itself: not with thought but with *sensation*, not with intention but with *reaction*. When the one that walks against the current passes through, the gorge *feels* it—the way a hand feels a splinter, the way a tongue feels a crack in a tooth—and the sound changes, and the change is the gorge's way of saying *something is here that should not be here*, or *something is here that has always been here*, or *something is here and I do not know what it is but I know that it matters*.

The gorge does not want anything. The gorge does not fear anything. The gorge does not hope or plan or remember or anticipate. The gorge simply *is*, in the fullest and most overwhelming sense of that word: it exists, it persists, it continues, it *happens*, and the happening is dense and layered and almost unbearably rich with small events that never resolve into a single thing, and the density of the happening is what makes the gorge feel alive, and the feeling is not wrong, and the feeling is not right, and the feeling is simply *what it is like to stand inside a place that is still becoming itself*, that has been becoming itself for a long time, that will continue to become itself for a long time after you have left, after you have died, after everything you know has been forgotten and the gorge is still there, still weeping, still settling, still singing its dense, layered, choral song of ten thousand small events happening simultaneously in the dim, bluish light of a place that is lit from within by nothing more than wetness and the stubborn, persistent, almost-luminous fact of its own existence.

---

### What Lives in the Gorge of Loosened Teeth

The water that thinks without a mind.

The dark thing that walks against the current.

The structures that wait for something that will not return.

And the gorge itself—young, raw, still forming, still weeping, still singing—alive in the way that a wound is alive, in the way that a scar is alive, in the way that a place where the ground has only recently decided to stop being flat is alive: not with the life of organisms but with the life of *process*, with the life of *becoming*, with the life of a thing that is always almost what it will be and never quite.
