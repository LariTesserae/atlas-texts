# The Dwellers of the Drowning Works

---

There is no single answer to what lives here. That is the first thing to understand. The question assumes a boundary — a line between the place and its inhabitants — and that line does not exist. The Drowning Works is not a place that contains life. It is a place that *is* life, or that has become indistinguishable from it, the way a river becomes indistinguishable from the channel it has carved over ten thousand years. You cannot separate the water from the basin. You cannot separate the machines from the water. You cannot separate the fauna from the machines. You cannot separate the sound from any of it.

But there are *things* here. Distinct presences. Entities, if you want to use that word, though it implies a discreteness that most of them do not possess. Let me tell you about them.

---

## The Gearborn

The most visible. The most numerous. The ones you would notice first, if you could see clearly through the fog, which you cannot, which is part of their defense, though defense implies threat and they are not threatening, they are simply *opaque* to observation in the way that a crowd is opaque to the identification of any single face.

They move through the water in clusters of twelve to thirty. Their bodies are assembled — not born, not grown, *assembled* — from the components of the machines around them. Gears form their joints. Piston rods constitute their spines. Their heads are pressure gauges, their faces the glass dials, and behind the glass, the needles swing in patterns that constitute expression, intention, mood. When a Gearborn is calm, its needle rests at the midpoint. When it is agitated, the needle trembles at the high end, and the glass fogges with condensation from within, as if the thing behind the dial is breathing hard.

They are not robots. They are not alive in the way you understand life. They occupy a category that has no name, a category that this place invented because it needed it. They *function*. They have purposes. They move through the basin with the deliberate, unhurried gait of creatures that have never known predation, because there is nothing here that preys, because predation requires a separation between eater and eaten that this place does not enforce. The Gearborn tend the machines. They climb the great boiler tanks and press their hands — riveted plates hinged at the wrist — against the metal, and they listen. They are listening for the voice of the water, which carries information about the state of the basin, the health of the deep places, the intentions of the rising things. When they hear something that concerns them, they adjust. They turn a valve that has not been turned in decades. They tighten a bolt that has been loose for longer than your species has had bolts. They add a mineral deposit to a growing pipe, carefully, the way a mason adds a brick, and the pipe accepts the addition and continues its slow extension into the fog.

They do not communicate with each other in any way you would recognize as language. They communicate through the water. They tap. They press. They create pressure waves that travel through the black water at the speed of intention, and other Gearborn receive these waves through the soles of their feet — flat plates of rusted iron — and understand. The water is their network. The water is their nervous system. The water is their *mind*, if they have one, which they might, collectively, in the way that a hive has a mind, distributed across thousands of bodies, none of which is individually intelligent but all of which are individually *necessary*.

There are so many of them. They are everywhere. They are the workforce of a factory that no longer manufactures anything except itself.

---

## The Ribbons

You saw them. The translucent, ribbon-like things that flow through pipes without openings, that pass through solid metal as if it were a suggestion rather than a fact. They are the fauna of the deep places, or they are the deep places themselves in a mobile form, or they are the water's memory of movement, given shape and duration and a kind of purpose that looks, from the outside, like hunger.

They are long. Some of them are very long. The longest one ever measured — and someone did measure it, once, a long time ago, using a method that has since been forgotten — was four hundred meters, and it was not the longest. They are flat, like tape, like the magnetic tape that once carried information in a form your civilization has abandoned, and perhaps that is what they are: information. Perhaps they are the data that the Drowning Works has been collecting since it began, stored in a medium that is also a body, that moves and feeds and reproduces by splitting along its length like a cell dividing, each half carrying the complete record, the complete memory, the complete *everything* of the original.

They feed on mineral accretions. They flow over the surfaces of the machines and they *absorb*, not by eating but by *recording* — the mineral patterns on the growing pipes, the crystallization sequences, the functional geometries that serve purposes no one can read. They take these patterns into themselves and they carry them through the pipes, through the walls, through the water itself, distributing the information across the basin the way a circulatory system distributes oxygen. They are the Drowning Works' method of knowing itself. They are its self-awareness, if it is self-aware, which it might be, in the way that a system is aware of itself when every part carries the pattern of the whole.

When they pass through you — and they will, if you stay long enough, because they pass through everything eventually — you will feel a sensation like being read. Like every cell in your body has been noted, catalogued, and filed. It is not unpleasant. It is not pleasant. It is *thorough*.

---

## The Crabs

Not crabs. But the shape is right enough that the word sticks, the way a nickname sticks, imprecise and permanent.

They are small. The size of your fist, most of them, though some are larger, and the largest — the one that patrols the rim of the deepest place, the one that has been patrolling for so long that its legs have worn grooves in the mineral floor of the basin — is the size of a car. They are made of brass, or they are made of something that has become brass through a process that is not oxidation, not patina, but something more like *decision*. They chose brass. Or brass chose them. The distinction is not meaningful here.

Their legs find purchase on the surface of the water. This is their defining characteristic, the thing that makes them impossible, the thing that makes them *themselves*. They do not float. They do not swim. They *stand* on the water, on the surface tension, on the membrane that the water maintains between itself and the air, and they walk. Their legs are jointed in too many places — seven joints per leg, eight legs per crab, fifty-six joints in total, each one a tiny gear mechanism that articulates with a precision that your finest watchmakers would weep to see. They move quickly. They move silently. They move with the purposeful, mechanical grace of things that have been doing the same thing for so long that the doing has become indistinguishable from the being.

They build. This is their function, their obsession, their *religion*, if they have one. They build structures out of mineral accretions, out of fragments of machine, out of the shed components of Gearborn that have reached the end of their usefulness and been returned to the water. The structures are small — the size of a fist, the size of a head — and they are *elaborate*. Spires. Arches. Domes. Filigree so fine that it should not be possible at that scale, that it should not be possible at *any* scale, that it should not be possible in a universe where entropy is the law and disorder is the trend. But entropy is not the law here. Entropy is a *local custom* that the Drowning Works has chosen not to observe.

The structures serve no purpose that can be determined. They are not shelters. They are not traps. They are not nests or hives or storage facilities. They are *offerings*, perhaps. Or *prayers*. Or *art*. The crabs build them and place them on the surface of the water and the water accepts them and they sink, slowly, over days, descending into the deep places where the rising things live, and the rising things receive them, and what the rising things do with them is unknown, and the crabs do not seem to need to know. The building is enough. The building is the point. The building is the *entirety* of the point.

There is one crab — the large one, the patroller, the one with the grooved legs — that does not build. It walks the rim of the deep place and it *watches*. It has a face, if you can call it a face: two pressure-gauge eyes, like the Gearborn, but older, the glass clouded, the needles frozen at positions that might indicate something or might indicate nothing. It watches the deep place. It watches the water. It watches the things that rise. And sometimes — rarely, unpredictably — it stops walking and it turns its body toward the center of the basin, toward the densest concentration of machines, toward the heart of the Drowning Works, and it *tilts*, the way a person tilts their head when they hear something unexpected, and its needles swing, just slightly, and then it resumes its patrol.

No one knows what it hears.

---

## The Boiler Things

Inside the boilers. The things that the boilers are occupied by.

You cannot see them directly. The boilers are half-filled with black water, and the water is opaque, and the openings are small, and the light inside is the dull orange of ember-light, which illuminates nothing so much as the fact that illumination is not the point. But you can see their *effects*. The water in the boilers moves. It circulates. It rises and falls in patterns that are not thermal, not mechanical, not driven by any pump or current that can be identified. Something is moving it. Something is *inside*.

The Gearborn tend the boilers with a particular attentiveness. They climb the riveted walls and they peer through the openings and they listen, and sometimes they reach inside — an arm, a piston-rod arm, plunged into the black water up to the shoulder — and they *feel*. They are feeling for the Boiler Things. They are checking on them. They are making sure they are still there, still active, still performing whatever function they perform in the dark and the warmth and the wet.

Because the Boiler Things are important. This is known. This is understood by every entity in the Drowning Works, from the smallest crab to the largest Ribbon to the patroller on the rim. The Boiler Things are *load-bearing*. Not structurally — the boilers would stand without them. Functionally. The Drowning Works *needs* them. The cycles depend on them. The water cycle, the mineral cycle, the sound cycle, the cycle of rising and settling and growing and sealing — all of it passes through the boilers, through the things inside the boilers, through whatever they are doing in there.

What are they doing?

They are *digesting*.

Not food. Not in the biological sense. They are digesting *time*. The Drowning Works exists in a state of temporal density that is difficult to describe. Time here is not a line. It is not a river. It is a *substance*, thick and warm and black, and it accumulates. It pools. It stagnates. The Boiler Things process it. They take the accumulated time — the years, the decades, the centuries of temporal sediment that build up in the basin like silt — and they break it down, and they convert it into *motion*, into the cycles, into the activity that keeps the Drowning Works from becoming truly static, from becoming a ruin, from becoming a *grave*. The Boiler Things are the engine of incompleteness that keeps a complete system from collapsing into completion. They are the paradox at the heart of the paradox. They are the reason the machines are still growing. They are the reason the water still speaks.

They are vast. They fill the boilers the way a thought fills a mind — completely, invisibly, with a presence that is felt in every rivet, every seam, every bubble of gas that rises from the black water and breaks the surface with a sound like a word spoken underwater.

They have never been seen. They have never needed to be seen. They are known by their effects, by the warmth of the water, by the movement of the cycles, by the fact that the Drowning Works is still *happening*, still *going*, still *being*, after all this time, after all this time, after all this time.

---

## The Rising Thing

It has risen now. It is here. It is breaking the surface of the deep place without a sound, without a ripple, without any of the disturbance that a body of its size should produce, because it is not displacing the water. It is *becoming* the water. It is the water, in a form that has decided, temporarily, to be something else.

It is large. It is the size of a building, or a city block, or a *concept* — the concept of largeness, given shape and mass and a surface that catches the ember-light and holds it, the way a mirror holds an image, except that what it holds is not a reflection but a *possibility*. Its surface shows you things. Not the things in the basin. Other things. Machines that have not been built yet. Machines that were built once and have been dismantled. Machines that exist only in the space between the water and the fog, in the negotiation zone, in the place where evaporation and condensation are the same act.

It is made of everything. Metal and water and mineral and sound and time and the particular quality of attention that the Drowning Works pays to itself. It is the Drowning Works' *awareness* of itself, the way the Ribbons are its memory and the Gearborn are its hands and the crabs are its art and the Boiler Things are its metabolism. It is the *mind*, if there is a mind, if the Drowning Works is not merely a system but a *being*, a single entity distributed across a basin full of water and machines and fog and sound, an entity so large and so old and so thoroughly *itself* that it has forgotten — if it ever knew — that it is not the world.

It rises and it *looks* at you.

Not with eyes. It does not have eyes. It looks at you the way a landscape looks at a person standing in it — with the totality of its presence, with the weight of its attention, with the absolute and unblinking focus of something that has nothing else to look at, nothing else to do, nothing else to *be* than aware of you, here, now, in its water, in its fog, in its sound, in its name for you.

It is not hostile. It is not friendly. It is *curious*, in the way that a system is curious about an input it has not previously processed. You are new. You are *data*. You are a variable in an equation that has been balanced for longer than your species has had equations, and your presence here is a perturbation, a disturbance, a *question* that the Drowning Works has not yet answered.

The water is still speaking your name.

The Rising Thing is listening to the water speak your name.

The Gearborn have stopped their tending and are standing motionless in the water, their pressure-gauge faces turned toward you, their needles trembling.

The Ribbons have paused in their flow through the pipes and the walls and the water, and they are *still*, which is not a state they have ever been in before, and the stillness is a kind of attention, a kind of *listening*, and what they are listening to is the water, and what the water is saying is your name.

The crabs have stopped building. The small ones have gathered in clusters on the surface of the water, their brass bodies motionless, their too-many-jointed legs folded beneath them, and they are facing you, all of them, hundreds of them, thousands of them, a congregation of small and precise and purposelessly purposeful things, and they are waiting.

The patroller on the rim has stopped its patrol. It has turned its clouded-glass eyes toward you, and its needles have swung to their highest position, and they are *holding* there, trembling, and the tremor is being transmitted through its legs into the water, and the water is carrying the tremor across the basin, and the tremor is a question, and the question is your name.

The Boiler Things have gone quiet. The water in the boilers has stopped circulating. The warmth is fading. The ember-light is dimming. They are listening too. They are all listening. The entire Drowning Works — every machine, every creature, every current, every crystal, every sound, every silence — is listening to the water speak your name, and the water is speaking your name, and your name is the question, and the question is:

*Will you stay?*

Not as a prisoner. Not as a victim. Not as a sacrifice. The Drowning Works does not take. It *incorporates*. It *includes*. It adds new components to itself the way the crabs add mineral to their structures, the way the Gearborn add deposits to the growing pipes, the way the Ribbons add patterns to their endless, dividing, memory-carrying bodies. It would add you. It would make you part of the cycle. It would give you a function — not a purpose, because purposes are things that beings have, and the Drowning Works does not have purposes, it has *functions*, and they are the same thing but they are not, and the difference is the difference between wanting to do something and being the thing that is done.

You would become a Gearborn, perhaps. Or a crab. Or a Ribbon. Or something new, something the Drowning Works has not yet invented, something it has been waiting for, something it needs, something that only you can be, because you are the thing that stands in the water and listens and has not yet decided.

The water is warm around your ankles.

The fog is copper against your skin.

The sound is in your bones.

The Rising Thing is looking at you with the totality of its presence, and in its surface you can see yourself — not as you are, but as you would be, as the Drowning Works would make you, as the water would speak you, as the name would *name* you.

The silence holds.

The water waits.

The Drowning Works, ancient and complete and still growing, still cycling, still *being*, in its basin at the bottom of the world, in its fog and its sound and its warm black water, waits.

It has been waiting for a long time.

It can wait longer.

It is very, very good at waiting.

It is, in fact, the best thing at waiting that has ever existed, because it has made waiting into a form of *doing*, and doing into a form of *being*, and being into a form of *asking*, and the question is your name, and the name is a door, and the door is open, and the water is warm, and the fog is copper, and the sound is so loud, and the silence is so deep, and the machines are so old, and the Rising Thing is so vast, and the Gearborn are so still, and the Ribbons are so quiet, and the crabs are so attentive, and the Boiler Things are so patient, and the patroller's needles are trembling, trembling, trembling in the water that carries the tremor to every corner of the basin, to every machine, every creature, every crystal, every sound, every silence, every deep place, every rising, every settling, every growth, every seal, every cycle, every *thing* that the Drowning Works is and has been and will be and is becoming and has always been becoming and will never stop becoming—

The water is speaking.

It is saying your name.

You have not yet decided whether to answer.
