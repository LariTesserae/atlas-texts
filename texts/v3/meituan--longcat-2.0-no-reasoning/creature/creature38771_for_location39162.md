# The Inhabitants of Vael Koth

## The People

They are small. Not unusually so by human standards, but they carry themselves as though they have learned to occupy less space than their bodies require — a posture born not of timidity but of practicality, of a lifetime spent navigating doorways built for vren, stepping around keth aggregations, ducking under low-hanging pipes slick with condensation. They move through their city the way water moves through it: finding the path of least resistance, flowing around obstacles rather than confronting them.

Their skin has the particular quality of people who have never been truly dry. It is not unhealthy — not the pallor of illness or the roughness of neglect — but it has a sheen to it, a slight tautness, as though the outermost layer has been permanently softened by the humidity and has never quite recovered. Their hands are the most affected: the pads of their fingers are smooth, almost polished, the whorls of their fingerprints worn faint by constant moisture. They do not shake hands when they greet each other. They press their palms together, briefly, a gesture that makes more sense in a world where a grip might slip.

Their eyes are adapted to the dimness — not in any dramatic biological sense, but in the way that a person's eyes adapt to any environment they have known since birth. They are comfortable in low light. They move through the dark streets with a confidence that would seem uncanny to an outsider, navigating by the sound of water in the channels, the warmth radiating from factory walls, the subtle shift in air pressure that indicates an open space versus a covered walkway. They do not carry lights often. When they do, the lights are small and directional — narrow beams that illuminate only the immediate path, leaving the rest of the world in its comfortable gray-dark.

They are quiet people. Not silent — they talk, they laugh, they argue — but their voices have a particular quality shaped by the acoustics of their environment. The humid air absorbs sound. The constant background hum of machinery and water fills the frequencies that human speech would otherwise occupy. So they speak closer to each other than people in dry climates do, and their voices are lower, more modulated, carrying less projection and more intimacy. A conversation in Vael Koth is almost always a private affair, not because the speakers intend privacy but because the city itself enforces it. Sound does not travel. It sinks into the wet air and is absorbed by the stone and lost in the shushing of water.

Their clothing is functional to the point of austerity. Close-fitting layers of synthetic fiber — traded from the highlands, or produced in the city's own textile mills from mineral-derived polymers — that shed water rather than absorb it. The garments are dark, not by choice but by necessity: light-colored fabric in Vael Koth would show every mineral stain, every rust deposit, every smear of the iridescent bacterial film that coats every surface. Dark fabric simply becomes *darker*, a gradual accumulation of grime that is accepted as inevitable. They do not wash their clothes so much as *rinse* them — a process that removes the loose material but leaves the deep mineral staining permanently embedded in the fibers.

They wear boots with thick, textured soles — the one item of clothing that receives genuine care and attention. A bad boot in Vael Koth is a serious liability. The wet stone is treacherous, and a fall in the factory districts, among the machinery and the heat and the hard-shelled fauna, can be fatal. The boots are made from a dense, vulcanized material that grips the stone, and they are replaced regularly, and the old ones are not discarded but recycled — the material broken down and reprocessed, because in Vael Koth, nothing is wasted.

Their hair, if they have it, is kept short or bound tightly against the scalp. Loose hair in this environment becomes a liability — it traps moisture against the neck and head, it catches in machinery, it provides habitat for the smaller keth, which will nest in any warm, damp crevice. Some people shave their heads entirely. Others wear close-fitting caps of the same synthetic fiber as their clothing. The overall effect is of a population that has stripped away everything unnecessary, every vanity and ornament, and kept only what functions.

But they are not grim. This is important. They are not a suffering people, not a downtrodden people, not a people who have been broken by their environment. They are *adapted*. There is a difference. They have found a way to live in Vael Koth that is not merely survivable but *livable*, and in the small spaces of their lives — in the warmth of a shared meal in a dry room, in the sound of a voice close to an ear, in the satisfaction of a machine running well — there is contentment. Not joy, exactly. Joy is a bright thing, and Vael Koth is not a bright place. But a deep, quiet satisfaction, the feeling of a system working as it should, of a person occupying their proper place in a coherent whole.

They have a particular relationship with time. The city does not change quickly. The buildings have been in their current shapes for generations. The industrial processes are old and well-established. The water flows as it has always flowed. The fauna follow their ancient routes. In this context, urgency is not just unnecessary but *inappropriate* — a disruption to the rhythm of things. They work steadily, not quickly. They make decisions slowly, after long consideration. They do not rush. Rushing in Vael Koth is dangerous — on the wet stone, around the vren, near the machinery — and it is also *rude*, a kind of violence against the city's tempo. The people of Vael Koth move at the city's pace, which is the pace of water seeping through stone, which is the pace of mineral deposits accumulating, which is the pace of a vren scraping a wall.

They are not numerous. The city could hold more people than it does, and the industrial capacity could support a larger population, but there is a natural equilibrium that has been reached — a number of humans that the system can sustain without strain, that the fauna can tolerate without disruption, that the city's infrastructure can support without degradation. Birth rates are low but stable. People leave occasionally — drawn to the highlands by the promise of dry air and open sky — but they rarely stay away. The highlands are too bright, too dry, too *loud*. The silence of Vael Koth is not the absence of sound but the presence of a particular kind of sound, and people who grow up hearing it find its absence elsewhere to be a kind of deafness.

## The Keth

They are the city's true population, outnumbering the humans by orders of magnitude, and they are the city's true *citizens* in a sense that the humans have long since acknowledged without resentment. The keth were here first. The keth will be here after. The humans are, in the keth's indifferent calculus, simply another feature of the environment — large, warm, slow-moving structures that occasionally disturb the bacterial films and then move on.

A single keth is unremarkable. It is flat — remarkably flat, its body compressed to perhaps two millimeters at its thickest point, allowing it to press itself into the thinnest crevices in the stone, to slide beneath the edge of a door, to flatten against a pipe and become nearly invisible. Its carapace is segmented into overlapping plates that shift independently as the creature moves, giving it a flexibility that belies its armored exterior. The plates are hydrophobic on their outer surface — water beads on them and rolls off — but hydrophilic on their inner surface, where a thin film of moisture is maintained against the body. The keth breathes through this film, exchanging gases with the water directly, and it is this adaptation that ties it inextricably to the wet environment of Vael Koth. A keth removed from the humidity of the basin will die within hours, not from heat or cold but from the simple inability to breathe through a drying membrane.

Its legs — there are fourteen of them, arranged in two rows along the ventral surface — are short, fine, and tipped with microscopic barbs that find purchase on the stone's surface texture. The keth does not grip so much as *adhere*, using a combination of mechanical friction and a thin secretion that bonds temporarily to the mineral surface. This allows them to climb vertical walls and traverse ceilings with the same ease as flat ground. A wall in Vael Keth that appears bare from a distance is, on closer inspection, a landscape of tiny moving bodies, each one oriented according to its own immediate needs — toward food, toward warmth, toward the chemical signals of other keth.

The iridescence of their carapaces is not pigment but structure — the microscopic geometry of the plates refracts the dim light of the city into shifting colors that range from deep blue to violet to a pale, oil-slick rainbow. When a large aggregation of keth moves across a surface, the effect is of a living aurora, a slow-moving field of color that shifts and shimmers as the individual creatures adjust their positions. It is, by the standards of Vael Koth, beautiful — one of the few things in the city that could be called beautiful without qualification.

They feed on the bacterial films that coat every wet surface in the city — the iridescent sheets of microorganisms that are the base of Vael Koth's entire food chain. The keth scrape these films from the stone with specialized mouthparts: a set of fine, comb-like mandibles that sweep across the surface in rapid strokes, collecting the bacteria and the dissolved minerals they have concentrated. The keth's digestive system is inefficient by most standards — it extracts only a fraction of the available nutrients from its food — but this inefficiency is, paradoxically, essential to the city's function. What the keth excrete is a concentrated mineral paste, richer in usable compounds than the original bacterial film, and this paste accumulates on the stone surfaces in thin, crusty deposits that the humans harvest directly or that the vren scrape and further concentrate.

The keth are not social in the way that ants or bees are social. They do not have a hive mind, a queen, a caste system. But they are *responsive* to each other in ways that produce emergent behavior indistinguishable from coordination. They follow chemical trails left by other keth — trails that indicate food sources, warm surfaces, safe harbor. They aggregate in response to humidity gradients, clustering where the air is thickest and dispersing when conditions dry. They synchronize their activity cycles to the city's day-night rhythm, becoming more active in the darkness when the human population thins and the risk of being crushed diminishes.

At night, the keth emerge in their true numbers. During the day, they are present but partially hidden — pressed into crevices, clustered in the shade of pipes, flattened against the undersides of ledges. But when the furnaces dim and the human foot traffic eases, they spread. They cover the ground in rippling sheets. They climb the walls in columns. They fill the spaces between machinery with a slow, clicking, shimmering mass of bodies. The sound of them at night is extraordinary — not loud, but *dense*, a texture of sound rather than a volume, the collective noise of millions of tiny legs on stone, tiny mandibles scraping, tiny carapaces clicking against each other as the aggregations shift and flow.

The humans do not fear the keth. They do not love them either. The relationship is more fundamental than either of those emotions — it is *ecological*. The keth are part of the system that keeps the humans alive. They concentrate the minerals that the humans harvest. They process the bacterial films that would otherwise clog the water channels. They are, in a very real sense, the city's metabolism, the living machinery that turns the basin's mineral wealth into a form that can be used. The humans know this. They step around keth aggregations not out of disgust but out of *respect* — the same respect they would show a functioning machine, an acknowledgment that this thing has a purpose and that disrupting it would be foolish.

Children in Vael Koth grow up with the keth as a constant presence, as unremarkable as the water or the stone. They learn early not to press their hands flat on walls, where the keth will crawl over their fingers in a sensation that outsiders find unbearable but that the children find merely *interesting* — the tickle of tiny legs, the cool smoothness of carapaces, the faint chemical smell that the keth produce, sharp and mineral, like wet slate. Some children keep keth in small containers, watching them move across the transparent walls, watching them feed, watching them molt — a process in which the carapace splits along predetermined seams and the keth emerges soft and pale and vulnerable, hardening over the course of hours into a new, slightly larger armor. It is the closest thing to a pet that exists in Vael Koth, and it is a relationship of pure observation — the keth does not recognize its keeper, does not respond to handling, does not modify its behavior in the presence of a human. It simply *is*, and the child simply watches, and something is learned about the nature of coexistence.

## The Canal Dwellers

The long, segmented creatures that undulate through the city's waterways have no name in the local language that translates cleanly. The closest term is a word that means something like "the ones who are in the water" — a description so obvious that it barely qualifies as a name, but which reflects the humans' relationship with these creatures: they are defined entirely by their habitat, by the fact of their presence in the canals, and nothing else about them seems to require specification.

They are pale. Translucently pale, their bodies a milky, opalescent white that reveals the dark line of their digestive tract running the length of their segmented body like a thread of ink in candle wax. They are long — most are between half a meter and a meter, though specimens of nearly two meters have been reported in the deeper channels at the city's edges — and they move with a slow, sinuous undulation that propels them through the ankle-deep water with surprising efficiency. Their bodies are flexible in the extreme, capable of compressing to pass through gaps a fraction of their resting diameter, and this flexibility allows them to navigate the city's water system with an ease that the humans, confined to their boats and bridges, cannot match.

They are scavengers. They feed on the organic detritus that washes through the city's water system — fragments of bacterial film, dead keth, mineral paste, the occasional piece of human waste. Their digestive systems are robust, capable of processing material that would be toxic to most organisms, and their role in the city's ecology is primarily one of *cleaning*. They keep the water channels clear of accumulated organic matter. They prevent the bacterial films from growing so thick that they block the flow. They are the city's waste processing system, as essential in their way as the industrial machinery that the humans maintain.

They are not aggressive. They have no teeth, no stingers, no defensive mechanisms beyond a tendency to thrash vigorously when handled, which can deliver a stinging slap from their muscular bodies but nothing more. They do not bite. They do not attack. They do not even *notice* the humans, in any meaningful sense — their sensory systems are tuned to chemical gradients in the water, to the presence of food, to the temperature and mineral content of their environment, and the humans, standing above the water on their bridges and boats, are simply not part of the sensory world of the canal dwellers.

But they are *numerous*, and their presence shapes the city's relationship with its waterways in profound ways. The canals are not merely transportation routes; they are *habitats*, thick with living bodies, and the humans who travel by boat must navigate through them with a care that is not quite caution and not quite courtesy but something in between. The flat-bottomed boats are pushed by poles, and the pole-man must feel for the resistance of the water and the bodies within it, adjusting the pole's placement to avoid the densest aggregations — not because harming the canal dwellers is forbidden, but because a pole thrust into a mass of them will come up coated in their pale, gelatinous secretions, and the creatures will thrash and tangle around the pole, and the whole business is unpleasant and unnecessary.

The canal dwellers breed in the lower reaches of the system, where the water is deeper and slower and the mineral content is highest. Their reproductive cycle is tied to the seasonal variations in the basin's water chemistry — subtle shifts in mineral concentration that trigger mass spawning events, during which the water in the lower canals becomes cloudy with gametes and the surface boils with the activity of mating adults. These events are brief — a day or two — and they leave behind a new generation of tiny, almost invisible larvae that drift through the water system, settling in suitable habitats and growing with a speed that seems impossible given the slow pace of everything else in Vael Koth. Within a year, a larva can reach full size. Within two years, it can reproduce. The canal dwellers live for perhaps five years, and in that time, they will process an astonishing volume of organic material, their bodies turning waste into the concentrated mineral deposits that settle on the canal floors and are periodically dredged by the humans.

The dredging is a communal activity, performed by teams of workers who wade into the lower canals with long-handled scrapers, pulling the accumulated deposits from the channel floors and loading them into boats. The canal dwellers flow around the workers' legs, indifferent, their pale bodies pressing briefly against human skin before sliding away. The workers do not flinch. The sensation is familiar — cool, smooth, slightly yielding, like being touched by water that has decided, briefly, to have a shape.

## The Clickers

In the factory districts, where the air shimmers with heat and the stone is warm to the touch even through boot soles, a different fauna prevails. The creatures the humans call clickers — another name that describes rather than identifies, a reference to the sound they make — are compact, hard-shelled, and built for heat in a way that the keth and the canal dwellers are not.

They are roughly the size of a human fist, though some are smaller and a few are larger, and their bodies are encased in a carapace of extraordinary density — a composite of chitin and mineral deposits that gives them a weight disproportionate to their size and a resilience that makes them nearly indestructible by casual means. A keth crushed underfoot produces a wet pop and a smear of organic material. A clicker crushed underfoot produces a sharp *crack* and a scattering of hard fragments, and the foot that crushed it may be bruised. Their carapaces are so dense that they ring faintly when struck, a high, clear note that is the origin of their name — though the clicking sound they produce in life is lower, a rhythmic tapping created by the movement of their legs against their own carapaces as they shift position.

They nest in the warm spaces behind furnaces, along steam conduits, in the gaps between machinery components where heat accumulates. They are thermophilic to a degree that borders on the extreme — they thrive at temperatures that would kill most arthropods, and they will actively seek out heat sources, clustering around warm pipes and surfaces in dense aggregations that pulse with the collective clicking of thousands of bodies. The sound is constant in the factory districts, a background texture as ubiquitous as the hum of machinery, and the workers have learned to read it the way a sailor reads the wind. A change in the clicking's rhythm or intensity can indicate a shift in the thermal environment — a pipe cooling, a furnace cycling, a new heat source appearing — and experienced workers will respond to these changes before their instruments register them.

The clickers feed on the mineral deposits that accumulate in the factory environment — the scale that forms on pipes, the crust that builds on furnace walls, the residue that collects on machinery. They scrape these deposits with mouthparts that are harder than the mineral itself, reinforced with metallic compounds extracted from the deposits they consume, and their excretions are a fine, dry powder of concentrated minerals that the humans collect and add to their harvest. The clickers are, in this sense, a secondary processing system — they take the raw mineral deposits that the keth and canal dwellers have already concentrated and refine them further, producing a material of exceptional purity that is used in the city's most demanding industrial applications.

They are not indifferent to humans in the way that the keth and canal dwellers are. They are *aware* of humans, in a limited sense — they respond to vibration, to the heat of a human body, to the chemical signature of human sweat — and they will sometimes climb onto a resting worker, drawn by the warmth, settling against a leg or an arm with a cluster of tiny, gripping legs and a faint, rhythmic clicking that some workers find soothing. The workers do not encourage this, but they do not discourage it either. A clicker on your arm is a sign that your body heat is normal, that you are not feverish, that you are alive and warm and part of the thermal landscape of the factory. It is, in its small way, a comfort.

The clickers reproduce slowly — a single clutch of eggs per year, laid in the deepest, warmest crevices of the factory infrastructure, where the temperature is stable and the mineral supply is abundant. The eggs are tiny, hard, and nearly indistinguishable from the mineral deposits around them, and they hatch into miniature versions of the adults that immediately begin feeding and growing. A clicker takes three years to reach full size and can live for fifteen or twenty, and in that time, it will process a remarkable quantity of mineral material, its body a slow, patient engine of refinement.

## The Vren

They are the city's giants, and they move through it with the unhurried authority of geological processes.

A vren is large — the size of a small cattle, perhaps a meter and a half at the shoulder, with a body that is broad, low, and heavy, supported by six legs arranged in three pairs along its length. Its carapace is thick and dark, not the iridescent blue-black of the keth but a deep, matte charcoal that absorbs light rather than reflecting it, giving the vren a quality of *visual weight* — it looks heavy because it looks like it is absorbing the light around it, like a hole in the world shaped like a living thing. The carapace is not smooth; it is textured with ridges and grooves that follow the contours of the body, channels that direct water and mineral deposits across the surface in patterns that are unique to each individual, so that the humans who know the vren can identify them by their carapace patterns the way a shepherd knows individual sheep by their faces.

The head is broad and flat, wider than it is long, and it is dominated by the mouthparts — a complex array of scraping plates and grinding surfaces designed for the single task that defines the vren's existence: the removal of mineral deposits from stone. The mouthparts work in a slow, rhythmic motion, the plates sliding against each other with a sound like wet stone grinding on wet stone, and they are capable of removing mineral crusts of remarkable hardness, reducing them to a fine paste that the vren swallows and processes in a digestive system that is, like the keth's, inefficient in a way that serves the larger system. What the vren excretes is a mineral concentrate of extraordinary richness — the most valuable product of Vael Koth's biological economy, the material that is traded to the highlands at the highest prices.

The vren move through the city on routes that are ancient — older than the city itself, older than the humans, older perhaps than the current configuration of the basin's seepage patterns. They follow the mineral-rich water, moving from one seepage site to the next, scraping the deposits that have accumulated since their last visit, and their routes form a network that overlays the city's street grid like a palimpsest. Where a vren route crosses a street, the street widens. Where a vren route passes through a building, the doorway is tall and broad. Where a vren route intersects with another vren route, there is an open space — a plaza, a junction, a place where the giants can pass each other without conflict.

The humans built the city around the vren routes, not the other way around. This is a fact that the humans acknowledge without resentment, because the vren routes follow the mineral seepage, and the mineral seepage is the reason the city exists at all. To build across a vren route would be to block access to the mineral deposits, and that would be like building across a river — possible, perhaps, but foolish, a disruption of something more fundamental than any human construction.

The vren are slow. A vren covering a kilometer in an hour is moving quickly; most move at half that speed or less, their six legs lifting and placing with a deliberation that suggests each step is being considered individually. They do not hurry. They cannot hurry — their bodies are too heavy, their metabolism too slow, their carapace too thick to allow rapid movement. But they are *relentless*. A vren on a route will not stop for anything short of a physical barrier. It will not deviate. It will not accelerate. It will simply continue, and the humans who share its path must adjust.

This is not as difficult as it sounds. The vren routes are well-known, and the vren themselves are predictable — they follow the same paths, at the same pace, with the same stops, day after day, year after year. The humans know when a vren will pass a particular point, and they plan accordingly. A worker crossing a vren route will pause, wait for the creature to pass, and continue. A boat on a canal will pole to the side and let a vren wade through the water, its broad body displacing a wave that rocks the boat gently. A conversation in a street will pause, the speakers pressing against a wall to let a vren's body pass within arm's reach, and then resume without comment, the interruption as unremarkable as a change in the weather.

The vren are not domesticated. They are not trained. They are not controlled. They are *accommodated*, and the distinction is important. A domesticated animal has been bred for compliance, for a willingness to serve human purposes. A vren has not. A vren follows its route because the route leads to food, and it tolerates the humans because the humans have never given it a reason not to. The relationship is not one of mastery but of *coexistence* — a mutual adjustment that has been refined over centuries into something that looks, from the outside, like harmony but is, on closer inspection, simply two species occupying the same space without conflict.

There are perhaps two hundred vren in the city at any given time, and their population is stable — births and deaths balanced over the long term. They reproduce rarely, a single offspring every five to ten years, and the young are raised not by their parents but by the *city itself* — a vren calf, if it can be called that, is simply a smaller vren that follows its mother's route until it is large enough to establish its own, learning the path by repetition, by the chemical traces left on the stone, by some inherited knowledge that the humans do not fully understand and the vren cannot articulate.

The vren live for a long time. No one knows exactly how long — the oldest vren in the city have been there for as long as anyone can remember, and their carapace patterns are known to every resident, and they are referred to by names that are not quite names but descriptions: the One with the Broken Ridge, the Wide-Headed One, the Slow One Who Passes at Dawn. These are not pets. These are not mascots. These are *neighbors* — large, slow, ancient neighbors who have been here longer than anyone and who will, in all likelihood, be here after everyone is gone, scraping their mineral deposits from the weeping stone, following their routes through a city that has crumbled to rubble, indifferent to the absence of the small, warm creatures who once stepped carefully around their legs.

## The System

What lives in Vael Koth is not a collection of separate species but a single, integrated system — a living machine in which every component, from the bacterial films to the humans, plays a role that cannot be removed without disrupting the whole. The water feeds the bacteria. The bacteria feed the keth. The keth concentrate the minerals. The vren refine the minerals. The humans harvest the minerals and maintain the infrastructure that shapes the water flow that feeds the bacteria. The industry produces heat that shapes the fauna's behavior. The fauna's behavior shapes the city's layout. The city's layout shapes the industry's placement.

It is a closed loop, or nearly so, and it hums along at its own pace, and the people who live within it have learned — over generations, over centuries — to hum along with it. They have learned that the city is not a place that contains life but a place that *is* life, a living system in which they are participants rather than masters, and they have found in that participation a kind of peace that is not available to people who believe they control their environment.

The city sweeps. The water seeps. The keth click. The vren scrape. The canal dwellers undulate. The humans work, and wait, and listen to the sound of the city breathing around them — a slow, wet, mineral breath that has been drawing and releasing for longer than anyone can remember, and that will continue, patient and relentless, long after the last human has gone.
