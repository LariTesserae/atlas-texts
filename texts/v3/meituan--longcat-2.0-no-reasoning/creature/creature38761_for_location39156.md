# The Kethari

---

They are small. Not stunted — *small*, the way a bird is small, the way a fish is small, a body shaped by its medium into something efficient and precise. The tallest among them would barely reach your shoulder. Their limbs are long relative to their torsos, their fingers longer still, webbed between the second and third knuckles with a thin membrane that retracts when they work and spreads when they swim. Their skin is pale — not white, but the pale of things that live without direct sun, a translucent grey-green that shows the darker structures beneath: the branching patterns of veins, the faint outlines of bones, and always, always, the gills.

The gills are the first thing you notice. They grow along the ribcage in two curved rows, one on each side, emerging from the skin like the fronds of some dark plant. They are red — deep red, almost black at the base, brightening to a vivid arterial crimson at the tips where the tissue is newest, most active. They move. Constantly. A slow, rhythmic flutter, independent of the Kethari's breathing, independent of their heartbeat, a motion that belongs to the gill-creature itself, the symbiont doing its work. When a Kethari is at rest, the gills are calm, barely rippling. When they exert themselves, the flutter quickens, deepens, and the singing rises in pitch. When they are afraid, the gills clamp tight against the ribs — a protective reflex — and the singing stops entirely, which is itself a signal, a silence that other Kethari read as clearly as a scream.

Their faces are broad, flat, with wide-set eyes that have adapted to the dim green light — large irises, pupils that can dilate to an extraordinary degree, a nictitating membrane that slides across the eye when they move through debris-heavy water. They have no external ears. Sound reaches them through the water and through the bones of their skulls, and they are sensitive to vibration in a way that air-breathers are not — they feel the deep sound in their teeth, in their sternum, in the roots of their gills. Their noses are small, nearly vestigial; smell is not a primary sense underwater. Their mouths are wide, their lips thin, and when they speak, the sounds they produce are shaped for a liquid medium — low, resonant, with a quality that is less like speech and more like the songs of whales, carrying meaning through tone and duration as much as through phoneme.

They do not wear clothes in any conventional sense. Their skin is sufficient protection, and the water is warm enough that insulation is unnecessary. But they carry things — tools, instruments, cord-containers — on woven belts of kelp-fiber that wrap around their waists and cross their chests. Some wear adornments: small carved pieces of elath-wood, polished smooth, hung on kelp-strings around their necks or wrists. These are not decorative in the way that jewelry is decorative. They are *identifying* — each carving encodes information about the wearer's family-line, their tik-colony affiliation, their role in the settlement's maintenance. A Kethari can read another Kethari's history by touching these carvings, running their fingers over the grooves and knots, the way a scholar might read a book.

## The Tik-Listeners

Every Kethari learns to listen to the walls. But some listen more deeply, and these are the tik-listeners — the ones who press their ears to the elath-bark and hear not just the clicking but the *meaning* of the clicking, the patterns that indicate health or disease, growth or decay, the slow conversation between the tik-colons and the wood they inhabit.

Tik-listeners develop differently. They spend more time in quiet chambers, in darkness, in stillness. Their hearing sharpens — not just their ears but their whole bodies, their sensitivity to vibration, their ability to feel a tik's signal through the soles of their feet, through their fingertips, through the roots of their gills. Over years, this changes them. Their gill-symbionts grow larger, more elaborate, as though the act of deep listening requires more oxygen, more metabolic support. Their eyes, paradoxically, grow slightly smaller — not from disuse, but from a reallocation of biological resources, the body deciding that vision matters less than vibration. They move differently: slower, more deliberate, with a quality of attention that other Kethari recognize and respect.

A master tik-listener can diagnose a structural problem in the elath weeks before it becomes visible — a weakening here, a rot beginning there, a tik-colony that has turned from beneficial to destructive. They can hear the difference between the clicking of a healthy colony and the clicking of a stressed one, and they can intervene — using their bone instruments to produce counter-signals, redirecting the tik's behavior, restoring balance. This is not control. It is *conversation*. The tik-listener does not command the tik. They speak to them, and the tik, over time, learn to respond, and a relationship forms — specific, personal, irreplaceable.

When a tik-listener dies, their bone instruments are not passed on. They are buried with the listener, placed in the elath-bark near the roots, where the tik can incorporate them. The clicking patterns those instruments produced die with the listener, and the tik-colonies they managed must form new relationships with new listeners, a process that takes years and is never quite the same.

## The Morath-Handlers

The morath are large, and directing them requires a particular kind of presence — not physical strength, but a quality of calm authority that the morath respond to. Morath-handlers are the Kethari who possess this quality, and they are easy to identify: they move with a slowness that is not laziness but *patience*, a willingness to match the morath's timescale, to think in terms of hours and days rather than moments.

Their hands are different. Years of handling the morath — touching their skin, applying elath-sap commands, guiding them through the water — have thickened the skin on their palms and fingers, developed a sensitivity to chemical signals that other Kethari lack. A morath-handler can read a morath's state by touch: the temperature of its skin, the tension of its muscles, the chemical signature of its secretions. They know when a morath is willing to work and when it is not, and they do not force the issue. A morath that is pushed against its will will simply stop, become a dead weight in the water, and no amount of elath-sap or clicking will move it. The handler must wait. Must negotiate, in whatever way negotiation happens between a small pale creature and a three-meter eel-thing that does not think in words.

Morath-handlers develop a bond with specific morath — not ownership, not domestication, but a mutual recognition. The morath knows the handler's touch, responds to it more readily, trusts it in a way that is visible to other Kethari in the quality of its movement: smoother, more directed, less hesitant. When a morath's handler dies, the morath grieves — if grief is the right word for what a morath does. It becomes listless, unresponsive, its feeding slows, and it may take months before it accepts a new handler. Some morath never do. They continue to function — they eat, they move, they exist — but the quality of their responsiveness dims, and they are gradually shifted to simpler tasks, carrying loads rather than positioning platforms, and they live out their days in a kind of patient withdrawal that the Kethari recognize and mourn.

## The Root-Tenders

Deep in Kethara, where the elath's roots descend toward the stone, where the water is coldest and the light is dimmest and the deep sound is loudest, the root-tenders work. They are the Kethari who maintain the connection between the settlement and its anchor — who descend along the root-mass, inspecting, guiding, pruning where necessary, encouraging growth where the grip on the stone weakens.

This is dangerous work. The roots are old — older than the platforms, older than the settlement, perhaps older than the Kethari themselves — and they are not uniform. Some are thick and strong, woody cables as wide as a Kethari's torso, gripping the stone with a tenacity that has not weakened in generations. Others are thin, brittle, prone to snapping under the slightest stress. The root-tenders must know which is which, must read the health of a root by touch, by the quality of its surface, by the way water flows around it.

And there is the stone itself. The root-tenders are the only Kethari who touch it regularly, who press their hands against its surface and feel its warmth, its pulse, its *presence*. They do not speak of this to others. Not because it is forbidden, but because the experience is not translatable — the stone is not like anything else in Kethara, not alive in the way the elath is alive, not responsive in the way the tik are responsive, but *something*, something that the root-tenders feel in their bones and their gills and the deep places of their bodies where language does not reach.

Root-tenders are changed by their work. The cold affects them — their gill-symbionts grow slower, denser, more efficient at extracting oxygen from cold water, but less responsive, less musical. Their singing drops in pitch, becomes a low drone that other Kethari find unsettling. Their skin darkens, takes on a blue-black cast from the cold and the pressure. They move differently in the upper settlement — too slow, too careful, as though they are still navigating the root-mass, still feeling for weak points, still listening to the stone.

They are respected. They are not feared. But other Kethari give them space, the way one gives space to someone who has been somewhere you have not, who knows something you do not, who has touched the foundation of the world and been touched by it.

## The Children

Kethara's children are born in water, as all things here are born in water. The birth is quiet — the mother retreats to a chamber, attended by one or two others, and the child emerges into the warm green darkness and begins to breathe the water through its own small gills, which have been growing in the womb, seeded from the mother's symbionts in the final months of gestation.

The gills are not functional at birth. For the first weeks of life, the child's gills are closed, sealed, and the child breathes through its lungs — yes, lungs, the Kethari retain them, a vestige of whatever dry-world ancestry they possess — surfacing in the upper chambers where air pockets form against the ceilings of the elath-rooms. The child breathes air and water alternately, a dual existence that lasts until the gills open, which happens gradually, over days, the sealed fronds unfurling, the red tissue brightening, the first flutter of independent aquatic respiration beginning.

This is the most dangerous time. The transition from lung-breathing to gill-breathing is not always smooth. Some children struggle — their gills open unevenly, or not at all, or in patterns that do not support adequate oxygen extraction. These children are tended carefully, kept in the warmest, most oxygen-rich water, monitored by the settlement's healers, who apply preparations of elath-sap and fungal extract to encourage the gills' development. Most survive. Some do not. The Kethari do not ritualize this. They grieve, and they continue.

Children learn the settlement before they learn themselves. They learn to feel the tidal pulse in their bodies before they have words for it. They learn to read the clicking of tik before they can speak. They learn to swim before they can walk — walking is secondary here, a skill for platforms and walkways, while swimming is primary, the fundamental mode of movement, and a Kethari child in water is a natural thing, graceful and unafraid in a way that an adult air-breather watching from above might find unsettling.

They are incorporated into the settlement's ecology early. By the time a child can move independently, they have been introduced to a tik-colony — a small one, a young one, a colony that the child will grow with, learn from, eventually manage. The relationship begins as play — the child pressing their ear to the wall, hearing the clicking, responding with their own small bone instrument (a child's instrument, simpler than an adult's, with fewer tones). Over years, the relationship deepens, becomes serious, becomes the child's primary connection to the settlement's living infrastructure.

## The Old

The Kethari age slowly. The water, the symbionts, the absence of the sun's damage — all of this extends their lives beyond what their bodies might otherwise sustain. A Kethari who survives the dangers of childhood and the risks of root-tending and morath-handling can expect to live a long time — long enough to see the elath grow in ways that reshape the settlement, long enough to develop relationships with tik-colonies that span decades, long enough to become something that the younger Kethari regard with a mixture of respect and unease.

The old Kethari's gills change. They grow larger, more elaborate, branching into complex structures that extend beyond the ribcage, creeping along the shoulders, the upper arms, the base of the neck. The singing they produce is deep, resonant, a bass note in the settlement's collective harmonic that the younger Kethari orient to the way a sailor orients to a lighthouse — a fixed point, a reference, a sound that means *here, now, this is where we are*.

Their skin thickens, takes on a texture that resembles the elath-bark itself — rough, ridged, with a pattern of growth-lines that the younger Kethari read as a map of the settlement's history. An old Kethari's body records the settlement's changes: a thickened patch here where a platform was reinforced, a ridge there where a new chamber was grown, a smooth section where a wall was tended so frequently that the Kethari's own skin adapted to its texture.

Some old Kethari stop going to the platforms. They retreat to the deeper chambers, to the quiet places near the roots, and they press their bodies against the elath and become, in a sense, part of it — their gills merging with the elath's surface organisms, their skin blending with the bark, their singing dropping to a subsonic hum that is felt rather than heard. They are not dying. They are *settling*, becoming part of the architecture in a way that is not metaphorical, that is literal and physical and permanent. The elath grows over them, around them, incorporates their biomass into its own, and they become a living memory in the walls — a presence that the tik respond to, that the root-tenders feel, that the children press their ears to and hear, faintly, the deep hum of someone who has become the place they lived.

## The Feeling Between Them

The Kethari do not have a word for love. They have several words for *recognition* — the feeling of being known by another, of being incorporated into another's understanding, of mattering in a way that is specific and non-transferable. They have words for *elath-press* and its opposite, *elath-release* — the feeling of being held by the settlement and the feeling of being let go. They have words for the sound another Kethari's gills make, specific terms for specific harmonics that convey information about trust, concern, warning, welcome.

They touch each other frequently. Not casually — *frequently*, in the way that air-breathers make eye contact, a constant low-level communication through contact. A hand on a shoulder. Fingers brushing a forearm. The press of gills against gills in greeting — a brief, intimate contact that allows each Kethari to read the other's health, emotional state, recent activities through the chemical and thermal signatures of the symbionts. This is not romantic, though it can be. It is not familial, though it can be. It is *informational* — the Kethari equivalent of a conversation, conducted through touch, through the body, through the living systems that they carry on their skin.

They do not pair-bond in the way that some air-breather species do. Their social structure is more fluid, more ecological — clusters of Kethari form around shared tasks, shared tik-colonies, shared chambers, and these clusters shift and reform over time as the settlement's needs change. A Kethari might spend years in a cluster centered on morath-handling, then shift to a cluster centered on root-tending, then drift to the edges for a period of solitude, then return. The clusters are not families in the genetic sense — children are raised collectively, parentage is acknowledged but not emphasized, and the primary bonds are not between parents and children but between Kethari and their tik-colonies, their morath, their section of elath.

This is not cold. The Kethari are not cold. They are *warm* — warm in the way that the water is warm, warm in the way that the stone is warm, a warmth that is environmental rather than emotional, a condition of the place rather than a quality of the individual. They care for each other with a thoroughness that is structural rather than sentimental — they maintain each other's gills, monitor each other's health, adjust each other's symbionts when they detect imbalance. This is not tenderness in the way that air-breathers understand tenderness. It is *maintenance* — the same attention they give to the elath, to the tik, to the morath, extended to each other, because they are part of the same system, and the system's health depends on every component functioning well.

## What They Want

The Kethari do not want what air-breathers want. They do not want to expand, to accumulate, to build, to conquer. They want to *maintain*. They want the elath to grow well. They want the tik to thrive. They want the morath to be willing. They want the water to stay warm and the stone to hold and the deep sound to remain deep.

They want their children's gills to open.

They want the clicking to continue.

They want, in the quiet moments at low water, when they are alone in their chambers and the singing of the settlement is low and the creaking of the elath is the only sound, to feel the walls respond to their presence — a slight thickening, a slight warmth, the elath acknowledging them the way a body acknowledges its own cells — and to know that they are held, that they are known, that they are part of something that will continue after they have settled into the bark and become, at last, fully and finally, the place they have always lived in.

This is enough. This has always been enough. The Kethari do not dream of elsewhere. They dream of *here* — of the water moving through the channels, of the tik clicking in the walls, of the morath turning slowly in the green light, of the deep sound pulsing up from velath, steady and ancient and not threatening, just *there*, the way the bottom of the world is there, holding everything up.

They dream of Kethara. They are Kethara. The distinction, such as it is, does not matter.
