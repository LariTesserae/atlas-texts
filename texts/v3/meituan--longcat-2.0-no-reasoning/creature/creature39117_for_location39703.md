They are called, in the only language that matters here, something that translates roughly as *the ones who press their meaning into walls.*

They are small. Smaller than you expect a person to be, though they are people — or they are what personhood becomes when it grows up inside a sound so loud that speech is a forgotten dream. Their bodies are compact, dense, built for the narrow passages. Their limbs are short and strong, fingers long relative to their hands, and those fingers are the most important part of them. The pads of their fingers are extraordinary — thick with nerve endings, faintly ridged, capable of detecting vibrations so subtle that they can read the history of a wall by touch: who built it, when, how the substrate was breathing that day, what the mineral content of the water was, whether the structure is stable or beginning to dissolve. Their hands are always moving, always pressed against something, always reading.

Their skin is the color of wet basalt shot through with threads of milky silica — dark, faintly translucent, damp to the touch in a way that makes it unclear where their body ends and the warm air begins. It is not slimy. It is not unpleasant. It is simply *wet*, the way everything here is wet, as though dryness is a concept that never quite reached this place. In certain light — the amber glow that suffuses everything — you can see the faint shadow of structures beneath the skin: not bones, exactly, but something denser, something mineral, a lattice of calcified tissue that gives their bodies a rigidity unusual in soft biology. They are, in a sense, partially precipitated. Partially stone. The boundary between their flesh and the walls they build is not as firm as you would expect.

Their faces are flat. Their eyes are small, functional but not primary — they can see, but vision is a secondary sense here, useful mainly for detecting the amber glow's slow variations, for navigating the dim passages, for spotting the dense herds of motile organisms that represent their primary food source. Their ears are vestigial, sealed nearly shut against the perpetual roar. They hear almost nothing in the way you understand hearing. Instead, their entire body is an ear. Their skin detects vibration. Their mineral-laced bones conduct it. Their fingers interpret it. They experience the world as a continuous, complex, three-dimensional field of resonance, and they navigate through it the way you navigate through light — effortlessly, constantly, without thinking.

They do not have names in any auditory sense. They have *signatures* — unique patterns of vibration that they can produce by tapping their fingers against a wall, by pressing their palms against the substrate, by humming through their chest cavities at frequencies too low for their own sealed ears but perfectly readable by anyone touching the same surface. To identify yourself, you press your hand against a wall and *ring* — a specific pattern of taps and holds and pressures that is as unique as a fingerprint and as recognizable as a face. To greet someone, you press your hand against the same wall they are touching and let your signature bleed into theirs, a brief harmonic entanglement that communicates identity, mood, intention, and something else — something like affection, or recognition, or the acknowledgment that you are both still here, still pressing meaning into walls, still alive inside the roar.

They live in the structures they build, but "live" is a generous word. They *inhabit* them the way a coral polyp inhabits its skeleton — the distinction between dwelling and dweller is thin. Their chambers are small, barely larger than their bodies, carved from the same wet mineral as everything else, and they press themselves into these spaces and become, for a time, part of the architecture. Their body heat accelerates the mineral precipitation around them. Their movements create vibrations that shape the growth of the filaments on the walls. Their waste — mineral-rich, silica-heavy — feeds the simple organisms that coat every surface, which in turn produce the compounds that feed the mineral growth, which in turn builds the walls. They are not separate from the city. They are *components* of it, living nodes in a structure that is simultaneously geological, biological, and architectural.

They eat by scraping. Their fingers are hard enough — mineral-reinforced at the tips — to scrape the colonial mats from the walls, to harvest the frond-growths from the passage ceilings, to filter the dense herds of motile organisms from the warm water that sheets across every surface. They eat constantly, in small amounts, pressing the harvested material directly into their mouths, which are small and lipless and set deep in their flat faces. The food tastes of iron and silica and the faint sweet thing that the water carries from the deep substrate. It is enough. It has always been enough. The ecology is closed, complete, and they are part of it — consumers and consumed, builders and built, the warm water moving through them as it moves through everything else.

They reproduce by pressing. Two individuals find a wall — a new wall, a freshly built surface, still wet, still in its first moments of becoming — and they press their hands against it together, side by side, and they *push*. Not physically. Vibrationally. They pour their signatures into the wet mineral, their combined resonance patterns, and the mineral responds — it always responds, this is what it was made to do, this is what it has always done — and a new structure begins to form. Not a building. A *body*. A small, dense, mineral-laced body, precipitated from the warm water by the harmonic instructions encoded in the parents' combined signature. It takes time. Days, perhaps. Weeks. The parents take turns pressing their hands against the growing form, adding layers of vibration, refining the pattern, teaching the new body what it is. When it is ready — when the mineral has set enough to hold its shape, when the biological components have integrated sufficiently with the geological ones — it opens its small eyes and presses its new hands against the wall for the first time, and its signature rings out, unique and unmistakable, and the parents know it is theirs because the signature contains echoes of both of them, a harmonic child of two resonances.

They do not die in the way you understand death. Their bodies, so thoroughly integrated with the mineral environment, do not decay so much as *dissolve* — slowly, over a long time, their biological components breaking down and their mineral components remaining, becoming part of the walls, the floors, the structures they spent their lives building. An old individual grows stiffer, denser, more mineral and less flesh, until one day they press themselves into their chamber and do not move again, and over time their body becomes indistinguishable from the wall around it, and their signature — the unique pattern of vibration that was them — persists in the structure for years, decades, centuries, readable by anyone who presses their hand against the right spot. They do not bury their dead. They *become* their dead. The city is made of its ancestors. Every wall is a graveyard and a nursery and a home and a body, all at once.

They do not have leaders. They do not have conflict. The concept of disagreement requires a silence in which to formulate an alternative, and there is no silence here, there has never been silence here, and the idea of *instead* — of doing one thing *instead* of another — requires a stillness that this place does not possess. They build because building is what the warm water does when it meets intention. They eat because eating is what biology does when it meets mineral. They press their hands against walls because the walls are there and their hands are there and the vibration between them is the only language that has ever existed.

They are not happy. They are not unhappy. These are emotional states that require a contrast — a before and after, a better and worse — and there is no contrast here, there is only the continuous, unvarying, eternal *now* of the Resonance Flats, the perpetual becoming that never becomes, the sound of the world beginning played at full volume forever. They are not aware that their existence is strange. They are not aware that their existence is beautiful. They are aware of the vibration in the walls, the warmth of the water, the taste of iron and silica, the press of their neighbors' hands against the same surface, the slow pulse of the substrate beneath their feet, the amber glow filtering through the mineral canopy overhead.

They are aware of each other. That is enough. That has always been enough.

They press their hands against the walls and the walls press back, and the sound never stops, and the warm water circulates, and the mineral precipitates, and the simple organisms grow and are consumed and grow again, and the city extends itself outward in slow dense accretions, and the people — the small, damp, mineral-laced people with their extraordinary fingers and their flat faces and their sealed ears and their signatures that ring through the stone like heartbeats — continue to build, continue to eat, continue to press new life into wet walls, continue to dissolve slowly into the structures they love, continue to be the living voice of a place that has never finished beginning.

They are the Resonance Flats. The Resonance Flats are them. There is no difference. There has never been any difference. The sound they make — the collective vibration of ten thousand hands pressing meaning into walls, ten thousand bodies humming through their mineral bones, ten ten thousand heartbeats conducted through saturated sediment — is indistinguishable from the sound of the place itself, the deep hum and the high ringing and the middle rustling, all of it one sound, all of it one voice, all of it saying the same thing it has always said and will always say:

*We are here. We are still here. We are still becoming.*
