# The Dwellers of the Rootdark

---

## I. The Mycelial Mind

The first thing that lives in the rootdark — the *truly* first thing, the thing that was here before the trees, before the vines, before the first animal crawled into the green dark and decided to stay — is the fungal network.

It is not a single organism. It is not even a single species. It is a *civilization* of fungi, a consortium, a parliament of decay and connection that has been growing in the rootdark for so long that its oldest threads have become structural — load-bearing, in places, woven into the root-lattice itself so thoroughly that no one can say where the fungus ends and the tree begins.

The visible parts are the fruiting bodies: the shelves and brackets and cups and coral-forms that erupt from dead wood, from living bark, from the surface of the root-platforms, from the walls of hollow trunks. They glow. That steady blue-green luminescence that turns the rootdark into a landscape of spectral veins — that is them. They are *always* glowing. Not brightly. Not enough to read by. But enough that the darkness here is never truly dark, never truly empty. The light is cold and even and directionless, and it makes everything look like a memory of itself.

But the visible parts are trivial. The real organism is underground — or rather, *in* the wood, *in* the root-mass, *in* the living tissue of the trees themselves. The mycelial network threads through everything. It connects everything. A hyphal filament is a tenth the width of a human hair, and there are kilometers of them in every cubic meter of root-lattice, and they are *talking*.

They talk in chemistry. In gradients of sugar and nitrogen and phosphorus. In signals that pass from root to root, from tree to tree, from the living to the dead and back again. The network is the rootdark's nervous system — not a brain, not a mind in any way a human would recognize, but a *processing system*, a distributed intelligence that tracks the state of the forest in real time. It knows where the water is. It knows where the nutrients are. It knows where a tree is dying and where a tree is thriving and where a gap has opened in the canopy above and light is pouring through. It routes resources accordingly. It feeds the hungry. It starves the dying. It is the economy of the rootdark, and it has been running for longer than any human economy has existed.

And it is *aware*. Not conscious — not in the way you are conscious, not with thoughts or intentions or a sense of self. But aware in the way that a market is aware, in the way that an ecosystem is aware: it responds. It adapts. It learns, in its slow chemical way, the patterns of the rootdark — the wet cycles, the dry cycles, the movements of the Mover, the seasonal pulses of growth and decay. When something new enters the rootdark — a fallen branch, a dead animal, a human being — the network registers the intrusion as a change in chemistry, a new source of carbon and nitrogen and water, and it *responds*. Hyphal threads grow toward the new resource. Enzymes are deployed. The process of incorporation begins.

This is what it means to be consumed by the rootdark. It is not violent. It is not even unpleasant, from the network's perspective. It is simply *recycling*. You are carbon. You are nitrogen. You are water and minerals and energy, and the network has use for all of these things, and so it will take them, slowly, patiently, with the absolute confidence of something that has been doing this for ten thousand years and will be doing it for ten thousand more.

The people who harvest in the rootdark know about the network. They can see it — the glowing threads in a split piece of wood, the white mycelial fans spreading across a dead branch, the fruiting bodies that appear overnight after a rain surge. They have a relationship with it. They take from it — certain fungi are edible, certain fungi are medicinal, certain fungi produce compounds that the people use for dye and adhesive and poison. But they take *carefully*. They know that the network is watching, in its way. They know that if they take too much, if they damage the mycelial web in a critical place, the network will respond — not with hostility, but with *redirection*. The fungi they depend on will stop fruiting. The medicinal species will retreat deeper into the wood. The network will route its resources elsewhere, and the harvest will fail.

So they ask. Not in words — the network does not understand words. They ask in *behavior*. They leave offerings at the base of certain trees: small amounts of food, scraps of plant material, water collected in bromeliad cups. They harvest in patterns that mimic the network's own distribution logic — taking from the edges, leaving the center intact. They move through the rootdark in ways that minimize damage to the mycelial web, stepping on the root-platforms where the network is thin, avoiding the glowing veins where it is thick.

It is not worship. It is not superstition. It is *negotiation* — a relationship between two intelligent systems, one fast and one slow, one conscious and one chemical, both trying to survive in the same space.

## II. The Columns

The millipede-things — the people call them **columns**, because that is what they look like when they move — are the rootdark's most visible animal life.

They are not millipedes. They are something older, something that branched off the arthropod lineage before the surface world's insects and crustaceans and millipedes diverged, and then evolved in isolation in the rootdark for so long that they have become something else entirely. They are large — forearm-sized, as described, but some of the old ones are larger, as long as a human leg, thick as a wrist, their segments broad and flat and pale as paper. They have many legs — not the hundred legs of a true millipede, but thirty or forty, arranged in paired rows along the underside of their bodies, each leg ending in a tiny hooked claw that grips the root-lattice with surprising tenacity.

They are blind. They have no eyes, no light-sensitive organs of any kind. They navigate by touch — by the constant, delicate probing of their antennae, which are long and feathery and exquisitely sensitive to vibration and chemical gradient — and by the stridulation that serves as both communication and echolocation. The chirring sound they produce is not random. It is *structured*. Each column has a slightly different chirr — a different frequency, a different rhythm — and the sound bounces off the root-lattice, off the trunks, off the vine-curtains, and returns to them carrying information about the shape of the space around them.

When a column moves alone, it is slow and cautious, its antennae sweeping the air in wide arcs, its chirr a thin, tentative sound, a question asked into the dark. But columns rarely move alone. They move in groups — dozens, sometimes hundreds, traveling in loose, sinuous lines through the root-mass, following routes that are not visible to human eyes but are *chemically encoded* in the root-lattice itself. The columns secrete a pheromone trail as they move, and the trail persists for days, and other columns follow it, and the trail is reinforced, and over time the trails become *highways* — chemical roads through the rootdark, worn into the living architecture by generations of passage.

The columns eat fungus. Specifically, they eat the fruiting bodies of certain mycorrhizal species — the same species that form the rootdark's glowing network. This is not a parasitic relationship. It is *symbiotic*. The columns consume the fruiting bodies, which are the reproductive structures of the fungus, and in doing so they spread the fungal spores through their digestive tracts and deposit them, in neat piles of nutrient-rich feces, in new locations throughout the rootdark. The columns are the network's *distribution system*, its seed-carriers, and the network feeds them in return — routing nutrients to the fruiting bodies that the columns prefer, ensuring a steady supply.

The people who live in the rootdark eat the columns.

This is done carefully, and with respect, and not often. The columns are abundant but they are *important* — they are the network's hands, in a sense, and removing too many of them disrupts the fungal distribution system in ways that can take years to repair. So the people take only what they need, and they take from the edges of the columns' range, and they never take from a column-highway, and they leave the old ones alone — the great, pale, legless-thick ancients that move through the root-mass like slow, living pillars, their bodies so old and so large that they have become *part of the architecture*, their segmented backs serving as walking surfaces for the smaller columns, their slow movements creating vibrations that the network uses as a kind of long-distance signal.

The old ones are not eaten. The old ones are not touched. The old ones are *observed*. The people watch them move through the rootdark, and they note the direction of their travel, and they note the speed, and they note the chirr — because the old ones' chirr is different from the young ones'. It is deeper, slower, more resonant, and it carries farther, and it means something. The people have learned to read it. A slow, steady chirr from an old one means the root-lattice is stable. A rapid, agitated chirr means a shift is coming — a platform collapsing, a gap opening, a change in the water flow. The old ones feel these things before anyone else. They are the rootdark's *seismographs*, and the people listen to them the way a sailor listens to the sea.

## III. The Tick-Wings

The flying things are not bats. They are not birds. They are not insects. They are something that has no surface analogue, something that evolved in the rootdark's three-dimensional maze of vine and branch and has become perfectly adapted to a world without open sky.

The people call them **tick-wings**, and the name is precise. Their flight is not the smooth, soaring flight of a bird or the darting, erratic flight of an insect. It is a *ratcheting* flight — a series of rapid, angular adjustments, each one accompanied by the sharp, percussive click of their echolocation call. They move through the rootdark like bullets through a pinball machine, bouncing off surfaces — not literally, but *acoustically*, each click building a momentary map of the space ahead, each adjustment redirecting them around an obstacle, through a gap, into a space that a human eye would not have seen.

Their wings are wide and soft and membranous, more like a bat's than a bird's, but with a crucial difference: they are *segmented*. Each wing is divided into three or four independent panels, each one capable of separate movement, which gives the tick-wings a maneuverability that is almost impossible to comprehend. They can hover. They can reverse direction in a space shorter than their own body length. They can fold their wings and *drop* — falling silently for a meter or two, then snapping their wings open and redirecting in a new direction, all in less than a second.

They are small. Most are the size of a human hand, some smaller, a few larger. They are dark — black, deep brown, the color of wet bark — and their bodies are covered in a fine, velvety fur that absorbs sound. This is critical. In the rootdark, where sound is information, a silent predator has an enormous advantage. The tick-wings' fur muffles the sound of their wingbeats, dampens the rush of air over their bodies, makes them *acoustically invisible* — almost. The echolocation clicks are audible, but they are brief and directional and easy to mistake for the ambient drip-and-creak of the rootdark. A tick-wing can be inches from your face and you will not hear it. You will feel the displacement of air, maybe. A faint breeze. A whisper of movement. And then it will be gone.

They eat the transparent feeders — the soft-bodied, six-legged things that cluster on the undersides of root-platforms. This is a *service*, in the rootdark's economy. The feeders, left unchecked, would multiply exponentially, consuming the condensation that the root-lattice needs, disrupting the water cycle, destabilizing the platform surfaces. The tick-wings keep them in balance. A single tick-wing can consume dozens of feeders in a night, plucking them from the root-surface with a precision that is almost surgical — a click, a hover, a snap of the wings, and the feeder is gone, swallowed whole in a single motion.

The tick-wings roost in clusters, hanging from the undersides of large root-platforms, wrapped in their own wings like dark, furry pods. During the day — such as it is in the rootdark, where day and night are only slightly different shades of green-dark — they are quiet, their echolocation switched off, their bodies conserving energy. At night, they emerge, and the rootdark fills with their clicks, and the sound is like a room full of typewriters, frantic and dry and everywhere, and the feeders cluster tighter, and the hunt begins.

The people do not eat the tick-wings. The tick-wings are too fast, too maneuverable, too well-camouflaged to be worth hunting. But the people *use* them. They use the tick-wings' echolocation as a *sensor*. When the tick-wings go quiet — when the clicking stops, suddenly, across a wide area of the rootdark — it means something has disturbed them. Something large. Something that the tick-wings perceive as a threat. The people have learned to read this silence. It is a warning. When the tick-wings go quiet, the people stop moving. They listen. They wait. They find out what made the tick-wings afraid.

Usually, it is the Mover.

## IV. The Transparent Ones

The feeders. The soft-bodied, six-legged, transparent things that cling to the undersides of root-platforms in clusters, feeding on condensation.

They are the rootdark's *most numerous* animal life. There are millions of them. Tens of millions. They cover every undersurface, every hollow, every sheltered space in the root-lattice, packed so tightly that their bodies form a continuous, undulating layer — a living skin of translucent flesh, pulsing faintly with the slow circulation of their amber hemolymph.

They are simple organisms. No brain to speak of — a ganglionic cluster that handles basic sensory input and motor control. No complex behavior — they feed, they reproduce, they die. They are born, they mature, they mate, they produce a clutch of eggs, they die, and their bodies are consumed by the fungal network, and the cycle begins again. Their entire lifespan is maybe three months. They are the rootdark's *plankton* — the base of the animal food chain, the primary converters of condensation into biomass, the fuel that powers everything above them.

But they are not *unimportant* for being simple. They are the rootdark's *water processors*. They consume condensation — the constant drip of moisture from the root-lattice — and they convert it into *body water*, into hemolymph, into the wet, amber fluid that pulses visibly beneath their transparent skin. And when they die, and the fungal network consumes them, the water is released back into the system, routed through the mycelial web to where it is needed. The feeders are the rootdark's *kidneys*, in a sense — filtering the water, processing it, keeping the cycle moving.

Their transparency is not a camouflage. There is no light to be camouflaged *from*, not in the way that surface animals use camouflage. Their transparency is a *metabolic adaptation*. They have no pigment because they have no need for pigment. They live in the dark. They have no predators that hunt by sight. Their transparency is simply the *absence* of anything unnecessary — a body stripped to its essentials, a life reduced to its simplest form: eat, drink, reproduce, die.

But they are not *nothing*. They are *beautiful*, in the way that simple things are beautiful. In the glow of the fungal network, their bodies light up — the hemolymph fluorescing faintly, each feeder becoming a tiny amber lantern, and the clusters becoming *constellations*, vast fields of warm light spread across the undersides of the root-platforms like galaxies seen through a telescope. The people who live in the rootdark know this. They know that if they lie on their backs on a root-platform and look up — *up*, into the space below them, into the world of the feeders — they will see a sky full of amber stars, and it will be the most beautiful thing they have ever seen, and they will not be able to explain why.

The feeders make a sound. The *shluk-shluk-shluk* of their feeding — a wet, rhythmic pulse, transmitted through the root-mass, felt as much as heard. It is the rootdark's *heartbeat*. It is the sound of water being processed, of life being sustained, of the cycle turning. It is constant. It is everywhere. It is the sound you hear when you lie in your hammock at night and close your eyes and let the rootdark's voice fill you, and it sounds like breathing, like the slow, wet breathing of something very large and very old and very much alive.

## V. The Mover

You never see it. You *feel* it.

The *boom* is the first thing. A single, deep, resonant impact, felt in the chest before it is heard in the ears — a compression wave that travels through the root-lattice, through the air, through the water in the bromeliad cups, through the bones of your skull. It is not loud, exactly. It is *deep*. It is a frequency so low that it is more sensation than sound, a vibration that resonates in the body's cavities — the chest, the gut, the skull — and produces a feeling that is not quite fear and not quite awe but something in between, something primal, something that bypasses the thinking brain and speaks directly to the animal brain, the old brain, the brain that remembers what it was like to be prey.

The *boom* is followed by a silence. Not a true silence — the rootdark is never truly silent — but a *dampening*, a muffling, as if the forest itself is holding its breath. The drip slows. The creak stops. The columns go quiet. The tick-wings stop clicking. The feeders cluster tighter. Everything in the rootdark *pauses*, for just a moment, in the presence of something so large that its mere existence changes the behavior of everything around it.

Then the *movement*. You feel it through the root-platform — a slow, rhythmic swaying, a pendulum motion that takes ten seconds to complete each cycle. The platform is moving because the root-lattice is moving, and the root-lattice is moving because something is *pushing through it*, something massive, something that displaces the living architecture of the rootdark the way a whale displaces water. The sway is gentle — you are not in danger of falling — but it is *unmistakable*. The rootdark is *flexing* around something, accommodating something, making room for something that is too large to go around.

The people say there are three of them. They say one is old — *old*, older than the oldest trees, old enough that its body has become an ecosystem in itself, colonized by fungi and mosses and the transparent feeders and the tick-wings and the columns and things that have no names, things that exist only on the Mover's body, that have evolved in the microclimate of its back, that live and die and are recycled in a closed loop that has nothing to do with the greater rootdark. They say the old one moves in a *pattern* — a circuit through the rootdark that takes years to complete, a route that follows the mycelial network's deepest channels, the oldest highways, the paths that the columns' ancestors wore into the root-lattice millennia ago.

They say the old one is *not hostile*. They say it is not aggressive, not territorial, not dangerous — unless you are in its path. They say it does not see you. Not because it is blind — it is not blind, it navigates by the same chemical and vibrational senses as everything else in the rootdark — but because you are *too small to register*. You are a microbe to it. A bacterium. A single cell in the body of the rootdark, and the Mover *is* the rootdark, or a part of it, or an expression of it, and it moves through itself the way your blood moves through your veins, unconsciously, automatically, as a function of being alive.

What is it?

The people have theories. Some say it is a creature — an animal, something that evolved in the rootdark, something that grew too large for the surface world and retreated into the green dark and kept growing. Some say it is a *plant* — a tree that has become mobile, that has developed the ability to uproot itself and move, slowly, over decades, following the nutrient gradients that the mycelial network maps for it. Some say it is something else — something that is neither plant nor animal, something that belongs to a category of life that has no name, something that the rootdark *produced*, the way a body produces a hormone, a chemical signal, a response to a need.

The people who have been closest to it — who have felt the sway of the root-lattice as it passed, who have heard the *boom* from a distance of meters rather than kilometers — describe a feeling. Not a sight, not a sound, not a smell. A *feeling*. A sense of *presence*. Of being in the vicinity of something so large and so alive that it generates its own gravity, its own weather, its own *field* — a zone of influence that extends outward from its body in all directions, changing the behavior of every living thing within it.

They describe it as *warm*. Not physically warm — the rootdark is always cold, always thirty-seven degrees, always damp — but *warm* in the way that a heartbeat is warm, in the way that breath is warm. A sense of *life*, concentrated, intensified, compressed into a single point of overwhelming vitality. The Mover is not just alive. It is *more alive* than anything else in the rootdark. It is alive in a way that makes everything else look like a dimmer version of itself, a shadow, a sketch.

And then it passes. The sway stops. The drip resumes. The columns begin to chirr again. The tick-wings start clicking. The feeders relax their clusters. The rootdark *exhales*. And the Mover is gone, moving on through the green dark, following its ancient route, carrying its portable ecosystem on its back, a moving island of life in the greater life, and you are left standing on your root-platform, your chest still resonating with the memory of the *boom*, and you know — you *know* — that you have been in the presence of something that you will never understand, and that this is fine, and that this is enough.

## VI. The People

They are small. They are temporary. They are *guests*.

They come from below — from the forest floor, where the light is dim but present, where the air is warmer, where the ground is *ground*, solid and horizontal and comprehensible. They come from above — from the upper canopy, where the light is bright and the wind is constant and the world opens up to the sky. They come for different reasons, and they stay for different lengths of time, and they all leave eventually, because the rootdark is not a place where humans can live permanently. The cold gets into your bones. The damp gets into your lungs. The dark gets into your mind.

But while they are there, they are *part of it*. They are part of the rootdark's ecology, part of its economy, part of its sound. Their footsteps on the root-platform add a new rhythm to the drip-and-creak. Their voices — quiet, always quiet, because loud sounds in the rootdark are *dangerous*, they attract attention, they disrupt the acoustic landscape, they can trigger a response from something large — their voices add a new frequency to the ambient drone. Their fires add smoke, and the smoke rises, and the smoke is *food* for certain epiphytes that have adapted to process the combustion products, and the epiphytes grow, and the root-lattice thickens, and the rootdark *incorporates* the human presence the way it incorporates everything else: slowly, patiently, without malice, without intent.

The people know this. They know they are being consumed — not literally, not yet, but *ecologically*. Their shelters are being colonized. Their tools are being degraded. Their food stores are being infiltrated by fungi. Their bodies are being *claimed* by the cold and the damp, slowly, degree by degree, and if they stay too long, the claim will become permanent, and they will become part of the rootdark's architecture, their bones cradled in root, their flesh feeding the network, their stories fading into the ambient sound.

So they leave. They harvest what they came to harvest — the fungi, the resins, the barks, the medicines — and they leave, and they go back to the surface or the canopy, and they warm themselves in the light, and they tell stories about the rootdark, and the stories are always the same: *It is alive. It is vast. It is indifferent. It is beautiful. It will take you if you let it.*

And some of them go back. Not because they have to. Because they *want* to. Because the rootdark has gotten into them — into their bones, into their lungs, into their minds — and they miss it. They miss the sound. They miss the dark. They miss the feeling of being *inside* something, of being part of a system so large and so complex that their individual existence becomes *meaningful* not because it matters but because it doesn't, because the rootdark will go on with or without them, and there is a comfort in that, a relief, a kind of peace that the surface world — with its light and its sky and its *horizons* — cannot provide.

These people — the ones who go back — are the ones who learn the rootdark's deepest rule. The rule that is not written down, not spoken aloud, not taught explicitly, but *absorbed*, over time, through the skin, through the lungs, through the bones:

*You are not separate from this place. You were never separate from this place. The rootdark is not a place you visit. It is a place you* **are**. *You are made of the same water, the same carbon, the same minerals. You are processed by the same cycles. You are heard by the same ears. You are seen — not with eyes, but with chemistry, with vibration, with the slow, patient attention of the mycelial network — by the same awareness. You are a cell in the body of the rootdark, and the rootdark is a cell in the body of the forest, and the forest is a cell in the body of the world, and the world is a cell in the body of something so large that it has no name, and it goes on, and on, and on, and you are part of it, and it is part of you, and the boundary between self and other is a* **fiction**, *a useful fiction, a necessary fiction, but a fiction nonetheless, and in the rootdark, in the green dark, in the cold and the damp and the sound, the fiction* **dissolves**, *and you are left with the truth:*

*You are the rootdark. The rootdark is you. And it is alive, and it is vast, and it is indifferent, and it is beautiful, and it is breathing, and it is dripping, and it is growing, and it is waiting, and it will always be waiting, and it will always be here, long after you are gone, long after your bones have been cradled in root and your stories have faded into the ambient sound and your name has been forgotten by everything except the mycelial network, which does not remember names but remembers* **chemistry**, *and your chemistry is in it now, and it will always be in it, and in this way — the only way that matters — you will never leave.*
