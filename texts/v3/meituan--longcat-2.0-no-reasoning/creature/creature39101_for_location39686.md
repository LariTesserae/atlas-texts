# The Residents of the Weeping Arcade

---

There are the small ones first, because you meet them first — you meet them immediately, the moment you set foot inside, before you've seen anything else, before your eyes have adjusted to the ambient fill of light. They are the ones that move. They are the ones that are most obviously *alive*, and they are the ones the place seems built to accommodate, as though the cart-paths were worn into the ironite floor by something other than carts.

Call them the Processional.

They travel in lines that do not deviate. Not lines you could map, exactly — they don't follow the corridors, they don't follow any single path. They follow *routes*, which are different from paths in that a route is a habit worn into behavior rather than a groove worn into ground. The ironite grooves in the floor are, in some sense, the fossil of the Processional's route, or the Processional's route is the continuation of the grooves into the living present — it is not clear which came first and it does not matter. They move at a uniform speed. They maintain uniform spacing. They do not accelerate when they are unobserved. They do not slow. They do not stop.

Their bodies are braided copper cable — not copper-colored, copper, actual copper, but copper that flexes and articulates and bends with a warmth and suppleness that copper should not possess. Each body segment is approximately two centimeters long, and each segment connects to the next with a joint that permits movement in more planes than a millipede's joint, more planes than any mechanical hinge. The segments compress and expand slightly as the creature moves, giving it a breathing quality, a peristaltic pulse that travels along its length from tail to front.

The legs are steel — thin, rigid, slightly tapered rods that end in points fine enough to catch the damp film on the ironite floor and part it without breaking. Each leg taps independently, and the tap is a real sound, a bright *tik* that rings with the frequency of a small bell. Each creature carries between forty and sixty legs (the number varies, and whether this represents individuals or species or simply different ages of the same creature is not something you can determine). The tap pattern is not regular. It is not random either. It has the quality of *information* — like a telegraph signal, like a language spoken too quickly and too quietly to parse, like data being exchanged at a rate just below the threshold of comprehension. When a hundred of them pass you in a corridor, the combined sound is a dense, dry rain, and if you listen long enough — and you will, because there is nothing else to do and the sound is not unpleasant — you begin to hear *structure* in it. Not rhythm. Structure. The way a city has structure from a height: not a pattern, but an organization.

The ball-bearing cluster at the front is the part that unsettles people most, though the people who live here — and there are people who live here, we will get to them — have long since stopped being unsettled by it. The bearings are steel, each about the size of a pea, arranged in a hemisphere of between twelve and twenty. They rotate on axes that have no visible mechanism. They track independently. When you enter a corridor, every cluster in the procession adjusts — not all at once, but in a wave, a ripple of reorientation that travels backward through the line at a speed slightly faster than the creatures' walking pace. They are watching you. They are not watching you the way an animal watches you — with interest, with threat assessment, with the possibility of flight. They are watching you the way a sensor watches you. They are *registering* you. You are a data point. You are a pressure disturbance in the damp film, a thermal signature, a sound source, a moving object in a space where nothing moves that does not belong. The Processional notes you and flows around you and continues.

They do not avoid you out of fear. They avoid you the way water avoids a stone — because you are there, and the route must continue, and the route is more important than the obstacle.

---

Then there are the Rollers.

They are larger — the size of a large cat, the size of a small dog, depending on the individual. Their bodies are flat, wide, low-slung, built along the geometry of a conveyor belt: a broad, flexible surface that contacts the ground along its entire underside, and a raised housing on top that contains whatever they contain. They move on rollers — actual rollers, steel cylinders that spin freely within housings built into their bodies, and the rollers contact the ironite floor and the creature *rolls* forward with a faint mechanical hum that is the only sound it makes.

They are slower than the Processional. They move through the buildings rather than the corridors, navigating the interiors of the workshops and the machine halls with a deliberateness that suggests purpose, though the purpose is not one you can identify. They do not interact with the machines. They do not interact with each other, as far as you can tell. They move through the spaces where the machines are, and the machines are still, and the Rollers pass between them like fish passing through a forest of coral.

Their bodies are a dark, gunmetal gray, and the surface of their underside — the part that contacts the rollers, the part that contacts the floor — has a texture like brushed steel, fine parallel lines that catch the ambient light and throw it back in a soft, directional sheen. The top housing is featureless. No eyes, no openings, no visible sensory apparatus. They navigate without apparent difficulty, turning corners, avoiding obstacles, finding doorways. They may sense through the rollers themselves — through vibration, through contact with the ironite floor and its permanent film of water. They may sense in a way that has nothing to do with what you would recognize as sensing.

They are solitary. You never see two together. You see one, and then later you see another, in a different building, moving in a different direction, and you cannot tell if it is the same one or a different one. They are interchangeable in the way that identical machines are interchangeable — not because they lack individuality, but because their individuality is not expressed in any dimension you can perceive.

---

The Clusters are the ones that live on the walls.

They are small — each individual is the size of a grain of rice, a small bead, a droplet of solder. They cling to vertical surfaces, to the undersides of pipes, to the faces of junction boxes, to the rusted flanks of concrete pillars. They are copper-colored, like the Processional, but their bodies are simpler — a single rounded form, featureless, with no legs, no visible means of adhesion. They simply *hold*.

What makes them a Cluster rather than a collection is the wiring. Fine copper filaments — finer than hair, finer than thread, fine enough that you see them only when the light catches them at the right angle — connect each individual to its neighbors in a mesh that covers the wall surface like a net, like a circuit diagram, like the mycelial network of a fungus rendered in metal. The filaments are not static. They *carry* something. The bioluminescence that pulses through the Cluster travels along the filaments, moving from individual to individual in waves that are too slow and too complex to read as signal but too organized to dismiss as random.

The glow is blue-white, the color of an electrical arc, the color of a fluorescent tube, the color of light that is produced by a process that is not combustion and not incandescence but something else — something that belongs to the vocabulary of this place, where life is made of metal and mineral and damp and the processes that sustain it are processes you have no name for.

The Clusters sing. The sound is high, thin, wavering — a tone that sits at the upper edge of hearing and flickers there, never quite steady, never quite resolving into a single pitch. It is the sound of a fluorescent tube about to fail, yes, but it is also the sound of a voice held at the very edge of audibility, the sound of something *trying to say something* in a frequency that is almost, but not quite, within your range.

When you stand in a room with a Cluster on the wall, the singing fills the space. It does not echo — the ironite walls absorb and diffuse sound in a way that prevents echo — but it *permeates*. It is in the air. It is in the damp. It is in the film of water on the floor, which vibrates faintly in sympathy, throwing tiny interference patterns of light across the surface.

---

The Skaters live in the tea-water.

They are flat — perfectly flat, two-dimensional in a way that seems impossible for a living thing, as though they have been pressed between two plates until all depth was eliminated. They are coppery, like the Processional and the Clusters, but their surface has a different quality — smooth, almost frictionless, with a faint iridescence that matches the sheen on the damp ironite floor. They move across the surface of the standing water without breaking tension, without creating ripples, without disturbing the surface film in any way you can detect. They *skate*.

Their movement is geometric. They travel in straight lines. When they reach an obstacle — the edge of a fracture in the floor, a wall, another Skater — they turn at precise angles and continue. The angles are always multiples of sixty degrees. Their paths trace hexagons, triangles, rhombuses across the surface of the water, and the paths of multiple Skaters interweave in patterns that are dense and complex and, again, *structured* — not random, not decorative, but organized in a way that suggests function without revealing it.

When two Skaters meet — when their paths intersect and they arrive at the same point at the same time — they exchange a spark. A small, bright flash of blue-white light, silent, instantaneous, gone before your eye can fully register it. And then both Skaters adjust course. The spark is information. The spark is the Skaters' equivalent of the Processional's leg-tapping, the Cluster's bioluminescent pulse — it is how they communicate, how they coordinate, how they maintain the geometric order of their movement across the water.

They are the most beautiful thing in the Weeping Arcade. They are also the most alien. The Processional, for all its strangeness, moves like something alive — it has legs, it has a body, it has a direction. The Skaters move like mathematics. They move like a proof being written across the surface of the water, each line a statement, each intersection a logical operation, each spark a conclusion that becomes the premise of the next line.

---

And then there are the Deep Ones.

You do not see them. You see the *effects* of them.

You see doorways that are too large to be doorways — openings in the walls of the largest buildings, openings that lead into spaces that the geometry of the settlement cannot contain, spaces that extend further back than the walls should permit, further down than the floor should allow. You see these doorways, and through them you see darkness — not the darkness of an unlit room, but a darkness that has *depth*, a darkness that recedes as you look into it, as though the space beyond the doorway is vast in a way that has nothing to do with physical dimension.

You hear the Deep Ones. The pulse — the subsonic throb that you feel in your chest, in your teeth, in the bones of your feet where they contact the ironite floor — is theirs. It is slow. It is deep. It is the lowest frequency in the Weeping Arcade's soundscape, the bass note beneath the Processional's tapping and the Rollers' humming and the Clusters' singing, and it is *constant*. It does not vary. It does not stop. It is the heartbeat of the place, if the place has a heartbeat, and the question of whether the place has a heartbeat is the question of whether the Deep Ones are creatures or whether they are something else — something more like a process, something more like a function, something more like the *reason the settlement exists at all*.

You glimpse them, sometimes, at the edges of those too-large doorways. A shape that is too large to be a shape. A movement that is too slow to be a movement — a shift, a reconfiguration, a *settling* of something massive in the darkness beyond the threshold. A gleam of copper in the dark. A cluster of ball-bearing eyes, but larger — the size of fists, the size of heads — rotating in the darkness, tracking you with the same mechanical precision as the Processional's smaller versions, but with a weight behind the gaze that the Processional does not possess. The Processional registers you. The Deep Ones *know* you.

They do not come out. They have never come out, as far as anyone can tell. They live in the deep spaces, in the rooms behind the too-large doorways, in the parts of the settlement that the geometry cannot account for. They are the largest things in the Weeping Arcade, and they are the most still. They do not move the way the Processional moves, or the Rollers, or the Skaters. They move the way a continent moves — imperceptibly, inevitably, on a timescale that makes the other creatures' activity look like the vibration of atoms.

---

And then there are the people.

Because there are people. There have always been people. The Weeping Arcade is a settlement, and settlements have people in them, and the people who live here are —

They are quiet. They are few. They live in the buildings that are not full of machines, in the rooms where the walls are stacked ironite blocks fitted without mortar, in the spaces that were built for habitation rather than production. They have been here a long time. Their families have been here a long time. How long, no one says, and the question is not one that gets asked, because the answer would be a number that doesn't mean anything — a number of years, or generations, or centuries, and the distinction between those things has collapsed in the Weeping Arcade the way the distinction between built and grown has collapsed in its architecture.

They are not afraid of the creatures. They are not comfortable with them either. The creatures are *part of the place*, the way the damp is part of the place, the way the ironite is part of the place, the way the machines are part of the place. You do not fear the floor. You do not fear the walls. You do not fear the Processional that flows around your ankles as you walk to the well (there is a well — a shaft in the ironite floor, lined with steel, filled with tea-water that is somehow clean enough to drink, though it is the same tea-water that fills the fractures and hosts the Skaters). You do not fear the Cluster that sings on the wall above your bed. You do not fear the Roller that passes through your workshop while you are working, moving through the space with its faint mechanical hum, not interacting, not interfering, simply *present*.

You respect them. You give them room. You do not interfere with the Processional's routes. You do not disturb the Clusters. You do not put your hand in the tea-water where the Skaters move. You do not approach the too-large doorways.

And in return — though "in return" implies a transaction, and the creatures do not transact — the creatures do not interfere with you. They flow around you. They pass through your spaces. They sing on your walls and skate on your water and pulse in the deep places, and you live your life in the spaces between their activities, and the equilibrium holds.

The people make things. Not with the machines — the machines are finished, the machines are complete, the machines are *done*, and no one touches them. The people make things with their hands, with tools that are their own, with materials they bring in from outside (there is an outside — there are ways out of the Weeping Arcade, cart-paths that lead to the surface, to the sun, to a world that is not made of ironite and damp and copper). They make small things. They repair things. They maintain the parts of the settlement that are not machines — the living spaces, the well, the paths between buildings. They do this quietly, carefully, in the ambient fill of light that does not change, while the Processional taps and the Rollers hum and the Clusters sing and the Skaters spark and the Deep Ones pulse in the darkness.

They are the most recent arrivals. They are also, in some sense, the most permanent. The creatures were here before them — or the creatures emerged here, or the creatures *are* the place in a way that makes the question of before and after meaningless. The people came later, or came first, or came simultaneously, and the settlement accommodated them the way it accommodates everything: by making room, by adjusting its equilibrium, by incorporating them into the pattern without changing the pattern.

---

This is what lives in the Weeping Arcade.

The Processional, tapping its information into the ironite floor.

The Rollers, humming through the machine halls.

The Clusters, singing their thin electric song on the walls.

The Skaters, writing their geometric proofs on the water.

The Deep Ones, pulsing in the dark behind the too-large doorways.

And the people, quiet and few, living in the spaces between.

All of it held in the permanent film of damp, in the ambient light that does not change, in the ironite that is not quite stone and not quite metal and has been here so long it has stopped pretending to be anything other than what it is.

Nothing moves that does not belong.

Nothing changes.

This is what it is.
