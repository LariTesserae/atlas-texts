# The Inhabitants of the Spine of Weeping Cities

---

They are small. Not in the way that implies deficiency — small in the way that everything here is small, scaled to the narrow margins the ecology permits. They are lean and compact, with the kind of bodies that come from a lifetime of stairs. Their calves are extraordinary. Their feet are broad and flat, with thick pads of callus on the balls and heels from walking on wet stone for every moment of every day of their lives. Their toes spread wide for balance. They move through the cities the way water moves through the rock — finding the path of least resistance, the route that has been worn smooth by generations of the same feet making the same journey.

Their hands are their other defining feature. The fingers are long, the palms are wide, and the skin is perpetually damp and slightly mineralized, with a faint sheen of calcium deposits embedded in the creases. They can read stone the way a musician reads a score — running their fingers along a wall and feeling the grain, the moisture content, the internal stresses, the places where the rock is tired and the places where it still holds. They know the difference between a crack that is cosmetic and a crack that is structural by touch. They can tell you which direction water is moving behind a wall by pressing their palm flat against it and waiting. Their hands are their primary tool, their primary instrument, their primary interface with the world.

Their eyes are adapted to the haze. They see well in the soft, scattered light — better than an outsider would, who squints and rubs their eyes and never quite adjusts. The pupils are large, and there is something about the quality of their gaze that suggests they are seeing not just the surface of things but the moisture in the air between them and the surface, the way light bends through the perpetual dampness. They navigate distances that would fool a newcomer — stepping across gaps that look wider than they are, judging the depth of a chasm through mist with a casual accuracy that comes from never having known a world where the air was transparent.

They are quiet. Not shy — quiet. They speak in low voices, not out of secrecy but because the acoustics of the cities reward quietness. Sound carries strangely in the built environment, bouncing off wet stone walls, channeling through corridors, amplifying in unexpected places. A whisper can travel three levels up through a ventilation shaft. A shout can echo for minutes. They have learned, over centuries, to modulate their voices to the architecture, to speak at the volume the space requires. In the narrow corridors, they communicate in murmurs, in gestures, in the slight movements of the hands that carry meaning the way words do in louder places. On the bridges, where the wind takes sound and the chasm swallows it, they speak normally, and the sound dies in the mist below.

Their language is old. It has no written form — there is no surface in the cities that stays dry enough to hold ink, no paper that wouldn't dissolve within days. The language lives in the mouth and the ear and the memory, and it has adapted to its environment the way everything here has adapted. It is full of words for water — not just water in the general sense, but water in its specific manifestations. There are words for the water that seeps through limestone. There are words for the water that runs in channels. There are words for the water that condenses on a surface overnight. There are words for the water that drips from a specific place at a specific rate, and the rate itself carries meaning — a faster drip means one thing, a slower drip means another, a drip that has stopped means something else entirely. The language is hydrological. To speak it is to describe the movement of water through stone, and since that is the primary activity of the world, the language is sufficient.

They do not name their cities. The cities are not separate entities — they are continuous, fused by mineral deposits and shared water systems and the walkways that connect them. To name one city would be to imply a boundary, and there are no boundaries. There are neighborhoods, identified by the fountain at their center — the place where the channeled water emerges from the rock and collects in a basin before being distributed to the surrounding buildings. You are from the Fountain of the Slow Drip, or the Fountain of the Three Channels, or the Fountain That Sings (where the water falls through a series of carved slots that produce a low, resonant tone). Your identity is hydrological. You are where your water comes from.

Their social structure is organized around water management. This is not a political system in the way that outsiders would recognize — there are no rulers, no councils, no formal hierarchy. There are the people who understand the channels. They are the ones who walk the waterways — the carved channels that run through the rock, some wide enough to walk through, some narrow enough to require crawling, some so deep in the mountain that they are accessed only through shafts cut at intervals from above. These people maintain the flow. They clear blockages. They redirect channels when the rock shifts. They know the entire hydraulic network of their section of the city the way a physician knows the circulatory system — not as a map but as a living thing, with its own rhythms, its own vulnerabilities, its own tendency toward certain failures. They are respected not because they hold power but because without them the city drowns. This is not metaphor. When a channel blocks, water finds its own path, and its own path is through the living spaces, through the walls, through the foundations. A blocked channel is a crisis. The people who clear it are the people who keep the world from dissolving.

They build. This is what they do, what they have always done, what they will continue to do until the last stone is absorbed back into the mountain. They build with the same stone the mountain is made of, quarried from the same cliff faces the cities cling to, and the new stone is indistinguishable from the old. They build by hand. They cut stone with tools made of stone — harder stone against softer stone, the ancient technique of striking along the grain, of finding the line of weakness and encouraging it. They shape blocks that fit together without mortar, held by gravity and friction and the precision of the cut. They carve channels into the rock with a patience that is almost geological — a channel that will take a generation to complete, that will carry water for ten generations after that, that will eventually silt up and need to be recarved by hands that have not yet been born.

They build in response to need, and the need is always the same: more space, more water, more stability. A family grows and needs a new room, so they carve one into the cliff face adjacent to their existing space, connecting it with a doorway cut through the wall. A channel shifts and a neighborhood loses its water supply, so they carve a new channel, redirecting the flow from a higher source. A terrace settles and threatens to collapse, so they shore it up with new construction, building supports that will themselves need supporting in time. The city grows the way a body grows — not by plan but by response, by the accumulation of small adaptations to immediate conditions, each one solving a problem and creating the conditions for the next problem.

They eat sparingly. The terraces produce fiber plants and a few hardy vegetables — root crops that can grow in the thin, mineral-heavy soil, leafy plants that thrive in the constant moisture. There is protein from the insects they cultivate in the moss beds — small, pale, flavorless creatures that are dried and ground into a powder and mixed with water to make a paste that is nutritious and without character. There are the birds — caught in simple traps near the cliff faces, their meat tough and dark, their eggs a rare delicacy. There is a kind of algae that grows on the wet stone in certain light conditions, scraped off and eaten raw or dried for storage. The diet is adequate. It is not varied. It is not pleasurable. It is the diet of people who live in a place that does not want to feed them, who have coaxed sustenance from a landscape that offers it grudgingly.

They are not unhappy. This is important to understand. They are not enduring their existence with grim determination, waiting for something better, dreaming of elsewhere. They are *of* this place in a way that makes the question of happiness irrelevant. The dampness is not an affliction — it is the condition of being alive. The stairs are not an obstacle — they are the shape of the world. The quiet is not oppressive — it is the sound of water moving through stone, which is the sound of everything working as it should. They have no concept of dryness as a desirable state. The idea of a place where the air is transparent and the ground is solid and the water stays where you put it would strike them as alien, inhospitable, a kind of desert.

They age quickly. The constant moisture, the mineral content of the water, the thin diet, the physical demands of vertical living — all of it takes a toll. Their skin is weathered by forty, deeply lined, with a grayish cast from the calcium deposits that accumulate in the pores. Their joints stiffen. Their hands, those extraordinary instruments, begin to lose their sensitivity — the calluses thickening past the point of usefulness, the fingers curling slightly from decades of gripping wet stone. By fifty, they are old. By sixty, they are ancient. By seventy, they are remarkable. The old ones sit by the fountains and listen to the water and feel the vibration in the rock through the stone they sit on, and they know things about the mountain that the young ones don't, because they have been listening longer.

They die, and their bodies are returned to the mountain. There is no ceremony — or rather, the ceremony is the act itself, which is simple and practical and carries the weight of meaning without the need for words. The body is carried to a deep channel, a place where the water runs fast and cold and deep in the rock, and it is placed in the flow. The water takes it. The mountain absorbs it. The calcium and phosphorus and iron that made the body become part of the mineral content of the water, which flows through the channels, which feeds the gardens, which grows the food, which feeds the living. There is no grief in this, or if there is, it is the grief of water — present, continuous, and ultimately indistinguishable from the medium that carries it.

They have no history in the written sense. They have stories, passed down through the generations, but the stories are not about the past — they are about the water. Every story is a story about a channel, about a time when the water changed course, about a blockage that threatened a neighborhood, about a new source discovered deep in the mountain. The stories are practical. They encode information — this is how you clear a blockage in limestone, this is how you find a new aquifer, this is how you know when a wall is about to fail. The stories are told by the water-keepers, the people who maintain the channels, and they are told while working, the words accompanying the hands, the story and the labor inseparable.

They have no concept of the future as a different place. The future is the same as the present, only wetter. The mountain is slowly dissolving. The cities are slowly being absorbed. The water is winning. They know this. They have always known this. It is not a source of despair — it is simply the condition of existence, the way an outsider might acknowledge that they will age and die without finding it particularly troubling. The cities will fall. The channels will silt up. The water will find new paths. And perhaps, in some distant time, someone will carve new channels, build new terraces, find new ways to live in the narrow margin between rock and water. Or perhaps not. The mountain will continue either way. The water will continue either way.

They are not waiting for rescue. They are not waiting for anything. They are walking home along stairways that descend through seven levels of construction, past fountains that have been running for centuries, through corridors where the walls are so close they must turn sideways, out onto bridges that span chasms of mist and dropping water, into doorways that open onto rooms lit by shafts of filtered light, and the rooms are warm enough, and dry enough, and they are home, and the water is moving through the rock beneath them, and the sound that is not quite sound is vibrating in their bones, and they press their palms against the wall and feel the mountain breathing, and they are not alone, and they are not afraid, and the dampness is the world, and the world is enough.
