# The Dwellers of Keth-Avani

---

## The Avani

They are called the Avani, and they are not quite what you would expect.

You might expect a people shaped by hardship — grim, hunched, defensive, hoarding warmth and food against a hostile world. And there is hardship here; do not mistake the beauty for gentleness. The cold is a constant predator. The work is unending. A child who misreads the walls can die, and has died, and will die again. But the Avani are not grim. They are *attentive*. There is a difference, and it is the difference that matters.

They are small, yes — the average adult barely clears five feet, with a wiry, efficient build that minimizes surface area and heat loss. Their bones are dense, heavier than you'd expect for their size, an adaptation to a lifetime of climbing wet stone. Their hands are their most remarkable feature: long-fingered, extraordinarily sensitive, with a tactile acuity that borders on the synaesthetic. An Avani can read a water-channel the way you might read a face — feeling, through the pads of their fingers, the temperature, the mineral content, the flow rate, the presence of biological contaminants, all at once, all without thinking about it. Their fingertips are permanently wrinkled, not from immersion but from a physiological adaptation — the skin there has developed a micro-structure of ridges and channels that increases surface area and sensitivity, so that their hands look, in certain lights, like the filter-colonies on the walls. Like something that has learned to drink the world through its skin.

Their eyes are large, dark, and possessed of that secondary membrane — the *vel*, they call it — a translucent inner eyelid that can be drawn across the eye to filter the blinding refracted light of the Warrens. When an Avani draws the vel, their gaze takes on a distant, milky quality, as though they are looking at something behind the visible world. They do this often. It is not a conscious act, any more than you consciously adjust your pupils. The vel moves on its own, responding to light intensity, and the effect is that the Avani always seem to be half-looking at something else — something you cannot see, something behind or beneath the surface of things. It makes them difficult to read. It makes them seem, to outsiders, slightly uncanny.

Their skin is pale, as you would expect, but the translucence is real and it is striking. In bright light — and there is so much bright light here — you can see the shadows of their veins, a faint blue-green tracery that maps their forearms and the backs of their hands like the channel-network of the Warrens itself. They do not sunburn; they have lost the melanin response entirely, replaced it with something else, some mechanism by which their skin *uses* the light that penetrates the overhang, not for vitamin synthesis but for something more subtle — a kind of supplementary energy, a metabolic trickle that supplements the sparse diet. They are not photosynthetic. But they are not entirely *not* photosynthetic, either. The boundary, as with everything in Keth-Avani, has dissolved.

Their hair is fine, colorless, and grows in a peculiar pattern — dense on the scalp and the forearms, sparse everywhere else, as though the body has decided that these are the surfaces most likely to benefit from insulation and has allocated resources accordingly. They keep it short, cropped close to the skull with bone blades, not for aesthetics but for function: long hair in this environment becomes a vector for the filter-colonies, which will colonize any stable wet surface, including, given the opportunity, human hair. There are stories — old stories, told in low voices — of people who let their hair grow too long and woke one morning to find it *moving*.

They dress in layers of a material that is unique to Keth-Avani: a felted fabric made from the processed fibers of the filter-colonies themselves. The colonies are harvested, dried, beaten, and woven into a cloth that is waterproof, insulating, and — this is the remarkable part — *alive*. Not alive in the way that a wool sweater is arguably alive (keratin, cellular residue, the memory of biology), but alive in a functional sense: the fabric retains the colony's responsiveness to moisture and temperature. In dry conditions, the fibers contract, tightening the weave and trapping air for insulation. In wet conditions, they expand, opening the weave to allow evaporation and prevent the clammy buildup of moisture against the skin. The Avani are, in a sense, wearing the walls. Wearing the city. Their clothing breathes with the same rhythm as the Warrens.

The colors are the colors of the place: bone-white, mineral-gray, the faint blue-green of phosphorescence, the amber of heavy-limestone water. Dye is unnecessary when your entire world is already saturated with color — the rainbows that crawl across every surface provide a shifting palette that the Avani simply *inhabit*, their clothing taking on the refracted light of whatever chamber they stand in, so that a woman crossing the great hall passes through a spectrum, her body briefly red, briefly gold, briefly violet, as she moves through the living light.

## The Way They Live

The Avani do not have families in the way you would recognize. They have *flows*.

A flow is a social unit defined not by blood but by proximity — by who shares a water-channel, who tends adjacent wall-sections, who harvests from the same fish-schools. A flow might consist of six or twenty individuals, bound together by the practical necessities of managing a section of the Warrens. Children are raised by the flow, not by their biological parents; biological parentage is acknowledged but considered irrelevant, a mere *inflow* into a larger current. When a section of the Warrens shifts — when a new channel opens, when a chamber floods, when the water demands a reconfiguration — the flows shift with it. People move between flows the way the fish move between channels: following the water, following the work, following the logic of the place.

This means that Avani society is fluid in a way that is not metaphorical. There are no fixed hierarchies, no permanent leaders, no inherited status. Authority is *situational* — the person who knows the most about a particular channel, a particular colony, a particular stretch of wall, is the person who makes decisions about that channel, that colony, that stretch of wall. And when the water changes, the authority changes with it. An elder who has spent forty years mastering a section of the Warrens may find, after a major shift, that their expertise is obsolete — that the channels they knew have sealed, the colonies they tended have died, the geometry they internalized has been replaced by a new geometry that they must learn from a younger person who happened to be standing in the right place when the change occurred. This is not a tragedy. It is the way of things. The Avani have a word — *keth*, the same word they use for the mountain itself — that means both "to change" and "to endure," because in their experience, these are the same act.

The work is constant but not frantic. There is a rhythm to it, set by the water and the light, and the Avani move through their days with the unhurried precision of people who have learned that haste is not efficiency — that the fastest way to clear a blocked channel is to understand *why* it blocked, which requires patience, which requires stillness, which requires a kind of listening that cannot be rushed. They listen to the water. They listen to the walls. They listen to the deep pulse of the aquifer, that subsonic heartbeat that they feel in their dense bones, and they time their activities to its rhythm the way a sailor times activities to the tide.

The fish harvest is the central communal activity. It happens in the afternoon, when the particular rainbow appears on the far wall of the great chamber — the signal that the light has reached the angle at which the fish, photosensitive despite their blindness, rise to the surface of the channels in feeding behavior. The harvest is not a hunt. It is a *gathering*. The Avani open the sluice-gates that lead to the harvest channels — specially designed channels with shallow, broad basins where the fish congregate — and they scoop them out with nets woven from colony-fiber, quick and practiced and gentle. The fish are not afraid. They have been tended by the Avani for so many generations that they have become, in a sense, domesticated — not tame, exactly, but *accustomed*, the way a river is accustomed to its banks. They know the nets. They know the hands. They come to the harvest basins the way the water comes to the channels: because this is where the flow goes.

The wall-colonies are tended more than harvested. The Avani prune them, guide them, encourage certain species and discourage others, the way a gardener manages a garden — except that the garden is also the walls, and the walls are also the house, and the house is also the city, and the city is also the garden. The distinction between infrastructure and ecology does not exist here. When an Avani scrapes an overgrown colony from a water-channel to restore flow, they are simultaneously performing maintenance, harvesting food, and managing a living system. The scraped colony is not waste; it is eaten, or dried and stored, or processed into fiber, or applied to a section of wall where a desired species is struggling to establish. Nothing is wasted. Everything flows.

## The Deep Dwellers

But the Avani are not alone in the Warrens.

There are others. Things that live in the deeper chambers, where the light does not reach, where the water is warmer — not warm, but *warmer*, a relative term in a place where warmth is measured in degrees above punishing — and where the pulse of the aquifer is strongest.

The Avani call them the *Keth-avani*. The same name. The mountain's own people.

They are not human. They were never human. They are something that evolved here, in the flooded chambers beneath the overhang, in the dark rivers that feed the Warrens from below — something that the Avani encountered when they first settled this place, centuries ago, and with whom they have since reached an accommodation.

They are difficult to describe because they do not hold a single form. They are colonial organisms — related to, but distinct from, the filter-colonies on the walls. Larger. More complex. More *organized*. Where the wall-colonies are a tapestry, the Keth-avani are a *body* — or rather, a network of bodies, connected by threads of living tissue that run through the rock itself, through the water, through the mineral deposits that line every surface. They are, in a sense, the mountain's immune system. Or its nervous system. Or its mind. The Avani are not sure, and the Keth-avani do not explain, because the Keth-avani do not communicate in any way that the Avani can fully parse.

What the Avani know is this: the Keth-avani are aware. They respond to the presence of the settlement — not with hostility, not with welcome, but with *attention*. When the Avani carve a new channel, the Keth-avani feel it. When the Avani harvest the fish, the Keth-avani feel it. When the Avani read the walls, the Keth-avani are reading the Avani reading the walls, and there is a recursion there, a mutual observation, that the Avani find both comforting and deeply strange.

The Keth-avani manifest — that is the closest word — in various ways. Sometimes as a sudden bloom of bioluminescence in a deep chamber, patterns of light that move across the walls in sequences too complex to be random, too structured to be meaningless. Sometimes as a change in the water — a shift in temperature, in mineral content, in flow rate — that carries information the Avani have learned to interpret. A sudden warming of the lower channels means *caused* — the Keth-avani are aware of a problem, a blockage or contamination in the deep system, and they are redirecting flow to compensate. A sudden cooling means *come* — there is something in the deep chambers that the Keth-avani want the Avani to see, or to do, or to understand.

And sometimes — rarely, and only in the deepest chambers, where the pulse of the aquifer is a physical force that presses against your chest like a hand — the Keth-avani manifest *physically*. A shape in the water. A mass of tissue that rises from the channel-floor like a slow exhalation, pale and branching and faintly luminous, large enough to fill a room, complex enough to have *structures* — nodes, junctions, channels of its own, a body that mirrors the body of the Warrens, a living map of the living rock. It does not speak. It does not need to. The Avani who encounter it — and they are few, and they are changed by the experience — describe a sensation of being *read*. Of being known, completely, down to the mineral content of their bones and the rhythm of their heartbeat, by something so vast and so patient and so utterly *other* that the experience is less like meeting a creature and more like being a single note in a chord that has been sounding since before there were ears to hear it.

The Avani do not worship the Keth-avani. Worship implies distance, implies a hierarchy of power, implies a relationship between the small and the large, the mortal and the eternal. The Avani's relationship with the Keth-avani is not hierarchical. It is *symbiotic*. The Keth-avani maintain the deep system — the aquifer, the underground rivers, the flooded chambers that are the Warrens' foundation. The Avani maintain the surface system — the channels, the colonies, the fish, the structures. Each tends the other's environment. Each reads the other's signals. Each is, in a sense, the other's sense organ — the Keth-avani feeling the surface through the Avani's hands, the Avani feeling the depths through the Keth-avani's water.

It is not a comfortable relationship. It is not an uncomfortable one. It is a *necessary* one, and the Avani, who have learned to find comfort in necessity, are content with it.

## The Children of the Flow

The children are the most Avani of all.

They are born small — impossibly small, by lowland standards, barely two pounds, a size dictated by the mathematics of heat loss and the mother's caloric intake in a place where calories are never abundant. They are born in water — in the warmest chambers, where the deep aquifer's pulse raises the temperature to something approaching tolerable, in shallow pools lined with living colony-tissue that regulates the temperature and cleans the water and, the Avani believe, *welcomes* the newborn into the system. The birth-pools are tended by the flow's elders, and the colonies there are the oldest and most complex in the Warrens — ancient, layered, pulsing with a slow rhythm that matches the aquifer's heartbeat, so that the child's first sensation, after the shock of cold air and bright light, is the deep pulse, the mountain's breath, the rhythm that will organize their entire life.

The children grow quickly. By the age of five, they can read the walls — not with the sophistication of an adult, but with the basic literacy of retraction and expansion, the ability to tell healthy colony from stressed colony, clean water from tainted water. By the age of eight, they can navigate the Warrens alone, moving through the stacked walkways and cantilevered platforms with the sure-footedness of something that has learned to trust the stone. By the age of twelve, they are contributing — harvesting fish, tending colonies, clearing channels, doing the small precise work that the Warrens demand and that small hands are best suited for.

But the most remarkable thing about Avani children is their relationship with the water itself.

They swim before they walk. The channels are their playgrounds, their classrooms, their temples. They dive into the cold water — the *ambitious* water, the water that pulls heat from your body with metabolic hunger — and they *laugh*. They do not feel the cold the way an outsider feels it. Their bodies, saturated with the same mineral-rich water that saturates the stone, have a different relationship with temperature — not immunity, but *integration*. They are cold the way the stone is cold, the way the water is cold. They are part of the cold. It does not attack them because it does not recognize them as other.

And in the water, they feel the pulse. The deep pulse. The aquifer's heartbeat. They feel it in their bones — those dense, heavy bones — and they feel it in the water around them, a vibration that is not a sound, a rhythm that is not a rhythm, a *presence* that is everywhere and nowhere and is the closest thing the Avani have to a god, though they would not use that word, because the Keth-avani are not above them and not below them but *around* them, *through* them, the way the water is around and through the stone, the way the stone is around and through the water, the way the mountain breathes and the light pours in and the rainbows crawl across the walls and the fish swim in their living channels and the colonies pulse on the walls like a second skin and the stalactites grow, fast, faster than they should, teeth in the mouth of a mountain that is slowly, slowly learning to speak, and the children hear it, the children feel it, the children *are* it, small and pale and mineral-heavy and luminous in the refracted light, swimming in the cold water of a city that breathes, in the heartbeat of a world that is alive, that has always been alive, that will go on being long after the children are old and the channels have shifted and the walls have changed and the water has found new paths through the patient stone, as it always does, as it always will, forever and forever and forever.
