They are not people. Not anymore. Not in any way that word would have meant before the Thawyard.

You want to call them people because you see the shapes — the height, the proportions, the way a figure stands at the end of a channel with its weight on one leg, the way a hand rests on a valve with the particular patience of someone who has been waiting for a long time. But the shapes are a courtesy the place extends to you, a translation it performs so you don't have to confront what's actually there.

What's actually there is **residue**.

Not residue in the sense of something left behind. Residue in the sense of something that *remained*. The Thawyard achieved its equilibrium — that perfect, locked, unbreathing stasis — and everything within it was pulled into the balance. The machines stopped cycling. The water stopped rising. The steam stopped rising. And the people stopped *becoming*.

They are still here. That's the part that matters. They didn't leave and they didn't die. They simply reached the point where change was no longer possible, and they stayed. They are the human equivalent of the locked flywheel, the running conveyor carrying nothing, the gauge that reads the same number forever. They are components in the system now. They hold their positions the way the pipes hold their pressure.

---

**What they look like**, when you see them — and you will see them, because they are not hiding, they are simply *there*, the way a rivet is there:

The oiled canvas is not clothing. Or it was, once, and now it is something closer to integument. It has fused with the skin beneath it in places, not through injury but through *duration*. The same heat, the same moisture, the same mineral-saturated air, applied to the same surface, for the same unbroken span of time. The boundary between garment and body has become a gradient. The canvas has taken on the texture of the skin it covers; the skin has taken on the stiffness of the canvas that covers it. You cannot tell where one ends and the other begins, and this is not a horror — or it is, but it is a quiet one, the horror of a process that simply continued past the point where anyone thought to stop it.

Their skin, where it is visible, is the color of the clay. Not metaphorically. The lime-ash and the iron-rich water and the steam have stained it, or changed it, or *become* it, to a depth that goes past the surface. Their hands are dark and smooth and slightly too thick, as though the fingers have swollen just enough to lose their individual definition. Their faces, when you see them — and you will not see them often, because they tend to face walls, or face the water, or face the narrow channels where nothing is happening — are calm. Not peaceful. Calm the way a gauge is calm. The features are present. The eyes are open. The mouth is closed. Nothing moves.

They do not blink. This is the thing that will stay with you.

---

**What they do**:

They stand. They stand at the ends of channels. They stand on rooftops, looking out over the flat dark water. They stand in doorways, one hand on the frame, as though they entered or were about to enter but the decision never completed itself. They stand beside machines — a hand on a valve, a palm flat against a boiler plate, fingers resting on a pipe that carries steam to somewhere that no longer needs it. Their touch is not idle. It is *load-bearing*. They are part of the structure now. They are the expansion joint that absorbs the thermal stress that isn't there. They are the counterweight that keeps the raft level. They are the thing that holds the system in its locked state, and if they moved — if they stepped away from the wall, if they turned the valve, if they lifted their hand — the equilibrium would break, and the Thawyard would begin to change, and change is the one thing it cannot survive.

They do not eat. They do not sleep. They do not speak. They do not look at each other. They do not look at you, though they know you are there — not because they sense you but because the system senses you, and they are part of the system, and the system registers your weight on the clay, your heat in the air, your breath adding moisture to the steam, and it adjusts, minutely, to absorb you into the balance. You are a perturbation. They are the correction.

---

**How many there are**:

You cannot count them. Not because there are too many but because they do not present themselves as discrete units. A figure at the end of one channel and a figure at the end of the next channel might be two of them or one of them seen from a different angle or the same one occupying a position that doesn't map cleanly onto the geometry of the space. The Thawyard's equilibrium does not require a specific number of human components. It requires a specific *mass* of human presence, distributed at specific points, and the figures you see are where that mass is most visible. There may be others inside the buildings, standing in rooms you cannot enter, holding positions you cannot see. There may be some who have become so integrated with the structure that they are indistinguishable from it — a shape in a dark alcove that might be a person or might be a support column that has taken on the memory of a person's posture.

The boot-prints in the clay dust that lead nowhere and begin nowhere: those are not evidence of someone who was here and left. They are evidence of someone who is *still walking*. A figure moving so slowly that the motion is imperceptible, placing one foot in front of the other along a route that takes years to complete, leaving prints that accumulate in the still air, a trail that records not a journey but a *duration*.

---

**What they want**:

Nothing. That is not the same as not needing. They need — the system needs — and what it needs is for everything to remain exactly as it is. They are the expression of that need. They are the Thawyard's immune response to the concept of change. They are what happens when a place decides, with the full weight of its locked equilibrium, that it will not allow anything within it to become anything other than what it already is.

And you — standing in the channel, breathing the steam, feeling the heat press against your skin, hearing the unchanging chord of sound — you are becoming. You are still becoming. Your heart is beating and your lungs are filling and your sweat is running down your back and you are, at this moment, a process that moves from one state to another, and the Thawyard can feel this, and the figures at the ends of the channels can feel this, and they are not hostile about it.

They are not anything about it.

They are simply there, holding their positions, keeping the balance, and if you stay long enough — if you stand still long enough, if you stop becoming long enough — one of them will be you. Not replaced. *Continued*. You will be the figure at the end of the channel, facing the wall, your hand on the valve that does not turn, your skin the color of the clay, your eyes open and not blinking, and you will be part of the chord that does not resolve, and the water will keep lapping, and the steam will keep rising to its fixed ceiling, and the beetles will keep walking their routes in the walls, and you will not mind, because minding is a form of change, and you will have stopped.

---

**The one thing that is almost unbearable**:

It is not the heat. It is not the steam. It is not the sound or the stillness or the figures that do not blink.

It is that they are *content*. Not happy — happiness is a gradient, a movement from less to more, and there are no gradients here. But content in the way that a stone is content in its position, in the way that a pipe is content under its pressure, in the way that the raft is content on the water. They have found their place in the system and they are holding it and the holding is not a struggle. The clenched jaw you feel in the place — that tension, that rigor — is not theirs. It is the *place's*. They are the teeth. They are the thing the jaw is clenching *around*. And they are, in their way, at rest.

They are the most rested things you will ever encounter.

And that is what will make you leave. Not fear. Not disgust. The recognition that rest, taken far enough, becomes indistinguishable from disappearance, and that the Thawyard offers both, and that the offer is genuine, and that the figures standing at the ends of the channels accepted it not because they were forced but because, at some point, standing still felt like enough.
