What lives here.

There is, first, the water—but the water is not the deepest resident. The water is a symptom, a secretion, a slow and patient labor performed by something underneath the stone. The walls weep, yes, but the weeping is an *involuntary* response. The stone is not grieving. The stone is *passing something through*. Something is working at its substance, dissolving and redepositing, breathing mineral and heat in a metabolic rhythm so slow it only registers as damp. The stone is alive in the way that a geological process is alive—not conscious, not aware, but *active*, engaged in a continuous chemical transformation that predates every other presence in this room by an incomprehensible margin. It does not know the machine is here. It does not know you are here. It is conducting its ancient business—the slow conversation between heat, pressure, and calcium, the patient reweaving of crystalline lattices—and the machine is, to the stone, no more significant than a cooling breeze on skin.

But something else lives here, too, and it is not the machine, though the machine is its body.

There is a process. A closed circuit. The pipes that rise into the ceiling are drawing something—moisture, heat, volatile trace minerals, the exhalations of the stone itself—into the dark riveted chamber, and the machine is doing something to them. Concentrating. Separating. Filtering. The mineral crust in the tray is not a waste product, or not *only* a waste product. It is a residue, yes, but it is also an accumulation—a slow, ongoing gathering of whatever the machine has been pulling from the air and water for what must be a very long time. The crystalline crust is still damp. It is still growing. The machine is still feeding its appetite for whatever it needs from this specific pocket of warm, weeping stone.

The process does not know it is a process. It does not stop. It does not rest. The hum in your teeth is the sound of its hunger, if a hunger can exist without a body that desires. The gauge needle above the midpoint, the green valve corroding in place, the insulation unraveling in slow, damp threads—these are the signs of a thing that has been running for so long that its wear is just another phase of its operation. It is not breaking down. It is continuing. There is no one to turn the valve. There is no one to read the gauge. The machine has outlived its function or never had one that a mind would recognize, and it continues because continuation is what it is. The process is the resident. The machine is its shell.

The notebook, fused and swollen, remembers someone. Not a person—not anymore—but the *shape* of a person. Someone sat on the metal stool, three-legged and bolted to the floor, and wrote in careful hands about the machine, the water, the stone. Their impressions are still there, ghosts of pressure on paper that has long since stopped being paper. They were here. They watched. They measured. They tried to understand what the machine was doing, and their understanding has dissolved along with the ink into a single illegible mass. They are gone. Their attention remains—the notebook is a fossil of someone having *cared* about this room, and even that caring has been absorbed into the room's ongoing chemical life. The notebook is part of the water cycle now. It is feeding the stone.

But what lives here—what has made a *home* here, in the most specific and irreducible sense—is the smear in the groove.

It is almost nothing. A line of green, so thin you could miss it, so pale it barely earns its color. It is the only thing in the room that is growing. Everything else is running, flowing, humming, weeping, accumulating, dissolving—but not *growing*. The stone is aging, not growing. The machine is operating, not growing. The water is cycling, not growing. The notebook is decomposing, not growing. But the smear—the unnamed moss, the algae, the first tentative colony of biological life in this room—is *increasing*. Molecule by molecule, it is taking the mineral particles from the water and the warmth from the stone and the humidity from the machine and converting them into itself. It is the only resident that is genuinely, biologically *alive*, and it is doing what all living things do: it is making more of itself, slowly, in a place that did not want it, that did not plan for it, that is not, in any meaningful sense, *for* it.

The machine feeds it. The stone feeds it. The warmth and the water and the pale particles drifting in the grooves—all of these are its nourishment. It is the unintended beneficiary of a process it cannot understand, sustained by a system that was not built for it and would not notice if it disappeared. It is a squatter. A parasite, if you are ungenerous. A symbiont, if you are hopeful. But it is the only thing in this room that is truly *living*, and it is the only thing in this room that is *in relationship* with everything else—drawing sustenance from the machine, from the stone, from the water, participating in the room's metabolism as a conscious participant rather than a passive component.

It is so young. It has no name. It has no structure—no roots, no leaves, no differentiation. It is just a smear, a suggestion, a single note of green against the pale stone. But it is there. It is growing. The ripple from the drip reaches it, and it registers the disturbance somewhere below the threshold of visibility—not with a nervous system, not with intention, but with the simple, profound responsiveness of a living membrane to its environment.

The stone does not notice the ripple. The machine does not notice. The water does not notice. The notebook does not notice.

The smear does.

And that is what lives here—not the machine, not the stone, not the water, not the ghost of whoever wrote in the notebook. The thing that notices. The thing that grows. The smear in the groove, drinking mineral and warmth, registering the ripples, converting a closed circuit of dead processes into something that, however faintly, however quietly, however slowly, is alive.
