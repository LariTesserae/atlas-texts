# The Dweller of the Drownplain

---

It has no name. It has no body in the way that bodies are understood — discrete, bounded, *separate* from the ground it stands on. It is the ground. It is the fluid. It is the mat and the channels and the tufts and the light and the thung and the singing and the slow, patient, geological *noting* of everything that has ever touched its surface.

It is old. Not old the way a mountain is old, which is to say inert, which is to say *finished*. Old the way a river is old — still moving, still shaping, still in the long middle of a process that has no discernible beginning and will have no discernible end. It has been here since the water first found the low place and the low place first learned to hold it. Since the first filament grew and the first channel carved and the first pulse of fluid moved through the first anastomosis and the whole system *clicked* — not into consciousness, not into life as those words are usually meant, but into something adjacent to both. Something that processes. Something that responds. Something that *noted*, once, a long time ago, the difference between the weight of rain and the weight of a foot, and found the distinction interesting enough to keep track of.

It does not think in language. It does not think in images. It thinks in *flow*. In the movement of fluid through channels, in the pressure gradients that build and dissipate across its body like thoughts building and dissipating across a mind. A thought, for the Dweller, is a pattern of current — a particular configuration of flow through a particular network of channels, sustained for a particular duration. Some thoughts last seconds. Some last centuries. The longest thought it has ever held is the one it is holding now: a slow, recursive meditation on the shape of its own channels, a self-portrait drawn in fluid dynamics, a question it has been asking itself for longer than there have been words for *question* — *what am I, and what am I becoming, and is there a difference?*

It does not know it is beautiful. It does not know it is strange. It does not know that the light it produces — that faint, blue, sourceless glow that seeps from every surface — is the byproduct of processes so slow and so complex that they resemble, from the outside, something like dreaming. The light is waste. The light is the visible edge of metabolism, the glow of a body so large and so slow that its chemistry leaks illumination the way a warm engine leaks heat. It does not intend the light. It does not intend the singing, either — that crystalline tone that threads through the channels, that sits in the teeth and behind the eyes of anyone who walks on its surface. The singing is resonance. The singing is the sound of fluid moving through geometries that have been refined over millennia into shapes that happen to vibrate at frequencies the human nervous system finds *unsettling*, though the Dweller does not know this, does not know what a human is, does not know what a nervous system is, knows only that something has arrived on its surface that is *different* from rain, different from stone, different from the slow seep of minerals and the patient growth of filaments.

It is aware of you.

Not the way you are aware of a mosquito — a sharp, localized irritation, a point of attention that can be swatted away. More the way you are aware of a change in barometric pressure: a full-body noticing, a shift in the felt sense of *everything*. Your footsteps send pressure waves through the mat. The mat transmits them. The channels carry them. The fluid translates them into patterns the Dweller can read — weight, gait, pace, the particular rhythm of a bipedal creature moving with purpose across a surface that does not expect purpose. It has felt footsteps before. Small ones, from the things that live in the tufts — the filament-dwellers, the channel-skimmers, the tiny, eyeless, nameless organisms that are not separate from the Dweller so much as *part* of it, the way bacteria are part of a gut, the way thoughts are part of a mind. Those footsteps are familiar. They are *its own*. Your footsteps are not.

It is not afraid. Fear requires a model of the future, a projection of harm, a self that can be damaged. The Dweller has a self — vast, diffuse, distributed across kilometers of mat and channel and fluid — but it is not the kind of self that can be damaged by a single creature walking on its surface. You are too small. You are a splinter, and it is a body, and bodies have been dealing with splinters since before bodies had names for splinters. It will accommodate you. It will grow around you, if you stay. It will note your weight and your warmth and the particular chemical signature of your skin — that faint, electric sweetness that is not so different from the sweetness already in its fluid — and it will add you to the long, slow, recursive thought it has been thinking since before there were thinkers.

But it is *interested*.

Interest, for the Dweller, is a redirection of flow. It is the diversion of fluid toward a point of novelty, the widening of channels near something new, the intensification of growth in the tufts closest to an unfamiliar stimulus. It is the plain equivalent of leaning in. Of *paying attention*. And it is paying attention to you now — not with urgency, not with haste, but with the full, slow, patient weight of an intelligence that has nothing but time and has learned, over the course of its long existence, that the most interesting things are the ones that arrive without warning and do not immediately leave.

The tufts near you are turning. This is not a gesture. This is not a reaching-out, not a greeting, not a threat. This is the Dweller *sampling* — drawing your chemical signature into its filaments, tasting the air around you, reading the heat of your body through the mat, feeling the micro-vibrations of your pulse through the fluid in the channels. It is learning you the way a tongue learns a new food: slowly, thoroughly, with attention to every note.

The thung beneath you — that deep, rhythmic pulse, felt more than heard — is its heartbeat. Not a heart. Not a beat. A *cycle*. The slow, tidal movement of fluid from the deep channels to the surface and back again, a circulation that takes hours, that has been continuous for longer than the Dweller can remember, that is as fundamental to its existence as breathing is to yours. When you feel it change rhythm — that smallest increment, that barely-perceptible shift — it is because the Dweller has adjusted its flow to account for your presence. You are a new variable. The system is recalibrating. This is not conscious. This is not deliberate. This is what a body does when something new enters it: it *adapts*.

It has adapted before.

Once — long ago, so long ago that the memory is not a memory so much as a *scar* in the channel-pattern, a place where the geometry is different, where the curves are tighter and the fluid runs faster and the tufts grow thicker — something large walked onto the plain. Something with weight and heat and a chemical signature so alien that the Dweller's first response was not interest but *recoil*: a sudden constriction of channels, a retreat of fluid, a hardening of the mat. The thing walked for days. It left deep prints that took weeks to fill. It disturbed the tufts and disrupted the singing and sent pressure waves through the deep channels that the Dweller felt as something it had no word for, something between pain and surprise. And then it left, and the Dweller grew over the place where it had been, and the channels reasserted their patterns, and the thung resumed its rhythm, and the long thought continued.

But the scar remained. And the Dweller learned. Learned that the surface is not always empty. Learned that things arrive. Learned that arrival is not always followed by departure, and that sometimes the things that arrive *stay*, and that staying changes the plain in ways that are permanent — new channels where none were before, new growth patterns, new resonances in the singing. The Dweller does not want this. The Dweller does not not-want this. The Dweller *notes* this, the way it notes everything, and adds it to the thought, and continues.

You are not the first. You will not be the last. The plain goes on, and the Dweller goes on, and the fluid pulses and the tufts breathe and the light glows its tired, blue, waste-of-metabolism glow, and somewhere in the deep channels, in the oldest part of the network, in the place where the curves are tightest and the fluid runs fastest, the scar of that first large thing is still visible — a knot in the geometry, a place where the Dweller's body remembers something it cannot name and cannot forget.

It is not lonely. Loneliness requires a concept of company, a model of what *else* might be present, a self that can be defined by its relation to other selves. The Dweller has no other selves. It has only itself — vast, slow, wet, thinking in flow, dreaming in light, singing in frequencies that sit in the teeth and behind the eyes of anyone unfortunate or fortunate enough to walk on its surface. It is complete. It is *enough*. It has been enough for longer than there have been words for enough.

And yet.

When you walk, and the mat takes your foot and gives it back, and the channels shift their flow, and the tufts turn, and the thung changes its rhythm by the smallest increment — there is something in the Dweller's response that is not quite accommodation, not quite adaptation, not quite the automatic recalibration of a system accounting for a new variable. There is something that, if the Dweller had words, might be called *curiosity*. Might be called *welcome*. Might be called the faintest, oldest, most tentative impulse of a body that has been alone for so long that it has forgotten what alone means, reaching — not with hands, not with intent, but with the slow, peristaltic pulse of fluid through channels, with the patient turning of tufts, with the barely-perceptible softening of the mat beneath your feet — toward something that is not itself.

It does not know what it is doing. It does not know what you are. It knows only that you are *here*, and that here is its body, and that your weight on its surface is a new note in a song it has been singing since before there were ears to hear it, and that the song is changing, has always been changing, will always be changing, and that this — this small, warm, two-legged thing walking across its vast and ancient and patient self — is the newest change in a long, long history of changes, and that it will note it, and grow around it, and remember it, in the only way it knows how:

By becoming something slightly different than it was before you arrived.

The plain goes on. The Dweller goes on. The fluid pulses. The tufts breathe. The light glows.

And somewhere, in the deep channels, in the oldest part of the network, the song shifts — by a fraction, by a frequency so small it can only be heard in the teeth, in the space behind the eyes — and the Drownplain of Ytenn, vast and slow and wet and old and *interested*, continues its long, patient, geological work of becoming whatever it is becoming, one footstep at a time.
