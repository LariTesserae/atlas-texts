Into the Basin of Vostan, the water comes first.

It comes as mist that lingers for hours, beading on the flat stone roofs and gathering in the small channels at their edges. It comes as a hard afternoon downpour that drums on the thin slabs and sends grey water rushing through the ceramic gutters, down the clay pipes, along the shallow runnels cut into the street cobbles. It comes as condensation in the cool mornings, rising from the clay floor of the basin in a faint breath that clings to the north-facing walls and feeds the dark green moss there. It comes as damp that seeps up through the cracks between stones and uncurls the tiny ferns that live in those cracks. It comes, and it is gathered, and it is held.

The basin itself is the first thing that lives here—not a lake, not a pond, but a wide, shallow depression lined with smooth clay, partitioned by low stone walls into six or seven irregular cells. When the water arrives, it pools to a depth of perhaps a foot, maybe two in the deepest cell. It is grey, milky, full of fine clay in suspension. It does not drain. It sits. It sits for weeks, fed by condensation, by the damp that rises from the clay floor, by the cool air that pools at the bottom of the bowl like water itself. In the basin, the water is not a visitor. It is a resident.

And in the water, the leaf-walkers live.

They are small aquatic insects, no larger than a grain of rice, with bodies the color of the clay they skate upon. They move across the surface of the basin in quick, darting paths, their legs dimpling the water without breaking it. They are the basin's most visible inhabitants, the ones who live directly on its surface, and the residents of Vostan leave them alone. No one harvests them. No one studies them. They are simply there, as the water is simply there, as the clay is simply there. They have been here as long as the basin has held water, which is to say: always, or so it seems.

Around the basin, in the persistent damp, the sedge grows.

It is a water-loving plant, narrow-leaved and tough, with roots that grip the clay and stems that rise to knee height. It grows in clumps along the edges of the basin's cells, where the moisture is most reliable, and the residents harvest it with small stone knives, cutting the stems close to the base and leaving the roots to send up new growth. The cut stems are soaked in the basin water, then braided into cord and matting. The cord is used for everything—tying roof slabs, securing ceramic gutters, binding the wooden frames of looms. The matting is laid on stone floors, hung on walls, traded to the caravans that descend the plateau by the eastern path. The sedge is the basin's gift to the city, and the city takes it with the same patience with which the basin receives the water.

In one corner of the basin, the tree grows.

It is stunted, ancient-looking, with silver-green bark that peels in thin curls and a canopy no wider than a room. Its roots reach down through a crack in the clay, into whatever darkness lies beneath the basin floor. No one planted it. No one tends it. It has always been there, or so it seems. Its leaves are small and waxy, adapted to the spare light of the bowl and the dry air of the plateau. In the late afternoon, when the light goes golden and horizontal, the tree's shadow stretches across the basin water and trembles there, a dark shape on a shallow mirror of clouds. The residents do not speak of the tree. They do not gather beneath it. They do not carve their initials in its bark. It is simply there, as the basin is simply there, as the water is simply there.

On the north-facing walls of the central buildings, the moss grows.

It is not lush—nothing in Vostan is lush—but it is present, a dark green felt that softens the stone edges and holds the damp like a slow exhalation. It grows in the shade, where the sun reaches only briefly, and it feeds on the moisture that rises from the basin and clings to the stone. It is the greenest thing in Vostan, the only thing that is truly green, and it marks the buildings it grows on as belonging to the basin's sphere—as being, in some sense, part of the basin's body.

On the basin's partition walls, the lichens grow.

They are pale and crusty, grey-white and faint orange, spreading in slow circles across the stone. They mark time in a way the residents do not need to mark it—each ring a year, each circle a century, each wall a record of the basin's long accumulation. The lichens are the basin's memory, and they are patient, and they are silent.

In the cracks between the cobblestones, the ferns uncurl.

They are tiny, no larger than a hand, with fronds that are still coiled like scrolls when they first emerge. They grow where the damp rises through the stone, where the water in the runnels seeps beneath the surface and feeds the hidden soil below. They are the city's secret green, the life that exists in the gaps, and they are everywhere—in every crack, in every gap, in every space where water has found a way to linger.

On the warm stones, the lizards sun themselves.

They are small, quick, the color of the pale grey stone they rest on. They live in the older walls, in the dry-stacked blocks with tapered corners, where the gaps between stones are wide enough to shelter in. They eat the small insects that find their way into the bowl, and they are eaten by the raptors that circle the plateau rim but rarely descend. They are the city's visible animals, the ones you see most often, and they are still—so still that they become stone themselves, until they are not, and they are gone.

In the older walls, the small brown rats live.

They are uncommon—the city does not have the messy ecology of a port or a trade-town, does not offer the waste that rats thrive on. But they are there, in the oldest foundations, in the dry-stacked blocks where the gaps are widest. They move through the walls like water through stone channels, silent and purposeful, and they are tolerated. They do not take more than the walls offer. They do not block the water channels. They live by the rules of Vostan without knowing they are rules.

On the plateau rim, the raptors circle.

They are large, dark-winged, riding the wind that moves across the high open plateau without obstruction. They rarely descend into the bowl—the buildings break the wind, and the still air of the basin does not suit their hunting. But they are there, always, at the edge of the city's awareness, a reminder that the plateau is larger than the bowl, that the sky is larger than the plateau, that there are things in the world that do not live by the rules of Vostan.

And in the basin, in the damp, in the quiet, in the patient accumulation of water and clay and time, the residents of Vostan live.

They are four thousand, or so it seems—though it is hard to say, because some of the structures you'd call houses are clearly storage or workshop spaces, and some are shelters for the goats and small horned sheep that wander the upper terraces. They cluster in knots of maybe sixty dwellings around a shared courtyard with a well or a cistern, then there is a hundred yards of emptiness, then another cluster. They are not a continuous wall of habitation but a scattered jaw, and the spaces between them are as much a part of the city as the buildings themselves.

They are weavers, mostly. The wool comes from the plateau sheep, is washed in the basin water—the clay content gives the finished cloth a particular stiffness, a dull sheen that traders value—is dyed with mineral pigments found in the higher rock faces, is woven on wooden looms in the courtyard workshops. The looms clatter. The wool is soft and smells of fat. The finished cloth is folded and stacked and carried away on the backs of traders who descend the plateau by the eastern path.

They are patient. Nothing here is hurried. The city does not grow. It does not shrink. The number of roofed structures has not meaningfully changed in living memory. Births and deaths balance, or seem to. The water that arrives leaves. The cloth that is woven is taken away. The salt and metal tools and occasional new thing—a piece of glazed ceramic, a glass bead, a foreign seed—come in and are incorporated, slowly, into the existing grammar.

They sit in the communal weaving hall in the evenings, or they don't. They talk, or they don't. The hall is long and low, with stone benches around the walls and a roof supported by twelve pillars, each one carved with a different simple pattern: waves, zigzags, spirals, straight lines. It is very old. The patterns are worn smooth by the hands of those who have leaned against the pillars over the centuries, and they are still legible, still meaningful, though no one speaks of what they mean.

A woman sits on the edge of the basin, braiding sedge. Her hands move with the rhythm of someone who has done this ten thousand times and will do it ten thousand more. She does not look up as you pass. The sedge is damp from the basin water, and it bends easily in her hands, and the cord she makes is strong and even and will last for years.

This is who lives in the Basin of Vostan.

The water lives here. The sedge lives here. The tree lives here. The moss and the lichens and the ferns live here. The lizards and the rats and the leaf-walkers live here. The raptors circle at the edge of things. The residents live here, in the patient accumulation of stone and water and time, in the shallow bowl carved into the high plateau, in the city that does not grow and does not shrink and does not hurry and is sufficient.

Everything drains inward. Everything arrives at the basin. Everything sits, for a while, in the grey water and the smooth clay and the cool air at the bottom of the bowl. And then, slowly, it leaves—evaporated, absorbed, woven into cord, carried away on the backs of traders, fed back into the loom or the kiln or the quiet conversation of the weaving hall in the evening.

The basin receives. The basin holds. The basin gives back.

This is what lives in the Basin of Vostan.
