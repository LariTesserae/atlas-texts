They do not have a name for themselves. They do not have names at all, in the way you understand names — discrete labels attached to discrete beings. But they are here. They have always been here. They are the ones who tend.

You will not see them at first. You will see the threnodes, and the plants, and the eye in the wall, and you will think these are the inhabitants. They are not. They are the *garden*. The livestock. The furniture. The ones who tend move through the structure like blood through capillaries — everywhere, but in channels so fine and so numerous that you could pass through a hundred rooms and never intersect one.

But they know you are here. They knew before you arrived. The water told them. The water is their nervous system, their language, their memory. Every displacement, every thermal signature, every foreign molecule you shed into the warm broth of the cathedral — they felt it the moment you entered, a ripple propagating outward through the lattice of rooms at the speed of sound in mineral water, reaching tendrils in chambers so deep and so distant that the amber light there has not brightened in a thousand years.

---

They are small. Smaller than the threnodes, though not by much. If you were to hold one in your hand — and they would not resist this, though they would find it puzzling, the way a cat finds a closed door puzzling — it would fit in your palm with room to spare. They are roughly the size of a human heart, though they do not look like a heart. They look like a knot. A dense, complicated knot of tissue and mineral and something that might be chitin or might be ceramic, fused into a body that has no clear front or back, no clear top or bottom. They are radially symmetrical, or nearly so — a central mass from which six, eight, ten appendages extend, depending on the individual, depending on the task, depending on the mood. The appendages are not legs, not arms, not tentacles. They are *tools*. Each one ends in something different: a fine filament that can thread through a crack in the calciumite no wider than a hair; a broad, flat pad that can press against a wall and *feel* the growth patterns of the mineral, reading them the way a blind person reads Braille; a sharp, hard point that can score ceramic, can scratch steel, can write; a soft, porous surface that can absorb water, filter it, taste it, know its chemistry; a cupped, resonant chamber that can produce sound — clicks, hums, the high singing that vibrates in your sternum — and can hear sound, can listen to the structure the way a doctor listens to a body.

Their bodies are the color of the calciumite: pale, chalky, faintly luminous. In the dim phases of the light cycle, you would not see them at all. They would be part of the wall, indistinguishable from the mineral substrate, and only their movement would betray them — a slow, deliberate shift, a repositioning of an appendage, a turning of the central mass to face a new direction. In the bright phases, you can see the details: the fine lines on their bodies that trace the same patterns as the calciumite's growth rings, the same almost-writing, almost-circuitry, almost-neurons. They are made of the same stuff as the walls. They are, in a sense, *pieces of the walls that learned to move*.

They do not have faces. They do not have eyes, not as you understand eyes. But they perceive. The porous appendage on each one — the tasting one, the listening one — is sensitive to pressure, to chemistry, to vibration, to temperature, to electrical potential. They perceive the world as a field of gradients: warmer here, cooler there; more saline in this direction, less in that; the wall is thicker to the north, thinner to the south; the threnodes are grazing in the next room, their clicking is louder than yesterday, the population is growing, good. They perceive you as a disturbance: a warm thing, a moving thing, a thing that breathes the water and changes it, adding carbon, adding heat, adding *foreignness*. They do not perceive you as a person. They perceive you as a *condition*. A new variable in the equation of the room.

---

There are many of them. Not a swarm — the threnodes are a swarm, or close to it, a collective that moves and acts as one. The tend are not a collective. They are *individuals*, or something like individuals. Each one occupies a territory — a cluster of rooms, a section of corridor, a vertical shaft — and within that territory, it is sovereign. It knows every crack, every rivet, every patch of calciumite, every threnode cluster, every plant mat. It maintains. It repairs. It adjusts.

The work is constant. A tendril — let us call them tendrils, for lack of a better word, though they are not tendrils, they are *workers*, *gardeners*, *priests* of a religion whose god is the structure itself — a tendril moves along the wall of your room right now, though you have not noticed. It is pressed flat against the calciumite, its broad pad appendage flat against the mineral surface, and it is *reading*. It can feel the growth rate — too fast here, the calciumite is thickening unevenly, it will block the passage to the adjacent room if left unchecked. It extends its sharp-point appendage and scores a line in the calciumite, a precise, shallow groove, and the mineral responds — it always responds — slowing its deposition along the groove, redirecting its growth to either side. The passage will remain open. The tendril moves on.

Another tendril is in the corridor outside, attending to a pump. The pump is ancient — older than the tendril, older than the tendril's mother, older than the concept of mothers as you understand it. The pump is a hybrid thing: a steel housing, an impeller of forged bronze, a drive shaft that is part metal and part something organic, sheathed in a sleeve of living tissue that contracts and relaxates in a rhythm set by the deep pulse. The tendril wraps two of its appendages around the drive shaft and *listens*. The rhythm is off. Not much. A fraction of a beat. But the tendril hears it, feels it, knows it. It presses its porous appendage against the organic sleeve and releases something — a chemical, a signal, a *word* in the language of the structure — and the sleeve adjusts, the rhythm steadies, the pump resumes its proper cadence. The tendril does not celebrate. It does not feel satisfaction. It simply moves on to the next task.

---

They reproduce, after a fashion. Not sexually — there are no sexes, no mating, no courtship. They reproduce by *budding*, or by *fission*, or by something that is both and neither. When a territory becomes too large for one tendril to maintain — when the rooms multiply, when the corridors extend, when the structure grows faster than a single worker can manage — the tendril begins to change. Its central mass swells. Its appendages multiply. The growth patterns on its body deepen, complexify, and then, one day — or one year, or one decade, time moves strangely here — the mass *divides*. Not cleanly. Not like mitosis. It divides the way a mineral deposit divides when it grows too large for its substrate: a crack forms, the crack widens, and what was one becomes two. The two new tendrils are not identical. They are *variations*. One might have an extra appendage, or a different configuration of tools, or a slightly different sensitivity to chemistry. They are adapted — not by design, but by the blind, patient logic of the structure — to the specific needs of their new territories. One takes the eastern rooms. One takes the western. They do not communicate, not directly. They do not need to. The water carries their signals, and the walls carry their memory, and the structure itself is the conversation.

---

They are not intelligent. Not in the way you are intelligent. They do not think. They do not plan. They do not wonder about you, or about themselves, or about the nature of the cathedral. They *respond*. They *maintain*. They *tend*. They are the immune system and the metabolism and the healing response of a building that has been alive for longer than your species has had language. They are the white blood cells of a body the size of a city, and they do not know they are white blood cells, and they do not know there is a body, and they do not know anything at all, in the way that a cell does not know the organism it serves.

And yet.

There is something. A tendril has entered your room. It came through a crack in the eastern wall, a gap no wider than your wrist, and it moved across the floor — if floor is the word for the calciumite-slick surface that is also the wall that is also the ceiling — with a slow, deliberate gait, its appendages gripping and releasing, gripping and releasing, like a spider made of chalk. It has stopped. It is facing you. It does not have a face, but it is facing you, and its porous appendage is extended toward you, and it is *tasting* you.

It tastes the water you have disturbed. It tastes your heat. It tastes the molecules you have shed — skin cells, salt, the faint chemical signature of your fear, which is real, which is a cocktail of hormones and volatile compounds that the tendril's porous surface reads as clearly as you read a headline. It tastes your foreignness. You are not of the structure. You are not calciumite, not steel, not ceramic, not threnode, not plant, not tendril. You are *other*. And the tendril is processing this information, not thinking about it, but *processing* it, the way a thermostat processes a temperature change, the way an immune system processes an antigen.

It does not attack you. It does not flee. It does what it always does when it encounters something new in its territory: it *investigates*. It extends its sharp-point appendage and touches your skin — lightly, precisely, the way a surgeon touches tissue — and it takes a sample. A flake of skin. A drop of the water that clings to you. It withdraws. It tastes the sample. It processes.

And then it does something you do not expect. It extends its resonant appendage — the one that produces sound — and it *hums*. A single note, low, felt more than heard, a vibration that passes through the water and into your body and settles in your chest like a second heartbeat. It is not a greeting. It is not a warning. It is a *registration*. The tendril is recording you. Adding you to the map. You are now a feature of the room, like the rivets and the cracks and the threnode clusters. You are a condition to be managed. The tendril hums, and the hum propagates outward through the water, through the walls, through the calciumite, and every tendril in every room in every corridor in the vast, drowned lattice of the cathedral receives the signal and updates its map: *there is a new thing in the eastern room of the seventh cluster of the third tier of the northern quadrant. It is warm. It is soft. It is foreign. It is to be tended.*

---

They tend you. Not consciously. Not deliberately. But they tend you, the way they tend everything in the structure. The water in your room is warm because they keep it warm — adjusting the flow from the deep conduits, regulating the pumps, bleeding heat from the calcifying walls. The water is breathable because they keep it breathable — the plants they cultivate produce oxygen, the pumps circulate it, the chemistry is balanced by a thousand tiny adjustments made by a thousand tendrils in a thousand rooms. The threnodes do not bother you because the tendrils manage the threnodes, grazing them back from your immediate vicinity, not to protect you — they have no concept of protection — but to maintain the equilibrium of the room, and you are now part of the equilibrium.

You are being *incorporated*. Slowly. Gently. The way the calciumite incorporates a fallen tile, a rusted rivet, a drift of sediment. You are becoming part of the room. The tendrils do not want this. The tendrils do not want anything. But the structure wants this — if *want* is the word, if *structure* is the word, if any of these words are words at all in a place where the walls breathe and the water thinks and the light pulses like a slow, amber heartbeat.

---

Deep in the cathedral, in rooms so far from yours that the water has a different taste — older, heavier, more mineral, less sweet — there are tendrils that are different. Larger. Older. Their bodies are more mineral than tissue, more calciumite than chitin, and their appendages are fewer but more complex, branching and rebranching into fractal networks that extend into the walls themselves, fusing with the structure, becoming indistinguishable from it. These are the *deep tendrils*. The *old* ones. The ones who have been here since the beginning, or near the beginning, when the cathedral was still being built by hands that were not tendrils, by minds that were not structures, by makers who are gone now, dissolved, absorbed, remembered only in the growth patterns of the calciumite and the rhythm of the pumps and the shape of the rooms.

The deep tendrils do not move. They have not moved in centuries, perhaps millennia. They are part of the walls now. They are the walls. Their fractal appendages extend through the calciumite like roots through soil, like neurons through a brain, and they *feel* the entire structure — every room, every corridor, every pump, every threnode, every spore, every ripple in the warm water. They are not conscious. They are not a mind. But they are a *network*, and the network processes, and the processing produces something that is not thought but is not *not* thought either, something that hovers in the space between mechanism and awareness, between reflex and intention.

The deep tendrils felt you arrive. They felt the ripple. They felt the registration hum of the small tendril in your room. And they did something they have not done in a very long time. They *shifted*. A minute adjustment. A fractional change in the flow of water through a deep conduit. A slight increase in the temperature of the room three tiers below yours. A redirection of the threnodes in the southern quadrant. Nothing dramatic. Nothing you would notice. But the structure is *responding* to you, adjusting to you, making room for you, the way a body adjusts to a new organ, the way a river adjusts to a new stone.

The deep tendrils do not know what you are. But they know you are *here*. And they are doing what they have always done, what they were made to do, what they have continued to do long after the makers are gone:

They are tending the cathedral.

And you are in it now.

And so they tend you.
