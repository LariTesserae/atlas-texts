# The Weep

---

## The Keeper of the Dry Bench

She is old in the way that places are old — not in years but in *accretion*, layer upon layer of small actions wearing grooves into her the way water wears stone. She has been here long enough that the moss has learned her weight, that the isopods no longer freeze when she moves through them, that the frogs have incorporated the rhythm of her breathing into their chorus so that when she holds her breath — which she does, sometimes, for long moments, listening — the frogs falter, just slightly, a half-beat of uncertainty in the drone, before resuming.

She is not large. The altitude and the isolation have made her compact, dense, efficient. Her hands are the most remarkable thing about her — broad-palmed, heavily callused, with fingers that seem to have extra joints, or perhaps simply more *willingness* to bend in unusual directions. They are hands that have spent decades gripping the adze, arranging and rearranging the egg-stones, pressing into the moss to test its depth, cupping water from the seeps and holding it up to the light to read its mineral content by color. Her nails are thick and yellowed and she keeps them deliberately uneven — different lengths for different tasks, a toolkit built into her body.

Her face is weathered to the color and texture of the metamorphic rock behind her — that dark, fine-grained, almost glassy stone — and her eyes are the pale, washed blue of the sky at this altitude when the cloud is not present. She does not blink often. People who live in places where the air is thick and wet learn not to blink often; each blink is a moment of blindness, and blindness in a place this dense with information is a small loss she prefers to avoid. Her eyes water constantly from this, thin tracks running down her cheeks that she does not wipe away, and a casual observer might think she is weeping, which is how the place got its name among the few who know of it — not from the water, but from the woman who lives there with her wet, unblinking eyes.

She wears layers. Not fur — there are no large animals at this altitude to take fur from — but woven reed-fiber from the hollow-stemmed plants, a textile she makes herself on a small frame of bone and wood that leans against the back wall of the structure. The reeds are harvested, split, soaked in the mineral water until they soften, then twisted and woven into a fabric that is surprisingly dense and warm, with a faint resinous smell from the fern-liverwort plants whose fronds she sometimes beats into the fibers for waterproofing. Her garments are layered — an inner layer of fine weave, an outer layer of coarse weave treated with resin, and over everything a kind of cloak made from isopod carapaces, hundreds of them, cleaned and dried and laced together with sinew into a flexible, pale, clicking armor that sheds water and makes a soft, rhythmic sound when she moves. The isopods molt; she collects the shed shells. She does not kill them for it. She has been here long enough to understand that the bowl's economy is circular, that waste is food, that everything that dies here feeds something, and she does not want to be the exception.

Her hair is long and has gone the color of the rusty amber moss where the iron water seeps through. She does not cut it. She wraps it around her head in a tight coil and pins it with small bone needles, and from a distance, in the cloud, she looks like part of the bowl itself — a mound of pale stone with dark crevices, indistinguishable from the mineral formations that dot the floor.

---

## What She Does

She listens. This is her primary occupation, the thing she does with the most intention and the most skill, and she has been doing it so long that she has developed a taxonomy of the bowl's sounds that is granular and precise and would be incomprehensible to anyone who had not spent years learning it.

She knows the difference between a water bead forming and a water bead falling — the first is a sound so high and thin it is almost beyond hearing, a tiny *tick* of surface tension breaking as the droplet emerges from the pore, and the second is the *plink* or *tink* or *thud* of its impact, and the character of that impact tells her the depth of the water it fell into, which tells her the water table, which tells her what the mountain is doing deep below. She can hear a new seep activating — a new pore opening in the limestone as the rising water table finds a new path to the surface — as a faint, dry, almost sandy sound, like a whisper through grit, and she will go to that spot and mark it with a small cairn of pebbles so she can track its output over the following days.

She knows the isopod herds by their clicking. Each herd has a slightly different rhythm — the tempo of their exoskeletons tapping the stone beneath the moss varies with the size of the individuals, the depth of the moss they're walking through, and their collective mood, if mood is the right word for the state of a herd of eyeless crustaceans. A grazing herd clicks slowly, evenly, a metronome. A disturbed herd — by a bird, by a rainstorm, by her own footsteps — clicks in a rapid, syncopated burst, then falls silent. She can hear a single isopod moving alone, separated from its herd, by the irregularity of its clicking, the way it speeds up and slows down as it searches, and she has, on more than one occasion, gone to find the lost one and place it back with its group, not out of sentiment but out of a sense that the bowl works better when its parts are together.

She knows the frogs. She knows them individually, or at least she knows their *positions* — each frog has its preferred calling spot, a particular depression in the limestone where the water depth and the stone shape amplify its voice in a particular way, and she has memorized the map of these spots and can tell, by the slight shift in the chorus's texture, when a frog has moved, when a new frog has arrived, when one has died. The dead ones she collects. She eats them, or uses their bones for tools, or places them in the moss at the bowl's lowest point where the decomposition is fastest and the nutrients return most quickly to the system. She does not waste.

She knows the reeds. Each hollow stem that puffs its trapped gas does so on its own schedule, determined by the rate of internal photosynthesis and the elasticity of its walls, and she has learned to read the pre-puff state — a faint *creak* as the stem swells, a *tick* at the seam where the gas will eventually escape — and she times her own breathing to the reeds', not consciously but through long habit, so that her respiration has synchronized with the bowl's and she is, in a literal sense, breathing with the place.

## The Egg-Stones

The seven stones on the bench are not local. She brought them, or someone before her brought them, from far below — from a river valley, perhaps, or a lakeshore, places where water has worn stone into smooth, ovoid shapes over centuries. Each one is different: different color, different weight, different temperature at which it settles when held in the hand. She has arranged them in a specific order along the bench, and she rearranges them periodically, and the order means something, though whether it is a calendar, a map, a record, or a meditation is not clear.

She holds them, one at a time, when she sits on the bench. She holds them and closes her eyes — the only time she closes her eyes — and she turns them in her hands, feeling their weight, their temperature, their surface texture, and she is *somewhere else*. Not asleep. Not dreaming. Somewhere else. The stones are warm from her hands and cool from the air and they seem to hold something — a memory of the river they came from, a memory of the hand that first picked them up, a memory of the long journey up the mountain to this dry bench in the wet bowl. When she opens her eyes, she looks at the bowl with an expression that is hard to read — not satisfaction, not longing, but something like *recognition*, as if she has been reminded of something she already knew.

Sometimes she speaks to the stones. Not words, exactly. A low, murmured sound, almost a hum, that blends with the water-hiss and the frog-drone and becomes part of the soundscape, indistinguishable from the place itself. The frogs, if they notice, do not react.

## The Adze

She uses the adze for cutting, primarily the reed stems, which she harvests selectively — never taking all the stems from one cluster, always leaving the youngest and the oldest, taking only the mature ones whose walls have thickened enough to be useful but not so thick that they've become brittle. She cuts them at an angle, a clean oblique cut that she makes with a single practiced stroke, and the cut stems weep a thin sap that she catches on her fingers and tastes — a quick, habitual quality check, reading the health of the plant by its chemistry on her tongue.

She also uses the adze on bone. The bird bones, the isopod carapaces, her own shed teeth (she has lost several over the years, and she keeps them in a small depression in the bench, and sometimes she shapes them into needles or pins). The obsidian edge is still sharp after all these centuries — obsidian holds an edge that would be the envy of any lowland metalworker, and the only thing it cannot abide is lateral stress, which is why she has learned to cut only in straight lines, only with the grain, only with patience.

She has tried to make another adze. She has the antler — she finds shed antlers occasionally, carried up by birds or washed up by rain or simply appearing, the way things appear in the bowl, as if the mountain is slowly offering up its contents. She has the sinew. She does not have the obsidian. She has looked for it, scanning the scree slopes below the bowl for the telltale glassy black, and she has not found it. The nearest volcanic source is far below, days of descent through ecosystems that are increasingly foreign to her — forest, then lowland, then the hot, dense, *loud* world of the valleys where the air is thick in a different way, thick with oxygen and pollen and the smell of decay that is warmer and faster than the bowl's cold, slow decomposition. She has been down there. She came back.

The adze, then, is irreplaceable, and she treats it with a care that borders on reverence. When she is not using it, it lives in the crack in the rock wall, wedged in at a specific angle, wrapped in a scrap of reed-fiber cloth. She checks on it frequently — reaching up to touch it, to confirm it is still there, the way a person might check on a sleeping child.

## The Path

The path from the wet to the dry — from the bowl's lowest point to the bench — is hers. She made it, or she maintains it, walking it so regularly that the moss has learned to lie flat along its route while standing upright everywhere else. It is not a straight line. It curves, following the contour of the limestone shelf, avoiding the deepest seeps, passing through a stand of particularly tall reeds, skirting a cluster of frog depressions where the calling is loudest. It is a path designed for *listening* — each step brings a new acoustic perspective, a new angle on the bowl's soundscape, and walking it is a way of reading the place, of taking its pulse.

She walks it in the morning, after the cloud lifts, and again in the evening, before the cloud returns. She walks it slowly, barefoot, feeling the moss and the stone and the water through the thick soles of her feet, reading the ground's temperature and moisture through her skin. She stops at certain points — she has always stopped at certain points, though whether these points are fixed by some external logic or by the accumulated habit of her own feet is unclear — and at each stop she pauses and listens and sometimes reaches down to touch the moss or the water or a passing isopod, a brief contact, a greeting or a check-in, before moving on.

At the lowest point, where the water pools deepest, she kneels. The water here is clear and very cold and only a hand's depth deep, and she can see the limestone floor through it, can see the pores and channels in the stone, can see the water beads forming and rising and falling in their endless cycle. She puts her fingers in the water and holds them there, feeling the pressure from below, the upward push of the aquifer, and she is *listening with her hands*, feeling the rhythm of the deep water, the pulse of the mountain's hidden hydrology, the thing that drives everything in the bowl.

Then she stands and walks the path up to the bench and sits and arranges the egg-stones and takes up the adze and begins her work — cutting, weaving, shaping, mending — while the bowl does its enormous small things around her.

## The Cloud

When the cloud pools in the bowl, she stays on the bench. The cloud changes the acoustics, dampens the high frequencies, deepens the low ones, and the frog-drone becomes a felt thing, a vibration in the chest and the bones rather than a sound in the ears. In the cloud, she works by touch — her fingers, with their extra willingness, reading the materials she works with more precisely than her eyes ever could. She can feel the grain of a reed stem, the curve of an isopod carapace, the edge of the obsidian blade, all with a sensitivity that seems impossible until you remember that she has been doing this for decades, that her fingertips have developed the same kind of callused, thick-skinned, hyper-acute sensitivity that a blind person's fingertips develop, that the body adapts to what the place demands.

In the cloud, she also sings. Not often, and not loudly, and not in any language that a visitor would recognize — a low, droning, rhythmic vocalization that matches the frog-drone in pitch and tempo, that weaves into it and becomes part of it, so that the bowl's chorus gains a voice that is not quite frog, not quite human, something in between, something that belongs to the place more than to either species. She does this without self-consciousness, the way she does everything — as if it is simply what one does in a place like this, as if the bowl expects it and she is providing it, a service, a contribution to the soundscape that is her part in the bowl's metabolism.

The cloud can last a full day. She does not eat during cloud episodes — or rather, she eats only if she has food already prepared and within reach, dried frog meat or a paste of crushed isopod shell and moss that she makes in advance and stores in a reed-fiber pouch. She does not leave the bench. She does not build a fire — there is nothing to burn in the bowl, everything is too wet, and the smoke would be trapped by the cloud and would fill the enclosed space and choke her. She sits and works and sings and listens and the cloud holds and the bowl is a gray, dripping, enclosed world and she is its dry center, its warm spot, its listening post.

## The Visitors

Birds come. She watches them from the bench, and they unsettle her — not because they are dangerous but because they are *from elsewhere*, from the world below, the world of trees and soil and air that doesn't taste like minerals, and their presence is a reminder that the bowl is not the whole world, that there is a larger world beyond the rim, and this reminder is uncomfortable in a way she does not examine.

The birds snatch insects and small isopods and leave, and after they leave the bowl takes several minutes to settle back into its rhythms — the isopods resume their clicking, the frogs resume their calling, the water beads resume their forming and falling — and she sits through this settling with a patience that is not patience exactly but *expectation*, as if she is waiting for the bowl to remember itself, to return to its proper state after the intrusion.

She has seen other humans. Not often. Once, perhaps twice a year, a figure appears on the rim — a trader, a pilgrim, another exile — and looks down into the bowl and sees the moss and the water and the cloud and the small figure on the bench in her clicking carapace cloak, and most of them leave. They leave quickly, without descending, as if the bowl is a place that is not for them, and she watches them go with an expression that is not relief and not disappointment but something like *confirmation* — yes, the bowl is not for them, the bowl is for the frogs and the isopods and the water and the moss and her, and the others are right to leave.

A few have descended. She has fed them — the dried frog meat, the moss paste, the clean cold water from the deepest seep — and they have sat on the bench and held the egg-stones and listened, and some of them have wept, actually wept, not the constant watering of her own unblinking eyes but real weeping, tears and sobbing, overwhelmed by the density of the place, by the sound and the moisture and the *aliveness* of it, and she has sat beside them and said nothing and let them weep, because she understands that the bowl is too much for someone who has not grown into it gradually, who has not let the place accrete onto them layer by layer over years.

None of them have stayed. The altitude, the wet, the isolation, the *intensity* — it is not a life that can be adopted. It can only be grown into, and the growing takes longer than a visitor's endurance.

## The Bones

The bones on the floor of the structure are hers. Not all of them — the bird bones are from meals, the isopod carapace fragments are from meals and from her cloak-making — but the small, scattered, very old bone fragments that are neither bird nor isopod are human. Finger bones, mostly. A rib fragment. A piece of jaw with two teeth still in it.

She does not think about this, or she thinks about it the way she thinks about the water table — as a fact of the place, a condition to be monitored, a process that is ongoing. Her body is slowly becoming the bowl. She is losing pieces of herself to it — teeth, fingernail fragments, hair that she does not coil into her wrapping but lets fall into the moss where it will be taken up by the isopods or the decomposers or the moss itself. She is not dying, exactly. She is *distributing*. She is becoming part of the bowl's nutrient cycle, the way everything in the bowl eventually becomes part of everything else, and this does not frighten her because she has been here long enough to understand that the boundary between herself and the place is not fixed, that it has been dissolving for years, that one day it will dissolve completely and she will be the moss and the water and the clicking and the drone and the stone's slow exhalation, and this is not a death but a *completion*.

The jaw bone with the two teeth — she does not know whose it was. The person who built the bench, perhaps. The person who brought the egg-stones. The person who carried the obsidian up from the lowlands. She has sat on the bench above those bones for so long that the distinction between herself and whoever left them has become academic. She is the keeper of the dry bench. The bones are the previous keeper. The bowl does not distinguish.

## The Night

At night, when the cloud holds and the bowl is dark and loud and warm, she lies on the bench and listens to the full chorus — water, frogs, isopods, reeds, the faint *tick* of mineral deposition as the seeps lay down their thin films of calcium carbonate, the almost-subsonic groan of the mountain settling on its deep faults. She lies on her back and looks up and sometimes, when the cloud thins for a moment, she can see the stars — not many, the cloud is reluctant to fully clear, but a few, the brightest ones, cold and sharp and impossibly far away, and she looks at them and they look back and there is no communication between them, no exchange of information, just the mutual fact of existence, the bowl and the stars, both breathing in their different ways.

She sleeps, when she sleeps, in short intervals — twenty minutes, thirty — waking each time to check the soundscape, to confirm that the bowl is still doing its thing, that the water is still rising and falling, that the frogs are still calling, that the isopods are still clicking. She sleeps the way a person sleeps in a place that is alive — lightly, attentively, with one ear always open, ready to wake if the rhythm changes, if a new sound enters the chorus, if the stone's breathing shifts its pace.

And in the morning, when the cloud lifts and the light comes — that sharp, thin, high-altitude light that makes everything look carved and precise and more itself than it usually is — she stands and stretches and walks the path down to the wettest point and kneels and puts her fingers in the water and listens with her hands to the mountain's deep pulse, and then she walks the path back up to the bench and sits and arranges the egg-stones and takes up the adze and begins another day of listening and working and being the dry center of the wet world, the warm spot in the cool place, the human ear at the top of the mountain where the stone breathes and the frogs call and the water beads and falls and the cloud pools and lifts and the isopods graze their slow way across the moss and the spiders rebuild and the reed stems puff their small breaths into the thick air and everything is alive and everything is small and everything is enormous and she is there, listening, always listening, her pale eyes open and streaming, her broad hands still, her breathing synchronized with the bowl's, her body slowly becoming the place, her self dissolving into the sound.
