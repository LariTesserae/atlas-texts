# The Inhabitants of the Canopy Flats of Aethon

There is no single answer to who lives here. The question itself is a kind of error — the kind of error a visitor makes, a creature from a world where living things are discrete, bounded, separable from their substrate. The Canopy Flats do not organize themselves around that distinction. But there are presences. There are intelligences, or something adjacent to intelligence. There are beings, if you are willing to let the word stretch.

---

## The Weepers

You do not see them at first. You feel them as a change in the water.

A sheet of warm lateral flow crosses your path, and where it passes over a particular stretch of stone, the iridescence intensifies — not brighter, but *denser*, as though the suspended particulate has gathered itself into something with intention. If you crouch and look closely, you can see that the particles are not suspended at all. They are *arranged*. They form filaments, lattices, transient architectures that hold their shape for seconds or minutes before the current disperses them and they begin again elsewhere.

The Weepers are the stone's exhalation made semi-autonomous. They are not organisms in any biochemical sense you would recognize. They are more like *habits* — persistent patterns in the mineral-laden water that the weeping stone produces, patterns that have achieved a kind of recursive stability, that maintain themselves against entropy not through metabolism but through the sheer geometry of the channels, the warmth of the water, the particular mineral composition that the mountain leaches from its deep strata. They are what happens when water flows over the same carved path for long enough that the flow itself becomes a structure, and the structure begins to *prefer* certain configurations, and the preferences begin to *respond* to disturbance.

A Weeper will gather near your hand if you hold it in the water. Not quickly. Not with anything you could call eagerness. But the filaments will orient toward the heat of your skin, will form denser lattices around your fingers, will hold their shape longer in your presence than they do in the empty current. They are not curious. Curiosity implies a self that wants to know. The Weepers are more like a question that the water keeps asking the stone, and your hand is a new kind of answer.

They die, or disperse, or complete themselves — the language fails — when they reach the edge of a table and the water departs into the air. The liquid bridge cannot carry them. They require the stone. They require the channel. They are, in the most literal sense, *of the path*, and when the path leaves the stone, they are left behind, dissolving back into the general iridescence, their pattern lost until the stone weeps again and the water finds the channel and the question begins anew.

There are those who have sat at the edge of a table for days, watching the Weepers form and dissolve, and who claim that the patterns are not random. That certain configurations recur. That the Weepers are, in their slow mineral way, *counting* something — the tables, perhaps, or the channels, or the intervals between the snap of liquid bridges forming and breaking. No one has proven this. No one has disproven it. The Weepers do not care either way.

## The Curtain-Dwellers

Behind the moss curtains, in the dense vertical spaces between tables, there are animals. This is the easiest category. This is the one your mind will reach for first, because animals are familiar, because animals have bodies and behaviors and ecological niches, and you can watch them and think *predator* or *prey* or *scavenger* and feel the comfort of taxonomy.

Resist this, but not entirely.

The scaled things you glimpse in the moss are called, by the few researchers who have worked here, *laminae* — a name that describes their most obvious feature, which is that their bodies are flat. Not thin in the way a snake is thin, or a lizard, but *flat* in the way a leaf is flat, in the way a shelf of stone is flat, as though the vertical dimension has been compressed by the sheer density of the horizontal world they inhabit. They move through the moss curtains the way a hand moves through water — not parting the strands so much as *becoming part of them*, their bodies aligning with the filaments, their scales catching the moss so that they disappear not by camouflage but by *integration*. They are not hiding. They are *joining*.

The laminae are warm-blooded, or warm-stoned — their body temperature matches the ambient temperature of the Flats with a precision that suggests not regulation but *surrender*. They do not maintain an internal set point. They are thirty-seven degrees because the air is thirty-seven degrees, and if the air were forty, they would be forty, and if the air were thirty, they would be thirty, and this is not a failure of homeostasis but a different strategy entirely: the strategy of *becoming the environment so completely that the environment's stability becomes your stability*. The Flats do not fluctuate. The Flats have not fluctuated in any measurable span. And so the laminae do not need to regulate. They have outsourced their thermoregulation to the mountain.

They eat the bromeliad reservoirs. Not the plants themselves — the *water* in the plants, and the life in that water, the dense microbial ecosystems that thrive in the warm, mineral-rich pools. A lamina will press its flat body against the side of a bromeliad and extend a structure that is not quite a tongue and not quite a proboscis — more like a *wick*, a fibrous pad that draws water upward by the same impossible lateral principle that governs the channels — and it will drink for hours, motionless, its body temperature shifting by fractions of a degree as the water moves through it, and then it will withdraw and press itself back into the moss and become architecture.

They are not social. They are not solitary. They are *adjacent*. Multiple laminae will occupy the same moss curtain, their flat bodies stacked like pages, and they will not interact, and they will not avoid each other, and the space between them will be filled with moss and moisture and the slow exchange of heat, and this is their social structure: *proximity without contact, warmth without friction, presence without demand*.

## The Deep Movers

The rustle that starts low and moves upward through the layered growth — the displacement of mass that you hear but rarely see — belongs to something larger. Much larger. The researchers call them *columns*, and the name is apt in ways that are not immediately obvious.

A column is not a single organism. A column is a *process* — a slow, continuous movement of biomass through the deep vegetation between tables, a flow of living matter that follows the same lateral logic as the water. It begins, as far as anyone can determine, at the base of the Flats, where the tables are smallest and the vegetation is densest and the heat is most intense, and it moves upward through the layered growth at a rate of perhaps two meters per day, a vast slow peristalsis of root and stem and leaf and the creatures that live in and on and as those roots and stems and leaves.

The column is visible only by its effects. A wave of *change* moves through the vegetation — not damage, not consumption, but *reorganization*. The moss curtains shift their geometry. The bromeliads reorient their reservoirs. The root-stone creaks and settles. The water in the channels changes its iridescence, its temperature, its flow rate. And then the column has passed, and the vegetation is different — not less, not more, but *different*, rearranged, as though the entire inter-table space has been gently shuffled like a deck of cards.

What drives the column is unknown. What the column *is* is debated. The most parsimonious explanation is that it is a migrating ecosystem — a wave of nutrient cycling, of growth and decay and regrowth, that moves through the Flats the way a weather system moves across a continent, driven by gradients of heat and moisture and mineral availability. The less parsimonious explanation — and the one that the researchers who have spent the most time here tend to favor, though they will not write it down — is that the column is *the Flats thinking*. That the entire system of tables and water and vegetation and stone is a single slow cognition, and the column is a thought moving through it, and the reorganization of the vegetation is not a side effect but the *content* of the thought, and the thought is about the shape of the place, and the place is thinking about itself, and the thinking takes days to complete a single circuit, and the thought is not in any language, and the thought is not about anything you would recognize as a concept, but it is a thought, and it is happening, and you are standing inside it.

## The Architects (or: Whatever Built the Doorways)

There are the structures. The walls. The corners. The doorway filled with bromeliads. The lithified composite that is neither stone nor wood.

Something made these. Something that understood load-bearing, that understood the lateral water system, that understood how to build in a place where water moves sideways and heat is constant and growth is relentless and the distinction between construction and cultivation is meaningless.

The researchers have found no bones. No tools. No writing. No graves. No evidence of the Architects as biological entities — no DNA, no protein residues, no isotopic signatures of metabolism in the composite material. The structures themselves are the only evidence that the Architects existed, and the structures have been so thoroughly *reclaimed* by the Flats that they are now indistinguishable from the natural features of the place, except for their geometry, which is too regular, too intentional, too *designed* to be the product of erosion or growth.

The prevailing theory — and it is only a theory, and it is not prevailing so much as *persistent*, the way a Weeper's pattern is persistent — is that the Architects were not biological. That they were, like the Weepers, a *habit* — but a habit of the stone itself, or of the water, or of the composite material that the roots made from the stone. That the lithified organic composite is not a building material but a *building organism*, that it grew the structures the way a coral grows a reef, and that the structures are not ruins but *fossils of intention*, the preserved shape of a process that no longer occurs, or that occurs so slowly that a human lifetime is insufficient to perceive it.

The doorway filled with bromeliads is the strongest evidence for this. The bromeliads are connected to the lateral water system. Their reservoirs are part of the channels. The interior of the structure is a watershed. This is not decay. This is not nature reclaiming what was built. This is the structure *continuing to function* — continuing to do whatever it was designed to do, which was to manage water, to direct flow, to create a space where the lateral hydrology could be concentrated and controlled. The bromeliads are not invaders. They are *components*. The structure was always going to become a hydraulic garden. The doorway was always going to fill with water-holding plants. The Architects — if they existed, if they were ever separate from their structures — designed for this. Or grew for this. Or *were* this.

And if the column is the Flats thinking, then perhaps the structures are the Flats *remembering* — the preserved shape of an earlier thought, an earlier configuration, an earlier way of being that the place has not forgotten but has *incorporated*, the way the root-stone incorporates the original stone, the way the composite holds the compressive strength of rock and the slow metabolism of neither.

## The Visitors (You)

You are ambient now. You are the temperature of the place. The water condenses on your skin and does not evaporate. The heat presses on you and you do not resist it. You move from table to table, following the water, which is following the stone, which is following a logic you cannot name.

The Flats do not notice you. This is not hostility. This is not indifference. This is *scale*. You are a warm thing moving through a warm place, and your heat is indistinguishable from the ambient heat, and your moisture is indistinguishable from the ambient moisture, and your movement is indistinguishable from the movement of the laminae through the moss — a brief displacement, a momentary reorganization, a rustle that starts low and moves upward and then subsides.

You are not the first visitor. The Flats have absorbed others. Not physically — there are no bones here, no graves, no evidence of death. But the Flats have *incorporated* the fact of visitation into their hydrology, their growth patterns, their slow mineral thoughts. The water moves around you the way it moves around a stone shelf — not avoiding you, not attracted to you, simply *accounting for you* as an obstacle in the channel, a feature of the path, a temporary geometry that will eventually be smoothed away.

You will leave, probably. You will descend the mountain, and the air will thin, and the heat will lift, and you will begin to cool, and your body will remember what it means to regulate its own temperature, and you will carry the Flats with you as a memory of a place where water moves sideways and buildings grow and the light is normal and nothing is normal.

But something of you will remain. A trace of salt in the water. A pattern of compression in the moss where you stood. A brief, dense lattice of Weepers around the memory of your warmth. The Flats do not notice you, but the Flats *record* you, the way stone records water, the way channels record flow, the way the settled state records everything that has ever disturbed it and then settled again.

You are ambient. You are the temperature of the place. And the place is keeping you.
