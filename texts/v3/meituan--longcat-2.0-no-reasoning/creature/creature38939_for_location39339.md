They are not the builders. Not anymore. Perhaps they were, once — the ones who laid the first substrates, who set the first machines turning, who cut the first terraces into the high country slope and said *here, this will hold*. But that was so long ago that the distinction between builder and built has dissolved, the way the distinction between ground and construction has dissolved, the way the distinction between the living and the made has dissolved. They are the current expression of the place. They are what the Weeping Terraces have grown.

You see them first as movement in the green — a flash of damp fabric between vine curtains, a hand reaching to test the moisture on a wall, a figure stepping over a root as thick as a thigh with the unconscious grace of someone who has stepped over that root ten thousand times. They are small. Not short, necessarily, but *compact* — bodies that have adapted to a world of close quarters and low ceilings and narrow passages between machinery. Their shoulders are broad from a lifetime of climbing the terrace gradients. Their hands are extraordinary: large, dexterous, perpetually stained with something — machine oil, plant sap, the rust-colored residue of old iron fittings, the pale dust of degraded concrete. They use their hands the way birds use their beaks, as primary instruments of engagement with the world. They touch everything. They test the give of a wall with their palm. They press their fingers into moss to gauge its moisture. They run their thumbs along pipe fittings to feel for vibration, for the subtle change in hum that means a blockage forming somewhere downstream.

Their skin has the quality of the place itself: damp, cool, slightly luminous in the dim light. The perpetual humidity has left its mark. Some of them have a faint mossy patina along their forearms and the backs of their necks — not actual moss, not quite, but a darkening, a texturing, as though the boundary between their skin and the environment has become negotiable. Their hair, where they have it, is long and perpetually wet, worn in thick ropes or woven into the vine curtains themselves so that a section of hanging green will suddenly resolve, on close inspection, into a sleeping figure, curled in a hammock of living plant matter, breathing slowly, dreaming whatever dreams come from a life spent entirely within a single ecosystem.

They do not speak much. The place speaks for them — the mechanical murmur, the weeping water, the creak of vine against structure, the distant clank of the beetle-machines dismantling some old wall. When they do speak, it is in a language that sounds like the place sounds: low, rhythmic, full of glottal stops and liquid consonants, words that seem to emerge from the throat the way water emerges from a seam in old concrete — not forced, but inevitable, the pressure behind them too great to contain. They speak to the machines as readily as to each other. A woman crouched beside a pump housing, adjusting a valve with a wrench nearly as long as her arm, will murmur to it — not words, exactly, but a low, crooning sound, the way you might speak to an animal in pain — and the machine's vibration will shift, settle, find a new equilibrium. Whether this is superstition or engineering or something that has become indistinguishable from either, no one can say. The machines respond. That is enough.

They eat what the place provides. The mycelium boards that digest waste also, in their slower cycles, produce fruiting bodies — thick, pale, dense-fleshed mushrooms that taste of mineral and smoke. The ferns yield fiddleheads in the upper terraces where the light is strongest. The vines produce a starchy, astringent tuber that must be soaked in running water for three days before it is edible. There are fish in the channels — small, blind, pale things that have adapted to the perpetual dimness and the warm, mineral-rich water that cascades down from the factory districts. The inhabitants catch them with nets woven from vine fiber, or sometimes with their hands, standing motionless in the channel for minutes at a time, waiting for the fish to nose against their fingers. They eat without ceremony. They eat standing up, walking, working. Food is fuel, and fuel is scarce enough that waste is unthinkable.

They maintain. This is their primary activity, the thing that fills the hours between sleeping and eating and the brief, intense periods of construction or demolition that punctuate the days like exhalations. They maintain the machines, the structures, the water channels, the vine curtains, the mycelium boards, the terraces themselves. Maintenance here is not repair — it is *negotiation*. The place is always trying to become something else. The vines want to pull down the awnings. The water wants to find new paths through the substrates. The mycelium wants to digest the structural timbers. The machines want to run faster, slower, differently, their ancient programs drifting like compass needles near a magnetic pole. The inhabitants move through this constant pressure the way a gardener moves through a garden — not fighting the growth, but directing it, shaping it, allowing it in some places and resisting it in others, always aware that the total victory of any single element would mean the collapse of the whole.

They have a relationship with time that is difficult to describe to someone from outside. They do not count years. They count *cycles* — the cycle of the water channels (clean, silt, clean), the cycle of the mycelium boards (feed, fruit, rest), the cycle of the machines (run, stutter, run). A life is measured not in duration but in the number of cycles witnessed, the number of maintenance passes completed, the number of times a particular vine has been redirected from a particular gutter. An elder is not someone who has lived long but someone who has *seen* long — who can remember the last time the seventh terrace smelter went cold, who can recall the configuration of the east terrace before the last major demolition, who carries in their body the knowledge of how the place was arranged in some previous iteration and can feel, in their bones and their damp skin, when the current arrangement is drifting too far from the pattern that holds.

They do not leave. There is nowhere to go. The valley below is mist and the mist is the place and the place is the world. They have heard — the oldest among them have heard, in the way that one hears things in a place where sound carries strangely through water and metal and living green — that there are other places. Other terraces, perhaps, or something else entirely. Flat places. Dry places. Places where the sky is visible and the ground is not built. These stories circulate the way rumors circulate in any small community: repeated, embellished, doubted, repeated again. No one has gone to see. No one has come back to confirm. The terraces are enough. The terraces are everything.

And at night — if night is the right word for the dimming that occurs when the factory cycles slow and the haze thickens and the light that filters through the condensation takes on a deeper, more amber quality — they gather. Not in a central place; there is no central place. They gather in the interstices, the gaps between terraces, the wide landings where three or four passages converge and the vine curtains part to reveal a view of the valley below, the mist pooling in the built folds, the distant glow of the smelter on the seventh terrace like a low, red star. They sit on the damp ground. They pass food. They speak, when they speak, in low voices, and the sound of their voices blends with the sound of the place — the water, the machines, the creak and sway of the green — until you cannot tell where the human sound ends and the environmental sound begins.

They are not unhappy. They are not happy. They are *of* the place, the way the moss is of the place, the way the machines are of the place, the way the water is of the place. They have been grown by it as much as they have grown it. Their bodies know its rhythms. Its rhythms know their bodies. When one of them dies — and they do die, though slowly, though rarely, the damp and the close air and the mineral-rich diet conspiring to produce a kind of dense, slow-burning vitality that lasts and lasts — the body is carried to the lower terraces, to the mycelium boards, and laid upon them. The boards accept what is offered. The cycle continues. The place weeps. The place grows. The inhabitants move through it, touching, murmuring, maintaining, and the terraces hold, and the green rises, and the machines turn, and the water finds its way down through the built world like blood through a body, like memory through a mind, like time through a place that has learned, at last, to hold its own shape against the pressure of becoming something else.
