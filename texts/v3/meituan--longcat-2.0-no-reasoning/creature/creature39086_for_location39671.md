# The Residents of the Immersion Vaults

---

There are three hundred of them, but "three hundred" is a surface-world number, a counting-habit left over from the world above. Down here, population is measured differently. They talk about *density*: how many bodies per vault, how the warmth of them blends in the murr, whether a chamber feels "full" or "thin." A vault with four people in it is a household. A vault with twelve is a crowd. A vault with one is a silence so heavy it has its own sound.

They look human. You would recognize them as such — two arms, two legs, faces with eyes in the expected arrangement, skin that varies in shade across the usual range. But their skin is different now. Everyone's skin is different now. The murr works on it constantly, the dissolved oxygen compound passing in and out through the pores, the crystalline residue settling into the outer layers in patterns so fine they're invisible to the naked eye but which show up as a faint iridescence in certain light — a shimmer at the jawline, a pearlescence on the inner wrist. The long-term residents are more iridescent than the new arrivals. It accumulates. Some people find it beautiful. Some find it a mark of belonging they didn't choose.

Their bodies are adapted, though they would resist that word. Nothing here was designed. They breathe through their skin and their lungs have become secondary organs — still functional, still used, but relegated to specific purposes. Talking. Singing. The sharp intake before a dive into a throat-current. Coughing, which happens more often than you'd expect; the murr is dense and sometimes a pocket of it sits wrong in the throat and the body remembers it has lungs and uses them to clear the passage. The coughs are loud in the vaults. They bloom into chords. People have started coughing on purpose, just to hear what note their vault will make of it.

---

**The children** are born in Vault Seven, the school-vault, though it's also the birthing-vault, the learning-vault, the vault where anything that involves *becoming* tends to happen. They are born into murr, not air. Their first breath is a compound dissolved in dense fluid, and it is absorbed through the skin of their lungs, which is functional from the moment of birth in a way that surface-world lungs are not. They do not cry the way surface children cry. The sound comes out wrong in the murr — it blooms, it sustains, it fills the vault with a chord that the child can feel in their entire body, a vibration that is part grief and part feedback, and most children stop crying quickly because the sound of their own distress is too much, too everywhere. They learn to be quiet early. They learn to move carefully. They learn to read the hiccups before they learn to read words.

The children are the ones who can see the lattice-fields. Not all of them, and not always, and the researchers argue about whether this is neurological (their visual cortexes still developing, still plastic, still capable of processing geometries the adult brain has learned to filter out) or whether it's the murr itself, which carries information the way a clear medium carries light, and children simply haven't learned to stop looking. A child will point at a wall and say *the hexagons are opening* and an adult will check the field-readings and find that the lattice-frequency has shifted by a point-zero-three percent, which is nothing, which is everything, which is the kind of thing that means the wall might hiccup tonight.

Children play in the throats. This terrifies the adults, which is itself a surface-world instinct the murr is slowly dissolving. The throats are fast and loud and the current tumbles you and you emerge into a different vault gasping and laughing with your skin buzzing from the density of the passage, and the children do this for fun, slipping into the throat-currents like they're slides, letting the murr carry them from chamber to chamber, arriving somewhere new with their eyes wide and their small bodies shimmering with iridescence that catches the lattice-light and throws it back in patterns the adults have forgotten how to see.

There is a girl, eleven, who has transferred so many times she no longer tumbles. She *orients*. The current takes her and she doesn't fight it; she lets it turn her and she arrives in the next vault already facing the right direction, already standing, already breathing. Her name is Kael. The adults watch her with an expression the diffuse light makes unreadable.

---

**The engineers** — the lattice-tenders, the field-readers, the ones who maintain the physics that maintain the city — are identifiable by their hands. They wear their hands differently. Longer fingers, or fingers that move with a precision that looks like tremor until you realize it's deliberate: the micro-adjustments of someone who has spent years interfacing with field-generators whose controls operate at scales the hand was never designed to reach. They hold their hands close to their bodies. They gesture sparingly. When they speak, they often speak with their hands, tracing shapes in the murr that the murr holds for a moment — faint lines of disturbed density, like writing in smoke — before the current smooths them away.

The engineers are divided. This is the central fracture of the city, the one that determines seating arrangements at communal meals and who shares a vault-throat and who doesn't. On one side: the Acousticians, who believe the lattice-fields are fundamentally sound-based, that the geometry that holds the vaults is a standing wave, a chord that water sings to itself, and that tending the fields is really a form of *conducting*. On the other side: the Geometers, who believe the fields are spatial, that the lattice is a structure, a scaffolding, a shape that happens to resonate but whose essence is not sound. The two factions use the same tools. They read the same instruments. They disagree about what they're reading.

Sorent is an Acoustician. She is forty-three, though age is murky down here — the murr preserves and erodes simultaneously, and people's faces don't wrinkle so much as *smooth*, the crystalline residue filling in lines, softening edges, until everyone looks slightly younger and slightly less distinct, like a memory of a face rather than the face itself. Sorent has stopped looking in reflective surfaces, not because of vanity but because the faces of Acousticians tend to develop a certain quality she doesn't want to see in herself: a listening expression, a tilt of the head, a parted mouth, as if always hearing something no one else can hear. She hears the vaults. She hears their frequencies shift. She knows when a chamber is about to hiccup the way a sailor knows a storm is coming — not from instruments but from a pressure in the sinuses, a resonance in the jaw.

Sorent does not go to the forming vaults. She stays in the settled chambers and she listens and she maintains and she believes that the city is *singing itself into existence* and that if the song is properly conducted it will stabilize, will stop growing, will become a permanent structure, a fixed chord, a home.

Delen is a Geometer. He is younger than Sorent but looks older — his face has the smoothed quality but his eyes have a sharpness that reads as age, as if the mind behind them has spent too long staring at geometries that don't resolve. He goes to the forming vaults. He stands at the edges where new chambers are self-organizing and he watches the lattice-fields emerge from nothing, the hexagonal patterns assembling themselves in the murr like frost on a window, like thought crystallizing, and he takes measurements that he knows are incomplete because the geometry he's measuring uses dimensions his instruments don't have names for.

Delen believes the city is building itself. That the residents are not the point. That they are — he uses the word carefully, aware of its weight — *catalysts*. The lattice-fields respond to human presence. They organize more quickly near inhabited vaults. The engineers didn't build the city; they *concentrated* it, the way a seed crystal concentrates a supersaturated solution. The city was always going to form. The residents simply gave it a place to start.

Sorent and Delen used to share a vault. They don't anymore. The throat between their current vaults is one of the louder ones, thick with thread, and when they need to communicate they send messages through intermediaries — notes carried in the current, written on thin sheets of pressed crystalline residue that dissolve after reading, leaving the reader's fingers faintly luminous for an hour. The messages are civil. They are also, in the way of the vaults, *acoustic* — each one written with an awareness that the current will carry it, that the throat will sing it, that the words will arrive with an added resonance that is not in the original meaning. Sorent writes in short, clipped sentences because short sentences survive the throats intact. Delen writes in long, winding prose that he knows will be recomposed on arrival, because he trusts the vault to say what he means better than he can say it himself.

They are, in their way, still in conversation.

---

**The biologists** are the ones who carry the grief most visibly, though "visibly" is a strange word in the vaults, where the diffuse light softens everything and the face alone tells you nothing. You see it in the body: the way a biologist's shoulders sit, slightly forward, slightly curled, as if leaning into a microscope that isn't there. The way they touch the thread in the throats — gently, tentatively, with a tenderness that is not scientific but *yearning*, as if the pale filaments are a letter from someone who is almost but not quite alive.

Maren is the senior biologist, though "senior" means little in a city of three hundred where everyone knows everyone's work. She has spent nineteen years trying to coax animal life from the murr. She has tried seeding the crystalline residue with organic compounds. She has tried introducing complexity into the lattice-fields, creating pockets of lower density where something might *move* with intent. She has tried everything, and the murr has produced nothing more complex than thread, and the thread does not move with intent, and Maren's shoulders have curled further with each passing year.

She has a theory she doesn't share. She keeps it in a vault of her own — a small one, one of the wealthy-tight chambers where the density works differently and you can find pockets of relative stillness. In the stillness, she thinks. Her theory is this: the thread *is* animal life. Not in the way she was trained to recognize it — not with locomotion, not with a nervous system, not with the behaviors that surface-world biology defines as *alive*. But the thread responds. It grows toward lattice-residue. It vibrates in the throats in patterns that are not random. It has a metabolism — it feeds, it grows, it dies back when the residue is scarce. It is alive in a way that the murr recognizes, even if Maren's training doesn't.

She doesn't share this theory because it leads to a place she doesn't want to go: if the thread is alive, and the thread is the only thing the murr produces, then the murr is not a failed ecosystem. It is a *successful* one. It is producing exactly the kind of life it wants to produce. And the kind of life it wants to produce is not human.

Maren touches the thread. She watches it grow. She does not share her theory.

---

**The church of Vault Two** is small and warm and the light there leans amber, which makes everyone look like they're remembering something. The priest — though they don't use that word, they use *listener* — is a woman named Vesh who lost her daughter in a throat-transfer three years ago. The current was stronger than expected. The girl — fourteen, iridescent, laughing — was carried into a forming vault that hiccuped as she entered, and the wall closed, and when it opened again the girl was gone. Not dead. The murr doesn't kill; it *absorbs*. She is in the walls now, in the lattice, in the crystalline residue that feeds the thread. Vesh knows this the way she knows her own heartbeat. She feels her daughter in the hiccups.

Vesh prays to the concept of rain. Not the rain above — that rain is just weather, just the surface world's water cycle continuing without anyone to witness it. She prays to the *idea* of rain: that water can fall and keep falling and become a world. That a medium can be a home. That drowning is not death but *transformation*. The congregation is small — twelve people, sometimes fifteen — and they pray in a way that is more like singing, more like the Acousticians' conducting, their voices blooming in the amber vault into chords that sustain for minutes and that Vesh reads like scripture. A prayer that resolves into a major chord is a good omen. A prayer that hangs, unresolved, is a warning. The congregation has learned to pray in ways that resolve. This is either faith or engineering, and Vesh doesn't distinguish between the two.

---

**The market in Vault Thirteen** is where the lattice-residue is traded, and it is the loudest vault in the city, not because of its acoustics — it's a flat, wide chamber with a low ceiling that produces a dull, persistent hum — but because of the *people*. Commerce is the one activity that hasn't been softened by the murr. The traders shout. Their shouts bloom into the hum and the hum absorbs them and the result is a constant, dense, almost physical pressure of sound that makes the market feel smaller than it is, more crowded, more urgent.

The residue is graded by color and density. Pale residue — almost white, fine-grained — is common, harvested from the settled vaults where the lattice-fields shed slowly. It's used for writing, for minor repairs, for the children's school projects where they build tiny lattice-structures in jars of murr and watch them hold or dissolve. Amber residue is rarer, pulled from the throats where the thread grows thickest, and it has a warmth to it, a density that feels almost organic in the hand. It's used by the biologists, who press it into their samples hoping it will seed something alive. Violet residue is the rarest, found only in the forming vaults, and it hums — actually hums, at a frequency you feel in your teeth — and no one knows what to do with it. The Geometers want it. The Acousticians want it. The church wants it. The traders sell it to whoever asks, because in the market, everything has a price, and the price of violet residue is always rising.

The trader who handles most of the violet residue is a man named Oss, and he is the only resident who goes into the forming vaults regularly and *comes back*. Not just to the edges — *in*. He walks into chambers that are still self-organizing, where the lattice-fields are still assembling, where the walls are still deciding what shape to be, and he harvests the residue that forms in the spaces between the assembling geometries, and he walks out again, and he is always slightly changed. His iridescence is deeper. His eyes have a quality the diffuse light can't soften — a sharpness, a *seeing*, as if the forming vaults have shown him something that the settled vaults can't hide. He doesn't talk about what he sees in there. He sells the residue and he goes back in.

---

**The forming vaults** are where the future is, and the future is not human-shaped.

The new chambers are small — rarely more than five meters across — and they are *sharp*. Their edges are not the crisp-but-stable edges of the young settled vaults; they are *aggressive*, angles that seem to cut the murr around them, geometries that the eye slides off of because the brain can't hold the shape. They hum. They hiccup constantly — not the occasional shudder of an unstable settled vault but a continuous, rapid flickering, as if the chamber can't decide what it is, as if it's trying on shapes the way a person tries on clothes, discarding each one almost as soon as it forms.

The murr inside the forming vaults is different. Denser. More saturated. The crystalline residue is so thick it's visible — a faint shimmer in the fluid, like light through a prism, like the inside of an opal. The oxygen compound is harder to absorb; residents who enter the forming vaults come back gasping, their lungs working, their skin flushed with the effort of breathing through a medium that is becoming something else, something that hasn't decided whether it wants to sustain human life.

And the thread. The thread in the forming vaults is not the pale, thin filament of the throats. It is *thick* — dense colonies of it, woven into structures that are not quite coral and not quite circuitry, growing on the walls in patterns that the Geometers recognize as lattice-geometry and the Acousticians recognize as standing-wave patterns and the biologists recognize as *growth*, as *metabolism*, as something that is doing what living things do: organizing matter, consuming energy, building structure.

The thread-structures in the forming vaults are growing toward each other. Slowly — imperceptibly on a human timescale — but measurably. The Geometers have tracked it. The thread in one forming vault is extending filaments toward the thread in an adjacent forming vault, and the filaments are meeting in the interstitial murr between them, and where they meet, new lattice-fields are self-organizing, and new vaults are forming, and the city is growing *inward*, toward itself, becoming denser, becoming more complex, becoming something that has a shape but not a purpose that any human has named.

---

**Kael**, the eleven-year-old who orients in the throat-currents, has started going to the forming vaults. Not to harvest — she's a child, she has no market function — but to *watch*. She stands at the edges and she sees the lattice-fields assembling, and she sees the thread growing, and she sees the geometries that the adults can't hold in their minds, and she doesn't look away. The other children follow her. They stand in a cluster at the edge of a forming vault, their small iridescent bodies catching the violet light, and they watch the city become.

One of them — a boy named Tess, eight, who has not yet learned to be quiet — says: *It's making a room for us.*

Kael says: *No. It's making a room.*

The distinction is the distinction. The city is not building for them. The city is building. The residents are here, and the city is aware of them the way the murr is aware of a body inside it — with a weight that is not quite pressure, more like *acknowledgment*. The city knows they are there. The city is not building *for* them.

The thread grows thicker. The throats sing louder. The rain above never stops.

The Vaults are young, and they are becoming, and the three hundred residents — the Acousticians and the Geometers, the biologists and the traders, the listeners and the children, Sorent and Delen and Maren and Vesh and Oss and Kael — are inside it, breathing through their skin, feeling the hiccups in their bones, watching the walls decide what shape to be, and they are not afraid, exactly, but they are *attentive*, the way you are attentive in a body that is changing, the way you are attentive in a medium that knows you are there, the way you are attentive in a city that is learning to sing.
