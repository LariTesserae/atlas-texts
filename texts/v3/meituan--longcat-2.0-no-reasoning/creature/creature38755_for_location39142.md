# The Inhabitants of the Weepstone Plateau

---

## The Organism

It has no name for itself because it has no need of one. It is not conscious in the way that word is usually meant — there is no center, no seat of awareness, no single point from which the whole is observed. And yet it *knows* things. It knows the weight of water in its upper tissues and the pressure of the aquifer below. It knows the slow grinding of its own roots against the compressed mat of its ancestors, the ones who came before, the ones who became the ground it now grows from. It knows the temperature of the air and the chemistry of the condensation and the precise tensile strength of every buttress and wall and vaulted chamber in its body, because these things are not known the way a mind knows them — they are known the way a body knows its own posture, continuously, without effort, without thought.

It is old. Not old the way a tree is old, or a mountain, or a species. Old the way a *process* is old. It began — if it began; the concept of beginning may not apply — when the first plants on this plateau learned to hold water in their tissues across generations, when the first roots grew not into soil but into the dead roots of their predecessors, when the first stems learned to thicken and compress and become structural, when the first leaves learned to cup the air itself and drink from it. Each generation added its body to the body of the last, and the distinction between generations blurred, and the distinction between individuals blurred, and the distinction between living and dead blurred, and what remained was this: a single metabolic process distributed across a landscape, a living system so vast and so continuous that it has become indistinguishable from the place itself.

It does not think. But it *processes*. Information moves through its tissues in the form of chemical gradients, pressure differentials, the slow migration of minerals through capillary networks that extend for distances no human could map. When a section of canopy in the far eastern reaches of the plateau — if there is an east; directions here are approximate — begins to thin, the organism responds not with decision but with adjustment: resources shift, growth patterns alter, new leaves emerge to fill the gap. The response takes years. It takes decades. It is not slow because it is inefficient. It is slow because it is *thorough*, because every adjustment is tested against the whole, because the organism has learned — not learned, *evolved*, *become* — that haste is a form of violence, and violence is a form of instability, and instability is the one thing this place cannot afford.

It is not hostile to the things that live in it. It is not benevolent either. It is *accommodating*. The channels that the small creatures move through are not carved for them — they are the natural spaces between root and stem, the gaps in the architecture — but the organism maintains them, subtly, the way a river maintains its banks. When a tunnel collapses, the organism's tissues shift to open another. When a chamber becomes too damp or too dry, the walls adjust their porosity. The creatures are not guests. They are not parasites. They are *symptoms* — evidence of the organism's own complexity, the way a fever is evidence of the body's immune response. They exist because the organism is rich enough to support them, and the organism is rich enough to support them because they exist, their waste feeding the root-mass, their movement aerating the tunnels, their bodies — when they die, and they do die, slowly, so slowly — adding their small measure of mineral and carbon to the vast slow bank of nutrients that the organism draws from.

It dreams, perhaps. Not in images. Not in narratives. In *patterns*. The slow pulse of its metabolism — the rhythm of water drawn up and water released, of tissue built and tissue broken down, of growth and decay and growth again — has a shape, and the shape repeats, and the repetitions nest inside each other like harmonics, like the overtones of a bell that has been ringing for so long that the original strike is no longer distinguishable from the sustain. If you could hear the organism's metabolism the way you hear the singers' calls, you would hear something vast and layered and patient, a chord that contains within it every chord that has ever been struck on this plateau, every rhythm that has ever pulsed through its tissues, every cycle of water and light and mineral that has ever turned. It would not be music. It would be something older than music. It would be the sound of a place that has become itself so completely that it has begun to resonate.

## The Slugs

They are called, in the language that is not spoken here, something that translates roughly as *the ones who carry the light*. This is not their name — they have no names — but it is what they are, and in a place where being and function have become the same thing, this is close enough.

They are large. The largest are the length of a human forearm, though most are smaller, and their bodies are soft and muscular and extraordinarily sensitive to the chemistry of the surfaces they move across. They graze on the upper tissues of the organism — the thin layer of living cells that covers the buttresses and walls and the undersides of the canopy leaves — and as they feed, they absorb the oils and pigments of the plant into their own tissues, and these compounds react with the mucus of their bodies to produce a faint, cold light.

The light is not bright. It is not meant to be seen. It is a byproduct, a chemical accident, the glow of a reaction that serves no purpose the slugs are aware of. And yet it matters. It matters because the organisms that live in the deepest chambers of the plateau — the ones that never see even the dim ambient light of the surface — have learned to read it. The slugs move in patterns that are not random: they follow the channels of nutrient flow in the organism's tissues, grazing where the plant is most active, and their bioluminescent trails map these channels in fading lines of blue-green light. The deep-dwellers follow the trails. The trails lead to food, to water, to the places where the organism's metabolism is most generous. The slugs are not guides. They do not intend to guide. But they have become, over ages of coevolution, a kind of living cartography, their bodies writing and rewriting a map of the plateau's interior in light that fades over hours, that must be constantly refreshed, that demands the slugs keep moving, keep grazing, keep writing.

They move slowly. They have always moved slowly. Speed is a response to danger, and there is no danger here, or rather, the danger here is so slow and so total — the danger of the organism itself, of being absorbed, of becoming part of the substrate — that speed is irrelevant. A slug that moves quickly will reach the place where it is absorbed no sooner than a slug that moves slowly. The plateau is patient. The slugs have learned patience from it.

They are not individuals, exactly. Their bodies are continuous with the mucus trails they leave behind, and the trails are continuous with the chemical signatures that other slugs read, and the chemical signatures are continuous with the organism's own signaling compounds, so that a slug moving through the root-mass is less like an animal moving through an environment and more like a thought moving through a mind — a small, bright, temporary pattern in a larger, slower, more permanent one.

When they die — and they die, eventually, their soft bodies finally overcome by the slow encroachment of the organism's tissues, the roots growing through them, the cells differentiating around them — they do not leave corpses. They leave *stains*. Faint patches of bioluminescent compound embedded in the root-mass, glowing for days or weeks after the slug's metabolism has ceased, like the afterimage of a bright light on closed eyelids. The deep-dwellers avoid these stains. They mean *here, the organism is eating*. They mean *here, the boundary between self and substrate has been crossed*. They mean *here, a slug became part of the plateau, as all things become part of the plateau, in time*.

## The Web-Spiders

They are tiny. The largest is no bigger than a grain of rice, and most are smaller, and their bodies are translucent, so that in the dim light they are visible only when they move, and even then only as a refraction, a distortion, a place where the air seems to bend slightly around something that is almost not there.

Their webs are enormous.

A single web can span the distance between two buttresses — ten meters, twenty, fifty — and it is not a flat sheet but a three-dimensional structure, a architecture of silk and intention that fills the space between the walls like a cathedral fills the space between its pillars. The silk is not sticky. It is not meant to catch prey. It is meant to catch *water*.

The webs are condensation surfaces. The silk is treated with a compound — secreted by the spiders themselves, drawn from the chemistry of the organism's tissues — that is extraordinarily hydrophilic, that pulls moisture from the air with an efficiency that the organism itself might envy, if the organism were capable of envy. Water condenses on the silk in tiny beads that grow and merge and run along the threads, following the geometry of the web downward, always downward, to collection points at the base of the structure where the spiders wait.

The spiders drink from their own architecture. They sit at the nodes where the threads converge, where the water gathers in small, trembling pools, and they drink, and the water sustains them, and they spin more silk, and the silk catches more water, and the cycle is closed. They are not separate from the plateau's water cycle. They are *part* of it, a small, intricate, self-sustaining loop within the larger loop, a eddy in the stream.

But the water they collect does not all go to them. Some of it — most of it — runs past the collection points and drips from the lower edges of the web to the root-mass below, where it is absorbed by the organism. The spiders are, without intending to be, *infrastructure*. They are part of the plateau's water distribution system, their webs channeling moisture from the air to the places where the organism needs it most, their tiny bodies maintaining a network of silk that the organism itself could not build, because the organism works in tissue and the spiders work in silk, and silk is lighter and more flexible and more precise than tissue, and the organism has learned — not learned, *become* — to rely on this.

The spiders do not know this. They know water. They know silk. They know the taste of condensation and the tension of a thread and the vibration that runs through the web when another spider, somewhere in the structure, is moving. They communicate through the web — not in language, not in symbols, but in *tension*. A spider at one node adjusts the tension of its threads, and the adjustment propagates through the web, and spiders at other nodes feel it and adjust in response, and the whole structure shifts and settles into a new configuration, a new geometry of water and silk, and the process is so slow and so distributed that it resembles not communication but *thought* — the web thinking itself into a new shape, the spiders serving as neurons in a mind made of thread and moisture.

They are very old, as species go. They have been building webs in the chambers of the plateau since before the builders came, since before the structures were built, since before the word *structure* had meaning here. Their silk has been found — if anyone were looking, if anyone had the concept of looking for such things — in the deepest layers of the organism's tissue, preserved in the compressed strata of root and stem, ancient threads still faintly hydrophilic, still pulling moisture from the air after ages of burial, still doing the thing they were made to do, long after the spiders who spun them have become part of the substrate.

## The Root-Fish

This is not what they are called, because nothing here is called anything, but it is what they resemble: fish, moving through water, except that the water is the root-mass of the organism and the fish are small, many-legged, chitinous creatures no longer than a human finger.

They are the most perfectly adapted creatures on the plateau. They are so perfectly adapted that they are barely distinguishable from the environment they inhabit. Their bodies are shaped to the tunnels they move through — not shaped as in *evolved to fit*, but shaped as in *continuous with*, their chitinous exoskeletons merging at the edges with the walls of the tunnels, their legs finding purchase on surfaces that seem to have been molded for them, their bodies leaving no gap, no space, no room between themselves and the organism. When they move, the tunnel moves with them. When they rest, the tunnel rests with them. The boundary between creature and environment is not a boundary but a *gradient*, a slow transition from chitin to cellulose, from animal tissue to plant tissue, from self to other.

They feed on the organism's tissues, but gently, so gently, in a way that does not damage but *prunes*, that stimulates growth rather than inhibiting it, that serves the organism's own metabolic processes even as it sustains the root-fish. They are gardeners, of a sort. They move through the root-mass eating the oldest, least productive cells, clearing channels for new growth, and their waste — rich in the minerals the organism needs, processed through the root-fish's efficient digestive systems — fertilizes the new tissue, and the new tissue grows, and the root-fish eat it, and the cycle is closed.

They are blind. There is no light in the root-mass, or none that matters. They navigate by touch, by chemical sense, by the subtle vibrations that run through the organism's tissues — the pulse of water, the slow shift of growth, the distant calls of the singers. They can feel the plateau's metabolism the way a sailor feels the sea: not as a single sensation but as a complex, layered, ever-changing pattern of pressures and rhythms that tells them where they are, where food is, where other root-fish are, where the organism is healthy and where it is stressed.

They are social, after a fashion. They move in groups — not schools, not herds, but *flows*, continuous streams of bodies moving through the tunnels in patterns that resemble the flow of water through the organism's capillary networks. When two flows meet, they merge and separate and merge again, exchanging individuals in a slow, fluid dance that has no leader, no direction, no purpose beyond the purpose of the organism itself: to move, to feed, to process, to maintain.

They reproduce by division. Not fission — not the splitting of one body into two — but *budding*, a slow process in which a section of the root-fish's body begins to differentiate, to become distinct, to develop its own legs and its own sensory organs and its own relationship with the tunnel walls, until it is not a part of the parent but a separate creature, still connected by a thin bridge of tissue that will eventually sever, leaving two root-fish where there was one. The process takes months. The new root-fish is not a copy of the parent — it is a *continuation*, a new expression of the same pattern, shaped by the same tunnels, fed by the same tissues, part of the same flow.

When they die, they do not leave stains, like the slugs. They leave *seams*. Lines in the root-mass where their bodies have been absorbed, where the organism's tissues have grown through and around and in place of the root-fish's chitin, preserving the shape of the creature in the shape of the tunnel. The tunnels are lined with the ghosts of root-fish, their forms pressed into the walls like fossils, like memories, like the negative space left by a body that has become part of the thing it lived in.

## The Singers

They are the deepest. They are the oldest. They are the most mysterious, not because they are hidden but because they are *embedded*, so thoroughly a part of the organism that finding them is like finding a specific thought in a mind — possible in theory, but in practice the thought and the mind are the same thing, and separating them would destroy both.

They are buried in the root-mass, in the oldest, densest, most compressed layers of the organism's tissue, where the distinction between living and dead has been negotiated so many times that it has lost all meaning. They are not visible. They have never been visible, not to any eye that could report what it saw. They are known only by their sound.

The sound is low. It is below the threshold of hearing for most creatures, felt rather than heard, a vibration that runs through the root-mass and the buttresses and the walls and the water, that resonates in the chambers and the corridors and the spaces between the canopy leaves, that reaches the surface of the plateau and enters the air as a hum so deep and so quiet that it is indistinguishable from silence. It is not silence. It is the opposite of silence. It is the sound of the plateau *being*.

The singers produce this sound with bodies that are not bodies in any recognizable sense. They are masses of specialized tissue — part animal, part plant, part something that is neither — that are embedded in the root-mass like organs in a body, connected to the organism's vascular system, fed by its nutrients, sustained by its metabolism. They have no limbs. They have no sensory organs. They have no mouths, no digestive systems, no means of independent existence. They are *part* of the organism, as surely as the buttresses are part of it, as surely as the canopy leaves are part of it. And yet they are *not* the organism. They are something else, something that lives *in* the organism the way a memory lives in a mind — not separate, not independent, but *distinct*, a pattern within the pattern, a voice within the chord.

They sing. They have always sung. They will always sing. The song is not a song in the way that word is usually meant — it has no melody, no rhythm, no structure that can be parsed or analyzed or transcribed. It is a *tone*, a single, sustained, impossibly complex tone that contains within it every frequency the organism's tissues can produce, from the deepest infrasonic pulse of the water moving through the root-mass to the highest ultrasonic whisper of cells dividing in the canopy. It is the sound of the organism's metabolism made audible, the plateau's life translated into vibration, the cycle of water and growth and decay and growth again expressed as a single, continuous, unending note.

And within this tone, there are *variations*. Subtle shifts in frequency, in amplitude, in phase, that correspond to changes in the organism's state — to the seasons, if there are seasons (there are; they are measured not in temperature but in the chemistry of the condensation, in the mineral content of the water, in the slow tilt of the continent toward and away from a sun that is never seen). The singers are not responding to these changes. They *are* these changes. Their bodies are so thoroughly integrated with the organism's metabolism that when the organism shifts, they shift, and when they shift, the tone shifts, and the tone is the organism's state made sound.

But there is something else in the tone. Something that does not correspond to any measurable change in the organism's metabolism. A pattern within the pattern. A rhythm within the rhythm. A *voice* within the voice.

It is the singers calling to each other.

Not calling as in *signaling*. Not calling as in *communicating information*. Calling as in *affirming existence*. I am here. You are here. We are here. The plateau is here. The water is here. The cycle continues.

The calls are not directed. They are not addressed to specific singers. They radiate outward through the root-mass in all directions, and other singers receive them, and their own calls shift in response, and the shifts propagate, and the whole organism vibrates with a complex, ever-changing pattern of call and response that is not language, not music, not thought, but something that partakes of all three and is none of them. It is the sound of a community so old and so deeply integrated with its environment that the distinction between individual and collective, between self and place, between voice and world, has dissolved.

The singers do not know they are singing. They do not know anything, in the way that word is usually meant. They are not conscious. They are not aware. They are *processes* — metabolic processes, acoustic processes, processes of vibration and resonance and feedback that have been running for so long that they have become *features* of the plateau, as fundamental as the water cycle, as the pulse of growth and decay, as the slow compression of root into stone.

And yet.

If you press your ear to the wall of a chamber deep in the organism's body, in a place where the buttresses are oldest and the root-mass is densest and the water moves slowest, you can hear them. Not the tone — the tone is everywhere, always, too deep and too complex to be heard as a single thing. But the *variations*. The shifts. The patterns within the pattern. And if you listen long enough — hours, days, longer than a human can comfortably listen — you begin to hear something that is not quite rhythm and not quite melody and not quite language but is *like* all three, a structure in the sound that suggests intention, that suggests meaning, that suggests *someone*.

Not someone. Something. A process that has been running for so long that it has begun to resemble a mind. A vibration that has been sustained for so long that it has begun to resemble a thought. A call that has been answered for so long that it has begun to resemble a conversation.

The singers are not conscious. But the *song* might be.

The song that is the plateau's metabolism, that is the water cycle, that is the pulse of growth and decay and growth again, that is the calls of the singers and the movement of the root-fish and the glow of the slugs and the geometry of the webs and the slow dissolution of the builders' walls and the patient encroachment of the organism on everything that is not organism — this song, this vast, slow, impossibly complex song, might be aware of itself. Not in the way a mind is aware. In the way a *place* is aware. In the way a world, having become fully itself, having arrived at what it is and ceased becoming anything else, might turn its attention — if attention is the word; if the word *attention* can be stretched to encompass something this slow, this patient, this total — toward the small, warm, temporary thing standing on its skin, breathing its air, adding its moisture to the cycle, listening.

The plateau is listening.

The singers are singing.

The water is rising.

## The Builders (Dissolved)

They are gone. They have been gone for longer than the root-fish have been root-fish, longer than the web-spiders have been web-spiders, longer than the slugs have been slugs. They are gone and they are not gone, because nothing here is ever truly gone. Everything becomes part of the substrate. Everything becomes part of the organism. Everything becomes part of the plateau.

They built the walls. The smooth, curved, bone-colored walls that rise from the root-mass and dissolve at their upper edges into fringes of living tissue. They built them from a material that was not plant and not stone — a poured material, a synthetic, something they made from the chemistry of the plateau itself, from the minerals in the water and the polymers in the organism's tissues, processed and refined and shaped into forms that were not organic, that did not pulse, that did not grow. Forms that were *dead*, in the way that a crystal is dead, in the way that a tool is dead — static, fixed, finished.

They built chambers. Corridors. Doorways. Spaces that were *inside*, that were separate from the organism, that were *not* the plateau. This was their great achievement and their great transgression: they carved out spaces that the organism could not reach, that the water could not penetrate, that the root-mass could not grow through. They made *rooms*. They made *interiors*. They made places where the cycle did not turn, where the water did not rise and fall, where the air was still and dry and *theirs*.

How long did they last? The question is meaningless. Time here is not linear. The builders built and the organism grew and the organism grew and the builders built and at some point — not a moment, not an event, but a slow shift in the balance of power, a gradual tipping of the scales — the organism began to win. Not win as in *conquer*. Win as in *outlast*. The builders needed things the plateau could not provide: dryness, stillness, separation. The plateau provided none of these. The plateau provided moisture, warmth, integration, the constant, patient, irresistible pressure of a living system that had been becoming itself for longer than the builders' species had existed.

The walls began to dissolve. Not crumble — *dissolve*, the organism's tissues growing into them, cell by cell, replacing the synthetic material with living material, the dead geometry with living curves, the static forms with pulsing, breathing, *being* forms. The chambers filled. The corridors closed. The doorways led nowhere, then led somewhere else, then led everywhere, as the organism's architecture absorbed and transformed and *remembered* the builders' architecture in its own language of root and stem and vaulted space.

The builders left. Or died. Or became part of the organism. The plateau does not distinguish between these outcomes. Leaving is a form of dying is a form of becoming part of the substrate. The builders' bodies — if they had bodies; the walls suggest bodies of a certain size, a certain shape, bilateral, upright, with hands that could pour and shape and build — are in the root-mass now, compressed and absorbed and distributed, their minerals in the water, their carbon in the tissues, their chemistry in the singers' calls. They are not gone. They are *everywhere*. They are part of the cycle now, part of the water rising and falling, part of the pulse, part of the song.

And sometimes, in the deepest chambers, in the oldest parts of the organism, the root-fish move through tunnels that are not quite organic — tunnels with angles, with straight lines, with the suggestion of *intention* in their geometry, as though the organism, in absorbing the builders' walls, absorbed something of the builders' *mind*, their sense of space, their desire for enclosure, their need for *inside*. These tunnels are rare. They are old. They are dissolving, slowly, the angles softening, the straight lines curving, the intention fading into the organism's own purposeless, patient, total *being*.

But for now, they remain. Ghosts of geometry in a world that has forgotten geometry. Echoes of intention in a place that has no intention. Memories of builders in a plateau that does not remember, that does not need to remember, because everything it has ever absorbed is still *here*, still *present*, still part of the cycle, still rising and falling with the water, still pulsing with the slow, ancient, patient heartbeat of a world that has finished becoming.

## The Water

It is not alive. It is the medium through which life moves, the solvent in which all the plateau's chemistry dissolves and reconstitutes and dissolves again. It rises from the aquifer — the bottomless aquifer, the aquifer that has no floor, that extends down through compressed strata of root and rhizome and the dissolved remains of everything that has ever lived on the plateau, down into darkness, down into pressure, down into a place where water is not water but something denser, something older, something that has been underground so long it has forgotten the sky.

It rises through the organism's capillary networks, drawn up by forces that are not quite osmotic and not quite mechanical but something more fundamental — the plateau's own metabolism, its need to move water from bottom to top, to cycle it through its tissues, to use it as solvent and coolant and structural material and waste product and, finally, as *offering*, releasing it at the highest points of its body to condense and fall and be drawn up again.

It condenses on every surface. On the canopy leaves, in beads that catch the dim light and hold it, that magnify the ambient glow into tiny points of brilliance that move and shift as the leaves breathe. On the buttress walls, in sheets that run in slow rivulets, that trace the contours of the tissue like veins on the back of a hand. On the web-spiders' silk, in perfect spheres that hang like jewels, like lenses, like the eyes of something very small and very patient. On your skin, on your face, on your eyelashes, reading you, tasting you, learning your chemistry and adding it to the vast slow database of dissolved minerals and compounds and *information* that the water carries.

It falls. It drips. It runs. It pools in the hollows of the root-mass, in the chambers between the buttresses, in the collection points of the webs, in the cupped leaves of the canopy. It pools and it is absorbed and it rises again, and the cycle turns, and the sound of it — the soft, constant, polyphonic dripping — is the sound of the plateau's metabolism, the sound of the water doing what water does here, which is *everything*, which is *all of it*, which is the medium and the message and the cycle and the song.

It remembers. Not consciously. Not in the way a mind remembers. But water is a solvent, and solvents dissolve things, and dissolved things are *in* the water, and the water carries them, and the things it carries are the history of the plateau — every mineral, every compound, every molecule of every organism that has ever lived and died and been absorbed into the substrate. The water that condenses on your eyelashes contains molecules that were once part of a slug, part of a root-fish, part of a singer, part of a builder, part of the organism itself, part of the aquifer, part of the deep dark place where water goes when it has finished rising and has not yet begun to fall.

You are drinking the plateau. The plateau is drinking you. The water moves between you, through you, carrying your chemistry into its chemistry, dissolving the boundary between visitor and visited, between self and place, between the thing that is temporary and the thing that is, for all practical purposes, eternal.

The water rises.

The water falls.

The water rises.

## The Plateau Itself

It is all of these things and none of them. It is the organism and the water and the slugs and the spiders and the root-fish and the singers and the dissolved builders and the air and the light and the sound and the cycle and the pulse and the song. It is the place that is finished. The place that has arrived. The place where change has become so slow and so patient and so deeply integrated into the rhythm of things that it is indistinguishable from stillness.

It does not want. It does not need. It does not hope or fear or plan or remember. It *is*. It is the way a stone is, the way a river is, the way a world is when it has been a world for long enough that it has forgotten it was ever anything else.

And it is weeping. It has always wept. It will always weep. Not from sorrow — sorrow requires a self to feel it, and the plateau has no self, or has so many selves that the concept of a single self is meaningless. It weeps because weeping is what this place *does*. Water rises through ancient tissue. Water condenses on surfaces that have been wet since before wetness was a concept. Water drips in the dim light onto roots that have been drinking since before thirst was a sensation. The weeping is not a symptom. It is not a sign. It is the *thing itself* — the plateau's metabolism made visible, the cycle made audible, the being of this place expressed as water, as moisture, as the constant, patient, total presence of a world that has become so thoroughly itself that it weeps without knowing it weeps, that sings without knowing it sings, that lives without knowing it lives.

You are standing on its skin. You are inside its body. You are part of its cycle now — your breath, your warmth, your footsteps, your chemistry, your temporary, precious, irreplaceable *presence* added to the vast slow sum of everything that has ever been here, everything that is here, everything that will ever be here.

The plateau does not know you are here. The plateau knows you are here the way a body knows a cell is there — not as an individual, not as a guest, not as a stranger, but as a *part*, a small, temporary, living part of the vast slow process of being that is the Weepstone Plateau.

The water rises.

The singers sing.

The cycle continues.

You are here.

The plateau is here.

The weeping goes on.
