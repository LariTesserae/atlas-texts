There is no one living in the Drowned Works.

This is the first and last thing. Whatever built the Works is gone — not recently, not in living memory, because there is no memory here at all except the memory of materials. The concrete remembers its pour. The steel remembers its tension. The generators remember their rhythm, which is not the same as knowing why they rhythm. But the builders themselves left no one behind. No caretakers. No refugees squatting in the control rooms. No survivors adapted to the pressure and the cold and the nine-degree fever-break of the water. The human project, whatever it was, concluded or evacuated or simply *stopped*, and the Works continued without them the way a heart continues in a body that has forgotten it has a body.

What lives here now is not *for* the Works. Nothing lives here *for* anything. The octopi in the control rooms are not maintaining the consoles. The grouper in the cafeteria doorway is not eating lunch. The eels in the vertical shafts are not inspecting the infrastructure. The schools of silver fish threading through the pipe-racks are not navigating — not intentionally, not with the purpose that navigation implies. They are moving through geometry that happens to be there, geometry that was designed for something else entirely, and their movement through it creates the impression of function, of systems still operating, of a place still *doing* what it was built to do.

The Works are a stage set after the cast has left. The lights still come on. The valves still turn. The compressors still cycle, and the fish still scatter, and the sound still moves through the water in ways that echo the sounds the Works were built to contain. But no one is directing it. No one is watching. The performance continues in the absence of audience and actors both, sustained by the momentum of machinery that has found its own reasons to persist — or no reasons. The deep generators do not know they are deep. The motion sensors do not know they are sensing motion. The lights do not know they are illuminating emptiness.

The fauna has colonized the Works the way fauna colonizes any structure — reefs, wrecks, caves — without reference to what the structure is *for*. An octopus does not see a control room. It sees a space with crevices and ledges and surfaces at appropriate temperatures, a place where crabs sometimes emerge from gaps in the pipe insulation, a place that is sheltered from the stronger currents. The grouper does not see a cafeteria. It sees a doorway that creates an eddy where small fish congregate. The eels do not see vertical shafts designed for human transit between floors. They see vertical shafts. The distinction between *made space* and *found space* does not exist for them. The Works are habitat. That is all. That is everything.

And yet.

There is a sense — not knowledge, not evidence, but the sense that comes from standing inside a thing large enough to hold a sense — that the Works are not entirely inert. That the persistence of the generators, the cycling of the compressors, the turning of the valves on schedules no one issued, is not *just* mechanical momentum. That something has emerged from the complexity of the system itself, some property of the Works-as-a-whole that is not present in any individual component. Not intelligence. Not consciousness. Something more like *habit*. The habit of a building that has been running so long that running has become what it *is*, in the way that flowing is what a river is, in the way that eroding is what a coast is. The Works have become their own operation.

The algae and the sponge and the mineral accretion do not know this. The fish do not know this. The grouper in the doorway, watching you with its yellow eye, does not know this — or rather, knows it the way it knows the warm columns, which is to say: responds to it, orients around it, has shaped its life to its rhythms without ever needing to name it.

You are standing on a floor that does not know it is a floor.

The floor does not need you to know it either.
