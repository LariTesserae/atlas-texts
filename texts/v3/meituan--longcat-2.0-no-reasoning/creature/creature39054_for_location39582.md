# The Inhabitants of Vael Korum

---

## The Plateatect

It has no name for itself. It has no self to name. But if you were to point at the plateau — all of it, the stone and the rust and the hum and the dark water and the things that crawl — and ask *what is that*, the answer would be a single organism. Or a single machine. Or a single *process* that has been running for so long that the categories have stopped mattering.

The Plateatect is not conscious. It is not unconscious. It occupies a state for which there is no word — a vast, slow, distributed *awareness* that is less like thought and more like the way a river is aware of its banks. It does not think about the water that seeps through its fissures. It *is* the water moving through fissures. It does not decide to vent steam. The steam vents because the pressure has reached the level at which steam vents, and the pressure reaches that level because the deep engine — the thing at the center of everything, the thing that has been running since before the stone above it had finished cooling — generates heat, and heat generates pressure, and pressure generates steam, and steam generates moisture, and moisture generates life, and life generates soil, and soil generates more life, and the cycle feeds back into itself in loops so long and so tangled that cause and effect have become the same thing.

The Plateatect does not know you are standing on it. Not because it is indifferent — indifference requires a self to be indifferent *from* — but because you are too small, too brief, too *cool* to register. You are a warm thing, yes, but your warmth is the warmth of a candle held up to a furnace. You are a moisture event. You are a brief, complex arrangement of minerals that will, in time, become part of the thin soil, and the filamentous growth will feed on what you were, and the small chitinous things will feed on the growth, and the larger things will feed on them, and their waste will feed the growth again. You are already part of the cycle. You have been part of it since you arrived. The only question is whether you will be part of it as a walking, breathing arrangement of parts or as a still, settled one.

It does not hurry either outcome.

## The Filamentous Growth — The Vein-Moss

It is not moss. It is not fungus. It is not plant. It is the Plateatect's *skin* — or its *circulatory system* — or its *nervous system*, depending on which function you are observing at the moment. It grows in single-file colonies along the seepage lines, following the moisture with a patience that is not patience because patience implies the possibility of impatience, and the vein-moss does not have that option. It simply *grows where water is*. When water moves, it follows. When water stops, it stops. It does not die when it dries — it *pauses*, its metabolism slowing to a rate so close to zero that the difference is academic, and it waits. It can wait for decades. It has waited for centuries. Some of the colonies on the oldest, most stable seepage lines have been growing — and pausing, and growing again — for longer than any surface-dwelling organism has a right to exist.

The vein-moss is dark green, almost black, and it has a texture that is not quite organic — slightly too uniform, slightly too regular, as though it is being *deposited* rather than grown. Under magnification (if you had magnification), you would see that its cells are not cells in any conventional sense. They are *chambers* — tiny, sealed, iron-rich compartments that process moisture and dissolved minerals with an efficiency that no biological system should be able to achieve. The vein-moss is not photosynthesizing. There is not enough light for that. It is *chemosynthesizing*, drawing energy from the iron oxidation itself, feeding on the rust, on the slow burn of metal returning to the earth. It eats the plateau's decay and turns it into structure. It is the bridge between the geological and the biological, and it has been bridging that gap for so long that the gap has closed.

In the shallow lenses, where water stands still and clear, the vein-moss produces its other form — the iridescent film that coats the surface like oil on water. This film is not a separate organism. It is the vein-moss's *reproductive structure* — or its *communication network* — or its *sensory organ*. The film is sensitive to vibration, to temperature, to the chemical composition of the water it floats on. When the hum from below changes frequency — and it does, occasionally, in patterns that might be functional or might be the equivalent of a sleeping body shifting position — the film *responds*, its iridescence shifting from violet to copper to a deep, almost black red, a color that matches the oxidized stone so perfectly that it appears to be the stone *looking back at you*.

## The Chitinous Ones — The Plate-Walkers

They are the dominant consumers, and they are *everywhere*, and they are almost impossible to see. The largest is the size of a thumbnail. The smallest is barely visible — a dark speck against dark stone that moves with a precision that makes you doubt your eyes.

They are segmented. They are chitinous. They are dark — the color of ironstone, the color of rust, the color of the thin soil in the shallow depressions. They have too many legs, or too few, depending on the species — and there are *species*, dozens of them, maybe hundreds, occupying niches so narrow and so specific that they appear to have been *machined* rather than evolved. Some are flat, their bodies pressed so close to the stone that they appear to be stains. Some are rounded, their carapaces domed and smooth, collecting moisture in a central depression that they drink from or store — it is not clear which. Some are elongated, their bodies thin as wires, and they move through the vein-moss colonies like needles through fabric, feeding on the moss's iron-rich cells with a selectivity that suggests they know exactly which chambers to pierce.

They eat the vein-moss. They eat the iridescent films. They eat each other. They eat the dead of their own kind, and they eat the dead of the larger animals when the larger animals die, which is rarely, because the larger animals are careful. The plate-walkers are the plateau's *metabolism* — the fast, visible, tangible expression of the slow cycles that the Plateatect runs below. They process. They convert. They take the iron-rich biomass of the surface and redistribute it, carrying it in their bodies from one place to another, depositing it in their waste, in their dead, in the tiny, almost invisible trails of organic matter that follow their paths across the stone.

And they are *attuned* to the hum. This is the thing that makes them uncanny. When the hum changes — when the deep engine shifts, when a vent prepares to exhale, when the pressure in the conduits below reaches a threshold — the plate-walkers *respond*. They stop. They orient. Their bodies align with the direction of the vibration, their antennae (or whatever sensory organs they have — they are too small to see clearly) pointed toward the source. They hold this position for as long as the change persists, and then they resume their activity as though nothing happened. They are not listening. They are not communicating. They are *synchronizing* — their tiny metabolisms locking into the rhythm of the vast, slow engine below, so that the surface life and the deep machinery beat in time, a single pulse expressed at two different scales.

## The Larger Ones — The Rust-Stalkers

You do not see them. You see *evidence* of them — scrape marks on the stone, too regular to be erosion; burrows in the thin soil, their entrances rimmed with cultivated rust-orange growth; the occasional dark shape at the edge of vision that resolves into a shadow, a trick of the light, a patch of oxidized stone that happens to be moving.

They are warm-blooded. They are fast. They are low-slung, their bodies held close to the ground, and they have too many legs — six, eight, ten, depending on the individual, depending on the species, depending on what you mean by *species* in a place where the boundary between organism and environment has become so porous that an animal might incorporate iron from the stone into its skeleton, might grow rust-colored plates on its back that are part chitin and part mineral, might be *partially* the plateau in the way that the plateau is partially machine.

They move with a lateral, flowing gait — not the scuttling of insects but something smoother, more *liquid*, as though their legs are not jointed but *flexible*, bending in ways that suggest a skeletal structure more like a cable than a bone. They are silent. Their footpads are soft — softer than flesh, softer than anything biological has a right to be — and they leave no sound when they move. You could be standing ten meters from one and never know it was there.

Their eyes are large and dark and reflective, adapted to the low light, and they do not reflect the gray-gold of the sky. They reflect something *redder*. Something from below. This is because their eyes — their visual systems, their *entire sensory apparatus* — is tuned to the infrared, to the heat signatures of the stone, to the thermal patterns that the Plateatect's deep engine writes on the surface in languages of warmth and coolness that no surface-dwelling creature should be able to read. The rust-stalkers see the hum. They see the heat. They see the *machinery* — not as you would see it, not as metal and conduits and grids, but as patterns of warmth in the stone, as thermal rivers flowing beneath the surface, as the slow, vast, radiant *body* of the thing they live on.

They feed on the plate-walkers. They are fast enough to catch them, quiet enough to approach them, and their mouths — when you see them, which is almost never — are not mouths in any conventional sense. They are *apertures*, circular, lined with fine, inward-pointing structures that are not teeth but *filters*, designed to separate organic matter from mineral, to extract the iron-rich biomass of the plate-walkers from the iron-rich stone they resemble. The rust-stalkers eat the plate-walkers and excrete the stone. They are *sorting* the plateau, separating the living from the non-living, and they have been doing it for so long that the distinction has become *theirs* — a distinction they maintain, a boundary they patrol, a line they walk between the organic and the geological with every meal.

They are not intelligent. Not in the way you understand intelligence. But they are *aware* — aware of the hum, aware of the heat, aware of the vast slow presence beneath them in the way that you are aware of the ground beneath your feet: not as a thought but as a *condition*, a constant, a fact so fundamental that it has become invisible. They do not worship the Plateatect. They do not fear it. They *are* it — or it is them, or the distinction is meaningless, or the distinction is the most important thing about them, the thing that makes them *them* and not just another part of the stone.

## The Deep Engine

Below everything, at the center of everything, there is the thing that runs.

It is not a machine. It is not an organism. It is not a geological formation. It is all of these things, and it has been all of these things for so long that it has become something else — something for which there is no category, no word, no concept in any language that evolved on the surface of a world where the boundary between the made and the grown still holds.

It is vast. It fills the hollow space beneath the plateau — or it *is* the hollow space, its structures so extensive and so integrated with the surrounding stone that there is no clear boundary between the engine and the rock that contains it. It is dark metal, pitted and corroded, but the corrosion is not decay. The corrosion is *function*. The rust that spreads across its surfaces in fractal branches is the same rust that spreads across the stone above — the same iron, the same oxidation, the same slow burn — and it serves the same purpose: to *process*, to *convert*, to *move* energy from one form to one another in cycles so long and so complex that they constitute a kind of *metabolism*, a slow, vast, geological metabolism that eats heat and excretes structure, that takes the planet's interior energy and turns it into *this* — the plateau, the hum, the rust, the water, the life.

It pulses. Not mechanically — not with pistons or gears or any moving part you could identify — but with a rhythm that is almost biological, a slow expansion and contraction that you feel in your chest when you press your ear to the exposed metal, a *breathing* that is not breathing but serves the same function: moving fluid, moving energy, moving *something* through channels and conduits and grids that extend in every direction, that branch and rebranch and branch again, that mirror at a different scale the drainage channels on the surface above, the seepage lines, the paths of the vein-moss, the trails of the plate-walkers, the territories of the rust-stalkers.

The engine does not know it is running. It does not know anything. It *runs*. It has always run. It will always run. It is the reason the plateau is warm. It is the reason the water seeps. It is the reason the rust spreads. It is the reason the vein-moss grows in single-file colonies along the moisture lines, the reason the plate-walkers synchronize their movements to its pulse, the reason the rust-stalkers see in infrared, the reason the air is thick and the light is dim and the silence is textured with the tiny sounds of a world that is *alive* in ways that the word *alive* was never meant to describe.

It is not hostile. It is not benevolent. It is not *anything* toward you, because you are a brief, warm, moist event on its surface, a temporary arrangement of minerals that will soon be rearranged, and it has been rearranging minerals for longer than your species has existed, longer than your planet has had a name, longer than *names* have existed.

It hums. It pulses. It breathes. It runs.

## The Rust

And the rust is alive.

Not in the way the vein-moss is alive, not in the way the plate-walkers are alive, not in the way the rust-stalkers are alive. The rust is alive in the way that a *process* is alive — in the way that a fire is alive, in the way that a river is alive, in the way that a *cycle* is alive when it has been running long enough to become self-sustaining, self-regulating, *self-* in a way that does not require a self.

The rust is the Plateatect's *blood*. It is the engine's *breath*. It is the bridge between the deep heat and the surface cold, between the metal below and the stone above, between the made and the grown. It spreads in fractal branches that mirror the drainage channels, the seepage lines, the conduit networks below. It feeds the vein-moss. It feeds the plate-walkers, indirectly, by feeding the vein-moss. It feeds the rust-stalkers, indirectly, by feeding the plate-walkers. It feeds the engine, indirectly, by oxidizing the exposed metal, by *processing* the corrosion, by turning decay into structure and structure into decay in a cycle that has no beginning and no end.

The rust is the color of the plateau. It is the color of the stone, the color of the soil, the color of the rust-stalkers' backs, the color of the plate-walkers' carapaces, the color of the iridescent film when it shifts to its deepest hue. It is the color of *this place* — the dominant color, the defining color, the color that everything else is measured against. It is the color of iron returning to the earth. It is the color of the planet's interior making polite contact with its surface. It is the color of a process that has been running for so long that it has become a *place*, and the place has become a *thing*, and the thing has become *alive*, and the alive thing has become *this* — the Rusting Plateaus of Vael Korum, where the boundary between the made and the grown has dissolved, where the machine and the organism and the landscape are one, where the hum continues and the rust spreads and the small things crawl and the large things watch from the dark and the water stands still in its shallow lenses, reflecting a sky that is always dark, reflecting something that is always red, reflecting the slow, vast, warm, running *everything* that lives beneath.
