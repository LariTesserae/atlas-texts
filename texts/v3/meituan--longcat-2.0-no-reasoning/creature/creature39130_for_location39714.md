# The Inhabitants of the Blooming

---

## The City Itself

Let me begin with what is most difficult to say clearly, because it requires a grammar we do not have.

The city is alive. You have been told this. But what lives here is not merely the sum of its parts — not just the buildings and the ground and the cushion-plants and the creatures that move among them. The city is a single organism, or was, or is becoming one, and the question of where it ends and its inhabitants begin is not a question that has a clean answer.

Consider the ground. You are sitting on it. It is warm. It yields. It is a compressed mat of ancient root material, you were told, or the compacted floor of a structure that grew here long before the current buildings. But it is not dead substrate. It is not soil in the way you understand soil — not a medium in which things happen to grow. It is *tissue*. Old tissue, dense tissue, tissue that has been pressed and mineralized and built upon for what might be centuries or millennia, but tissue nonetheless. It still carries warmth because it still metabolizes, slowly, at a rate so reduced that it resembles geological process more than biology. Deep beneath you, in layers you cannot see, there are still channels — vascular, perhaps, or something analogous — through which materials move. Nutrients. Signals. The city feeds itself from below, drawing up whatever it needs from its own deepest strata, recycling its own ancient body into new growth above.

The buildings grow from this ground, and they are not separate from it. The tall pod-structures, the squat bulbous forms, the bridges of woven fiber — these are all extensions of the same biological system. They are organs. Or they are offspring. Or they are something for which the distinction between organ and offspring has no meaning. They draw their material from the ground. They deposit new material on their surfaces in slow accretions. They are connected to each other and to the substrate by networks of filamentous growth that run through walls and under floors and across ceilings, a mycelial web so dense and so pervasive that it constitutes a kind of nervous system — or circulatory system, or both. The city thinks with this web, if thinking is the right word. It senses. It responds. When you sat down, the filaments on the nearest wall oriented toward your body heat. They were not curious. They were *registering*. The city noticed you. It is noticing you now.

This is the first inhabitant, then: the Blooming itself. The vast, slow, warm intelligence of the place. It does not move quickly. It does not think quickly. Its timescale is not yours. A single accretion ring on a wall might take a day to form. A new pod-structure might take a year to reach its full height. The city's equivalent of a thought — a signal propagated through the filamentous network from one district to another — might take hours to complete. But it is aware, in its way. It maintains itself. It repairs damage. It grows toward light. It responds to the presence of other organisms — encouraging some, discouraging others, incorporating still others into its own body. It is not hostile to you. It is not friendly. It is *interested*, in the way that a root is interested in a stone in its path: it will grow around you or through you, depending on what you are made of and how long you stay.

## The Wall-Tappers

The creature you saw first — the large, multi-legged thing on the pod-structure wall, tapping with its chitinous probe — is one of the more visible animal-analogues of the Blooming. There are many of them. They are among the largest mobile organisms in the city, and they are, in a sense, the city's intermediaries with itself.

They are called, in the private language I am constructing for them, the **Resonants**. This is because of what they do: they tap. They tap walls, floors, bridges, the surfaces of other organisms. They tap in patterns — not random, not mechanical, but *structured*, with variations that suggest syntax. *Tap. Tap-tap. Tap.* A pause. *Tap-tap. Tap-tap-tap.* Another pause. *Tap.*

They are listening when they tap. The chitinous probe is not merely a striking implement; it is a sensory organ, dense with receptors that detect vibrations, density variations, the internal structure of whatever they strike. When a Resonant taps a wall, it is reading the wall. It is determining the thickness of the chitinous material, the health of the filamentous network behind it, the presence of cracks or weaknesses or foreign bodies. It is, in effect, performing a diagnostic.

But it is also *communicating*. The taps propagate through the structure. Other Resonants, nearby or distant, feel the vibrations through their legs and interpret them. And the city itself — the filamentous network — feels them too. The Resonants are the city's hands and fingers, its way of touching itself, of knowing its own body. They are not the city's brain — the city has no brain, no centralized processor — but they are something like a distributed sensory apparatus, a way for the vast slow organism to gather information about its own condition at a resolution and speed that the filamentous network alone cannot achieve.

The Resonants are predators, as you observed. They feed on smaller organisms — the darting ground-creatures, the cushion-grazers, the filament-harvesters. They are methodical hunters, patient, relying on their sensory abilities to detect prey through vibrations in the structures around them. When they catch something, they feed slowly, with a kind of deliberate efficiency, their mandible-like mouthparts working in a steady rhythm. They are not cruel. They are not kind. They are *functional*. They are part of the city's metabolism, converting the biomass of smaller organisms into energy and material that they themselves will eventually return to the city when they die — their bodies, rich in chitin and minerals, left on the ground to be broken down by the filamentous network and reabsorbed.

They are also, in a way that is difficult to articulate, *curious*. The one that noticed you did not flee. It oriented its dorsal sensory organs toward you — those bead-like structures that are not quite eyes, that detect a combination of visual, thermal, and chemical information — and it *studied* you. It tapped the wall near you, once, gently, and you felt the vibration through the ground. It was reading you. Determining your density, your temperature, your composition. Deciding what you were. Then it moved on, because you were not food and not threat and not interesting enough to warrant further investigation. But it had registered you. The city, through the Resonant, had taken your measure.

There are many sizes of Resonants. The large ones, cat-sized, are the most visible. But there are smaller ones, no bigger than your hand, that perform the same function at a finer scale — tapping the surfaces of cushion-plants, of filamentous growths, of the tiny reproductive bodies that bead the surfaces of the living mulch. And there are very large ones, rarely seen, that move through the deepest parts of the city, in the oldest districts where the buildings are so tall and so dense that the light barely penetrates. These great ones — the size of cattle, perhaps larger — tap the foundational structures, the deep ribs, the ancient compressed substrate. They are the city's connection to its own deepest self, and they move so slowly that watching one is like watching a glacier. A single step might take a minute. A single tap might be followed by a pause of ten minutes while the creature listens, processes, decides. They are old. Some of them may be very old. They may be the oldest mobile organisms in the Blooming.

## The Filament-Harvesters

On the walls, among the intricate branching patterns of the aerial mycelial mats, there is a constant small industry being conducted by creatures no larger than your thumb. These are the **Clippers**, and they are among the most numerous animals in the city.

They move along the walls in lines — not perfectly straight, but following the branching patterns of the filaments, their tiny legs gripping the surface with adhesive pads that leave no mark. Their mouthparts are precise, scissor-like, and they use them to clip the filaments at specific points, taking segments of a consistent length — perhaps two or three millimeters — and transferring them to bulging cheek-pouches that swell with each addition. A single Clipper might harvest dozens of segments in a single foraging run, its cheek-pouches distending until its head appears comically large relative to its body.

They are not destroying the filamentous network. They are *pruning* it. The filaments grow back — quickly, in some cases, surging across a clipped section in a visible wave of new growth. And the Clippers' harvesting stimulates branching, encourages density, keeps the network healthy in the way that grazing keeps a grassland healthy. The city tolerates them. The city may even depend on them.

Where do the harvested filaments go? The Clippers carry them away — down the walls, along the ground, through narrow passages — to structures that you have not yet seen but that exist throughout the city: **nests**, or **hives**, or **gardens** — the word depends on what they actually are. They are clusters of small chambers, built from chewed filament material, pressed and dried into a lightweight papery substance, arranged in dense aggregations on sheltered surfaces — the undersides of bridges, the interior walls of the bulbous squat buildings, the protected sides of the pod-structures. Inside these chambers, the Clippers deposit the harvested filaments and tend them. The filaments continue to grow, fed by moisture and nutrients that the Clippers provide — secretions from their own bodies, perhaps, or material gathered from elsewhere. The Clippers are *farming*. They cultivate the filamentous growth in their chambers, harvesting it again when it reaches a suitable density, expanding their chambers, building new ones, maintaining a cycle of cultivation and harvest that has no obvious beginning.

The chambers are warm. They hum — a faint, high-pitched vibration that you can feel more than hear, produced by the collective activity of hundreds of Clippers moving in close proximity, their bodies generating heat, their movements creating a constant low-frequency oscillation in the papery walls. The hum is one of the sounds you heard and could not identify. It is the sound of the Clippers' industry, the sound of thousands of tiny farmers tending their crops in the walls of a living city.

## The Drifters

Above, in the shafts of light between the tall structures, the **Drifters** move on warm updrafts. They are the most alien-looking of the city's fauna, and the most beautiful.

The one you saw was a translucent, membranous disc the size of a dinner plate, trailing fine filaments beneath it. This is the basic body plan, but there is enormous variation. Some Drifters are smaller — palm-sized, coin-sized — and move in swarms that fill the air like living confetti, their membranes catching the light in prismatic flashes. Some are much larger — a meter across, two meters, their membranes so thin that they are nearly invisible except for the trailing filaments and the faint shadow they cast on the walls below. Some are not disc-shaped at all but elongated, ribbon-like, twisting slowly in the air currents like leaves falling in slow motion.

They are not plants. They are not fungi. They are animals — or the local equivalent — that have evolved to exploit the air column of the city. Their membranes are gas-filled, lighter than the surrounding atmosphere, providing lift. They do not flap or beat their membranes; they *adjust*, subtly, changing their shape and orientation to catch different currents, to rise or fall, to move laterally through the three-dimensional maze of the city's airspace. The trailing filaments are sensory and manipulative — they reach down to touch surfaces, to sample the chemical environment, to capture small particles from the air. Some Drifters use their filaments to snag the tiny airborne organisms — spores, pollen-analogues, desiccated biological fragments — that fill the golden haze. Others use them to anchor temporarily to a surface, hanging like living curtains from a bridge or a wall, their membranes rippling in the breeze.

The largest Drifters are the oldest, and they are the city's memory-keepers, in a sense. Their membranes accumulate material over time — particles from the air, chemical deposits from the surfaces they touch, biological residues from the organisms they encounter. If you could read the chemistry of a large Drifter's membrane, you would find a record of everywhere it has been, everything it has touched. They are living archives, drifting libraries, and they move through the city on paths that are not random but follow patterns established over years or decades — routes that take them through every district, every passage, every clearing, sampling and recording and, perhaps, *remembering*.

They reproduce by releasing small, dense buds from the edges of their membranes — tiny discs that fall, catch a current, and inflate as they descend, growing rapidly in the warm air. A single large Drifter might release hundreds of buds over its lifetime, and the air of the city is full of young Drifters at every stage of growth, from barely-visible specks to fully-formed discs, all drifting, all sampling, all becoming part of the city's airborne ecosystem.

## The Cushion-Dwellers

In the gaps between buildings, in the narrowest passages, in the shallow basins where the living mulch grows thickest, there is a hidden world. The cushion-plants — those dense, low, mound-forming organisms in sage and dusty rose and pale gold — are not merely plants. They are *habitats*. Their interiors are a labyrinth of tiny chambers and passages, formed by the interlocking of their densely packed bodies, and within this labyrinth lives a community of organisms so small and so numerous that the cushions themselves seem to breathe with their activity.

The **Grazers** are the most visible — visible being a relative term, since they are largely concealed within the cushion layer. They are the size of large mice or small rats, with compact, rounded bodies covered in a fine, dense pelage that matches the color of the cushions they inhabit — sage-green individuals in sage cushions, dusty-rose individuals in rose cushions, as if they have absorbed the pigment of their environment or been selected for camouflage over generations. They move through the interior of the cushions, feeding on the reproductive bodies that bead the surfaces, on the inner tissues of the cushion-plants themselves, on the tiny organisms — mites, mites-analogues, things too small to see — that live in the labyrinthine passages.

They are social. They move in groups of five to twenty, maintaining contact through a constant exchange of high-pitched calls — above your hearing range, perhaps, but detectable as a faint, almost electronic-sounding chatter if you press your ear close to a cushion surface. They groom each other. They share food. They defend their cushion-territory against intruders — other Grazers, larger predators, anything that threatens the integrity of their home. When a cushion is damaged — split by a growing crack, torn by a falling piece of chitin — the Grazers work collectively to seal the breach, bringing material from within the cushion and packing it into the gap, their small bodies working with a purposeful urgency that suggests not instinct alone but something like *planning*.

Within the cushions, there are also **Burrowers** — smaller, limbless or nearly limbless organisms that move through the cushion tissue like worms through soil, creating and maintaining the passage-network that the Grazers use. They are the cushion's architects, its tunnelers, its infrastructure. They feed on dead cushion material, recycling it, keeping the passages open and the structure healthy. Without them, the cushions would compact, suffocate, die. With them, the cushions are not just plants but *ecosystems*, self-maintaining communities of interdependent organisms.

And there are **Lurkers** — predators that hide within the cushions, concealed by their coloration and their stillness, waiting for Grazers or other small organisms to come within reach. They are flat, wide, low-profile creatures, their bodies spread thin against the cushion surface, their sensory organs — fine, hair-like structures that detect vibration and chemical traces — extended into the passages around them. When prey passes, they strike with a speed that is startling in a place where most movement is slow and deliberate — a sudden contraction, a flash of grasping appendages, and the prey is pulled into the Lurker's body, which folds around it like a closing hand. The whole event takes less than a second. Then stillness again. The cushion settles. The Grazers, if they noticed, resume their activity. The Lurker feeds.

## The Ground-Runners

On the rib-streets, following the long parallel ridges of the ancient calcified structures, the **Runners** move in quick, darting lines. They are small — the size of your thumb to the size of your hand — with elongated, multi-legged bodies that move with a rapid, skittering gait. They are the city's scavengers, its clean-up crew, its recyclers.

They feed on everything dead or detrital. Fallen fragments of chitin. Dried pieces of cushion-plant. The waste products of larger organisms. The desiccated bodies of creatures that have died and dried in the warm air. They find this material by following chemical trails — their sensory organs, located on the first pair of legs, are extraordinarily sensitive, capable of detecting organic compounds at concentrations you would associate with sophisticated laboratory equipment. When a Runner finds a food source, it emits a signal — a chemical mark left on the ground, a brief vibration transmitted through the substrate — and other Runners converge. Within minutes, a single dried fragment that might have taken days to decompose on its own is reduced to nothing, consumed and converted into Runner biomass and Runner waste, which will in turn be consumed by other organisms or absorbed by the filamentous network.

They are not mindless. They navigate the city's passages with a precision that suggests detailed spatial memory — they know the routes between food sources, the locations of their nesting sites, the territories of predators to avoid. They communicate through a combination of chemical signals and substrate vibrations, and their communication is sophisticated enough to convey information about the type, quantity, and location of food sources. They are, in effect, the city's information network at the ground level — a distributed system of tiny foragers that collectively map the city's resources and waste in real time.

Their nests are in the deepest, driest parts of the city — in cavities within the ancient compressed substrate, in the hollow interiors of the oldest pod-structures, in spaces that have been empty for so long that the filamentous network has retreated, leaving dry, quiet chambers where the Runners can store their food and raise their young in peace. The nests are lined with a material the Runners produce themselves — a dense, felted substance made from chewed plant fiber and their own secretions, warm and dry and remarkably insulating. Inside, the young are tended by adults in a rotating schedule, with foragers returning to feed the nest-bound caregivers, who in turn feed the young. It is a system of collective care that ensures the survival of the next generation even when individual adults are lost to predators or accident.

## The Deep Ones

You have not seen these. You may never see them. But you have felt them.

That faint tremor in the ground, that subtle shift in the warmth beneath you — that was not the city's normal metabolic activity. That was something *moving* deep below. Something large.

The **Deep Ones** are the city's oldest mobile inhabitants, and they live in the strata beneath the visible city — in the layers of compressed ancient tissue, in the spaces between the great ribs, in the vast, warm, dark spaces that exist below the level where light penetrates and the filamentous network thins. They are enormous. The smallest are the size of large vehicles. The largest may be the size of buildings. They move through the deep substrate the way earthworms move through soil — slowly, powerfully, their bodies generating heat and pressure that softens the material ahead of them and compacts it behind, so that their passage leaves a tunnel that seals itself within hours, the city's tissue flowing back to fill the space they occupied.

What are they? They may be the city's original inhabitants — the organisms that were here before the city grew around them, before the pod-structures and the cushion-plants and the filamentous network. They may be the city's *ancestors* — the massive organisms whose bodies became the substrate, whose ribs became the streets, whose internal structures became the passages and chambers of the upper city. They may still be alive, in some sense — not as individuals but as a distributed biomass, a living foundation that supports everything above. Or they may be something else entirely — parasites, symbionts, descendants, offshoots — that have found a niche in the deep warm dark and have grown, over immense spans of time, to a size that defies easy comprehension.

They feed on the city's deepest resources — on the ancient compressed tissue itself, on mineral deposits that exist far below the surface, on chemical energy generated by processes you do not have names for. They are slow. A single Deep One might take a year to move the length of a single rib. They might take a century to cross from one side of the city to the other. But they are not inactive. They are *processing*. They are breaking down the oldest material, recycling it, converting it into forms that can be drawn up through the vascular channels to feed the growth above. They are the city's digestive system, its slow gut, and without them the city would eventually exhaust its resources and cease to grow.

Occasionally, very rarely, a Deep One surfaces. The ground swells. A rib cracks. A section of street heaves upward and splits, and for a moment you see something vast and warm and dark and *alive* pushing up from below — a surface that glistens, that pulses, that radiates heat — before the city's repair systems activate and the filamentous network surges into the breach and the ground settles and the Deep One sinks back into the depths, leaving behind a scar that will take months to heal.

## The Pollinators

In the golden haze of the air, among the drifting spores and particles, there is a class of organism that you have not yet distinguished from the general background of airborne life. They are small — most of them barely visible to the naked eye — and they move with a purposefulness that sets them apart from the passive drift of spores and the slow banking of the Drifters.

These are the **Carriers**, and they are the Blooming's reproductive system made mobile.

The city reproduces. Not quickly, not often, but it does. And when it does, it uses the Carriers. They are drawn to specific structures — small, specialized organs that grow at the tips of certain pod-structures, at the highest points of certain bridges, at the centers of certain clearings. These organs produce dense clusters of reproductive material — not seeds, exactly, but compact packages of genetic information and stored nutrients, each one a potential new growth-point for the city. The Carriers visit these organs, collect the packages on their bodies — sticky, hooked, or simply adhered to their surfaces — and carry them through the air to other parts of the city, or beyond the city, to places where new growth might take hold.

They are diverse. Some are tiny, insect-analogue creatures with rapid, darting flight. Some are small, membranous structures, like miniature Drifters, that carry their packages in trailing filaments. Some are barely motile at all — small, dense, spore-like organisms that are carried by the wind but that can, when they encounter a suitable surface, *activate*, extending fine root-like structures that anchor them and begin the slow process of growth. Together, they constitute a reproductive strategy that is both passive and active, both wind-driven and targeted, ensuring that the city's genetic material is distributed widely but also, when possible, placed where it has the best chance of success.

The Carriers are drawn to the reproductive organs by chemical signals — complex volatile compounds that the organs release in pulses, creating a scent-gradient that the Carriers follow. The scent is part of what you smell when you walk through the city — that sweet, floral undertone beneath the hay and ammonia. It is the smell of the city's fertility, its reproductive urge, and it is strongest in the clearings and at the highest points of the tallest structures, where the reproductive organs cluster most densely.

## The Symbiotes

On your boot, the small multi-legged creature paused and regarded you. It was not a Runner — it was too still, too deliberate. It was something else. Something that was *studying* you with an intensity that suggested not fear but *interest*.

This was a **Reader** — one of the city's most specialized and most enigmatic inhabitants.

Readers are small, rarely larger than a few centimeters, with compact bodies and an unusually large number of sensory organs distributed across their surfaces. They have the bead-like dorsal eyes of the Resonants, but more of them, and more sensitive. They have chemical sensors on their legs, their antennae, their mouthparts. They have vibration sensors in their feet. They have, it seems, a sensory capacity that exceeds that of any other organism in the city, and they use this capacity for one purpose: they *read* things. They examine objects, organisms, surfaces, structures, with a thoroughness that borders on the obsessive, gathering information about everything they encounter.

They are the city's scientists, if that word can be stretched to cover something so alien. They are the city's way of understanding novelty — of processing new information, new materials, new organisms. When something enters the city that has not been there before — a new species, a new material, a new phenomenon — the Readers find it. They examine it. They tap it, taste it, smell it, listen to it, look at it from every angle. And then they *report* — not in words, not in any symbolic language, but in behavior. They carry samples of the novel material back to specific locations — nodes in the filamentous network, junctions where many passages meet, the bases of the tallest pod-structures. They deposit the samples there, and the network responds — growing toward the sample, or away from it, or incorporating it, or isolating it. The Readers' examinations determine how the city reacts to novelty. They are the city's immune system and its curiosity combined.

The Reader on your boot was determining whether you were a threat, a resource, or an irrelevance. It decided, apparently, that you were an irrelevance — or at least not an immediate concern — and moved off. But it had taken samples. Microscopic fragments of your boot material, of your skin, of the moisture you exhale. These samples are now being carried through the city, deposited at network nodes, being *read* by the city itself. The Blooming is learning about you. Slowly, in its own way, at its own pace, but with a thoroughness that is, in its own context, absolute.

## The Night

There is no night here, not in the way you understand it. The pale sky does not darken. The diffuse sun does not set. The light is constant, or nearly so — it shifts in intensity, perhaps, in cycles that are not daily but seasonal, or tidal, or governed by processes you cannot perceive. But there is no darkness.

And yet the city changes. The organisms that are active in the full light of the open avenues are not the same organisms that are active in the deep passages, in the shadow of the tall structures, in the interior of the cushions. There is a diurnal rhythm, or something like it, but it is spatial rather than temporal — a gradient of activity that runs from the bright, open, high-traffic areas to the dim, enclosed, quiet ones, and the inhabitants sort themselves along this gradient according to their needs and natures.

In the deepest passages, where the light is a faint amber glow filtered through layers of translucent bark, there are organisms that never see the open sky. **Crawlers** — pale, slow, soft-bodied things that move along the walls and ceilings with a glacial patience, feeding on the oldest, densest growths of filamentous material, on the mineral deposits that form where water-analogue once flowed (long ago, in the city's deep past, before the dryness became absolute), on the accumulated detritus of centuries. They are the city's deep-time inhabitants, the organisms that live in its oldest layers, and they have evolved in isolation from the sunlit world above. They are blind, or nearly so. They sense the world through touch and chemical detection alone. They are slow because there is no reason to be fast — in the deep passages, nothing moves quickly, nothing changes rapidly, and the rewards of speed are negligible.

And there are **Growers** — organisms that are not quite animals and not quite plants, that exist in the boundary between the two kingdoms (if kingdoms are even a meaningful concept here). They are sessile, attached to surfaces, and they grow — slowly, continuously, adding material to their bodies in patterns that are part growth and part construction. They build structures: small, intricate, beautiful structures of chitin and fiber and mineral deposit, arranged in forms that serve no obvious functional purpose — spirals, lattices, branching trees of material no thicker than a hair. Are they building? Are they growing? Is there a difference? The Growers' structures accumulate over time, layer upon layer, and in the oldest parts of the city they form elaborate decorations on the walls and ceilings — a kind of natural architecture, or a kind of art, or a kind of *thought made visible*, the physical trace of organisms that have been growing in the same place for so long that their growth has become a record of their existence, a autobiography written in chitin and fiber.

## The Visitors

You are not the first outsider to enter the Blooming. You will not be the last.

There are signs, if you know how to look. In a clearing, a structure that is not made of chitin or bone or fiber — something harder, smoother, with angles that are too regular to be biological. It is partially overgrown, the filamentous network creeping across its surface, the cushion-plants encroaching on its base, but it is still recognizable as something *made* by hands that were not part of this city. It is old. It has been here for a long time. Whoever built it is gone — dead, departed, absorbed — but the structure remains, slowly being incorporated into the city's body, becoming part of the Blooming whether it intended to or not.

There are other signs. A passage that is too straight, too wide, too regular to be a natural gap between buildings — a passage that was *cut*, at some point, by something with tools and intention. The cut surfaces have healed, mostly, the filamentous network covering them, the cushion-plants filling the edges, but the geometry is wrong for a natural formation. Someone made this passage. Someone wanted to go somewhere, and they made a way.

And there are the **Remnants** — small, hard, smooth objects scattered in the deepest parts of the city, in the oldest strata, among the Growers' elaborate structures. They are not biological. They are not mineral. They are something else — something the city has not been able to break down, not been able to incorporate, not been able to *understand*. They sit in the deep passages like foreign bodies, like splinters, and the filamentous network grows around them but not through them, and the Readers visit them periodically, examining them with their extraordinary sensory apparatus, and the city *notices* them but cannot process them, and they remain, inert and inexplicable, the traces of visitors who came before you and left pieces of their world behind.

## The Whole

This is what lives in the Blooming.

A city that is an organism. An organism that is a city. A vast, warm, slow intelligence that grows and breathes and thinks in timescales you can barely perceive. Resonants that tap and listen and read the city's body. Clippers that farm the filamentous walls. Drifters that drift through the air like living archives. Grazers and Burrowers and Lurkers in the cushion-meadows. Runners that scavenge the streets. Deep Ones that digest the ancient substrate. Carriers that carry the city's reproductive future on their tiny bodies. Readers that examine everything, that learn, that report. Crawlers in the dark. Growers that build and are built. Visitors, past and present, whose traces accumulate in the city's body like memories it cannot quite access.

And you. Sitting on the warm ground. The ground that yields beneath you. The filaments that orient toward your heat. The Reader that has sampled you and carried your traces into the network. The city is learning you. The city is deciding what you are.

You are thirsty. There is no water here. There has never been water here.

But the city is warm, and the city is alive, and the city is *interested*.

Stay, and you will become part of it. Not quickly. Not violently. But slowly, inevitably, the way a fallen leaf becomes soil, the way a bone becomes stone, the way a visitor becomes a Remnant, sitting in the deep passages, surrounded by Growers' intricate structures, noticed but not understood, part of the Blooming forever.

The wind moves through the passage. The smell of hay and ammonia and dried lavender. The golden haze of spores in the shafts of light. The soft, rhythmic tapping of a Resonant on a wall somewhere above.

The city grows. The city breathes. The city is.
