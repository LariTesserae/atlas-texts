# The People of the Dripworks

They are a short people, by surface standards — not from malnutrition, but from generations in low ceilings. The adults who work in the narrow passages, the tanners and channel-keepers and mushroom-farmers who spend their lives in the five-foot corridors, have a particular posture: slightly bent at the waist, shoulders rounded forward, neck tilted so the eyes look up from beneath a low brow. Even those who work in the high districts carry the habit. It lives in their bodies like an old song.

Their skin is pale but not fragile. The damp has changed them in subtle ways. Their skin holds moisture differently than a surface-dweller's — it seems almost to welcome the wetness, to absorb it rather than resist it. The healers call this *deep-skin*, and it is considered a mark of belonging. New arrivals from the surface spend their first weeks in a state of perpetual discomfort, their skin prickling, their clothes never dry, and the residents watch this with a quiet, knowing patience. You will adjust or you will leave. The Dripworks does not argue with anyone.

Their hands are their most important feature. The people of the Dripworks have large hands — or rather, they have *articulate* hands, long-fingered, deft, with an extraordinary sensitivity to moisture. A water-carrier can tell by touch alone whether a channel's flow has changed, can feel the difference between seepage and runoff, can detect the early stages of fungal rot on a structural beam by running her fingertips along its surface. Children are tested young for *channel-sense* — the ability to read water through the stone — and those who show aptitude are taken into the guild, which is the closest thing the Dripworks has to a ruling class.

They dress in layers of hide and woven reed-cloth, treated with animal fat and tanning solutions that make them water-resistant without making them stiff. The colors are muted: tans, greys, the occasional deep brown from alder-bark dye. Metal ornaments are rare but not unheard of — a copper ring, an iron pin. Most decoration is carved: bone beads, stone pendants, patterns incised into leather bracelets. The tanners' guild favors white ash rubbed into their forearms, a mark of their trade, and the ash also serves as a drying agent against the perpetual moisture. You can identify a tanner from across a market by their pale, cracked forearms, which they display without shame.

Their hair tends toward dark, and they keep it short or tied back tightly — long hair in the damp becomes a problem, harboring fungus, tangling in the narrow passages. Many of the channel-keepers shave their heads entirely. The mushroom-farmers grow their hair long and wear it wrapped around their skulls in elaborate bindings, because their work requires them to press their heads against low ceilings to peer into dark corners where the fungi grow, and the wrapped hair protects their scalps. This is a practical adaptation that has become, as such things do, a mark of identity. You can tell what a person does by how they wear their hair.

---

The children of the Dripworks are water-confident in a way that would terrify a surface parent. They swim before they walk — or rather, they paddle in the shallow flood-markets, bobbing in ankle-deep water with the ease of seals, learning to navigate the channels that run through every street. By the age of six or seven, most children can read the Sumps at a glance — the color of the water, the sound of it, the rate of seepage on the surrounding stone — and predict whether the lower districts will flood within the day. This knowledge is not taught formally. It is absorbed, the way surface children absorb language, through constant immersion in a world where water is the primary fact.

The children are also the city's primary catchers of cave crickets. The upper reaches — the passages nearest the surface throat, where the air is slightly warmer and the stone is dry enough for insects — are the domain of the young. They go up in groups of three or five, carrying reed-traps and resin-lamps, and they spend hours in the dark with their traps set, waiting in a patience that adults find remarkable. A good cricket-catcher can bring home enough protein for a family of four in a single evening. The best catters — the ones who seem to *feel* the crickets before they hear them — are celebrated. Their names are known throughout the district.

---

The guilds are the skeleton of Dripworks society, though no one would use that word. The word they use is *currents* — as in, the flows that connect things. There is the Water-Current, which maintains the channel system and manages distribution. There is the Hide-Current, the tanners, who are second in prestige only to the water-carriers because their work makes everything else possible — clothing, roofing, containers, bindings, the stretched membranes that seal doorways. There is the Stone-Current, the carvers and builders who cut new rooms from the basalt and repair the structural damage that the water perpetually inflicts. There is the Spore-Current, the mushroom-farmers, who feed the city. And there is the Lamp-Current, the resin-collectors and lamp-makers, who trade with the surface for raw resin and manufacture the amber light that makes the Dripworks visible to itself.

These currents overlap and interlock. A mushroom-farmer needs water from a water-carrier, hides from a tanner for her growing-beds, light from a lamp-maker, and a stone-carver to expand her growing-room when production needs to increase. No one is self-sufficient. The Dripworks has no concept of self-sufficiency. Interdependence is not a political philosophy here; it is a physical reality, as obvious and unremarkable as gravity.

The water-carriers hold the most power, and they hold it lightly. Their leader — not a chief, but a *primary*, the first among the channel-keepers — is the closest thing the Dripworks has to a ruler. The current primary is a woman named Ossik, who is sixty-three years old and has been a water-carrier since she was nine. She is missing four fingers on her left hand — two lost to frost-rot in her youth, two to a fungal infection she contracted while clearing a blocked channel in the deep passages. She is the most respected person in the city, and she lives in the same size room as everyone else, a carved chamber in the basalt near the Fourth Sump, with a hide curtain for a door and a single resin lamp on the wall. Her authority comes not from wealth or force but from *knowledge*. She can read the Sumps better than anyone. She predicted the Great Surge of nine years ago — the event that flooded the lower districts for three weeks and killed fourteen people — two days before it happened, and her warning saved hundreds.

---

The surface-dwellers who stay are a particular type. They are not refugees, exactly, though some are fleeing something — a debt, a crime, a marriage, a war. They are people who have a certain tolerance for enclosure, for dampness, for the absence of sky. The Dripworks does not recruit. It does not advertise. People find their way down through the throat of stacked stone for their own reasons, and they either stay or they don't. The ones who stay are the ones who, on their first night, lying in a borrowed room with the sound of water everywhere and the darkness absolute, feel not claustrophobia but something else — a settling, a recognition, as though the stone has been waiting for them.

These arrivals are watched carefully for the first season. They are given work — usually in the Spore-Current, which always needs hands — and they are observed. Can they read the damp? Can they sleep in the dark? Do they flinch at the sound of water in the night, or do they learn to hear it as silence? The ones who adapt are absorbed. The ones who don't are guided back up through the throat, gently, with provisions for the journey. The Dripworks does not keep anyone who does not want to be kept.

---

There is a spiritual life, though it has no priests and no temples. The people of the Dripworks practice what they call *listening to the stone*. It is not worship, exactly. It is more like a form of attention — a practice of sitting in the deep rooms, in the dark, and listening to the water move through the rock. The practitioners believe that the stone is alive in some slow, vast way, and that the water is its blood, and that by listening carefully enough, one can hear the stone's intentions. What the stone intends, no one can say with certainty. The practitioners describe it differently: some say the stone is dreaming, some say it is breathing, some say it is remembering a time when it was molten and free. Ossik, the water-primary, says the stone is not alive at all, but that listening to it teaches you to listen to everything, and that this is enough.

The practice has produced no doctrine, no scripture, no orthodoxy. It has produced a population that is, by surface standards, remarkably calm. The people of the Dripworks do not shout. They do not, as a rule, panic. When the Sumps rise and the lower districts flood, people move upstairs with a quiet efficiency that looks, to an outsider, like indifference. It is not indifference. It is the practiced patience of people who live inside a thing larger than themselves and have learned, over generations, to move with it rather than against it.

---

The old people of the Dripworks are its memory. They sit in the deep rooms and they remember — the patterns of the Sumps across decades, the names of the dead, the history of the channels, the location of every carved room and hidden passage. They are consulted before any major construction. They are the ones who remember the founding — the first families who came down through the throat, three generations ago, and found the Sumps and decided to stay. The story is told differently by every family, but the core is the same: they came down, and the water was there, and they understood that the water meant the place could sustain them.

The oldest living person is a man named Thel, who is — by his own estimation — somewhere between eighty-five and ninety. He was a child when the first channels were carved. He remembers the city before the guilds, before the Sumps were mapped, before the population grew beyond a few hundred. He sits in a deep room near the Seventh Sump, wrapped in hides, his eyes clouded with the milky film that afflicts many of the elderly in the damp, and he tells stories to anyone who will sit and listen. His stories are not always accurate. He conflates events, invents details, forgets names. But the *feeling* of the stories is true — the feeling of a small group of people in a vast dark space, learning to read the water, learning to trust the stone, learning to build a life in a place that was not built for them but that accepted them anyway.

---

The Dripworks has no army. It has no walls, no gates, no defenses. The throat of stacked stone that serves as its entrance is a chokepoint, and in theory it could be defended, but in practice it is not. The people of the Dripworks have no enemies that they know of. The surface world is distant, abstract, a place of light and dryness and incomprehensible scale. They trade with it — wood, metal, resin, surface herbs — but they do not depend on it. They could survive without surface trade. It would be harder. The lamps would burn less bright. The bridges would age without replacement timber. But the water would still come, and the mushrooms would still grow, and the stone would still hold.

This is the deepest fact of the Dripworks, the thing that its people know in their bodies and their bones: the water comes. It has always come. It comes through the stone in patterns that can be learned but not controlled, and the city exists not because people built it but because the water allowed it. The Dripworks is not a conquest of the underground. It is a *conversation* with it. The people listen, and the water speaks, and between them, in the space where listening meets speaking, a city has grown — damp, dark, alive, and still becoming.
