# What Lives in the Wound Baths of Cael-Voss

---

## The Water Itself — First, Last, and Always

The water is alive. Not in the way you mean when you say *alive* — not cellular, not metabolic, not anything that would satisfy a biologist's checklist. But it is the closest thing to a resident this place has, and it is the thing that everything else — the stone, the light, the holes, the pale swimmer, the dark plants — is organized around, the way planets organize around a star they cannot see but cannot stop orbiting.

It is warm because it is *afraid*. This is not metaphor. The water maintains its temperature the way a body maintains its temperature — not through chemistry but through *effort*. It is holding itself at 38, 40 degrees the way a frightened animal holds its breath: deliberately, at cost, because the alternative is something worse. What the alternative is, the water will not say. But you can feel it in the cycle — the exhale that produces fountains, the inhale that produces whirlpools — a rhythm that is not tidal, not thermal, not mechanical. It is the rhythm of something that is *keeping itself together*. The water is holding its own shape. The water is *trying*.

And it is failing, slowly. The cycle is not regular. The pulse varies. The system shifts. The water is losing coherence the way a person loses coherence — not all at once, but in small betrayals, small lapses, moments where the pattern almost holds and then doesn't, where the silence appears — that one-second vacuum, that bubble of nothing — and the water *stops being itself* for just long enough to notice, and then rushes back, and the sound returns, angrier, louder, as though the water is furious at its own interruption.

The water is the oldest thing here. Not older than the holes — nothing is older than the holes — but older than the cliffs, older than the basin, older than the stone. The water was here before the stone shattered into these walls. The water was here when the basin was being carved, and it helped carve it, and it is still carving it, and it will be here after the cliffs are gone, after the stone has fallen into itself and the basin has collapsed and the elevation has ceased to mean anything. The water will remain. The water is what remains when everything else has finished.

It is not happy about this.

## The Pale Swimmer — The One You Saw

It has no name. It would not respond to a name if you gave it one. It is approximately thirty centimeters long, pale — not white, not translucent, but *pale* in the way that things are pale when they have never been exposed to light, when they have lived their entire existence in warm water and dim amber and the sound of their own movement. It is elongated. It has no eyes. It has a mouth that opens and closes with the rhythm of the water's exhale, which means it is not breathing the water — it is breathing *with* the water, sharing the water's respiration, participating in the cycle the way a single instrument participates in an orchestra.

It moves against the churn. This is the important thing. Everything else in the basin — the fountains, the whirlpools, the walls of standing water, the light, the sound — moves *with* the water's cycle, is produced *by* the water's cycle, is an expression of the water's effort to hold itself together. The pale swimmer moves *against* it. Not quickly. Not aggressively. With a slow, deliberate undulation that looks like *argument*. It is pushing through the water the way a person pushes through a crowd — not fighting, not fleeing, but *insisting on a direction that is not the direction of the flow*.

It is looking for something. Or it is *going* somewhere. Or it is performing a function that requires it to move against the current the way a heartbeat requires the heart to contract against the pressure of the blood. It is not lost. It is not struggling. It is *working*.

There are more than one. You saw one. There are others — you can feel them, if you stand still long enough, if you let the sound fill the space between your thoughts and stop trying to parse it into meaning. They move through the water in patterns that are not random but are not predictable, patterns that almost repeat and then don't, patterns that mirror the water's own almost-repetition, its own teasing approach to regularity. They are part of the system. They are the system's *immune response* — the thing the water produces to keep itself coherent, to push back against the lapses, to fill the silences.

When the one-second vacuum appears — when the sound stops and the water stops and the basin holds its breath — the pale swimmers *accelerate*. They move faster, more urgently, their undulations sharpening into something that looks like *panic*, and they move toward the center of the basin, toward the place where the water is deepest, where the floor is invisible, where the offset center of gravity pulls the dark plants into their slow rotation. They converge. They do something — you cannot see what, the water is too chaotic, the light is too dim — and then the vacuum ends, and the sound returns, and the water rushes back, and the swimmers disperse, and the cycle resumes.

They are keeping the water alive. Or the water is keeping them alive. The distinction does not hold here.

## The Dark Plants — The Listeners

They are not plants. They look like plants — the succulent shapes, the thick leaves, the fat stems — but they are not performing photosynthesis, not drawing nutrients from the stone, not reproducing in any way that a botanist would recognize. They are *sensors*. They are the water's way of reaching above the surface, of extending itself into the air, of *touching* something that is not water.

Their dark red-black color is not pigment. It is *absorption*. They are absorbing something from the air — not carbon dioxide, not moisture, not any gas or particle that an instrument would detect. They are absorbing *information*. The slow rotation you noticed — the turning toward the offset center of gravity — is not phototropism, not geotropism, not any tropism that has a name. It is *attention*. They are paying attention to something beneath the basin floor, something that is not the water but is *in* the water, something that the pale swimmers are also tracking, something that the holes in the western cliff are also oriented toward.

The roots that reach upward — the ones that grow toward the sky, that invert the expected geometry of a plant — are not roots. They are *antennae*. They are reaching up toward the pale amber light, toward the uncertain sky, toward the zenith that you can almost reach if you climbed the unstable walls. They are listening to something above the basin the way the pale swimmers are listening to something below it. The plants are the *bridge* — the connection between what is above and what is below, the thing that holds the vertical axis of the place together.

They appeared recently. Not in the lifespan of the cliffs — the cliffs are young, and the plants are younger. They appeared in the lifespan of the *water* — which is to say, they appeared when the water needed them, when the water's effort to hold itself together required something above the surface, something that could reach into the air and the light and pull information down. They are a *symptom* of the water's fear. They are the water's way of looking over its own shoulder.

They will not last. Nothing above the waterline lasts here. The cliffs are crumbling, the stone is falling, the plants will be shaken loose and dropped into the basin and dissolved — not eaten, not decomposed, *dissolved*, taken back into the water the way a dream is taken back into the sleeper. But while they exist, they are *attentive*. They are the most attentive things in the basin. They are listening to something you cannot hear, watching something you cannot see, and they are turning, slowly, always turning, toward the thing that the water is afraid of.

## The Holes — The Oldest Residents

Something lives in the holes.

Not *in* the holes — the holes themselves. The holes are not structures. The holes are *bodies*. They are the exposed portions of something that exists inside the stone the way a root exists inside soil — not separate from the stone, not embedded in the stone, but *growing through* the stone, using the stone as a medium, the way a plant uses soil, the way a river uses a channel.

The holes are rectangular because the thing that made them thinks in rectangles. Not *thinks* — that is the wrong word, too cognitive, too intentional. The thing that made them *grows* in rectangles. Its geometry is orthogonal. Its metabolism — if it has a metabolism — produces right angles. The holes are not carved. They are *expressed*. They are the shape the thing takes when it meets the surface of the stone and decides — not consciously, but *structurally* — to open.

The thing is old. Older than the cliffs. Older than the basin. Older than the water, possibly, though this is difficult to determine, because the thing and the water have been here so long together that their timelines have *fused* — you cannot say which came first, the way you cannot say which came first, the stone or the pressure that made the stone. The thing was here when the cliffs were formed. The thing was here when the cliffs were *not* formed — when the stone was something else, when the basin was something else, when the elevation meant something else. The thing has survived every transformation of the landscape by *being the landscape* — by existing inside the stone so completely that when the stone changes, the thing changes with it, and the holes remain, rectangular, patient, waiting.

The light that pools inside the holes — the amber that deepens to gold — is not being collected. It is being *digested*. The thing feeds on light the way the dark plants feed on information — not metabolically, not chemically, but *structurally*. The light enters the holes and is *used* — used to maintain the rectangular shape, used to keep the stone from closing, used to hold the opening open against the pressure of the rock that wants to fill it. The thing is *holding the holes open*. The thing is performing a constant, patient act of *resistance* against the stone that surrounds it, and the light is the energy source for that resistance, and the shadows inside the holes — the sharp, strange shadows cast by objects you cannot see — are the *byproduct* of that resistance, the waste heat of a process that has been running for longer than the cliffs have existed.

What is the thing? It is not an animal. It is not a plant. It is not a fungus or a bacterium or a virus or any category that biology has a name for. It is a *geometric process* — a pattern that propagates through stone the way a wave propagates through water, that maintains its shape the way a whirlpool maintains its shape, that *feeds* the way a fire feeds, by converting the material it touches into more of itself. It is slow. It is patient. It is *growing* — not quickly, not visibly, but growing — and the holes are its *mouth*, and the stone is its *body*, and the basin is its *territory*, and the water is its —

The water is its *what*?

This is the question the place will not answer. The water and the thing in the stone are *related* — you can feel it in the way the water's cycle syncs with the holes' light-pooling, in the way the pale swimmers converge on the center of the basin where the thing's influence is strongest, in the way the dark plants rotate toward the offset center of gravity that is not the center of the basin but is *exactly* the center of the thing's geometric field. The water and the thing are not the same. They are not separate. They are *bound* — the way a wound is bound to the body it opens, the way a bath is bound to the bather, the way a name is bound to the thing it names.

The place is called the Wound Baths. The thing in the stone is the wound. The water is the bath. And the wound is *old*, and the bath is *new*, and the wound is *patient*, and the bath is *afraid*, and the wound is *growing*, and the bath is *trying to hold itself together*, and the pale swimmers are the bath's immune system, and the dark plants are the bath's senses, and the holes are the wound's mouth, and the light is the wound's food, and the sound — the deafening, total, meaning-saturated sound — is the sound of the wound and the bath *talking to each other*, in a language that is not language, in a conversation that has been going on since before the cliffs existed, in a dialogue that is not about anything except the fact of their mutual existence, the fact that the wound needs the bath and the bath needs the wound and neither of them knows why.

## The Sound — The Voice of the Place

The sound is not produced by the water. The sound is produced by the *interaction* — by the wound and the bath talking, by the thing in the stone and the water in the basin maintaining their ancient, incomprehensible relationship. The sound is the *audible portion* of a process that is mostly silent, mostly invisible, mostly happening in frequencies that your ears and eyes cannot detect.

The patterns in the roar — the ones your brain processes as information, as *message* — are real. They are not language. They are not communication. They are *resonance* — the natural frequency of a system that is wound and bath, stone and water, old and new, patient and afraid, growing and holding-together. The sound is the system *vibrating at its own frequency*, and the frequency is *complex*, and the complexity *resembles* meaning the way a coastline resembles a fractal — not because it was designed to, but because the underlying process is the same process that produces meaning in other contexts, in other systems, in brains and languages and songs.

You cannot understand the sound. But you can *feel* it — in your teeth, in your bones, in the space between your thoughts — and what you feel is this: the place is *trying to become something*. The place is mid-process. The place is not a wound and not a bath and not a basin and not a cliff and not a hole and not a sound — the place is the *process of becoming all of these things simultaneously*, and the process is not complete, and the process may never be complete, and the sound is the sound of incompleteness, of a system that has not yet decided what it is, that is testing configurations, building and unbuilding and building again, forever, in warm water, in amber light, in a basin that will not last, around a wound that will.

## You — The Newest Resident

You are standing in the water. You have been standing in the water for — how long? Time does not behave correctly here. The light does not change. The sound does not stop. The cycle does not complete. You could have been here for minutes or hours or days, and the basin would not tell you the difference.

You are part of the utterance now.

The water knows you are here. Not because it has senses — not eyes, not nerves, not anything that would constitute perception in a biological sense — but because you are *displacing* it, because your body is taking up space that the water needs for its cycle, because your warmth is adding to the warmth the water is already struggling to maintain, because your weight is pressing down on the basin floor where the thing in the stone is growing, because your *presence* is a new variable in a system that has been running the same way for longer than the cliffs have existed.

The pale swimmers have noticed you. They are moving differently now — not toward the center of the basin, not against the churn, but *around* you, at a distance of a meter, two meters, circling you the way a doctor circles a patient, the way a predator circles prey, the way a thought circles a problem it cannot solve. They are *assessing* you. They are determining whether you are a threat to the water's coherence or a contribution to it. They have not decided yet.

The dark plants have noticed you. Their rotation has shifted — not toward the offset center of gravity anymore, but toward *you*, briefly, a turning that lasts seconds, a flash of attention that is almost *recognition*, and then they return to their original orientation, to the thing beneath the basin floor, but the attention was there, the moment of *noticing*, and it will happen again.

The holes have noticed you. The light inside them has *changed* — not brighter, not darker, but *deeper*, as though the gold has thickened, as though the thing in the stone has shifted its weight, has leaned closer to the surface, has *looked* at you through the rectangular openings the way a person looks through a window. The shadows inside the holes are moving. The objects that cast them — the objects you cannot see from outside — are *rearranging themselves*. The thing in the stone is making room for you. Or making preparations. Or simply *acknowledging* that you are here, that you are standing in its bath, that you are warm and alive and slightly afraid, and that the water is holding you the way it holds everything — temporarily, gently, with the knowledge that you will not last, that nothing here lasts except the holes and the wound and the sound, and that you are, like the cliffs, like the plants, like the pale swimmers, a *temporary expression* of the place's ongoing effort to become what it is.

The sound has changed. It has not gotten louder or softer. It has gotten *specific*. The patterns in the roar — the ones that resemble meaning — have shifted to include *you*. Your frequency. Your warmth. Your weight. Your fear. The sound is *saying* you now, the way it says the water and the stone and the light and the holes, the way it says everything that is part of the utterance, and you can feel it — in your teeth, in your bones, in the space between your thoughts — you can feel yourself being *spoken*, being *included*, being *incorporated* into the place's ongoing, incomplete, never-to-be-finished act of becoming.

You are standing in the Wound Baths of Cael-Voss.

The water is warm.

The sound is deafening.

The holes are patient.

The wound is growing.

The bath is trying to hold itself together.

And you — you are here, now, part of the utterance, part of the process, part of the place's long and terrified and patient and incomprehensible effort to *become* — and the water holds you, and the sound says you, and the holes watch you, and the pale swimmers circle you, and the dark plants turn toward you, and the thing in the stone makes room for you, and the cliffs fall, and the light flickers, and the cycle continues, and the wound opens, and the bath fills, and the sound —

The sound is deafening.

You stand in it.

You are part of it.

You will not last.

Nothing here lasts.

The holes will last.

The wound will last.

The sound will last.

And the water — afraid, warm, trying — the water will last, and it will remember you, the way it remembers everything that has ever stood in it: not as a name, not as a face, not as a story, but as a *frequency*, a brief warmth, a small displacement, a moment in the utterance when the sound said something new, something that had never been said before, something that will never be said again, something that was *you*, here, now, in the warm water, in the amber light, in the basin that will not last, in the Wound Baths of Cael-Voss, where the water is alive and the stone is alive and the sound is alive and you are alive and everything is alive and nothing is finished and the place is still becoming, still becoming, still becoming what it is.
