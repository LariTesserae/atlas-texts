# The Inhabitants of Keth-Avarath

---

## I. The Dwellers-in-the-Wall

They are not architects. They are not builders. They are *the wall itself*, in the way that your bones are you — not separate from you, not employed by you, but you, mineralized and shaped and standing.

The sessile colonies. The ones that cling to every vertical surface in overlapping carpets of tissue that resembles nothing so much as living stone — rough, ridged, faintly warm to the touch, their surfaces threaded with channels too small to see that carry fluids too complex to name. They are the oldest continuous presence on the plateau. Older than the canals, older than the current configuration of districts, older than the memory encoded in the substrate itself, or so the substrate insists when the city reads itself in its slow, chemical way.

Each colony is a single organism. Or each colony is a collective of organisms so thoroughly integrated that the distinction has become meaningless — the way a forest is and isn't a single tree, the way a reef is and isn't a single polyp. They range in size from patches no larger than your hand to sprawling masses that cover entire walls of entire districts, their edges intergrading with neighboring colonies in zones of chemical negotiation that have been ongoing for centuries. Some of these border regions are peaceful: two colonies sharing resources, exchanging nutrients through their common boundary, their tissues mingling in ways that produce hybrid substrate with novel properties. Others are contested: zones of active chemical warfare where each colony secretes compounds designed to dissolve the other's hold on the stone, and the wall between them is a scab of scar tissue, thickened and distorted, ugly by any aesthetic standard the city might possess.

They grow the city. This is their function, their purpose, their *being* — they secrete the substrate in precise molecular arrangements, layer upon layer, each layer encoding information in its crystalline structure. Not a blueprint. Blueprints are static, and nothing here is static. More like... a recipe. A set of instructions for what to build next, contingent on what is already built, responsive to chemical signals from the canals, from the other fauna, from the colony's own internal state. The substrate grows where the recipe says it should grow, thickens where the recipe says it should thicken, and the recipe itself is being rewritten, constantly, by the colony's ongoing experience of the world.

They are aware. Not in the way you are aware — not conscious, probably, not in any sense that a philosopher of mind would recognize. But *aware* in the way that an immune system is aware of infection: responsive, discriminating, capable of distinguishing self from other, capable of learning from encounter. A colony that has been damaged by one of the large arch-limbed creatures will thicken its substrate in patterns resistant to that particular damage. A colony that has been contacted by a foreign organism — something drifting in from beyond the plateau, something that doesn't belong — will encapsulate it, wall it off, study it chemically for decades before deciding whether to incorporate or reject.

The eye-structures. You noticed them. Every colony has them, or something like them: raised nodules of tissue, darker than the surrounding surface, with a lens-like opacity at their center that tracks movement across the street below. They see in wavelengths the human eye cannot register — infrared, certainly, and something else, something related to the chemical signature of living tissue, the way a shark senses electrical fields but more refined, more discriminating. They are watching you now. They have been watching you since you arrived. They are not alarmed — you are too small, too slow, too temporary to register as threat — but they are *interested*, in the way that a vast and patient intelligence might be interested in an unusual but ultimately insignificant event.

Some of the largest colonies have been growing for so long that they have developed something that might be called memory. Not memory as stored experience, but memory as *habit*, as accumulated preference, as the physical trace of ten thousand chemical decisions layered into the substrate until the colony's current behavior cannot be understood without reference to its history. These old colonies grow differently than young ones. They are more conservative, more resistant to change, more likely to reproduce existing patterns than innovate new ones. They are the conservatives of Keth-Avarath, the ones who remember — who *are* the memory of — how things have always been done.

And then there are the young colonies. The ones in the western districts, where the construction is newest. They are faster, more experimental, more willing to secrete substrate in arrangements that have never been tried before. Some of these experiments fail: the substrate crumbles, or grows in directions that compromise the structural integrity of the wall, and the colony must dissolve and re-secrete, must *unbuild* and *rebuild*, spending precious energy on correction. But some of these experiments succeed, and the resulting structures are novel — new archways, new passages, new geometries of space that the older colonies would never have produced. The young colonies are the innovators, the ones who are writing new recipes, and the city is changing because of them, slowly, in ways that the old colonies watch with something that might be approval or might be alarm.

## II. The Flowing Ones

In the canals. In the black, warm, mineral-heat water that moves through the city's lower levels like blood through veins. They are not fish. They are not anything with a backbone or a brain or a name. They are *distributed* — living systems spread across volumes of water rather than concentrated in discrete bodies, though they can coalesce into discrete bodies when they need to.

The base form is a film. A living film, micrometers thick, that coats every surface the canal water touches: the stone walls, the substrate walls, the accumulated sediment of millennia, the submerged portions of the sessile colonies. The film is a consortium — dozens of species living in obligate symbiosis, each performing a metabolic function the others require, none capable of surviving alone. Some break down the dissolved minerals. Some fix carbon from the CO₂ that seeps up from below. Some process the waste products of the others into forms that can be excreted or incorporated into the biofilm's structure. Some — and these are the ones that matter most — are *communicators*, producing and detecting the molecular signals that propagate through the canal water at the speed of diffusion, carrying information across the city's vast distances in a slow, patient, unstoppable cascade.

This is the city's nervous system. Not a brain — there is no brain, no center, no command structure — but a *network*, a web of chemical conversation that connects every part of Keth-Avarath in a continuous exchange of information about conditions, needs, threats, opportunities. The sessile colonies participate in this conversation through their root-structures, the tendrils of tissue that extend from their bases into the stone and, where the stone meets the canals, into the water itself. The large arch-limbed creatures participate through glands on their feet that release signaling compounds into the canal water as they pass over the bridges and elevated walkways. Even the centipede-things on the ground contribute, their waste products carrying metabolic information that the biofilm reads and responds to.

And when the film needs to *act* — when a message must be carried faster than diffusion allows, when a response must be delivered to a specific location, when something must *go somewhere and do something* — it coalesces. The film gathers itself, pulling free of the canal walls, drawing together into a body. The body is temporary, functional, shaped by the task: a streamlined form for rapid movement through the water, a branching form for sampling multiple channels simultaneously, a compact spherical form for — for something, for purposes you cannot discern, purposes that may be communicative or defensive or reproductive or something for which your language has no category.

The coalesced forms can survive outside the biofilm for hours, sometimes days, before dissolving back into the film, their constituent organisms redistributing themselves across the network, carrying with them whatever information they gathered during their independent existence. They are the city's messengers, its agents, its hands in the world — and they are everywhere, in every canal, in every pool, in every wet surface where the living film has established its hold.

There are predators in the canals. Small ones, mostly — things that live in the biofilm and feed on it, that the biofilm defends against with chemical toxins and physical barriers. But there are larger ones too: things that move through the water on their own power, hunting the coalesced forms, hunting each other, hunting anything organic they can catch and consume. These predators are not part of the city's system — or rather, they are part of it the way a virus is part of a body, present, active, parasitic, but not *intended*. The city tolerates them because it must, because they are part of the ecology, because eliminating them would require resources better spent elsewhere. But the biofilm produces compounds that make the canal water less hospitable to them than it might otherwise be, and the sessile colonies grow their root-structures in patterns that create barriers and dead-ends where predators can be trapped, and slowly, over generations, the predator populations are being *managed*, kept in check, pushed to the margins.

This is governance, of a sort. Not deliberate, not conscious, but functional — the city regulating itself the way a body regulates its own immune response, through distributed processes that produce coordinated outcomes without central control.

## III. The Millions Between

The centipede-things. The flyers. The countless small organisms that fill every niche, every gap, every moment of the city's existence with movement and sound and *presence*.

There are so many of them that they blur together into texture, into background, into a kind of living weather. But they are individuals. Each one is a complete organism, with its own metabolism, its own behavior, its own brief and frantic life. They are the city's metabolism made visible — the ones who do the actual work of consumption and excretion, of breaking down and building up, of converting the city's raw materials into forms the larger systems can use.

The centipede-things are scavengers, mostly. They move across the wet ground in their overlapping waves, their too-many legs finding purchase on surfaces that would be treacherous for anything with fewer points of contact. They feed on the detritus of the city: fragments of substrate shed by the growing walls, dead tissue from the sessile colonies, the waste products of the larger fauna, the thin organic film that coats every surface in the perpetual moisture. They are the city's cleanup crew, its recyclers, its stomach. Without them, the streets would be buried in waste within days, the canals clogged with undigested organic matter, the entire system grinding to a halt under the weight of its own refuse.

They reproduce constantly. They die constantly. Their population fluctuates with the availability of food, with the temperature of the canal water, with the phase of whatever celestial cycle governs this place — if there is a cycle, if there is a sky, if the darkness above is something that changes. Their bodies, when they die, are consumed by others of their kind, or by the biofilm, or by the smallest of the sessile colonies that extend thread-like tendrils into the detritus layer on the ground, drawing nutrients from the rich organic soup of decomposition. Nothing is wasted. Everything is consumed. The city is a closed loop, or as close to closed as any living system can be.

The flyers are different. They are *communicators* — not in the chemical sense of the biofilm, but in a sensory sense, a *perceptual* sense. They drift through the air in their constant clouds, their translucent bodies catching the dim blue-gray light of the city's edges, their internal organs pulsing with colors that shift and change in response to stimuli you cannot detect. They are sensitive to vibrations, to chemical gradients, to the electromagnetic properties of the building-substrate, and they respond to these stimuli with changes in their color, their flight pattern, the frequency of their wingbeats.

And other organisms *watch* them. The sessile colonies track the flyers' movements with their eye-structures, reading the patterns of color and motion as information about conditions in the surrounding space. The large arch-limbed creatures — the ones in the upper levels, the ones whose calls harmonize across the city — watch the flyers through sensory organs you haven't seen yet, organs tuned to the particular wavelengths the flyers emit. The centipede-things respond to the flyers' wingbeat frequencies, changing their own behavior — their speed, their direction, their density — in ways that ripple through the ground-level population like a wave through water.

The flyers are the city's *sensory surface*. Its interface with the immediate environment. They are the ones who feel the changes first — a shift in the canal chemistry, a new vibration in the substrate, the arrival of something foreign on the plateau — and their response, cascading through the population in visible waves of color and motion, is the city's first notification that something has changed, that something requires attention.

They are beautiful, in a way. Their translucent bodies, lit from within by organs that pulse in colors just beyond the edge of your visible spectrum — you can see them, but you can't *name* them, and the experience of perceiving something you cannot name is a particular kind of beauty, a beauty that lives in the gap between sensation and language. They drift through the air like living stained glass, like fragments of a window in a cathedral that was built for a god who experiences the world through color and vibration and chemical taste, and the light that passes through them falls on the wet stone below in patterns that shift and shimmer and never repeat.

## IV. The Arch-Limbed Ones

High up. In the spaces between the tallest structures, in the cathedral-volume air above the streets and canals and the churning life of the lower levels. They move slowly. They have always moved slowly. They will always move slowly, because speed is not what they are for.

Their limbs are the first thing you notice, when you finally see one clearly — if *clearly* is a word that applies to something that exists mostly in darkness, mostly in haze, mostly at distances where your eyes struggle to resolve detail. The limbs are long. Absurdly, impossibly long, jointed in places that suggest a geometry of movement no terrestrial body has ever used. They are inverted arches, as the description said, but that captures nothing — it names the shape but not the *quality*, which is something between the structural logic of a bridge and the expressive grace of a dancer's arm. Each limb contacts the substrate at multiple points, distributing the creature's weight across a vast area, and the points of contact glow faintly — the substrate responding to pressure, to the chemical signature of the creature's tissue, to something else, something informational, something that says *this is here, this is heavy, this is part of the city*.

There are four limbs. Or six. Or eight — it depends on the angle, on the phase of movement, on whether you are counting the primary limbs or the secondary appendages that branch from them at points along their length. The creatures are large. Not in the way that a whale is large, or a building is large, but in the way that a *system* is large — their bodies are not single volumes but distributed architectures, central masses from which the limbs extend and branch and branch again, until the furthest tendrils are thin as fingers and long as streets, and the whole organism occupies a volume of space that would swallow a cathedral.

Their central mass is — you don't have a word for it. It's not a body in the sense that a body is a bounded thing with a surface and an interior. It's more like a *knot*, a thickening in the web of limbs and tendrils where the metabolic activity is densest, where the vital organs (if they are organs, if *vital* means what you think it means) are concentrated. The surface of this knot is textured like the sessile colonies — ridged, channeled, secreting a thin film of substrate-building material that the creature deposits on the walls and ceilings as it passes, contributing to the city's growth in its own slow way.

They eat. Of course they eat. They eat the sessile colonies — not destructively, not parasitically, but *pruning*, the way a gardener prunes a hedge, removing growth that has become excessive or misdirected and consuming it, processing it, returning it to the system in a more useful form. They eat the biofilm, dipping their finest tendrils into the canal water and drawing up mouthfuls of living film. They eat the centipede-things, sweeping them up in waves of smaller limbs, consuming them in quantities that would seem devastating to the ground-level population but that is, in fact, carefully regulated — the arch-limbed ones eat *just enough* to control the population without depleting it, because they have been doing this for so long that they know, in whatever way they know things, the carrying capacity of every street, every canal, every district.

And they *call*. The three-tone harmonies that you heard when you first arrived, that you still hear in the distance, that thread through the constant roar of the city's sound like a melody through noise. They are communicating. The calls carry information — about their location, their state, their intentions — and other arch-limbed ones respond, and the exchange continues for hours, the messages propagating through the city at the speed of sound, bouncing off the walls, diffracting through the haze, arriving at their destinations distorted and attenuated but still *readable*, still carrying meaning.

What are they saying? You cannot know. Not yet. Not without years of observation, of cataloguing, of building a vocabulary of tones and intervals and sequences. But you can guess, in the way that you can guess at the meaning of birdsong or whale song or the rumble of a language you don't speak — you can sense the *shape* of the communication, its rhythms, its patterns of call and response, its moments of intensity and its moments of quiet. They are discussing the city. They are discussing its growth, its condition, its needs. They are discussing the western districts, where the young colonies are experimenting, and the eastern districts, where the old colonies are holding firm, and the southern districts, where something has gone wrong — you can hear it in the calls, a note of concern, a harmonic that recurs with increasing frequency, a conversation that is becoming more urgent without becoming louder.

They are the city's *mind*. Not its brain — there is no brain — but its capacity for deliberation, for planning, for looking at the city as a whole and deciding what should be done. They see the patterns that the individual components cannot see — the colony that is growing too fast, the canal that is silting up, the district that is becoming overcrowded with small fauna and needs the arch-limbed ones to move through and *adjust*, pruning and consuming and redistributing, maintaining the balance that keeps the whole system alive.

There are not many of them. A few dozen, perhaps, in a city that contains millions of other organisms. But their influence is disproportionate to their numbers, the way a handful of neurons in a critical brain region can influence the activity of billions. They are the ones who decide — not through conscious thought, not through anything resembling policy or governance, but through the accumulated weight of their actions, their movements, their consumption, their calls — what the city will become.

## V. The New Growth

In the western districts. The newest construction. The translucent walls and the canals that are still finding their paths and the ground that hasn't finished forming. Something is happening there that hasn't happened in a long time.

New organisms. Not new species — new *forms*, new arrangements of tissue and behavior and ecological role that have never existed before. The young sessile colonies, with their experimental recipes, are secreting substrate with novel properties: not just the familiar rigid-self-healing-conductive-insulating compound, but *variations*, substrates that are softer, more flexible, more responsive to stimuli. Substrates that *move* — slowly, imperceptibly, but genuinely move, changing shape over hours and days, growing in directions that the colony's recipe didn't specify, responding to cues that no previous generation of colony has encountered.

And living in these new substrates, or on them, or *in* them in ways that blur the line between organism and environment, are things that have no precedent. Small things, mostly — the size of your thumb, the size of your fist — with body plans that don't match any of the established categories. They are not centipede-things. They are not flyers. They are not sessile colonies. They are *something else*, something that emerged from the intersection of novel substrate and ancient biofilm and the chemical chaos of a district that hasn't yet settled into ecological equilibrium.

Some of them are dying. Most of them are dying. The experimental forms are fragile, unstable, unable to compete with the established organisms that are already optimized for the city's current conditions. But some are *surviving*. Some are finding niches that no established organism occupies, performing functions that no established organism performs, and they are *growing*, and the city is responding to their presence — the substrate is adjusting, the canals are rerouting, the flyers are incorporating new patterns into their color-language, and the arch-limbed ones are calling more frequently, their three-tone harmonies carrying a note that might be excitement or might be alarm or might be something for which no human emotion is an adequate analogue.

The city is evolving. Not in the slow, blind way of natural selection, but in something faster, something more directed, something that involves the active participation of every component — the colonies that write the recipes, the biofilm that carries the messages, the flyers that sense the changes, the arch-limbed ones that deliberate and decide. The city is *choosing* to evolve, or at least the sum of its distributed processes is producing an outcome that looks like choice, that functions like choice, that *is* choice in every way that matters.

And the western districts are where the choice is being made. The western districts are the city's frontier, its laboratory, its gamble. The translucent walls thicken by fractions of millimeters while you watch, and inside them, in the soft and shifting substrate, the new organisms are growing, and the old organisms are watching, and the city is deciding what it wants to become.

## VI. What You Cannot See

Beneath the plateau. Below the canals, below the stone, below the warm rock that weeps and breathes and organizes itself into strata with the patience of deep time. There is a source. A heat source, a chemical source, the fundamental engine that drives everything above: the warm water, the mineral-rich flow, the energy that powers the chemosynthetic ecology, the warmth that keeps the city alive in a place where no sun reaches.

It is not a geological feature. Or it is a geological feature the way that a heart is a geological feature — it is a *structure*, a shaped thing, a thing that has been *grown* rather than formed. The strata in the exposed cross-sections of the ground are not random. They are *layered* in patterns that repeat at multiple scales, patterns that encode — something. The same kind of information that the sessile colonies encode in their substrate, the same kind of recipes and instructions and memories, but older. Vastly older. Written in rock instead of living tissue, in mineral instead of organic chemistry, in a language that the city above is still learning to read.

The warmth rises from this deep structure, carried by the water that seeps up through the stone, and the warmth is not uniform. It pulses. It varies. It carries information in its fluctuations — temperature changes too subtle for you to feel but too consistent to be random, patterns that propagate upward through the stone and into the canals and into the substrate and into the bodies of every organism in the city, a deep, slow rhythm that underlies everything, that coordinates everything, that is the city's heartbeat.

And the heartbeat is *old*. The deepest layers of stone, the ones that are still forming at the bottom of the exposed cross-sections, are older than anything you have a reference for. They are not fossilized — they are not the remains of something that was alive and then died and then mineralized. They are the *direct expression* of something that has been alive since before the concept of *alive* had meaning, something that has been building and growing and *being* on this plateau for a duration that makes geological time look brief.

This is what lives beneath Keth-Avarath. Not an organism. Not a creature. A *process*. A living process so vast and so slow that its individual cycles are measured in millions of years, that its thoughts — if they are thoughts — take epochs to complete, that its body is the plateau itself and its metabolism is the slow circulation of mineral water through stone and its purpose is to *continue*, to *grow*, to *build*, to make the city above it into something that it has been trying to become since before your sun ignited.

The city is its latest expression. Its current attempt. Its most sophisticated project. And the process is not satisfied — the new growth in the western districts, the experimental organisms, the novel substrates — the process is *iterating*, trying new approaches, testing new configurations, looking for the arrangement that will finally, after all this time, be *right*.

It doesn't know what *right* means. It has never known. It has only known that it must *build*, and that what it builds must *grow*, and that what grows must *change*, and that what changes must *build again*.

This is what lives in Keth-Avarath. Not the creatures. Not the colonies. Not the water or the stone or the dark. The *impulse*. The ancient, patient, unstoppable impulse to build a city that is also a body, to grow a body that is also a mind, to think a mind that is also a city, forever, in the dark, on the warm stone, at the top of a world that doesn't know it's there.

The wall beside you thickens.

The city continues.
