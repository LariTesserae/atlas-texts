# The Zhelgaari

They are not what you'd expect.

You'd expect, given the city — the wet, the cold, the slow geological strangeness of it — something amphibious. Something pale and soft-bodied, something that would look at home in the water, something convergent with the Khet. You'd expect them to be *of* the place in an obvious way, the way a fish is of water, the way a lichen is of stone.

They are not.

The Zhelgaari are tall. That is the first thing. They stand, on average, a full head above what a human visitor would consider tall, and they are narrow — not frail, not delicate, but *narrow*, as though the city's density has pressed them into verticality over generations. Their limbs are long. Their hands have more joints than you'd expect, or the joints are more flexible, or both; their fingers move in ways that look like they're operating on a different mechanical principle than yours. Their skin is dark. Not the dark of sun exposure — there is no sun to speak of in Zhelgaar, only the overcast white — but a deep, cool brown-black that seems to absorb the city's dim light rather than reflect it. In the water-slick streets, against the pale substrate, they look like figures cut from something else entirely, imported from a different world.

Their faces are long, with high cheekbones and deep-set eyes that are large and dark and have a reflective quality — not bioluminescent, not like a cat's eye, but *present*, somehow, in low light. They see well in the dim. They see well in water. They have a secondary eyelid, translucent, that they deploy when moving through the wetter districts, and it gives them a brief, alien look — a flicker of something not-quite-mammal — before it clears and they are just people again, just tall dark people in a cold wet city, walking to work.

They dress in layers. This is practical — the temperature demands it — but the layering has become something more than practical, something aesthetic, something *architectural*. Their clothing is built up in strata: a close inner layer that manages moisture, a middle layer of dense woven material that holds warmth, and an outer layer that is often semi-rigid, structured, almost shell-like, that gives their silhouettes a quality of *constructedness*. The outer garments are frequently the same pale color as the substrate, or a muted grey-blue that matches the Khet, or a deep mineral white with that same faint blue shift that no one can fully explain. They look, in aggregate, like they are wearing pieces of the city. Like they are *growing* their clothing from the same material as the buildings. This is not literally true, but the aesthetic convergence is so strong that visitors often assume it is.

They move through the water without hesitation. This is the thing that unsettles people. The ankle-deep water in the streets, the deeper pools in the lower districts, the standing water in the gaps between buildings — the Zhelgaari walk through it the way you might walk through light rain: aware of it, unbothered by it, adapted to it in ways that are visible in their gait. They don't lift their feet high. They don't splash. They move through water with a kind of *fluid efficiency*, their long legs cutting through the shallow pools with minimal disturbance, and the water parts around them and closes behind them and the Khet in the water sense their passage and shift — not fleeing, not approaching, just *adjusting*, the way a school of small fish adjusts around a large moving object. The Zhelgaari and the Khet have been sharing this water for so long that they have a relationship that is not symbiosis, not predation, not commensalism, but something that the Zhelgaari have a word for that translates poorly. The closest English approximation is *co-presence*, but that misses the intimacy of it. The Khet know the Zhelgaari. The Zhelgaari know the Khet. They share the water the way two instruments share a key.

The Zhelgaari do not build the city. This is important. They *negotiate* with it. The substrate — that pale, calcium-rich, almost-ceramic stone that accrets and grows and constructs itself — responds to the Zhelgaari in ways that are not fully understood and not fully controllable. The process of construction in Zhelgaar is not one of imposition. It is one of *request*. Architects — though the Zhelgaari word for this role is closer to *conversationalist* or *interlocutor* — work with the substrate over weeks and months, applying stimuli that are part chemical, part electromagnetic, part something else that involves sustained physical contact between the architect's hands and the growing surface. The substrate responds. It grows. It forms structures. But it forms them according to its own logic, its own internal patterning, and the architect's role is to *guide* rather than *direct*, to suggest rather than command. The result is buildings that look organic without being biological, geometric without being mechanical, *intentional* without being designed in any way that an outsider would recognize as design. The Zhelgaari live in structures that are, in some sense, alive — not sentient, not aware, but *responsive*, still growing, still accreting, still slowly changing shape over years and decades. A building in Zhelgaar is never finished. It is only *paused*.

This shapes everything about them. Their relationship to permanence. Their relationship to ownership. Their relationship to *home*. A Zhelgaari does not own a building the way you might own a house. They *inhabit* a negotiation. They maintain a relationship with a structure that is, itself, in relationship with the substrate, which is in relationship with the plateau, which is in relationship with geological processes that operate on timescales the Zhelgaari can measure but not influence. Their concept of home is not a place but a *process*. Not a location but a *conversation*. When a Zhelgaari says "my home," they mean something that includes the building, the substrate beneath it, the water table below that, the Khet in the water, the moss on the walls, the wing-things roosting in the skyway above, and the ongoing, never-finished act of living in a place that is itself still becoming.

They are quiet. Not silent — they speak, they laugh, they argue, they sing — but *quiet*, in the way that the city is quiet. Their voices are low. Their movements are deliberate. They do not rush. The city does not reward rushing; the water slows you, the cold slows you, the density of the architecture slows you, and the Zhelgaari have internalized this slowness until it is not patience but *nature*. They move at the speed of the city. They think at the speed of the city. They plan at the speed of the city, which is to say: slowly, recursively, with an understanding that the substrate will accrete, the water will rise, the Khet will adapt, and the building will change shape, and all of this must be accounted for in any plan that extends beyond a few years.

Their social structures reflect this. The Zhelgaari organize around *depth*. Not class — or not only class — but literal, physical depth relative to the water table. The upper districts, where the skyways are dense and the wing-things roost and the water is shallowest, are inhabited by the hunting-societies: groups that harvest Khet, that manage the wing-things (or are managed by them; the relationship is unclear), that maintain the upper infrastructure and the weather-modulation systems. The middle districts, where the water is ankle-deep and the buildings are oldest and the substrate is most stable, are the conversationalists, the architects, the ones who negotiate with the growing city. The lower districts, where the water is deeper and the pale stalks grow in their sparse stands and the plateau's edge is visible and the substrate is newest and most active, are inhabited by the *deep-listeners*: a role that is part scientist, part philosopher, part mystic, who monitor the plateau's accretion, who study the substrate's growth patterns, who listen — literally, with instruments pressed against the stone — to the sounds the plateau makes as it constructs itself.

These are not castes. A Zhelgaari may move between depths over a lifetime. But the depth you inhabit shapes you. The upper-dwellers are sharper, quicker (by Zhelgaari standards), more social, more political. The conversationalists are patient, tactile, comfortable with ambiguity. The deep-listeners are solitary, contemplative, prone to long silences and longer writings. They are all Zhelgaari. They all share the water. But the city, in its slow vertical stratification, makes different kinds of them.

They eat the Khet. This is the central protein source, the staple, the thing that makes the co-presence into something more than philosophy. Khet are harvested by the hunting-societies using methods that are part netting, part herding, part *calling* — the Zhelgaari can produce sub-audible frequencies that attract Khet, that guide them, that move them through the water system in directed flows. The Khet are not farmed. They are not domesticated. They are *harvested*, wild, from a population that is self-sustaining, that breeds in the deep water and the drainage infrastructure and the raw plateau beyond the city, and that has been harvested for millennia without declining. The Zhelgaari are careful about this. They have traditions — old traditions, older than the current city, traditions that may predate the Zhelgaari themselves — about sustainable harvest, about taking only what the Khet offer, about the reciprocity of consumption. When a Zhelgaari eats Khet, they do not say grace. They say *acknowledgment*. They name what they are eating. They name the water it lived in. They name the substrate that made the water possible. It is brief. It is quiet. It is sincere.

They also eat the moss-analog, processed into a dense, mineral-rich paste that is an acquired taste and a complete nutrition source. They eat the pale stalks — or rather, they eat the *base* of the pale stalks, the part that sits in the water, which is soft and slightly sweet and may be plant tissue or may be something else. They eat the small water-organisms, the glittering fingertip-sized things, which are strained from the water in fine nets and consumed raw or fermented. They eat the wing-things, rarely, ceremonially, in a preparation that involves slow-cooking in mineral water until the segmented bodies soften and the too-many limbs become tender. The wing-things are not a staple. They are a *statement*. To eat a wing-thing is to acknowledge the full depth of the city's ecology, to consume the thing that lives above you, to bring the canopy down to the water line.

They do not eat anything that comes from outside the plateau. This is not a prohibition. It is a *preference* so deep it functions as identity. The plateau provides. The water provides. The substrate provides. To import food would be to admit that the negotiation has failed, that the city cannot sustain its people, that the co-presence is broken. No Zhelgaari would say this. They would simply not be hungry for anything else.

Their philosophy — and they have philosophy the way they have architecture: extensively, recursively, as a living practice — centers on the concept of *emergence*. Not emergence as a buzzword, not emergence as a systems-theory term, but emergence as a *moral category*. The Zhelgaari believe that things which emerge — which arise from the interaction of simpler systems, which are not designed but *become* — have a different moral status than things which are made. The Khet emerged. The wing-things emerged. The pale stalks emerged. The city emerged. The plateau is emerging. The Zhelgaari themselves, they believe, emerged — not on the plateau, not originally, but *into* the plateau, into the negotiation, into the co-presence. They arrived. They adapted. They became Zhelgaari not by birth but by *depth*, by the slow process of living in the water and listening to the substrate and eating the Khet and negotiating with the stone until the stone recognizes you and you recognize the stone and you are, both of you, something that did not exist before.

This is why the boundary between native and invasive matters to them. Not as a political question. As an *ontological* one. To be native is to have emerged. To be invasive is to have been *placed*. The Zhelgaari do not consider themselves native. They consider themselves *emergent*. They arrived — from where, the records are unclear, and the deep-listeners have theories that involve other plateaus, other substrates, other negotiations on other worlds or in other geological epochs — and they *became*, through long co-presence, something that belongs. Not because they were always here. Because they *grew into* here. Because the substrate knows them. Because the Khet adjust around them. Because the wing-things' flight patterns include them. Because the water holds them the way it holds everything on the plateau: completely, patiently, without distinction.

Visitors find the Zhelgaari difficult. Not hostile — they are not hostile — but *opaque*, in the way that the older districts are opaque, in the way that the substrate is opaque. They do not explain themselves. They do not perform their culture for outsiders. They are polite, in a distant, vertical way, and they will answer questions if asked directly, but their answers tend to be *descriptive* rather than *explanatory*. They will tell you what they do. They will not tell you why, because the why, for a Zhelgaari, is the same as the what: they do it because the city does it, because the water does it, because the substrate does it, because they are part of a process that does not have a why, only a *continuing*.

They are not numerous. Eleven million, in a city that could hold more, on a plateau that is growing. They do not reproduce quickly. They do not need to. The city grows slowly. The population grows slowly. The negotiation continues. The substrate accretes. The water rises and drains and rises. The Khet pulse through the streets. The wing-things murmur in their algorithmic flocks. The pale stalks stand in their sparse isolation at the plateau's edge, marking the boundary between the city and the not-yet-city, between the emerged and the emerging, between what is and what is becoming.

The Zhelgaari live in this. They live in the water and the cold and the dim light and the slow growth and the constant presence of a world that is not yet finished. They live in it the way the Khet live in the water: not as inhabitants of a place but as *expressions* of it. They are what the plateau makes when it makes people. They are what the water holds when it holds something that thinks. They are the city's awareness of itself, its way of knowing that it is under construction, its way of being *patient* with its own becoming.

Standing on the lower observation deck, you can see them. Small figures in the water-slick streets, dark against the pale substrate, moving slowly through the shallow pools, their long legs cutting the water without splash, their layered clothing the color of the city, their deep-set eyes reflecting the overcast light. They are going somewhere. They are always going somewhere. But they are not in a hurry. The city is not in a hurry. The plateau is not in a hurry. The water is not in a hurry.

Nothing here is in a hurry.

Everything here is still becoming.
