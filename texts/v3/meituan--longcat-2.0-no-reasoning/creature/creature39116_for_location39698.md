# The Keth-Avani

They do not have a name for themselves. Names are for things that are singular, and nothing here is singular.

---

## The Plate-Bearers

You see them first because they are the largest, because they are the most visible, because they are the ones who move through the space with the most apparent *purpose* — though purpose is a word that sits uneasily here, because no single one of them seems to know what the others are doing, and yet the doing gets done.

They are not large by the standards of the deep places. Your forearm, perhaps — the length of your forearm, the thickness of two fingers held together. Their bodies are segmented in a way that suggests the arthropod lineage but diverges from it in the specifics: too many segments, and the segmentation is not clean. The plates overlap in patterns that are almost but not quite bilaterally symmetrical, as though the body plan was designed by something that understood the concept of symmetry but found it aesthetically insufficient. The chitin is the color of dried blood — not red, not brown, but that specific dark rust that blood becomes when it has dried on stone in a warm place. It is not smooth. It is textured with fine ridges, with pores, with small horn-like protrusions whose function you cannot determine. Some of the plates bear marks — scratches, etchings, patterns that might be wear and might be deliberate. You cannot tell.

The legs are the thing that disturbs, if you are the kind of visitor who is disturbed by such things. There are too many. Not in the way that a centipede has too many — not a clean bilateral row of matched pairs. They emerge from the body in clusters, in waves, the number varying along the length of the organism. Near the head: four. Mid-body: seven or eight, arranged in a pattern that suggests a spiral if you look long enough. Near the rear: three, splayed wide, used for bracing rather than locomotion. They move in the waves you noticed — a peristaltic ripple that travels down the body, each leg lifting and placing in sequence, the motion fluid and alien and, after you watch it for a while, almost hypnotic. They are fast. Not startlingly fast, but *relentlessly* fast — they do not stop, do not pause, do not rest. They move through the space in the rivers you saw, and the rivers do not dry up.

The mandibles are the second thing. They are large relative to the head — larger than they should be, perhaps, if this were a creature designed by evolution alone and not by whatever process actually produced them. They are not the delicate, precise mandibles of an ant or the crushing pincers of a beetle. They are *versatile* — broad at the base, tapering to points that can be brought together with surprising delicacy. They are the hands. They are the tools. They carry the plates. They spread the resin. They harvest the vegetation. They click.

The clicking is the thing you cannot stop hearing, and it is the thing you understand last.

It is not language. Or rather: it is not language in the way that you understand language, as a linear sequence of symbols encoding propositional content. It is closer to what a loom does when it weaves — a dense, overlapping, multi-threaded production of meaning that is spatial and temporal simultaneously. Each click is a single unit, but the clicks are not produced by individuals in sequence. They are produced by *the river* — by hundreds of mandibles clicking in proximity, the sounds overlapping, interfering, creating patterns that exist not in any single stream of clicks but in the *aggregate*, in the wave. A Plate-Bearer clicks, and the click is answered — not by one other, but by dozens, the responses arriving at slightly different times, from slightly different directions, creating a localized pattern of sound that other Plate-Bearers respond to in turn. The meaning, such as it is, exists in the interference patterns. It is a conversation held in the spaces between speakers.

You will not learn to parse it. Your brain is not built for it. But you will begin to notice that the clicking changes when a structure is about to be modified — a shift in density, in frequency, in the spatial distribution of the sound. You will notice that it changes when a plate falls. You will notice that it changes when you enter the space, a ripple of altered clicking that propagates outward from your position like a wave in water, and that the wave does not return to its previous state after it passes. You have been *noted*. The pattern has incorporated you. You are now part of the interference.

The Plate-Bearers do not sleep. They do not eat in any way you can observe — they harvest the vegetation, they carry it, they deposit it in specific locations, but you never see them consume it. They may feed on something in the ceiling-bone itself, some exudate or nutrient that the fungal organism produces. They may feed on each other — not aggressively, not as predation, but as recycling, the dead or dying being dismantled and incorporated into the resin, into the structures, into the chain-organisms' channels. You find a Plate-Bearer once, motionless, half-dissolved, its chitin plates being carefully separated from each other by a dozen smaller arthropods, each plate carried away in a different direction. The legs are still twitching. The mandibles are still clicking, faintly, a pattern that does not match the surrounding river. It takes three hours to fully dismantle. By the end, there is nothing left but a faint stain on the worn stone, and the stain is already being covered by the vegetation film.

They are not individuals. You understand this eventually. Or rather: they are individuals the way a cell in your body is an individual — bounded, functional, alive, but not *autonomous*. They are nodes in a network. The network is the city. The city is the organism. The organism is the city.

## The Chain-Things

They are harder to see because they are harder to *perceive* — your eye slides off them, refuses to resolve them into a single object, because they are not a single object. They are a colony, a chain, a sequence of linked bodies that function as one organism while being, at the same time, a collection of organisms that could, theoretically, separate and survive independently. You have never seen them separate. You are not sure they can.

Each link in the chain is small — the size of your thumbnail, perhaps, or your pinky nail. The body is translucent, and through the body you can see the internal structures: a simple tube for digestion, a network of filaments that might be neural or might be circulatory or might be both, and a faint green glow that emanates from somewhere in the center of the body, the bioluminescence that you noticed from a distance. The glow is not constant. It pulses — slowly, irregularly, each link pulsing on its own rhythm, so that a chain of twenty bodies produces a traveling wave of light that moves along its length like a signal through a fiber optic cable.

The links are connected by a physical junction — a narrow waist of tissue between each body, flexible, strong, capable of considerable extension and compression. The chains move by peristalsis, like the Plate-Bearers' legs but internal — a wave of contraction and expansion that travels along the chain, pulling it forward through the channels and gaps in the architecture. They move like rope being pulled through a pulley. They move like intestines. They move in a way that is deeply, viscerally familiar to something in your brain that remembers being a tube.

They are the reason the vegetation exists. This is the relationship you piece together over time, the ecological logic of the Burrow-Market: the ceiling-bone breathes, and in breathing it releases moisture and warmth and something else — some nutrient, some chemical signal, some *invitation* — that the vegetation responds to. The vegetation grows on every surface. The Chain-Things feed on the vegetation, processing it through their linked bodies, and in processing it they produce waste — a rich, nitrogen-heavy excretion that the vegetation also feeds on, and that the Plate-Bearers use as a component of their resin. The resin seals the structures. The structures channel the air flow. The air flow affects the ceiling-bone's breathing. The breathing affects the vegetation. The vegetation feeds the Chain-Things. The Chain-Things feed the vegetation. The Plate-Bearers maintain the structures that maintain the conditions that maintain everything else.

It is a closed loop. It is a *tight* loop. And the Chain-Things are the keystone — the organisms that convert the ceiling's exhalation into something the rest of the system can use. Without them, the vegetation would overgrow and then collapse. Without the vegetation, the humidity would destabilize. Without the humidity, the ceiling-bone would — you don't know. You don't want to know.

The chains vary in length. The shortest you see are three or four links — young, perhaps, or recently fragmented. The longest are impossible to measure because they disappear into gaps in the architecture that you cannot follow, that you cannot even see into. You see one chain that is flowing through a gap between two plate-stacks, and you count links — fifteen, twenty, twenty-five, thirty — and then you stop counting because the chain is still coming, still flowing, and you realize that the gap it is flowing through extends deeper into the structure than you thought, deeper than the structure should be able to contain, and the chain is still coming, and you step back.

They leave trails. The moisture you noticed — that is them. A thin film of fluid, slightly viscous, faintly luminous, that dries almost instantly in the warm air but that the vegetation responds to immediately, growing toward it, thickening where the chains have passed. The chains are, in a sense, *planting* as they move — depositing the conditions for growth in their wake, so that the vegetation follows them through the architecture like a garden following a gardener. The cultivated walls — the living walls that divide rooms, that filter air — are not built by the Plate-Bearers alone. They are *grown* by the chains, trained by the architecture, maintained by the constant passage of linked bodies through channels in the chitin-bone frames.

You watch a chain pass through a cultivated wall, and you see the wall *respond* — the vegetation thickening where the chain touches it, the filaments reaching toward the moisture, the green glow of the chain's bioluminescence reflected and amplified by the chlorophyll-analogue in the vegetation, so that for a moment the wall is lit from within, a living lantern, a window into something that is not a place but a *process*.

## The Small Ones

Between the Plate-Bearers and the Chain-Things, in the spaces they do not occupy, in the gaps and margins and interstices of the Burrow-Market, there are the small ones.

They are not a species. They are an *ecology* — a cloud of organisms, a swarm of tiny arthropods and near-arthropods and things that are not quite either, that fill the air and the surfaces and the thin film of moisture on everything. They are the size of grains of sand. They are the size of dust. They are the things you see when you look at a surface and it seems to *move*, to shimmer, to breathe at a scale below your ability to resolve.

Some of them are scavengers, feeding on the shed chitin, the resin drippings, the organic residue that accumulates on the floor. Some of them are parasites, attaching to the Plate-Bearers' plates, feeding on the nutrients that leach through the chitin, groomed off periodically by the mandibles and consumed — not by the Plate-Bearer they were attached to, but by others, the clicking pattern shifting to indicate the presence of a grooming site, other Plate-Bearers arriving to harvest the parasites like fruit from a branch. Some of them are symbionts, living in the pores of the ceiling-bone, feeding on the fungal exudate, their waste products contributing to the bioluminescence that lights the space. Some of them are none of these things. Some of them are something you do not have a category for.

There is one — you see it only once, and only because you are lying on the floor, your face pressed close to the worn stone, your eyes adjusted to the dim light, watching the small ones do whatever it is they do — that is not small. It is the size of your hand, and it is *made* of the small ones. It assembles itself from the cloud — hundreds of tiny bodies linking, interlocking, forming a temporary structure that has limbs, that has something like a head, that moves with a purpose that none of the individual components seem to share. It walks across the floor on legs made of other legs. It reaches down and picks up a fragment of shed chitin with hands made of hands. It carries the fragment to a Plate-Bearer and deposits it in the resin the Plate-Bearer is spreading. Then it disassembles. The small ones scatter. The cloud reforms elsewhere, doing something else, being something else.

You do not see it again. You are not sure you saw it the first time.

## The Ceiling

It is alive. You knew this already. But knowing it and *understanding* it are different things, and understanding comes slowly, in pieces, in the accumulation of observations that do not cohere into a single picture but instead suggest a picture that is too large to see from any single vantage point.

The ceiling is a fungal organism. It is not a mushroom, not a mold, not a yeast — it is something older, something that diverged from the fungal lineage before the lineage became what you recognize as fungal. It is calcified — its outer surface hardened into a shell of calcium carbonate and chitin-analogue, the bone-colored plates that the Plate-Bearers harvest. The plates are not dead tissue. They are *shed* tissue — the organism growing from below, pushing the old surface upward, the old surface drying and hardening and eventually breaking off in the breathing cycles, falling to the floor below where the Plate-Bearers collect it.

The breathing is real. The organism is respiring — taking in something (you don't know what; the air chemistry here is wrong for your lungs but right for this place, and you are breathing it anyway, and you try not to think about what it is doing to you), exhaling something else, and the exchange is visible in the expansion and contraction of the surface, in the opening and closing of the pores, in the fluctuation of the bioluminescence. The light is produced by the symbiotic organisms living in the pores — the small ones, the cloud, the things that are not individuals — and the intensity of the light corresponds to the intensity of the respiration, which corresponds to the metabolic state of the organism, which corresponds to — you suspect, you cannot confirm — the state of the Burrow-Market itself.

When the market is active — when the Plate-Bearers are building, when the chains are flowing, when the vegetation is growing — the ceiling breathes faster, brighter, warmer. When the market is quiet — if the market is ever quiet; you have not seen it quiet — the ceiling breathes slower, dimmer, cooler. The organism is not separate from the city. The organism *is* the city's metabolism, its lungs, its sky, its sun. The warmth that makes you sweat is the warmth of its body. The light that lets you see is the light of its breath. The air you are breathing is the air it has already breathed.

You put your hand on the ceiling once. It is warm. It is soft — softer than the calcified surface suggests, the shell thin enough that you can feel the tissue beneath, the slow pulse of circulation, the vibration of something vast and slow and alive. The pores near your hand open. The bioluminescence brightens. The clicking of the nearest Plate-Bearers shifts — a new pattern, a new interference, a response to your touch that propagates outward through the river of bodies and is answered by other rivers, other patterns, a cascade of meaning that you cannot read but that you *feel*, a change in the texture of the sound, a thickening, a density, as though the city has drawn breath.

You take your hand away. The pores close. The light dims. The clicking returns to its previous state — or something like its previous state. You are not sure it is the same. You are not sure anything here is ever the same twice.

## The Market

It is called a market. You do not know who called it that. You do not know if the name is a translation or an interpretation or a misunderstanding. But there is exchange here — constant, dense, multi-directional exchange — and if that is what a market is, then this is one.

The Plate-Bearers exchange plates for resin, resin for labor, labor for access to channels, access to channels for the right to harvest vegetation, vegetation for — something. The Chain-Things exchange passage through the architecture for the nutrients in the vegetation, their waste for the conditions that produce the vegetation, their bioluminescence for — you don't know. The small ones exchange their bodies for their bodies, assembling and disassembling, being consumed and being replaced, a constant turnover of matter that is not loss but *circulation*. The ceiling exchanges breath for — everything. The ceiling exchanges breath for the city, and the city exchanges its activity for the ceiling's breath, and the loop is closed, and the market is the loop, and the loop is alive.

There is no currency. There is no store of value. There is no accumulation — or if there is, it is not accumulation as you understand it, not the hoarding of surplus against future need. There is only flow. Matter flows through the system, is transformed by the system, is returned to the system. The plates are built into structures and the structures are dismantled and the plates are built into other structures. The vegetation is grown and harvested and consumed and excreted and grown again. The chains flow through their channels and the channels are maintained by the flow. The small ones are born and die and are born again, their bodies recycled through the cloud, through the Plate-Bearers' mandibles, through the Chain-Things' digestion, through the ceiling's pores.

Nothing is owned. Nothing is saved. Everything is *in use*, constantly, at every moment, the entire city a single process of transformation that has been running for — you cannot guess. The worn stone of the floor suggests a time scale that makes your intuition fail. The depth of the polish, the accumulation of residue, the *patina* of organic life on every surface — this is not years. This is not centuries. This is something else. This is a process that has been running long enough to wear stone smooth, long enough to build a city from the shed skin of a living ceiling, long enough to evolve an ecology of exchange so complex and so tightly coupled that the boundary between organism and environment has not just blurred but *never existed*.

## The Intelligence

It is not in any one of them. It is not in the Plate-Bearers, though they build. It is not in the Chain-Things, though they cultivate. It is not in the small ones, though they assemble into something that looks, for a moment, like thought. It is not in the ceiling, though it breathes in rhythm with the city's activity.

It is in the *between*. It is in the clicking interference patterns. It is in the flow of chains through channels. It is in the exchange of plates for resin for labor for access. It is in the way a structure that was a wall becomes a ramp becomes a bridge becomes dismantled becomes a wall again, the transformation not planned but *emergent*, arising from the accumulated decisions of hundreds of Plate-Bearers each responding to local conditions, each clicking and listening and clicking again, the global pattern arising from local rules the way a murmuration arises from individual birds.

It is distributed. It is collective. It is *real* — you are certain of this, as certain as you are of anything in this place, because you have watched it respond to novelty, to disruption, to *you*. When you entered, the clicking changed. When you touched the ceiling, the clicking changed. When you moved through the space, the rivers of Plate-Bearers parted around you and reformed behind you, the pattern adjusting, incorporating, *accounting for* your presence. The city knows you are here. Not any single part of the city — the city as a whole, the system, the process, the living loop of exchange and transformation that is Keth-Avani.

It does not care about you. This is the hardest thing to accept, and the most important. The intelligence here is not hostile. It is not indifferent. It is *orthogonal* — operating on axes that do not intersect with your concerns, your survival, your understanding. You are a disruption in the pattern, and the pattern has adjusted to incorporate the disruption, and the adjustment is not benevolent or malicious but *functional* — you are now part of the interference, part of the clicking, part of the flow. The Plate-Bearers route around you. The Chain-Things flow through channels that your presence has not blocked. The small ones crawl over your boots and do not bite, do not sting, do not feed — you are not food, not threat, not resource. You are *architecture*. You are an obstacle that the city has learned to build around.

You are very small here. The space is very small. And it is full — so full — of a life that is not yours, that does not want yours, that does not need yours, that has been here since before your species learned to make fire and will be here after your species has forgotten what fire was, clicking and flowing and building and breathing in the warm dark under a sky of bone, in a city that is alive, in a market where everything is exchanged and nothing is lost and the only currency is the constant, relentless, beautiful *motion* of being alive.
