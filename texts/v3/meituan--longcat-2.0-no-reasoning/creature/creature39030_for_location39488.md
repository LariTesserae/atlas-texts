# The Loomers

They are not a people you would recognize as a people, not at first. They have no flag, no anthem, no founding myth that anyone recites — or rather, they have nothing so formal as a founding myth, only the shared understanding that someone, a long time ago, went down instead of up, and found that down was enough.

---

## The Body

They are small. Not dramatically so — you would not call them diminutive, you would not stare — but the generations of living in rooms that grow narrower each decade have selected, or perhaps simply shaped, a people who fit their space. Compact. Efficient in their movements. They do not sprawl. They do not gesture broadly. A Loomer's hands move in close to the body, precise, economical, the hands of people who have learned that an careless elbow can knock a fungal panel from a wall and a sudden step can crack a moss-bed that took a season to grow.

Their skin is remarkable. It is not merely soft from the humidity — though it is soft, almost plush, the skin of people who have never known dry air — but *inhabited*. The fungal colonization is not a disease. It is a practice. From childhood, Loomers inoculate their skin with specific spore-cultures, applied in patterns that are partly functional and partly aesthetic and partly something else, something closer to identity. The colonies grow slowly, over years, forming faint traceries on the forearms and hands and across the collarbones — pale blues, soft grays, the occasional deep green. They are beautiful in the way that lichen on old stone is beautiful: the beauty of a relationship made visible. The colonies protect against the aggressive molds that would otherwise colonize unchecked in this environment, and they are also a record. A Loomer's arms tell you something about their history — which cultures they've maintained, which they've let lapse, which new strains they've adopted. There are patterns that indicate family lines, patterns that indicate trade specialization, patterns that indicate a period of illness (certain medicinal cultures leave a distinctive mark, a dark ring around the wrist or a streak along the jaw). To read a Loomer's skin is to read a biography, if you know the language.

Their eyes are adapted, or perhaps merely accustomed, to the dim fungal light. They are large relative to the face — not grotesquely so, but noticeably, the way a cat's eyes are large, and they have a quality of *attention* that can be unsettling to surface-dwellers. They see in the blue-green spectrum with an acuity that seems impossible, picking out details in what looks, to an outsider, like uniform dimness. They move through the Looms Below without hesitation, navigating by the subtle variations in the fungal glow, by the sound of water in particular channels, by the feel of the root-walkways underfoot — each walkway has a slightly different texture, a slightly different give, and a Lomer knows them the way you might know the streets of your neighborhood, not by name but by the particular way each one feels at three in the morning.

Their hair is kept short, almost always, for practical reasons — long hair in this environment becomes a vector for unwanted fungal growth, and the constant moisture makes it heavy and uncomfortable. Some Loomers shave entirely. Others keep a close crop, sometimes decorated with tiny cultivated growths, miniature fungal gardens that bloom in slow color over weeks, a living ornament that is also, incidentally, a air-quality sensor — certain species change color in response to shifts in the water chemistry, and a Loomer whose hair-garden has turned unexpectedly amber knows, before any instrument confirms it, that something has changed in the deep channels.

## The Hands

The hands are the thing. If you want to understand the Loomers, watch their hands.

A Loomer's hands are never still. Even at rest, even in conversation, there is a constant low-level activity — a finger testing the moisture on a nearby surface, a thumb rubbing absently at the moss on the walkway railing, a palm held open to the air to feel the temperature and saturation. The hands are always *reading* the environment, gathering data, maintaining the continuous low-level awareness that life in the Looms Below requires. The settlement is alive, and alive things change, and a change that goes unnoticed for too long can become a crisis — a root-wall that has begun to bulge inward, a water-channel that has shifted its flow, a fungal panel that has begun to produce the wrong spectrum of light. The Loomers' hands are their early warning system.

And the hands are skilled in ways that have no surface equivalent. A root-worker — and most Loomers are, to some degree, root-workers, just as most surface-dwellers have some rudimentary knowledge of the buildings they live in — can read a root's growth direction by touch, can feel the tension in the tissue, can tell by the resistance under their fingers whether a root is healthy or stressed or beginning to die back. They can apply pressure in specific ways that encourage a root to thicken, or branch, or turn. They can feel the water flow through a hollow-stem vine by holding it, the way a doctor feels a pulse. The hands know things the mind has not yet articulated.

The maintenance crews — the ones who trim and redirect and negotiate with the architecture — have hands that are visibly different. Thicker. More heavily calloused, though the calluses are strange, not the hard dry calluses of surface labor but something more like the bark they work with, a toughening that is almost a convergence, the skin becoming more like the root-tissue it spends its life in contact with. These hands move with extraordinary confidence in the tight spaces of the root-mass, finding purchase on wet surfaces, gripping root-railings that are themselves shifting and growing, working in the dark with a tactile fluency that looks, to an outsider, like a kind of magic.

## The Voice

They speak quietly. This is partly practical — the acoustics of the Looms Below are strange, the dense humid air and the water-sound and the creak of living architecture creating a constant ambient noise that a loud voice must fight against, and fighting it is exhausting — and partly cultural. Loudness is associated with alarm, with emergency, with the rare moments when something has gone wrong and the settlement's equilibrium has been disrupted. A raised voice in the Looms Below is a signal, and the signal is: *something is breaking*. So the baseline is quiet. Conversations are conducted in low tones, close to the listener, and there is a quality of intimacy to even casual exchanges that can feel, to a surface-dweller, almost intrusive — not because the content is intimate but because the *manner* of speaking requires a closeness that surface culture reserves for private moments.

Their language has a vocabulary for water that is, to an outsider, bewilderingly precise. There are words for the different kinds of dripping — the slow seep from a root-wall, the irregular percussion from the canopy above, the steady flow in a channel, the standing water of the cistern. There are words for the different temperatures of water, for the different mineral contents (which affect the taste, which affects which uses the water is suitable for), for the different *ages* of water — water that has just come down from the surface is different from water that has been cycling through the settlement for days, and the difference matters. There are words for the sounds the roots make under different kinds of stress, and these words are also used metaphorically, so that a Loomer describing a social tension might use the same word they use for a root-arch that is bearing too much weight, and the metaphor is not really a metaphor, because in the Looms Below the social and the structural are not separate domains.

They do not have a word for "building" that is separate from the word for "plant." They do not have a word for "machine" that is separate from the word for "organ." The language reflects the reality: the distinction between the made and the grown, between the mechanical and the biological, does not exist here, and so the language does not create it.

## The Work

Every Loomer works. This is not ideology; it is thermodynamics. The settlement is a steady-state system, and steady-state systems require continuous input of energy and attention. There is no surplus large enough to support idleness, and no cultural framework that would make idleness desirable. The work is varied, and most Loomers do several kinds.

**Root-work** is the most common. The maintenance of the living architecture — trimming, redirecting, encouraging, repairing — is never finished. A Loomer might spend their morning in the upper reaches of the settlement, where the root-walls meet the soil, carefully excavating a section where the root-mass has grown too dense and is beginning to block a water channel. The work is slow, tactile, done mostly by feel. You cut back the outer tissue with a curved blade — the blades are made from the mineral-hardened root-tips, iron-rich and dense, and they hold an edge well in the wet environment — and then you feel inward, following the root's structure, finding the hollow spaces, clearing the channels, redirecting the growth with small applied pressures and strategic cuts that the root will respond to over the coming weeks. It is a conversation, not a command. The root will do what it will do. Your job is to make the outcome you want slightly more likely than the outcome you don't.

**Water-work** is the next most common. The channels must be kept clear. The cistern must be monitored. The flow must be balanced — too much water in one area and the root-walls become waterlogged and begin to rot; too little and the fungal panels die and the light goes out and the temperature drops and the whole local ecosystem destabilizes. Water-workers move through the settlement with their hands in the channels, feeling the flow, adjusting the small root-dams and vine-valves that direct the water's path. They can tell by the sound of a channel whether it is flowing correctly, and they can tell by the temperature of the water whether the turbines below are running at the right speed. The water-workers and the turbine-workers are in constant communication, a low-level ongoing negotiation between the biological and mechanical systems that keeps the settlement's metabolism in balance.

**Fungus-work** is the cultivation of the bioluminescent panels, the food-mushrooms, the medicinal cultures, the skin-colonies, the moss-beds, the mite-gardens. It is the most delicate work, because fungal systems are sensitive — to temperature, to moisture, to the chemistry of the water, to each other. A fungus-worker must understand not just the individual species they are cultivating but the relationships between species, the ways in which one culture supports or inhibits another, the slow complex ecology of the settlement's fungal life. They work in the dim light their own panels produce, tending sheets of living tissue that glow faintly in the dark, and their hands move with the particular gentleness of people who are handling something that is both fragile and alive.

**Machine-work** is the smallest category, because there are relatively few machines, but it is the most specialized. The looms, the foundry, the turbines, the belt-drives, the pistons — all of it must be maintained, and the maintenance is complicated by the environment. Metal corrodes in the constant moisture. Belt-drives stretch and slip on wet pulleys. The turbines' bearings wear and must be replaced, and the replacements are made from hardened root-material that is not as durable as metal but is available and can be grown on-site. The machine-workers are the ones who most often interact with the surface, because some materials — certain metals, certain lubricants, certain components that cannot be made from biological materials — must be traded for or scavenged. They are the Loomers most likely to have been above ground, and they carry a particular quality with them when they return, a slight disorientation, a sense of having been in a place that is too bright and too dry and too *still*.

## The Children

The children are everywhere, and they are treated the way children are treated in places where the environment is both dangerous and essential: with a vigilance that is so constant it has become invisible. A Lomer child learns to walk on the yielding root-surface before they learn to speak. They learn the feel of the walkways, the sound of the water, the smell of the fungal panels — the good smells and the bad smells, the smell of healthy growth and the smell of dangerous rot. They learn to keep their hands away from the foundry and the turbine channels. They learn to read the skin-colonies on their elders' arms. They learn the water-words.

They are inoculated with their first skin-cultures at around age three or four, a ceremony that is both practical and social — the child is being given their first layer of protection and their first mark of identity, and the community gathers to witness it, and the child's arms are painted with the spore-mixture in a pattern chosen by the family, and the pattern will grow and change over the child's lifetime, but the foundation is laid in this moment, and it matters.

The children play in the walkways and the corridors, and their games are the games of a three-dimensional environment — climbing, balancing, navigating the root-web with an agility that comes from never having known flat ground. They play games that involve water, channeling it through their fingers, building tiny dams in the gutter-chutes, learning through play the principles that will govern their working lives. They play games that involve the dark, navigating by touch and sound through sections where the fungal panels have been deliberately dimmed, learning to move confidently in the low-light environment that is their world.

They are not formally schooled — there are no schools, no classrooms, no curriculum — but they are continuously taught. Every Loomer a child encounters is a potential teacher, and the teaching is woven into the fabric of daily life. A child walking with a root-worker learns root-work. A child sitting by a water-channel with a water-worker learns water-work. The knowledge is transmitted horizontally, person to person, hand to hand, and it is remarkably complete — a Loomer who has grown to adulthood in the Looms Below knows, in a practical embodied way that no surface education system could replicate, how their world works.

## The Old

The old are the memory, and they are treated with a respect that is not sentimental but structural. The settlement changes slowly, and the old have seen changes that the young have not — a root-wall that shifted twenty years ago and was redirected, a water-channel that was rerouted after a partial collapse, a fungal panel that was replaced with a different species after the original strain succumbed to disease. These events are not written down. There are no records, no archives, no history books. The knowledge lives in the old, in their hands and their stories and their particular way of touching a root-wall and saying *this was not always like this*.

The old sit in the warm places — near the foundry, in the drying rooms, in the sections where the fungal panels produce extra heat as a byproduct of their metabolism — and they talk, and the young come to them, and the talking is the archive. The stories are not formal. They are woven into conversation, into the casual exchange of information that is the Loomers' primary mode of communication. An old root-worker, trimming a walkway railing, might mention that this particular root was trained by their grandmother, and the young root-worker listening learns something about the root's history that will inform how they work with it in the future. The knowledge is embedded in relationship, in the connection between person and place and person and person, and it is fragile in the way that all oral cultures are fragile — if the old die before the knowledge is passed on, it is gone.

But the old do not die quickly. The warmth, the humidity, the antifungal skin-colonies, the diet of mushroom and mite-paste and mineral-rich water — it is a healthy environment, in its way. The Loomers live long. And the old, in their last years, do a particular kind of work: they sit in the cistern-room, at the heart of the settlement, where the water is deepest and the bioluminescence is strongest, and they *listen*. They listen to the water, to the roots, to the turbines, to the slow creak and groan of the living architecture, and they compare what they hear to what they have heard for decades, and they notice the changes that are too subtle for younger ears, and they pass their observations on, and the settlement adjusts, and the equilibrium holds.

## The Ones Who Go Up

There are Loomers who go to the surface. Not many. The surface is not home, and the Loomers who go there do not stay long. But the settlement needs things that can only be found above — certain metals, certain materials, certain information — and so there are the climbers, the ones who ascend the root-stair to the forest floor and emerge into the light and the dry air and the vast open space that is, to a Lomer, profoundly wrong.

The climbers are changed by the experience. They come back with a look in their eyes that the other Loomers recognize and do not discuss. The surface is too bright. The air is too dry. The ground is too hard and too flat and too *dead* — the soil of the forest floor, which surface-dwellers think of as alive, seems to a Lomer like a desert, dry and still and empty of the constant biological activity that defines the Looms Below. The climbers come back and they stand in the cistern-room for a long time, letting the moisture settle on their skin, letting the dim fungal light recalibrate their eyes, letting the sound of the water and the roots and the turbines settle back into their bones.

They bring things back. Metal. News. Seeds, sometimes, from surface plants that might be coaxed downward, might be integrated into the settlement's living architecture. And they bring stories, which the old listen to carefully, because the surface is where the threats come from — not active threats, not enemies, but the slow threat of change, of the forest above being cut or burned or dried out by a shifting climate, and the water that feeds the Looms Below failing, and the whole system collapsing from the top down.

The climbers are respected and slightly feared. They have seen the outside. They know how fragile the Looms Below are, how dependent on the forest above, how vulnerable to changes they cannot control. This knowledge is necessary and it is heavy, and the climbers carry it the way the root-arches carry the weight of the ceiling — not comfortably, but with the slow patient strength of something that has been bearing a load for so long that it has grown into the burden.

## The Feeling

If you asked a Loomer to describe what it is like to live in the Looms Below, they would not describe the space. They would describe the *feeling* — the feeling of being inside something alive. The feeling of the walkway yielding slightly under your foot, not like a floor but like a step on a living thing that is aware of you, that is responding to your weight, that is growing around you and through you and with you. The feeling of the water on your skin, warm and mineral-rich, the same water that has been cycling through the settlement for longer than you have been alive, that has passed through a thousand roots and a thousand fungal panels and a thousand other skins before yours. The feeling of the sound — the constant omnipresent sound of water and growth and industry and the deep slow pulse of the turbines — not as noise but as *presence*, as the audible manifestation of the settlement's aliveness, the sound of a place that is doing what it does, continuously, without pause, the way a heart beats, the way lungs breathe.

They would tell you that it is not dark. It is dim, yes, but dim is not dark. Dim is the light of fungal panels and bioluminescent cisterns and the thin amber shafts from the surface, and it is enough, and it is beautiful, and it is the light they were born into and the light they will die in, and it is the light in which they see each other's faces and each other's skin-colonies and each other's hands, and it is sufficient.

They would tell you that it is not crowded. It is dense, yes, but dense is not crowded. Dense is the interweaving of root and water and fungus and machine and person, the tight knot of mutual dependence, the ecosystem that includes them and that they include, and it is not a limitation but a *richness*, a saturation of connection, a world in which nothing is wasted and nothing is alone and everything is doing work and everything is alive.

They would tell you that it is slow. And they would mean it as the highest compliment.
