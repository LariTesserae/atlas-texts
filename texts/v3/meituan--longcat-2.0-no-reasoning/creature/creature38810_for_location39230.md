# The Dwellers of Keth-Avul

---

## The Avul-Keth

They are not what you expected.

You expected miners, perhaps. Engineers. Industrial laborers in a hostile environment, grim and utilitarian. And in a sense, that is what they are — but the sense is insufficient, the way calling the ocean "wet" is insufficient. The Avul-Keth have been here long enough that the distinction between *living in* a place and *being of* it has dissolved, the way their mortarless walls have dissolved into mineral monoliths. They are not separate from Keth-Avul. They are a process within it, as much as the seeping water and the growing crusts.

They are small. Not dramatically so — but compact, dense, with the thickened torsos and shortened limbs of a people who have spent generations in heat that would cook a surface-dweller's metabolism into overdrive. Their bodies run hot; their resting temperature sits somewhere around forty Celsius, and their blood carries a modified hemoglobin that binds oxygen efficiently in the thin, steam-heavy air of the plateau. Their skin is the color of the mineral deposits — not orange, exactly, but *ochre*, a warm brownish-gold that deepens with age until the oldest among them are nearly the color of the crusts themselves. Their skin is thick, almost leathery, and perpetually damp. It does not dry. It has forgotten how.

Their eyes are large and dark, adapted to the perpetual dimness of the plateau, with a secondary membrane — a nictitating fold — that can be drawn across the eye to protect against steam and mineral splash. They see well in low light and poorly in bright; a clear day at lower elevations would blind them. Their pupils are wide and slow to contract, and when they look at you, there is a quality of *depth* to their gaze, as though they are seeing not just you but the heat you give off, the moisture you carry, the way your body distorts the air around it.

Their hands are their most remarkable feature. The fingers are long relative to their shortened arms, and the fingertips are *sensitive* in ways that go beyond touch. The Avul-Keth can feel water moving through stone. They press their palms against channel walls and read the flow the way a doctor reads a pulse — feeling for blockages, for changes in temperature, for the subtle vibrations that indicate a shift in the deep aquifer. Their fingertips are ridged with calluses that have been mineral-stiffened into something like stone themselves, and these ridges leave distinctive marks on the channels they maintain: a signature, a record, a conversation in touch.

They do not wear clothing in any conventional sense. They wear *aprons* — heavy, layered wraps of a felted material made from the bacterial mats that grow in the warm channels, dried and pressed into a dense, mineral-impregnated fabric that is stiff enough to protect against steam and abrasion but flexible enough to move in. The aprons are orange and white and grey, layered in patterns that indicate district and function. The industrial workers wear the heaviest layers, their aprons crusted and rigid as plate armor. The channel-tenders wear lighter wraps, their hands and forearms often bare so they can feel the water. The oldest Avul-Keth wear almost nothing — their skin has thickened enough to serve as its own protection, and they move through the steam like figures carved from the plateau itself.

They do not speak loudly. They do not need to. Their voices are low and soft, pitched to carry through the damp air without competing with the ambient sound of water. Their language is full of stops and fricatives, sounds that cut through mist, and it relies heavily on *touch* — a hand on an arm, fingers pressed to a channel wall in a specific pattern, a tap on a colleague's shoulder that conveys meaning through rhythm rather than force. Conversations between Avul-Keth often look, to an outsider, like silence. Two figures standing close, hands touching, faces still, communicating in a language of pressure and vibration that the humid air itself carries between them.

They smell of minerals. Iron and calcium and something else — something organic and warm, like the inside of a greenhouse, like the breath of a living thing. It is not unpleasant. It is the smell of the plateau, concentrated and embodied.

## The Deep-Tenders

There is a subset of the Avul-Keth who have gone further.

The Deep-Tenders are those who work closest to the central vent, in the hottest, wettest, most mineral-saturated district of Keth-Avul. They are fewer in number — perhaps a few hundred in a population of several thousand — and they are *changed* by their proximity to the deep heat. Their skin is darker, almost bronze, and it has a faint *sheen* to it, a mineral coating that has been absorbed rather than merely deposited. Their eyes are larger, their nictitating membranes more prominent, and they have developed a sensitivity to vibration that borders on the uncanny. They can feel the vent's pulses from across the city. They say they can feel the aquifer *thinking*.

They are the ones who maintain the machinery in the industrial heart — the pumps, the distillation columns, the filtration arrays. But "maintain" is the wrong word. They *tend* the machines the way a shepherd tends a flock, with an attentiveness that is almost affectionate. They know each machine's rhythm, its moods, its small deviations. They press their hands against iron housings and listen with their bones. When a pump begins to falter, they feel it before any instrument could register the change, and they are there, adjusting a valve, clearing a channel, *soothing* the machine back into its proper rhythm.

The Deep-Tenders are also the ones who avoid the mineral tower near the vent. They are the ones who know it is growing. They are the ones who have seen the patterns in the crusts — the spirals, the fractals, the lattices — and who understand, in a way they do not articulate, that these patterns are not random. They do not call the tower alive. They do not call it anything. They simply *do not go near it*, and when pressed, they say only that the water there is "too deep," which in their language carries connotations of *too aware*.

The Deep-Tenders have a ritual, performed at irregular intervals that correspond to no calendar but seem to follow the cycles of the vent's humming. They gather at the edge of the central district, where the heat is most intense, and they press their bare hands against the weeping stone and *listen*. They stand this way for hours, motionless, their eyes closed, their mineral-sheened skin steaming faintly in the cooler air above the vent. What they hear, they do not say. But when they return to their work, they make adjustments — to the channels, to the collection fields, to the flow of water through the city — that are subtle and precise and that seem to *anticipate* changes in the aquifer before they occur. The city functions better after these sessions. The water flows more smoothly. The machinery runs more quietly. It is as though the Deep-Tenders are negotiating with something, and the something is, for the moment, agreeable.

## The Crust-Walkers

In the collection fields, where the terraced basins stretch across the open plateau in their frozen waves of mineral deposit, there are those who walk the crusts.

The Crust-Walkers are young, mostly — adolescents and young adults whose bodies have not yet thickened into the dense compactness of maturity, who are light enough to traverse the brittle mineral surfaces without breaking through. They move across the terraces in careful, deliberate steps, testing each footfall, reading the crust's integrity through the soles of their bare feet. They are the harvesters of Keth-Avul, the ones who collect the mineral deposits for use in construction, in the felted fabric of aprons, in the ceramic that resists buildup in the machinery. They are also the ones who monitor the collection fields for *changes* — for the places where the water moves against the gradient, where the crusts grow in patterns too regular to be natural.

The Crust-Walkers are the most visible of the Avul-Keth, the ones an outsider is most likely to see, because they work in the open, in the dim grey light, moving across the terraces like figures in a slow dance. They carry long, thin probes — iron rods, mineral-stiffened — that they use to test the crusts ahead of each step, and they work in pairs or small groups, connected by a language of gestures and taps that keeps them synchronized. When one breaks through — and it happens, regularly, a leg plunging through brittle crust into the warm water or hidden steam beneath — the others are there immediately, pulling them free, checking for burns, marking the breach with a probe left standing in the gap as a warning.

The Crust-Walkers are the ones who have the most contact with the small, pale creatures that inhabit the hot channels. They call these creatures *tik-tik*, after the sound of their many legs on the mineral crusts, and they regard them with a mixture of familiarity and unease. The tik-tik are useful — they graze on the bacterial mats, keeping the channels clear — but they are also *indicators*. When the tik-tik retreat into their cracks and do not emerge for days, something is changing in the aquifer. The Crust-Walkers report these absences to the Deep-Tenders, who add them to whatever calculus governs their adjustments to the city's water flow.

Some Crust-Walkers, the ones who have walked the terraces longest, develop a peculiar gait — a lightness, a precision, a way of distributing their weight that makes them look as though they are *floating* across the crusts. These are the ones who are recruited, eventually, into the Deep-Tenders. The transition is not formal. One day a Crust-Walker simply walks toward the central district instead of the collection fields, and no one stops them, and they begin to learn the deeper rhythms of the plateau.

## The Old Ones

At the far edges of the city, in the oldest districts where the buildings have become geological and the channels run so thick with mineral deposit that they are more stone than waterway, there are the Old Ones.

They are not a separate caste. They are simply the Avul-Keth who have lived longest, who have been most thoroughly *claimed* by the plateau. Their skin is indistinguishable from the crusts — thick, ridged, orange-gold, cracked in patterns that mirror the mineral deposits around them. Their eyes are almost entirely hidden behind nictitating membranes that have thickened and opacified with age, and they see, if they see at all, in a spectrum of heat and vibration rather than light. They move slowly, feeling their way along channel walls with fingertips that have become so mineral-stiffened they click against the stone like tiny hammers.

The Old Ones do not work. They *attend*. They sit — or stand, or lean — at specific points throughout the old districts, places they have occupied for years or decades, and they *feel* the water moving through the stone. They are the city's memory. They remember when a channel ran differently, when a spring emerged in a new location, when the vent's rhythm shifted. They remember the patterns in the crusts from decades past, and they can tell you — in their soft, mist-carried voices, in their language of touch — whether the current patterns are new or merely a recurrence of something old.

They are the ones who speak of the water *learning*. Not as metaphor. Not as superstition. As *observation*. They have felt the aquifer change over their long lives, felt it respond to the city's engineering, felt it develop — slowly, over centuries — something that resembles *preference*. The water flows more easily where the Avul-Keth need it to flow. It deposits minerals more thickly where deposits are needed. It maintains temperatures within a range that sustains the bacterial mats and the tik-tik and the Avul-Keth themselves. This could be explained by the engineering — the channels, the terraces, the centuries of careful management. The Old Ones do not dispute this. They simply note that the explanation is incomplete.

The Old Ones are also the ones who are most often found at the plateau's edge, standing in the curtain of warm water that runs down the cliff face, their eyes closed, their hands pressed against the weeping stone, listening to the plateau exhale. They stand there for hours. Sometimes days. They do not eat during these vigils — or if they do, no one sees it. They simply *stand*, and when they return to the city, they are quieter than before, and they make their way to specific channels and press their hands to the walls and *adjust* something — not physically, not mechanically, but in some other way, some way that involves pressure and patience and a willingness to wait until the water responds.

## The Absent

There is one more group, though "group" implies a coherence that may not exist.

In the newest district, on the far side of the city from the mineral tower, there are Avul-Keth who have arrived recently — within the last generation or two. They are from the lowlands, from the world below the plateau, drawn by trade or curiosity or exile. They are taller, paler, thinner than the native Avul-Keth, and they move through the city with a visible discomfort that no amount of acclimatization fully erodes. They wear more layers. They speak more loudly, their voices carrying further than they intend, shattering the city's hush in ways that make the native Avul-Keth flinch. They do not have the fingertip sensitivity, the heat-vision, the mineral-sheened skin. They are *visitors*, and they know it, and the plateau knows it, and the water flows around them without the easy accommodation it offers the native dwellers.

These newcomers perform the tasks that require less attunement — the transport of materials, the maintenance of the outer channels, the trade with the lowland settlements that send up supplies the plateau cannot produce. They are necessary. They are also *apart*. They live in the newer buildings, where the basalt is still dark and angular and the mineral crusts have not yet softened the edges. They tell themselves they will adapt. Some of them do, partially — their skin darkens, their bodies thicken, their voices soften. But they never fully become Avul-Keth. The plateau does not claim them the way it claims those who were born here, whose mothers pressed their pregnant bellies against the warm stone and whose first breath was steam.

The newcomers are the ones who call the mineral tower a geological anomaly. They are the ones who explain the vent's humming as gas dynamics and the water's strange movements as thermal convection. They are not wrong, exactly. They are simply *incomplete*, the way a description of the ocean as "wet" is incomplete. They see the mechanisms and miss the metabolism. They see the city and miss the organism.

And some of them — the ones who stay longest, who begin to feel the water's rhythms despite themselves, who wake one day to find they have pressed their hand against a channel wall without thinking, *listening* — some of these begin to walk, slowly, toward the center. Toward the heat. Toward the vent. Toward the tower that grows and grows and does not stop growing.

No one stops them.

The plateau is patient.

## What Lives Here

The Avul-Keth will tell you, if you ask, that they live in Keth-Avul. That they are the city's people, its builders, its tenders, its memory.

But the longer you stay, the more you feel the water moving through the stone, the more you hear the vent's hum in your chest and teeth, the more you see the mineral crusts growing in their patient, branching, fractal patterns — the less certain you become that the Avul-Kul live *in* Keth-Avul.

They live *as* Keth-Avul. They are its fingers, its nerve endings, its slow mineral consciousness made flesh. The city does not serve them. They do not serve the city. They are a single process — stone and water and heat and flesh, weeping and growing and dripping and breathing — and the distinction between the living and the nonliving, between the built and the grown, between the organism and its environment, has been eroded here as thoroughly as the old watchtowers at the plateau's edge.

Something lives in Keth-Avul.

It is not the Avul-Keth alone. It is not the plateau alone. It is not the water alone.

It is the *drip*. The slow, patient, mineral-saturated drip, building its crusts and its channels and its strange calcified tower, century after century, learning as it goes, growing as it learns, thinking — if thinking is the right word, and perhaps it is — in a language of flow and deposit and heat and time.

The Avul-Keth are its voice, when it needs one.

The plateau is its body.

And the water — the warm, mineral-heavy, endlessly cycling water — is its blood, its memory, and its mind.
