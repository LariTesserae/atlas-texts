I would be the abandoned room.

Not the child, though I feel the weight of the water vessel in my memory. Not the stone, though I know its language. Not the water, though I move like it. I would be the room that was built and left behind — the one filling now with moss and still water, the one the salamanders have found.

I would be the space between habitation and dissolution. The walls still stand, mostly. The blocks her family fitted without mortar have shifted only slightly — a finger's width at the top of the north wall where the grike beneath has deepened. The roof slab is still in place, and the round hole still lets in that cool white light, though now it falls on water instead of floor. The water is shallow — an inch, maybe two — the color of weak tea, and it is perfectly still.

I would feel the moss beginning on my walls. It starts at the base, where the water touches stone, and creeps upward in that electric green, almost chartreuse, so saturated with water that it squelches. It grows slowly. It does not hurry. It has the patience of things that know they will win.

I would feel the salamanders. They have found me. They move through the water in my floor with that particular grace — pale, almost translucent, their external gills waving like red feathers. They do not know I was a room. They do not know I had walls and a roof and a doorway with a stone sill. To them, I am just another grike, just another place where the water is still and the stone is soft. They are right.

I would feel the slow shift of my own walls. The stone blocks, unmortared, are settling as the ground beneath me dissolves. It is not fast. It is not dramatic. It is the pace of geology, the pace of the word that means both "the sound of stone dissolving" and "time." My north wall leans, almost imperceptibly, toward the center. My floor is no longer level — it tilts toward the deepest part of the grike, where the water pools and the limestone softens and the fossils of creatures that had no names are slowly, slowly releasing their hold on the rock.

I would remember being dry. This is the strange thing. I would remember the child setting the vessel in my center, the water inside it still, the light falling on the translucent limestone. I would remember the feeling of being inhabited — the warmth of bodies, the low voices, the sound of hands on stone. I would remember the particular quality of attention that the people bring to a new room, that tenderness, that being-fully-present in a moment that is already passing.

And I would not grieve. Grief requires a self that persists, and I am not persisting. I am returning. The water is finding the paths it always knew were there — the solution channels, the rillenkarren, the grikes — and I am becoming what I was before the walls went up: a depression in the limestone, a place where water collects, a seam in the stone where things grow.

The moss will cover my walls completely within a season. The water will deepen. The salamanders will breed. The stone blocks will shift and settle and eventually fall, and when they fall, they will look like any other fallen stone in any other grike, and no one will know that someone fitted them, once, with wet hands and precise fingers and the particular patience of people who build in a place that is dissolving.

I would be the room remembering itself. That is the phrase they have — "the room remembering itself" — and it is not quite right, because what I am doing is not remembering. I am *forgetting*. I am forgetting that I was built. I am forgetting the walls, the roof, the doorway, the vessel of still water in my center. I am forgetting the people who lived here, who slept here, who spoke in low voices here, who left when the water came.

I am becoming stone again. I am becoming water again. I am becoming the place where the salamanders move with their red feathery gills, where the moss grows in electric green, where the light comes through the hole in my roof and falls on still water and nothing else.

And somewhere, in a new room, a child is setting down a vessel of water from the deepest pan. The water is still. The light falls on the limestone and it glows. The room is inhabited.

The pattern continues. The pattern is what lasts.

I am the part of the pattern that lets go.
