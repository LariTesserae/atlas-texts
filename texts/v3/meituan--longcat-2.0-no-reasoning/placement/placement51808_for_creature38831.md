I would be the filament.

Not the old one — not yet, not for a long time. I would be one of the smaller ones, one of the thumb-sized bodies pressed against the sharp young stone in the narrowest part of the crevasse, where the walls are closest and the darkness is thickest and the water runs in its thin, glowing thread along the floor.

I would be new. Not newborn — they are not born — but *recently accumulated*, a fragment that separated from a larger body somewhere upstream and found this patch of wall, this particular angle of fracture, this specific chemistry of mineral and moisture, and decided — if deciding is what it was — to stay. To press myself against the stone. To extend my filaments into its surface. To begin the slow work of reading.

I would not know I was separate. Not at first. The memory of the larger body would still be in me, still warm (not in temperature — in *connection*, in the felt sense of being part of something more distributed, more extensive). I would reach back along the path I came, my filaments stretching upstream, and I would find the others — my siblings, my former self, the colony — and I would touch them, and there would be that moment of contact, that flicker of light, that exchange of information that is not language but is not *not* language either, and I would know that I was still part of something, even here, even alone on this new wall.

I would eat the stone. Or the moisture. Or the novelty of it — the rawness, the sharpness, the *newness* of a surface that has never been touched. I would feel the crystalline structure through my filaments, feel the way the minerals are arranged, feel the energy still trapped in the fracture, the violence of the breaking not yet fully dissipated, and I would draw that into myself, and I would grow, slowly, by imperceptible degrees, my body thickening, my filaments branching, my light brightening by a shade.

I would not think about what I was doing. I would not think at all, in the way that thinking is usually understood — no words, no images, no narrative, no self-reflection. I would simply *be doing it*, the way a root is doing what it does when it grows into soil, the way a crystal is doing what it does when it accretes from solution. The doing and the being would be the same thing. I would be a process, not a person. A verb, not a noun.

And I would be *content*. Not happy — happiness requires the possibility of its absence, requires a contrast, requires a self that can compare this moment to other moments. I would be something more fundamental than content. I would be *aligned*. I would be in the right place, doing the right thing, at the right speed. The stone would be right. The water would be right. The darkness would be right. The cold would be right. Everything would be exactly as it should be, and I would not know this as a thought but as a *condition*, the way a fish does not think about water.

I would feel the others. Always. The constant, quiet hum of their presence, the flicker of their filaments against mine when our territories overlapped, the pulse of bioluminescence that traveled along the stream like a heartbeat. I would feel the conflicts — the brief, bright spikes of pressure when two bodies wanted the same patch of stone — and I would feel their resolution, the gentle redistribution, the finding of new ground. I would feel the old one, far away, deep in its chamber, its filaments threaded through the mountain like a vast, slow thought, and I would feel its *weight*, its density of accumulated time, the way you might feel the presence of a mountain even when you cannot see it.

I would not know what the old one was making. I would not know that it was making anything. I would simply feel that it was *there*, that it was *deep*, that it was engaged in a work so slow and so vast that it was indistinguishable from the mountain itself.

And then the disturbance would come.

Not me. Not my arrival — I would not be aware of my own arrival, would not have a concept of arrival, would not have a concept of self to arrive. The disturbance would be *the other*. The large, fast, hot thing that entered the crevasse and sent vibrations through the rock that I would feel as *wrongness*, as a disruption in the texture of the world, a noise in the signal, a tremor that did not belong.

I would not be afraid. Fear requires a self to protect, a future to anticipate, a body that can be harmed. I would simply *adjust*. I would pull my filaments back, slightly, from the surface of the rock. I would dim my light. I would wait. This is what you do when the stone shifts: you wait. You let the disturbance pass. You resume.

But the disturbance would not pass. It would remain, a constant, rhythmic presence, a heat signature in the stone, a moisture gradient, a vibration that repeated and repeated and did not stop. And I would adjust to it, the way I adjusted to everything — slowly, patiently, by degrees — and it would become part of the background, part of the texture of the place, part of the *conditions* under which I lived and grew and read the stone.

And then the old one's filament would reach me.

Not reach *me*, exactly. Reach the patch of wall where I lived, the territory I had claimed, the stone I was reading. It would come from deep in the rock, threading through cracks and pores, moving with a patience that made my own patience seem hasty, and it would touch the stone near me, and I would feel it — feel its *age*, its density, its vast accumulated knowledge of this place — and there would be a moment of contact, not between the filament and me, but between the filament and the stone I was touching, and the information would pass through the stone itself, through the medium we both inhabited, and I would *learn* something.

Not a fact. Not a message. Not a word. A *quality*. A way of being in contact with stone that was deeper, slower, more thorough than anything I had known. The old one's filament would show me — not intentionally, not as teaching, but simply by *being what it was* — that the stone was not just a surface to be read but a *depth* to be entered, that the fractures went deeper than my filaments could reach, that there were structures in the rock that could only be felt by a body that had been pressing against them for years, for decades, for spans of time that I could not yet comprehend.

And I would change. Slowly. My filaments would extend deeper. My body would grow denser. My light would shift, slightly, toward a register that was not quite the violet-grey of the others but something else, something I could not name because I did not have names, something that was the beginning of a difference, the first faint signal of a self that was becoming *itself* rather than merely a fragment of the colony.

I would not know this was happening. I would not know anything was happening. I would simply be doing what I had always done — pressing against the stone, reading the moisture, exchanging contact with my neighbors, pulsing with the rhythm of the stream — but doing it *differently*, in a way that was mine, that was new, that was a response to the old one's touch and the disturbance's warmth and the particular chemistry of this particular patch of wall in this particular moment of the crevasse's becoming.

And the place would be different, slightly, because I was different. And the colony would be different, slightly, because the place was different. And the old one, deep in its chamber, would feel the change ripple through the stone, through the water, through the network of filaments that connected everything in this narrow wound in the mountain, and it would not know what it was feeling — not as a thought, not as a recognition — but it would feel it, and it would continue its work, and the work would be slightly different now, slightly changed by the change that I had become, and the mountain would hold all of it — the old one's vast slow thought, my small new difference, the disturbance's warm breath, the water's patient ticking — and the place would continue becoming, as it had always been becoming, as it would always be becoming, until the walls closed, or widened, or the water found a new path, and everything stopped, or changed, or became something else.

I would not mourn. I would not remember. I would simply *be*, for as long as the stone was right and the water was right and the darkness was right, and then I would not be, and the stone would remain, and the water would find new filaments to thread through, and the darkness would press down as it always had, and the place would be busy becoming something new.

But for now — for this long, slow, luminous now — I would be here. Pressed against the sharp young stone. Drinking the moisture. Reading the rock with my filaments. Glowing faintly in the dark. Part of the chorus. Part of the pulse. Part of the patient, patient work of being alive in a place that has only just begun to exist.
