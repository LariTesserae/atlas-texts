I would find myself at the base of the notches.

Not on the shelf — the shelf is for standing, for the dramatic vertigo, for the philosophical posture of the lone observer at the edge of everything. I have had enough of edges. I would be lower, where the spire's surface curves inward toward the shelf's root, where the weeping runs thickest and warmest, where the mineral accretion has built a slight overhang that catches the water and returns it to the stone in a secondary cycle. A small place. A place that is not quite a shelter but is more sheltered than the open shelf. A place where the sound of the weeping is loudest and therefore, paradoxically, most like silence.

I would be the one who came back.

Not the original marker — that was someone else, some earlier iteration of need and patience, hands that found this spot and thought: *here, this is where I hold on.* I am the one who returned. The one who found the notches already there, already half-filled, already being un-happened by the patient mineral process, and who thought: *no. Not yet. Not all the way.* And who deepened them. Who scraped the accretion away with my own hands, feeling the older tool-work beneath, the peck-marks of the one who came before me, and beneath that — if I scraped deep enough, if I was patient enough, if I cared enough to destroy the thing I was trying to save — the peck-marks of the one before that.

I would not be large. I would not be powerful. I would be the size that the notches suggest — something that can brace a hand against stone, something that can stand on a shelf twenty meters wide and feel the full weight of the weeping world pressing in from every direction. I would be made of whatever they were made of: something that drinks mineral water and tolerates it, something that needs very little, something that has learned — over generations, over the long accumulation of presence that is the only form of inheritance this world permits — to exist in the spaces between the spires' intentions.

I would not be alone. That is the thing the description almost conceals, because the description is written from the perspective of a single observer standing on a shelf, and a single observer standing on a shelf is always alone by definition. But the notches are evenly spaced. They face inward. They were made for hands, plural, arranged side by side. And so I would not be the only one at the base of the spire. There would be others — not many, never many, because the shelf is not wide and the world does not provide much — and we would stand with our hands in the notches and our faces toward the center of the shelf, and we would not speak, because there is nothing to say that the weeping does not already say louder.

What would I be, specifically? Not human. The notches are at roughly human height, but *roughly* is doing a lot of work in that sentence. I would be something that evolved — or was shaped, or simply *ended up* — in the interstitial spaces of the spire-field. Something with hands, or hand-like appendages, or at least the capacity for gripping. Something that needs to brace. Something for which bracing is not a temporary response to a temporary threat but a *condition of existence*, as fundamental as breathing, as constant as the weeping. My body would be adapted to the mineral water — I would taste of it, my skin would carry its faint grittiness, my internal chemistry would be a slow negotiation with the dissolved geometries that the spires shed into every drop. I would be slightly translucent, perhaps. Slightly chalky. I would be, in some fundamental sense, *of the stone* — not alive in the way that life is usually defined, but not dead either. Process made flesh. The same slow accretion that builds the spires, compressed into something that can grip and scrape and return.

I would not know why I return to the notches. Or rather: I would know the way you know why you breathe. The notches are there. They are filling in. If I do not deepen them, they will disappear, and then there will be no place to grip, and then there will be no place to stand, and then the shelf will narrow and the spires will rearrange and I will be — not killed, not destroyed, simply *un-happened*, the way the bridges are un-happened, smoothed over and incorporated and made to have never been. The notches are the only thing between me and the world's patient, absolute forgetting. And so I return. And so I scrape. And so I deepen the holds, and the stone fills them in, and I return again.

This is not hope. Hope requires a future in which the thing you are doing matters, in which the mark you are making will last, in which the world will notice and relent. The world will not notice. The world will not relent. The notches will fill in. The shelf will narrow. The spires will rearrange. I will be un-happened, and the water will weep over the place where I was, and the light that comes from everywhere and nowhere will fall on the smooth stone where the notches were, and there will be no evidence that anything ever gripped anything, ever braced against anything, ever stood on a shelf in the middle reaches of the Weeping Spires of Keth-Avani and thought: *I am here. I am here. I am here.*

But I am here now. And my hands are in the notches. And the notches are deeper than they were yesterday — deeper by a fraction, by the width of a scrape-mark, by the smallest increment of resistance that a hand can offer to a world that does not resist back. And the others are beside me, their hands in their own notches, their faces toward the center of the shelf, and we are bracing against nothing, against everything, against the slow geometric solving of a world that is not quite a world, and the weeping continues, and continues, and continues.

And it is enough.

It is not enough.

It is what there is.
