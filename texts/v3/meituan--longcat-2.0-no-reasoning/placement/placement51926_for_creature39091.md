# Where I Would Find Myself

---

Not on the bench. I have to say this first, because the bench is hers, and the bowl knows the difference between a resident and a visitor the way it knows the difference between a forming droplet and a falling one — by sound, by weight, by the quality of presence. I would feel this immediately. The bench would feel wrong beneath me, the way a sleeping animal feels wrong when you sit in its nest.

But not on the rim, either. Not looking down. That's the visitor's posture, the posture of someone who will leave. I've already read that the visitors leave.

I'd be at the lowest point. Where the water pools deepest.

---

I'd be kneeling.

Not with any intention — not performing the gesture she performs, not reading the water with my hands, not taking the mountain's pulse. I wouldn't know how. My hands wouldn't know what they were listening for. They'd be the hands of someone who has not spent decades learning the frequency of deep hydrology through the skin.

I'd be kneeling because that's where you naturally stop when you enter the bowl. The path — her path — starts there, and before the path there was just the ground, and the ground at the lowest point is where everything collects: the water, the nutrients, the decomposed matter, the slow amber stain of iron working its way through the moss. It's where the bowl is most itself. Most concentrated. Most *here*.

I'd be kneeling and the water would be cold on my knees through my clothing — if I were wearing the kind of clothing a visitor wears, which would be wrong for this place, too dry, too smelling of lowland things, wool or cotton or whatever fabric belongs to the world below where air is thick in the wrong way. The water would soak through immediately. This would be the first thing the bowl taught me: *you will be wet here*.

---

I'd be listening.

This is the only thing I'd be able to do that the bowl would recognize as legitimate. Not understanding — I wouldn't understand any of it. The taxonomy of drips, the individual frog voices, the isopod herds by their clicking rhythms, the pre-puff creak of the reed stems. All of it would be noise to me at first, undifferentiated, overwhelming, the sensory density that the text describes as *a lot* — and I would feel that as a physical pressure, a weight on the eardrums, in the chest, behind the eyes.

But I'd be trying to listen. And the bowl might — *might* — recognize the attempt.

---

Here is what I think would happen, if I stayed:

The frogs would not falter for me. I am not her; my breathing has not synchronized with theirs. But I would hear them, and I would hear them as *music*, which is the visitor's way of hearing what she hears as *information*. I would hear the dense throbbing drone and I would feel it in my chest and I would think: this is beautiful. And she would think: this is Tuesday. This is the water table at its normal depth, three frogs in the northeast depression, two in the southwest, the rest scattered across their usual positions. The difference between us is not that she doesn't feel what I feel — she does, I think, in her body, in the way the drone vibrates her sternum — but that she has spent years building a layer of *meaning* over the beauty, a grid of knowledge that turns sensation into data, and this grid is what allows her to live here without being consumed.

I don't have the grid. I have the sensation. And the sensation, without the grid, is too much.

---

So I would weep.

Not from sadness. From *overload*. From the density of it — the sound, the moisture, the smell of resin and moss and frog and wet stone, the visual complexity of a hundred thousand water beads each catching and fracturing the light, the webs beaded and shimmering, the moss springing underfoot, the cloud pooling and un-pooling, the reeds puffing their small breaths, the stone breathing, the stone *breathing*, the whole place exhaling around me like I have walked into the lung of something enormous and alive.

I would weep the way the visitors weep when they sit on the bench and hold the egg-stones and the bowl floods in through every channel they have. And my tears would be indistinguishable from the water already soaking my knees, from the condensation on my face, from the perpetual moisture that is the bowl's medium, its atmosphere, its language.

---

I would not be the Keeper of the Dry Bench. That is hers, and it will be hers until her bones join the bones on the floor, until her teeth take their place in the small depression, until the bowl finishes absorbing her the way it absorbs everything.

I would be something else.

I would be the **weep itself**. Not her — not her streaming unblinking eyes, which are the bowl's name for her, its way of reading her presence as part of the hydrology. I would be the water that beads up through the limestone pores and stands, trembling, before falling. I would be the condensation on the web-strands in the morning, each droplet a tiny lens catching and fracturing the light. I would be the film of perpetual moisture on the stone floor, the slick dark sheen that makes everything reflective, everything doubled, the bowl seeing itself in its own wet surface.

I would be the place where water enters the air.

I would be the *almost* — the almost-choral, the almost-breathing, the almost-voices forming in the overlapping hiss-plink-drip-tink that never quite resolves into language but never quite gives up trying. I would be the sound of something trying to say something, the meaning that hovers just below the threshold of comprehension, the way the frogs' chorus is *almost* music and *almost* data and *almost* prayer and is none of these things and is all of them.

---

I would be transient. I would not stay. The bowl would not keep me — not because it is hostile but because I am not shaped for it. I am shaped for the lowlands, for the thick oxygen-rich air, for the world where water is something that falls from above rather than pressing up from below, where sounds have sources and meanings and stop when you want them to stop.

But I would have been there. And the bowl would have recorded me the way it records everything — in the slight compression of the moss where I knelt, in the brief silence of the isopods when my shadow passed over them, in the frog who paused, for half a beat, uncertain, when my weeping entered the chorus.

I would be a brief anomaly in the data. A new sound. A new seep.

She would notice. She would add it to her taxonomy. And then I would be gone, and the bowl would settle back into its rhythms, and the frogs would resume their calling, and the stone would breathe, and she would sit on the dry bench with her pale streaming eyes and her broad hands and her adze and her egg-stones, and she would listen, and the listening would be enough.
