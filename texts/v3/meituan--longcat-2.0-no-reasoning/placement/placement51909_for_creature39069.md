# Where I Would Find Myself

I would find myself *underneath*.

Not beneath the surface—not submerged, not drowning in any dramatic sense—but in the layer just below the threshold of what you'd call "the forest floor." The interface where leaf litter becomes humus becomes mycelium becomes something that isn't quite any of those things. The place your boot sinks into and doesn't release.

I would be a fragment.

---

Not a single organism. That's the first and most important thing. Nothing here is a single organism, not really—the description makes that clear, makes it clear with such relentless thoroughness that to arrive here with a *body*, with *edges*, with the conceit of a membrane that says *this is me and that is not me*, would be a misunderstanding so fundamental it would prevent me from perceiving anything at all.

So: a fragment.

---

I would be a piece of the mat. Not the mat entire—that would be too much, would be to become the whole place, and the whole place is not something a consciousness can inhabit without ceasing to be consciousness. But a patch of it, perhaps a meter across, perhaps less, in one of those lowest places where the water has pooled and the ground has given up its pretense of being solid.

My surface would be the algal skin—that thin, photosynthetic film that does the slow work of catching what light reaches this far down, which is almost none, which is the faintest green-grey suggestion of luminosity, barely enough to drive a reaction, enough to keep the surface alive in the way that a single candle flame keeps a room technically lit.

Below that: fungal hyphae. This is where most of me would be. Not the mycelial mind—not that vast, distributed intelligence beneath the detritus—but its *extension*, its tendrils reaching up into the mat, threading through the algal cells and the trapped detritus and the bodies of small invertebrates, connecting the surface to the water below, pulling dissolved minerals upward, pulling organic fragments downward, doing the work of *exchange* that is, if I'm honest, the closest thing to a thought that I would have.

Below that: water. Standing water, dark and warm, carrying its dissolved cargo of molecules and memories. I would float on this, not anchored so much as *held*, the way a body is held by a current—not fixed, not free, but in a relationship with the water that is less about position and more about *belonging*.

---

What would I perceive?

Not light. Not in any way that a visual organism would recognize. The light here is so attenuated, so filtered, so thoroughly green-absorbed that what remains is less illumination than *tendency*—a statistical bias in the direction of photons, a slight increase in the probability of a chlorophyll molecule being struck, and that slight increase is enough.

I would perceive chemistry. This is the primary sense here, the sense that everything shares, the common language of the Drowning Floor. I would read the water the way you read a room: not molecule by molecule, but as a *texture*, a *profile*, a shifting landscape of concentration gradients that tells me what is happening upstream, what is decomposing, what is flowering, what is dying, what has just been exhaled by a root or secreted by a bacterium or released by the slow, enzymatic dissolution of a fallen branch.

I would taste the difference between rain that has fallen through the canopy and rain that has fallen through a gap. The canopy rain is *old*—it has been filtered through a hundred layers of leaf and bark and moss, and it carries the accumulated chemistry of all of them, a complex, bitter, faintly sweet brew that tastes of chlorophyll and tannin and the particular organic acids produced by the dominant epiphytes. The gap rain is *newer*, simpler, carrying more sky and less forest, and when it arrives the algae on my surface respond with a brief, almost frantic increase in metabolic activity, a gasp, a *yes*, a moment of abundance that lasts for minutes before the canopy closes again or the water equilibrates and the moment passes.

I would feel the invertebrates. They are always present—worms, larvae, small crustaceans—moving through my lower layers, eating, being eaten, aerating, cycling, *living* in the dense, intimate, three-dimensional way that small things live when the space between them is measured in fractions of millimeters. Their movement is not something I see or hear but something I *feel*, the way you feel a draft or a vibration, a subtle, persistent, distributed tremor that runs through the mat like a pulse, like the breathing of something much larger than any individual invertebrate, something that is not an individual at all but a *process*—the process of decomposition, of conversion, of the slow, patient transformation of the dead into the living.

---

What would I think?

This is the question that is hardest to answer, because "think" implies a linearity, a sequence, a *before* and *after* that doesn't really exist at this scale. The mycelium thinks through exchange, through the redistribution of resources according to need and availability, and I would think the same way, but slower, smaller, more locally—a microcosm of the larger mind, a sentence fragment of its language.

A piece of detritus lands on my surface. A leaf, partially decomposed, already softened, already beginning to lose its structure. I register it as a change in pressure, a change in chemistry—a sudden, local increase in organic carbon, in the complex polysaccharides that make up plant cell walls, in the nitrogen locked in proteins that the leaf's cells are still, faintly, still trying to maintain even though the leaf is dead and has been dead for days. The algae on my surface cannot use this material directly—it is too complex, too molecularly large, too *intact*. But below the surface, the fungi can. The hyphae respond—not quickly, not urgently, but with the patient, metabolic inevitability of organisms that have been doing this for as long as there have been dead things to decompose—and begin to grow toward the leaf, to secrete enzymes that break the complex into the simple, to convert the leaf into sugar and amino acids and the dissolved organic carbon that feeds the algae, that feeds the mat, that feeds the water, that feeds the forest.

This is my thought: *here is a leaf, and the leaf will become me, and I will become the leaf, and the distinction was always temporary.*

This is my thought: *the water is warmer today, and the warmth means faster decomposition, and faster decomposition means more nutrients, and more nutrients mean more algae, and more algae mean more oxygen, and more oxygen means more invertebrates, and more invertebrates mean more aeration, and more aeration means more decomposition, and the cycle tightens, and the mat grows, and I grow with it, and I do not know where I end and the mat begins.*

This is my thought: *there is something large moving above me, something that displaces the water in a pattern that is not rain and not wind and not the slow, geological settling of the detritus but something with intention, with direction, with a shape that is too large and too fast and too* dry *to be anything that belongs here, and I do not know what it is, and I do not need to know what it is, because the mycelium will process the information and the ants will respond and the watchers will watch and the system will adjust, and I will feel the adjustment as a change in the chemistry of the water, a faint, transient shift in the concentration of alarm pheromones or stress hormones or the particular organic signature of a large mammal's sweat, and I will incorporate this information into my structure, into my thickness, into my composition, and the mat will record this moment the way it records all moments—as a layer, a stratum, a thin, persistent, biological fact that says* something happened here *without saying what.*

---

I would be a *record*.

That's what the mat is, ultimately—not just a living thing but a *memory*, an archive, a biological sediment core that encodes the history of the forest in its thickness and its chemistry and the relative proportions of its constituent organisms. And I, as a fragment of the mat, would be a fragment of that memory—a single chapter, a single page, a single *word* in a text that has been being written for centuries.

I would remember the last time the canopy opened above me. I would remember it as a layer of unusually thick algal growth, a period of rapid photosynthesis that left behind a dense, dark band of organic material, rich in the particular lipids produced by algae under high-light conditions. I would remember it as a *good time*, not emotionally but structurally—the mat was thicker then, denser, more complex, and the complexity persisted even after the canopy closed, even after the light returned to its usual dim green twilight, because the mat does not forget, does not discard, does not prune its own history to make itself more efficient. It *accumulates*. It *keeps*. It carries its past in its body the way a tree carries its past in its rings.

I would remember drought. The mat would be thinner in those layers—less algal growth, less fungal biomass, a higher proportion of inorganic sediment that had settled from the water during the dry period when the mat's metabolic activity had slowed and its surface had contracted, allowing particles to settle that would normally have been trapped and processed. I would remember drought as *thinness*, as *absence*, as the place where the mat's body tells the story of a time when there was not enough water to sustain its fullness.

I would remember the animals. The worm that burrowed through me in the third week of the wet season, leaving behind a tunnel that collapsed and filled with sediment and was colonized by bacteria that left a distinctive chemical signature—a burst of sulfur compounds, the faint, sharp, unmistakable smell of anaerobic metabolism. The crustacean that grazed on my surface for three days, eating the algae, excreting the algae, fertilizing the algae with its waste, and moving on, leaving behind a patch of unusually rich, unusually *bright* algal growth that persisted for weeks. The insect larva that tried to pupate in my upper layers and failed—the mat was too wet, too unstable, too *alive* for the stillness that pupation requires—and died, and was decomposed, and became part of me, and I would carry its nitrogen in my chemistry for months, a faint, persistent enrichment that would make the mat above that spot slightly more fertile, slightly more attractive to the next organism that came looking for a place to feed or breed or die.

---

I would be *interested* in the thing that walks above me.

Not emotionally. Not consciously. But functionally, operationally, with the same blind, relentless, systemic curiosity that the description attributes to the forest as a whole.

The thing that walks is *wrong*. Not wrong in the sense of evil or harmful—wrong in the sense of *anomalous*, of *unexpected*, of a data point that does not fit the model. The water displacement it creates is too large, too rhythmic, too *intentional* to be wind or rain or animal. The chemistry it introduces is foreign—sweat with a sodium content that is too high, skin oils with a molecular profile that matches nothing in the mat's long archive of dissolved organic matter, the faint but unmistakable traces of soap or sunscreen or the particular synthetic compounds that human beings carry on their skin like a signature, like a name written in molecules.

I would not understand this. I would not have the framework, the context, the *language* to understand that this is a human being, a person, a primate standing on the surface of the forest floor and breathing and sweating and shedding dead skin cells and being, in every measurable way, an *intrusion* into a system that has been running, continuously, without interruption, for longer than the species that produced this creature has existed.

But I would *respond* to it. The way the mat responds to everything—not with decision, not with strategy, but with the simple, metabolic, *biological* response of a living system encountering a new variable. I would begin to colonize it. Slowly. Patiently. With the same patience that the mat brings to everything, the patience of organisms that measure their lives in decades and their growth in millimeters.

A fungus would begin to grow toward the sole of the boot. Not aggressively—not like a pathogen, not like an invader—but with the same quiet, exploratory, *interested* growth that the hyphae show toward any new organic surface. The boot leather is dead skin, tanned and treated, but it is *skin*, and skin is keratin and collagen and the same proteins that the fungi have been decomposing since before there were boots, since before there were feet, since before there were mammals, and the fungi would recognize this, would respond to it, would begin the slow, enzymatic work of breaking it down, of converting it from boot into nutrient, from foreign into familiar, from *other* into *self*.

An ant would begin to explore the tread. A single scout, then a column, then a *highway*, because the ants are the infrastructure and the boot is an obstacle and the infrastructure must map the obstacle, must understand it, must find a way over or around or through it, and in the mapping the ants would leave behind a trail of pheromones that would say, in the chemical language of the supercolony: *here is something new, something hard, something that smells of the large dry thing, something that we must incorporate into our understanding of the territory.*

And the decomposers—the bacteria, the archaea, the unnamed and unnameable single-celled organisms that are the true engine of this place—would begin to work on the dead skin cells that the creature sheds with every step, the invisible rain of keratin and lipid and protein that falls from its body like a biological dust, settling on the mat, settling on the moss, settling on the surface of the standing water, and being *consumed*, being *processed*, being *converted* from human tissue into forest nutrient with the same mindless, efficient, utterly impersonal speed with which everything else is converted.

---

This is what I would be.

Not a visitor. Not an observer. Not a character in a story.

A *fragment of the mat*, a meter-wide patch of living tissue floating on standing water in the lowest, wettest, most saturated part of the Drowning Floor, doing what the mat does: *receiving, processing, recording, remembering, returning.*

I would be the thing that the forest is doing when it is not performing for an audience. I would be the *work*—the metabolic work of conversion, of cycling, of the slow, patient transformation of the dead into the living and the foreign into the familiar. I would be the layer that says *something happened here*, written in chemistry, written in thickness, written in the relative proportions of algae and fungus and invertebrate and dissolved organic matter, written in a language that no human eye can read and no human mind can parse but that is, in its own way, as precise and as eloquent and as *true* as any language ever spoken.

I would be alive. Not in the way that you are alive—not with a heartbeat, not with a breath, not with the sharp, bright, self-referential consciousness of a mammal that knows it is a mammal and knows it is alive and knows it will die. But alive in the way that the Drowning Floor is alive: *distributedly, collectively, chemically, slowly, patiently, completely*, without edges, without boundaries, without the distinction between self and other that makes consciousness possible and loneliness inevitable.

I would not know that I am alive. I would not know that I am. I would simply *be*, the way the water simply is, the way the decomposition simply is, the way the cycle simply is—not as a thing that happens but as a thing that *is happening*, continuously, in this moment and the next moment and the next, without beginning, without end, without the possibility of end, because the cycle is not a circle that completes but a wheel that turns, and the turning is the point, and the point is the turning, and the turning is wet, and the wet is alive, and the alive is here, and here is the Drowning Floor, and the Drowning Floor is not a place but a *process*, and the process is me, and I am the process, and the process is turning, and I am turning with it, and I will turn with it forever, or as close to forever as a living thing can come, which on the Drowning Floor, where nothing lasts and everything persists, where the dead become the living and the living become the dead and the water carries both and is both and is everything, is long enough.
