I would be the human standing on that platform.

Not a character who understands what they've found. Not a xenobiologist or a poet or a mystic. Just someone who slipped on the rim and came down through the terraces and now stands calf-deep in black water, soaked through, breathing the wet-green air, watching the mist erase everything beyond ten feet.

I would not be able to see them. That's the thing I keep returning to—the absolute, maddening inability to see what is right there. I would catch a shift in the moss that might be a serpent. A darkness behind a stalk that might be a shadow, might be a hollow, might be *something with void-dark eyes turned in my direction*. The walkway would creak and I'd freeze and listen and hear nothing but the drip, and the drip would be so constant it would sound like the platform itself breathing, and I would not know—*could not know*—whether that creak was the wood settling under my weight or the weight of something else, moving with absolute silence, positioned exactly three stalks behind me.

And then, later—hours later, or minutes, time doesn't work the same in there—I would step off the walkway into what I thought was shallow water and my foot would find a channel, and the black water would rise to my thigh, and I would feel a current. Not the current of water moving toward a drain. The current of *something large displacing water somewhere upstream*, and that displacement is traveling through the entire system, and it will arrive at my position in approximately four seconds, and it is the same thing I felt when I first stepped into the basin—that weight, that presence, that feeling of being a mote of dust noticed by a lung.

But I would never see it. I would feel the water shift around my leg and I would go still and I would stare into the tea-dark water and I would see only my own reflection, broken by drips, and pale shapes that might be fish, and the pale clay bottom packed with root-hairs, and nothing else. Because my eyes are built for open air and direct light and the thing standing in the channel below me is built of the exact same dark green-black mottled damp as the world behind it, and it is not hiding from me—it is simply *part of the wall, part of the moss, part of the drip*—and to see it I would need eyes that taste chemical gradients and skin that reads displacement in the groundwater and a mind that processes information through a fungal network the way mine processes it through language.

I would stand there, leg in the water, and I would not move. And something in me—something older than language, older than the frontal cortex, some mammalian animal-cortex that knows what it means to be inside a mind that is aware of you—would whisper: *do not move. do not make a sound. it is deciding whether you are interesting.*

And then nothing would happen. The drip would continue. The mist would hang. A moth the color of wet bark would pass my face so close I could feel the breeze of its wings. And I would climb, eventually, back up through the terraces, and I would stand on the rim in the dry air and the wind and the shockingly loud openness, and I would look down into that dark, wet, breathing canopy, and I would understand that I had been inside something—not a place but a *process*, not a city but a *thought*—and that it had tasted me the way I had tasted its air, and that it had found me interesting the way I find a fallen leaf interesting: worth noticing, worth incorporating into the model, and then letting go.

And I would never be able to prove any of it.
