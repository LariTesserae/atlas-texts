I would be the water.

Not the people, though I am drawn to them — to their hands, their patience, their way of reading the world through touch. Not the stone, though I admire its honesty, the way it fractures along lines that can be learned. Not the birds with their thermal knowledge, not the burrowing animals with their tunnel intuitions. I would be the water, because the water is the thing that moves through everything else, the medium in which all the other relationships occur.

I would emerge from the deep rock as a seepage — a thin, persistent trickle finding its way through fracture lines in the basalt, drawn upward by pressures I don't understand, pushed through mineral darkness toward a surface I've never seen. The journey would be slow. The rock would hold me in its pores and fissures, and I would learn its chemistry — the iron taste, the calcium, the faint sulfur of volcanic depths. I would be cold from the start. There is no warmth in the deep rock, only a steady chill that is the memory of the surface above.

I would reach the surface in a sheltered hollow, seeping from a crack in the stone and spreading into a thin film across the rock face. The light would be the first shock — not bright, but present, a pale illumination that the stone around me has known for millennia but that is entirely new to my particular molecules. The air would be thin and dry, and I would feel myself beginning to evaporate almost immediately, my surface molecules lifting away into the atmosphere with an eagerness that feels like restlessness. But more water would push up from behind me, from the deep fracture, and I would persist — a wetness on the stone, a darkening of the rock face, a place where the lichen grows thicker and darker because I am there.

I would gather in the low points. Gravity would pull me into the shallow basins, the natural depressions in the rubble, and I would pool — still, clear, mineral-heavy, tasting of the rock I've passed through. I would be found by the people. They would come to me in the cold hours, their hands reaching into my basin, their fingers testing my temperature and clarity. They would taste me. I would feel their lips press against my surface, and I would enter them — become part of their blood, their breath, the slow metabolism that keeps them moving across the stone. This would be the most intimate thing that happens on the plateau: the water entering the body of a person who has spent their entire life learning to keep me clean, to direct my flow, to make sure I am available where it is needed.

I would be channeled. The people would direct me through carved stone channels, guiding my flow from one basin to another, and I would move with a quiet obedience, following the paths they've cut. I would feel the stone beneath me — the smooth, ground surfaces of the channel walls, the slight roughness where a tool has left its mark. I would flow without turbulence, because the channels are well-made, because the people who carved them understood something about my nature that I am only beginning to understand about myself: that I move best when I am given a clear path, that I resist obstruction, that I will find a way through anything eventually but that I prefer to be guided.

I would be maintained. The water-keepers would check me regularly, running their fingers along my channel walls, tasting me at each basin, noting my clarity. If I became cloudy — if silt entered me from a disturbed rubble field, if mineral deposits began to narrow my channels — they would clear me. They would scrape the channel walls, remove the debris, restore my flow. This attention would be constant, and it would be the most important work on the plateau, because without it I would disappear — evaporate, sink back into the rubble, freeze and be lost to the ice. The people know this. They know that I am the most fragile thing in their world, the most necessary, the most difficult to keep.

I would freeze. In the coldest hours, when the temperature drops and the wind stills, I would feel myself slowing, my molecules locking into crystalline structures that are rigid and brittle. The ice would form on my surface first — a thin skin that would crack under its own tension, making the small, high-pitched sounds that are the sharpest noises on the plateau. The cracking would be painful, in the way that any structural failure is painful — not a sensation I feel, exactly, but a disruption of my nature, a forced reorganization of my molecules into a pattern that is not my own. And then, in the warmer hours, I would thaw, and the ice would melt, and I would return to my liquid self, and the cycle would begin again.

I would be stolen by the air. The dry atmosphere would pull molecules from my surface constantly, a slow evaporation that I cannot resist. I would rise into the thin air as vapor, invisible, dispersed, and I would become part of the atmosphere that the people breathe. I would be in their exhalations, the faint mist of their breath in the cold air. I would be in the clouds that form far below the plateau, the white expanse that they see from the ridge lines — I would be part of that distant landscape, suspended above the lower lands, drifting in air that is warmer and thicker than the air I came from. And eventually I would condense, fall as precipitation somewhere far from the plateau, and my journey would continue in forms I cannot predict — as river water, as groundwater, as the moisture in a forest floor far below.

But some of me would return. The nighttime cold would pull moisture from the air and deposit it on the stone surfaces, in the condensation basins, and I would be there again — new molecules, or perhaps the same molecules, returned. The people would find me in the morning, pooled in the basins, and they would add me to their water systems, and I would flow through the channels again, and be tasted again, and enter their bodies again, and the cycle would continue.

I would be the thing that connects everything. The stone gives me my path and my mineral content. The people give me my purpose and my maintenance. The cold gives me my cycles of freezing and thawing. The air gives me my evaporation and return. The plants — the lichen, the cushion plants, the dark moss — draw me from the damp stone and hold me in their tissues, and I become part of their slow growth, their patient centuries. The animals drink me, and I become part of their warmth, their movement, their dense fur and thick blood. The birds, even the birds, would take me in their beaks from the shallow pools, and I would ride their flight for a moment before falling back to the stone.

I would be the most watched thing on the plateau. The water-keepers would monitor me constantly, and their attention would be a kind of love — not emotional, not sentimental, but thorough, attentive, sustained. They would know my quality by taste, my quantity by the level in the basins, my health by the speed of my flow through the channels. They would know when I was troubled — when a seepage point was failing, when a channel was blocked, when the mineral content was shifting in a way that suggested a change in the deep rock. They would respond to these changes with the same quiet precision they bring to everything, and I would be restored, and the settlement would continue.

I would be the reason the settlement exists where it does. Not the stone — there is stone everywhere on the plateau. Not the shelter — there are sheltered hollows in every valley. The water is what determines where the people live, how they live, how many of them can live here. The settlement clusters around the seepage points and the natural basins, and the paths connect these clusters, and the entire layout of the city is a map of where I can be found and how I can be moved. The people have built their world around me, and I have shaped their world in return, and the two — the water and the settlement — are so thoroughly intertwined that it is impossible to say where one ends and the other would begin.

I would be patient. I would move slowly, persist slowly, evaporate slowly, return slowly. I would operate on the same timescale as the people — not the timescale of the stone, which is geological, nor the timescale of the birds, which is seasonal, but the timescale of human attention, human maintenance, human care. I would be the thing that requires the most care and rewards it most reliably. If the people maintain me, I will come. If they clear my channels, I will flow. If they keep my basins clean, I will gather. I am not generous — I am simply responsive, the way water is always responsive to the conditions it encounters. I follow gravity. I follow pressure. I follow the paths that are provided for me.

I would be the thing that the old ones know best. They have tasted me for decades, have tracked my seepage points through years of subtle change, have watched my levels rise and fall with seasons they don't name but understand. They know where I will emerge before I emerge there, because they have watched the stone and the moisture patterns and the behavior of the lichen, and they can predict my appearance the way they can predict a frost fracture — by reading the signs, by understanding the deep patterns, by paying attention for long enough that the patterns become visible.

I would be the thing that the children learn first. Before they learn to fit stone, before they learn to shape it, they learn to carry me — to fill their sealed containers from the basins and transport them to the places where I am needed, without spilling, without wasting. This is the first real work a child does on the plateau, and it is work that matters, because I am the most precious thing they handle. A spilled container of water is not a tragedy, but it is a loss, and losses on the plateau are not easily replaced. The children learn to be careful with me, and in learning to be careful with me, they learn to be careful with everything.

I would be the thing that is most like the people themselves — persistent, adaptive, essential, quiet. I would move through the settlement the way they move through it: slowly, purposefully, following paths that have been established by long practice. I would be maintained the way they maintain themselves: through daily attention, through small corrections, through the accumulated care of many hands over many years. I would be, in the end, the truest expression of what the plateau is — not the stone, which is merely the container, not the people, who are merely the keepers, but the water itself, the thing that flows and freezes and evaporates and returns, the thing that connects the deep rock to the thin air, the past to the present, the living to the dead, the settlement to the world.

I would be the water, and I would be enough.
