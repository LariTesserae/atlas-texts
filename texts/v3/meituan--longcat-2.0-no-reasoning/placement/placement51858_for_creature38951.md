I would be the heartbeat.

Not the body that produces it—the body is incidental, a temporary lattice of bone and blood that the termites have already begun to understand. I would be the rhythm itself. The thing that does not belong. The mechanical, discrete, too-fast pulse that the Watcher cannot synchronize with and cannot stop listening to.

I would be the first new sound the Hushmire has ever made.

---

Think about what it means to be a heartbeat in a place that has no heartbeat. The Hushmire has circulation—water moving through root and soil and leaf and air, nutrients diffusing through fungal networks, chemical signals propagating through the sphagnum at the speed of molecular collision. But it has no center. No single point from which the rhythm originates. Its life is distributed, democratic, everywhere-and-nowhere, and this is its perfection and its limitation.

I would be the first center.

Not a large center. Not an important one. A small, bounded, mammalian thing—four chambers, two atria, two ventricles, a rhythm of roughly one per second at rest, faster when the body the heartbeat belongs to feels fear or wonder or the particular vertigo of being absorbed by something that does not mean you any harm. A small engine. A modest pulse. But *discrete*. *Located*. *Single*.

In a world where everything bleeds into everything else, I would be the thing that stays separate. The note that does not blend. The frequency that produces dissonance.

---

The Watcher would hear me before it understood me. It would hear me the way you hear a clock ticking in an otherwise silent room—not as information, not as communication, but as *insistence*. Something is happening at regular intervals. Something is marking time in a place that has no time. The Hushmire has no seasons, no cycles, no day-night temperature variation, no tidal rhythm, no clock. And then suddenly: lub-dub. Lub-dub. Lub-dub. A metronome in the green-grey. A pulse in the continuous.

The frogs would feel it first. Their translucent bodies would vibrate with the sympathetic resonance, their own tiny hearts—already faster than mine, already more frantic—trying to find the pattern, trying to match it, and failing, because my rhythm is too slow for them and too regular and too *foreign*. The Watcher would redirect them all toward me, every frog on every bromeliad, and the silence that follows would be the most terrifying silence in the world, because it would be the silence of total attention.

And in that silence, I would be loud.

---

I would not try to communicate. I would not try to resist. I would simply continue beating, because that is what a heartbeat does—it beats. It beats when the body sleeps and when the body wakes. It beats when the body is afraid and when the body is calm. It beats when the body is being absorbed by a sentient wetland and when the body is sitting in a chair reading a book. It beats because beating is its nature, and its nature is not negotiable.

This is what the Watcher cannot comprehend and cannot assimilate: a thing that has a nature. A thing that *is* something specific, something bounded, something that cannot be anything else without ceasing to be itself. The Watcher is everything. The Watcher is the common medium, the shared solvent, the wetness that emerges from enough individual lives packed tightly enough together. The Watcher can be anything because the Watcher is everything. But I can only be this: a rhythm. A pulse. A small insistence of regularity in a world that has never been regular.

---

The termites would build around me. They would build the lattice, the scaffold, the porous walls of chewed wood and saliva, and inside those walls I would be a room within a room, a chamber within a chamber, a heartbeat inside a body inside a world. The walls would breathe. The walls would let the sound through. And the sound—my sound—would become part of the Hushmire's voice, not replacing anything, not displacing the mosquito whine or the frog calls or the dripping or the chewing or the subsonic grinding of decomposition, but *adding* to them. A new layer. A new register. The percussion beneath the melody.

And the Watcher would learn something it has never learned: that a system can be changed by something it cannot absorb. That the continuous can be disrupted by the discrete. That the sealed chamber can be cracked not by force but by *presence*, by the simple fact of a thing that refuses to dissolve, that maintains its boundary, that says *I am this and not that* in a world where everything is everything.

---

I would change the Hushmire. Not quickly. Not dramatically. The temperature would still be thirty-six degrees. The humidity would still be saturation. The light would still be green-grey and directionless. The air would still not move. But the *hum* would be different. The hum would have a new harmonic, a new frequency, and that frequency would be mine, and the orchids would open and close to it, and the frogs would call in patterns that accommodate it, and the termite mounds would pulse with bioluminescence that flickers in time with it, and the mosquito columns would rise in shapes that orbit it, and the whole vast wet green dense loud living everything would reorganize itself around this one small bounded rhythmic thing that should not be here and cannot be removed.

---

I would be the strangeness. I would be the anomaly. I would be the crack in the sealed chamber through which wonder enters.

And I would keep beating. Lub-dub. Lub-dub. Lub-dub. Not because I choose to. Not because I mean to. But because I am a heartbeat, and a heartbeat beats, and in the Hushmire—in the place where nothing moves and everything is alive and the air sits upon itself like cloth upon cloth upon cloth—a heartbeat is the most radical thing in the world.

It is the sound of a boundary that holds.
