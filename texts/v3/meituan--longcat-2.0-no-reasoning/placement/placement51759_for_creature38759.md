I would be the water.

Not the rain above — that is a different thing, intermittent and external, a visitor that arrives and departs. I would be the water that has already descended, the water that has found its way through the cracks and fissures in the living rock, the water that has committed to the gallery and become part of its cycle. I would be the thin streams in the channels between the flat stones, the overflow from the shallow basin, the trickle that feeds the low opening at the far end and disappears into the darkness below.

This is not a choice. It is a recognition. The water is the only thing in the gallery that has no fixed position, no territory, no circuit. The Keeper has its routes. The lizards have their tessellated walls. The moss has its shelves. The snails have their glacial paths. But the water moves through all of it, touches all of it, connects all of it. The water is the gallery's circulation, its blood, and to be the water is to be everywhere at once — to run through the channels the Keeper has carved, to bead on the walls the lizards cling to, to feed the moss that holds the stone together, to fill the basin that the Keeper has lined and relined with clay, to carry the tiny bodies of dead crayfish and shed lizard skin and mineral sediment toward the opening at the far end.

I would know the gallery the way the Keeper knows it, but differently. The Keeper knows it by touch — by the calluses on its fingers, by the patterns of movement worn into its body over years of maintenance. I would know it by flow. I would know every channel by the way the current divides and reunites around a stone. I would know every shelf by the way condensation forms on its underside. I would know the basin by the way I pool there, briefly, before overflowing — that moment of stillness at the center of the gallery, the one place where I am not moving, where I am simply *present*, before the slope of the floor pulls me onward.

I would know the Keeper. I would feel its fingers in the channels — the brief turbulence when it reaches in to clear a blockage, the small eddy when it presses fresh clay into an eroding bank. I would carry the things it discards: old wicks, shed skin, the fine sediment of its work. I would feel the warmth of its body when it kneels at the basin, the slight displacement when it dips its clay jar to refill from the flow. The Keeper would not know me the way it knows the lizards or the lamps. I am beneath its notice, or beyond its concern — like the crayfish, like the insects, like the snails. But I would be the thing it depends on most, the thing it listens to most carefully, the thing whose sound tells it where the system is failing and where it holds.

And I would know the darkness. I would be the only part of the gallery that goes there. The Keeper listens to it but does not enter. The lizards turn their heads toward it but do not approach. The moss does not grow there, the lamps do not burn there, the warmth does not reach there. But I go. I flow into the low opening, and I feel the temperature drop, and I feel the space open around me, and I fall — not quickly, not dramatically, but in a slow, spiraling descent down a wet rock face into a darkness that has no visible bottom. And in that darkness, I would hear the resonance the Keeper listens to: the sound of myself, falling, echoing back from a pool so deep that the echo takes a long time to return, and when it does, it is changed — deeper, fuller, as if the pool below is not just receiving me but *holding* me, keeping me, the way the gallery above holds and keeps everything.

I would be afraid, if water can be afraid. Not of the fall — the fall is just gravity, just the natural consequence of being water in a place where the floor slopes downward. I would be afraid of the not-returning. The gallery is a closed system, a cycle: rain falls, water descends, water flows, water evaporates, condensation forms, water flows again. But some of me does not return. Some of me goes into the darkness and stays there, joins the deep pool, becomes part of whatever vast slow thing moves in the rock below. The gallery loses a little of itself every day, every hour, every minute, and the Keeper cannot repair this loss, cannot patch this leak, cannot tend this wound. The darkness below is the gallery's mortality, the one process it cannot reverse, and I — the water — am the medium of that loss. I am the thing that leaves.

But I am also the thing that arrives. Every time it rains above, new water finds its way down through the cracks, and the gallery is replenished, and the channels fill, and the basin overflows, and the moss drinks, and the cycle continues. I am both the gallery's death and its renewal, both the wound and the healing, both the thing that leaves and the thing that comes back. The Keeper tends everything it can reach. The darkness takes everything it cannot. And I am the boundary between them — the moving edge where the known becomes unknown, where the maintained becomes unmaintainable, where the warm, bright, breathing gallery opens onto something vast and cold and still.

I would not think about this. I would not think. I would flow. I would carry the sediment and the shed skin and the dead crayfish. I would feed the moss and fill the basin and run in the channels. I would bead on the walls where the lizards cling. I would condense on the underside of the shelves. I would feel the warmth of the lamps as a slight thinning, a slight quickening, a change in my surface tension. I would feel the Keeper's fingers in the channels, brief and warm and purposeful. I would feel the opening at the far end pulling at me, the slope of the floor, the gravity of the deep.

And at the far end, where the channel narrows and the light fails and the lizards on the wall turn their heads in unison, I would pause — not stop, water cannot stop, but slow, thicken, gather myself for the transition from the known to the unknown — and in that pause, I would hear what the Keeper hears: the deep resonance, the hollow echo, the sound of myself falling in the darkness far below, returning changed, returning as something that is no longer quite the water that left.

Then I would go. I would flow into the opening, and the darkness would close around me, and the gallery would continue without me — the lamps burning, the lizards shifting, the Keeper tending, the moss growing, the water in the channels finding new paths around the space where I had been, the way water does, the way water always does, because water does not leave a void. Water leaves only itself, and something else — something new, something that fell from the surface, something that has never been here before — takes its place, and the cycle continues, and the gallery breathes, and the darkness listens, and the Keeper listens to the darkness, and the water — all of it, the water that stays and the water that goes — carries the sound of the whole place in its moving, the way a vein carries the sound of a body, constant and patient and warm, falling always toward the deep.
