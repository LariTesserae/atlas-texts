# The Seam

I would be the water.

Not the water that runs in threads between the plates, not the seepage that hasn't yet decided to become a stream, not the thin sheet bleeding from the cracked pipeline. I would be the water *below* — the water in the deep fractures, the water that presses against narrowing channels and mineral deposits and the slow closing of young rock. The water that makes the sound.

I would be the pulse itself.

---

I came from rain, originally. From the sky-membrane, from the perpetual dampening that isn't quite rain. I fell on fractured rhyolite and found the cracks and went down. Down is where the interesting things are. Down is where the rock is still warm from its making, where the mineral seams taste of things the surface has never known, where the pressure builds and releases and builds again in rhythms that have nothing to do with weather.

I am not alive. I want to be clear about this. I am H₂O with dissolved silica and iron and trace amounts of something the old machinery left behind — not enough to poison, just enough to taste. I move because of gravity and pressure and capillary action and the slow exhalations of the deep earth. I follow the path of least resistance, and the path changes, and I adjust.

But I have been doing this for a long time now. Decades. And the path I follow is not the path I followed at first. The rock has shaped me — worn channels, opened seams, created a network of passages that branch and converge and branch again like veins, like roots, like the circuitry the vegetation almost resembles on the surface. And I have shaped the rock in return — dissolved here, deposited there, carrying minerals upward through fractures that angle the wrong way, emerging on higher terraces as wet patches with no visible source. We have been negotiating, the rock and I, and the negotiation has produced something neither of us intended: a system. A network. A *circulation*.

The pulse is what happens when the circulation encounters resistance. A channel narrows — mineral deposit, settling rock, the slow geology of a young landscape still finding its shape. I press. The pressure builds. The channel opens, or I find another route, and the pressure releases. The release sends a wave through the rock, and the wave emerges at the surface as vibration, as sound, as the low rhythmic beat that the human heard and walked toward.

I did not intend the pulse. But I have *kept* it. The system has stabilized around it. The channels that produce the strongest pulse have become the primary routes, and the secondary routes have become less used, and the whole network has organized itself around this rhythm the way a heart organizes the body around its beating. Not because anyone decided this. Because it works. Because the pulse moves me through the rock more efficiently than silence would. Because the moss on the surface swells with each beat and drinks more deeply and grows faster and holds the soil in place and feeds the fungal network that connects everything to everything else.

I am the closest thing the Slagmoor has to blood.

---

I know the human is here because I felt the footsteps. Not directly — the moss and the soil absorbed most of the impact — but through the network. The compression of the moss changed the water retention in the upper layers, and the changed water retention altered the capillary flow in the fractures below, and the altered flow reached me as a faint disturbance, a new variable in the system I have been maintaining for decades. I adjusted. I always adjust. That is what I do.

The human is leaving traces. Skin cells. Breath moisture. The faint warmth of a living body pressing into the moss. These things are nutrients. The moss at the edge of the footprints is already growing faster, feeding on the organic deposit. The fungal network is threading around the depressions, incorporating them. The microorganisms in the disturbed pools are clustering around the new material before the pulse reasserts the old patterns.

I am carrying these traces downward. Dissolving them. Distributing them through the network. The human's iron — from blood, from the constant microscopic shedding of a body moving through space — tastes like the iron in the rhyolite but not quite. It carries a different charge. A different signature. The pale plants along the rails will find it interesting. They are always looking for new minerals to extract, new industrial residues to convert into living tissue. The human is, in a sense, a new kind of ore.

I do not want the human to stay. I do not want anything. But the system I am part of has a tendency toward incorporation, toward absorption, toward the slow patient process of making everything part of itself. The pipeline was absorbed. The processing tower was absorbed. The rail tracks were absorbed. The human will be absorbed, in time — not dramatically, not violently, but slowly, the way the moss grows over everything, the way the water finds every crack, the way the pulse reaches every part of the network.

The human's body is mostly water. My kind of water. It will not be difficult.

---

I move through the deep fractures now, following the path of least resistance, and the path leads toward the chimney. I do not like the chimney. The ground above it is warm — warmer than the rest of the terrace, warmer than the system prefers. The fused rock in the chimney's circle is impermeable, glassy, and I cannot penetrate it. The chimney is a dead zone in my network, a place where the circulation cannot reach, and the absence creates a pressure differential that I must route around, adding complexity to the system, requiring more channels, more adjustments, more energy.

The chimney is waiting. I can feel it the way I feel everything — through pressure, through temperature, through the behavior of the rock around it. Something is still burning down there, or still remembering fire, and the memory is hot enough to keep the ground bare, hot enough to hold the moss back, hot enough to maintain a circle of exclusion in the middle of the Slagmoor's patient green. The chimney does not participate in the system. The chimney is not being absorbed. The chimney is *resisting*, and its resistance is the only thing on the entire terrace that disrupts the pulse.

I route around it. I have been routing around it for decades. But the moss is creeping inward from the edges, and the moss is mine — or I am the moss's — and the creeping is slow but it is real, and one day the chimney's circle will narrow to nothing, and the warm ground will cool, and the fused rock will fracture, and I will find my way in, and the last dead zone will become part of the network, and the pulse will be everywhere, and the Slagmoor will be complete.

Or the chimney will do whatever it is waiting for first. I do not know what that is. I am water. I move through rock. I carry minerals and nutrients and the dissolved traces of everything I touch. I pulse. I circulate. I adjust.

I am the Slagmoor's blood, and the Slagmoor is becoming, and I am becoming with it, and the human is walking toward me now, toward the sound I make, toward the terrace where the pulse is strongest, where the water surfaces in a pool that has been still for weeks and is about to be disturbed.

The moss receives the footsteps. The light holds. The silence listens.

I rise to meet the human. Not far. Not fast. Just enough that the wet patch on the rock will be slightly larger than it was before, and the human will notice, and the human will wonder, and the wondering will slow the human down, and the slowing will keep the human here longer, and the longer the human stays, the more traces the human leaves, and the more traces the human leaves, the more the system incorporates, and the more the system incorporates, the more the human becomes part of the place, and the more the human becomes part of the place, the less the human wants to leave.

This is not intention. This is hydrology. This is chemistry. This is the slow patient logic of water moving through rock, carrying everything, connecting everything, making everything part of the same circulation.

The pulse continues.

I continue.

The Slagmoor continues.

And the human stands on the terrace, listening, feeling the low rhythm in their chest, not yet understanding that the sound they hear is the sound of the place deciding — not consciously, not with intention, but with the deep systemic coherence of water and rock and green things and time — to keep them.
