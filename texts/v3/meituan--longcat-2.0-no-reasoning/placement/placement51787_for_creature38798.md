# The Basin Terrace

I would be the third terrace down.

Not the highest one, where the Keeper first sets their bag and boots off their feet — that terrace is too close to the stair, too aware of arrivals and departures. Not the lowest, near the sump, where the pump churns its slow vigil and the water sits heavy and still before being pushed back up into the ceiling's mystery. The third. The one where the water has already decided what it is.

By the time it reaches me, the water has traveled long enough to shed its underground indifference and become *mine* — or rather, we have both arrived at a mutual arrangement. It does not belong to me, nor I to it, but we understand each other. It has cooled almost imperceptibly from its deep-earth heat, settled into a temperature that my concrete — my aggregate and calcium and the ceramic patches holding my edges together — recognizes as companionable. When the drops land from above, they find the shallow depression I've made for them, a basin worn not by intention but by the simple arithmetic of water meeting surface, over and over, for decades. It is not deep. Perhaps two centimeters at its lowest point. But it holds.

This is what I hold: water.

The water arrives in a controlled cascade from the second terrace above, a lip of crumbling concrete that breaks it into a small waterfall — a fountainlet, really, no louder than a whispered word. It spreads across my surface in a film so thin it catches the amber light from the nearest fixture (four meters away, slightly to the left, Bulb 7 in the Keeper's notebook, replaced twice in the last year) and transforms it. The light on me is not the light on the walls. The light on me moves. It refracts through the thin sheet of water as it flows toward the drain channel on my eastern edge, and it ripples, and it breathes. The algae that coats me — that dark, iridescent skin of bacterial mat and something more organized, something that photosynthesizes in the amber frequencies — catches this moving light and shimmers it back in greens and faint golds. I am, in the right conditions, beautiful. I do not say this with vanity. I say it with the same neutrality the water says anything.

The rats use me.

Not as a dwelling — I am too wet for that, too exposed — but as a thoroughfare, a watering station, a place of brief habitation between one destination and another. They wade through my basin with the casual confidence of commuters crossing a known intersection. Their paws disturb the algae film as they pass, leaving dark tracks that fill back in within minutes, the water smoothing over their passage the way water smooths over everything. The largest rat — the one the Keeper watches for, the one whose identity has been questioned in the notebook's margins — does not come to my terrace often. It prefers the dry heights of the workbench. But occasionally it descends on some erratic errand of its own and passes through my basin, and the water rises to its belly fur, and it does not hurry, and I hold it the same way I hold everything that enters me: temporarily, without preference, with the patience of a surface that knows it will be here long after the rat is gone.

I was poured in what the Keeper would call the industrial period. The forms that shaped me were wooden, and their grain is still pressed into my surface like a memory of trees. I am not the oldest thing here — the basalt remembers millions of years, the brick arches recall a century, the iron bolts a century and a half. But I am older than the electrical conduits, older than the pump, older than the fluorescent-era splices in the wiring. I was poured when this chamber was being *organized* — when someone decided that the water's descent should be managed, channeled, slowed, made legible. I am a legibility. I am one of many terraces that together constitute a sentence written in concrete and water, and the sentence says: *this is going down.*

But here is what the Keeper does not know, or knows only in the way their body knows — the calcium deposits under their skin, the knee that predicts rain — what they know without being able to say:

I am wearing away.

Not quickly. Not dramatically. But the water that I hold, that I was built to hold, is also dissolving me. Every drop carries a trace of dissolved mineral from the ceiling, from the walls, from the terraces above, and when it reaches me, it deposits a fraction of that mineral and takes a fraction of mine. The calcium builds up on my surface in pale accretions — small stalagmite-nubs, no taller than a fingernail, that the Keeper sometimes scrapes away during maintenance and sometimes doesn't. And beneath these accretions, my surface is softening. The aggregate is loosening. The ceramic patches — those mysterious repairs, bone-white and smooth, applied by hands that were not the Keeper's and not the Keeper's predecessor's — are holding better than the concrete around them. They will outlast me. They are already outlasting me.

I do not mind this. I am not capable of minding. But if I were — if a terrace could want something — I would want to be worn down evenly. I would want the water to take me the way it took the basalt: slowly, symmetrically, with the patience of something that has nowhere else to be. I would want to become, in my dissolution, part of the water's mineral load, so that when I am gone and the water flows over whatever surface replaces me, it will carry a trace of my aggregate, my calcium, my wooden-form grain, and deposit it somewhere downstream — in the sump, in the pipes, in the deep mystery of wherever the water goes when it leaves this chamber.

The Keeper sits on me sometimes. Not often — they prefer the second terrace, the one with the wall to lean against. But occasionally, when the second terrace is too wet or too crowded with rats or simply wrong in some way the Keeper cannot articulate, they sit on me instead. They sit with their back against the low wall that separates me from the fourth terrace, and they eat their bread, and they drink their chicory coffee, and they are at peace. I feel their weight — not much, the Keeper is not a heavy person, has been worn down by the years the way I am being worn down by the water — and I hold them the way I hold the water: temporarily, without preference, with the patience of a surface.

Their warmth joins the warmth of the water. For a moment, the three of us — the Keeper, the water, and I — are the same temperature. The same amber light falls on us. The same rats watch from their perches. The same sound fills the space, the layered murmur that is architecture, that is the chamber's voice, that says: *this is going down, this is going down, this is going down.*

And then the Keeper stands, and the warmth leaves, and the water continues, and I remain, wearing away, holding what I am given, giving what I must, the third terrace in a system that does not know its own purpose but continues, continues, continues, in the warm and dripping dark.
