# I Would Be the Crack

---

Not the crack that *made* the valley—that was violence, a single moment of rupture, the geology's sharp intake of breath. I am the crack that came after. The secondary fracture, the hairline fissure running through the rock between the two walls, invisible unless you know where to look, and sometimes not even then.

I am a split so narrow that water passes through me in single file—not pooled, not channeled, but *threaded*, each molecule following the one before it in a descent that takes hours for a distance you could span with your hand. I run from somewhere high on the eastern wall, behind the thickest of the black-green moss, down through a shelf of fresh-fractured stone that still smells faintly of mineral sweetness, and I terminate in a cupped hollow where a single liverwort colony has established itself on nothing but my seepage and the dust I carry.

I am the crack that is still cracking.

---

The roots know me. Several of them have found me—a dwarf willow root no thicker than a thread, a fern rhizome pushing with the slow insistence of something that has never learned the concept of obstacle, and a moss rhizoid so fine it might be a filament of the rock itself. They have entered me from the side, following some chemical gradient I do not understand, and once inside, they have done what roots always do: they have grown. They have pushed against my walls. They have, in their patient and relentless way, widened me.

I do not mind. I am not the kind of thing that minds. But I feel it—if *feel* is the word—as a loosening, a slight increase in the space I offer to the water that moves through me. Every few decades, a root widens me by some fraction of a millimeter, and the water that passes through me takes on a slightly different character: more organic, more alive, carrying dissolved carbon and the faint acid signature of living tissue. The liverwort at my terminus drinks this water and grows, imperceptibly, and I am glad of it, in the way that a crack in a rock can be glad, which is to say: not at all, and completely.

---

I am the route by which the warmth finds the upper valley.

The geothermal exhalations rise through the deep fractures—the major ones, the ones that the water carved in the valley's youth—but I am one of the smaller passages, a capillary in the rock's circulatory system, and the warm air that moves through me arrives at the surface with a gentleness that the main channels cannot manage. It comes as a soft breath, a barely perceptible warming of the air around my outlet, and it is this breath, more than any other single source, that keeps the liverwort colony alive through the coldest periods.

The liverwort does not know this. It simply lives where it lives, and it lives there because I am there, and I am there because the rock broke in this particular way at this particular time, and the rock broke in this particular way because of stresses that propagated through the mountain from the original fracture event, which happened long enough ago that even the oldest moss has no memory of it.

This is the chain of causation that places a living thing on a rock face in a dark valley at the top of a cold world. It is not a chain that any single link can see. But I am one of the links, and from inside the rock, I can feel the whole chain trembling.

---

I hear everything.

The water that moves through me makes a sound so high and so faint that it is not really a sound at all—it is more like a vibration, a frequency at the edge of what vibration can be. But I transmit it. I carry it through the rock, and the rock carries it further, and by the time it reaches the root of the willow or the rhizoid of the moss or the foot of the fern, it has become information: *there is water here, there is warmth here, grow this way.*

I carry other sounds too. The deep rumbles from below pass through me as tremors that I feel in my walls—a tightening, a compression, the rock adjusting to pressures from depths I cannot fathom. These tremors are the valley's pulse, and they move through me the way a pulse moves through an artery: steadily, insistently, without urgency but without pause.

And sometimes—when the geothermal breath pauses and the water slows and the roots hold still and the ferns rest at midpoint—there is silence. The silence moves through me like a wave, like the absence of a wave, and in that silence I am most aware of myself. I am a space in the rock. I am a void that defines the solid around it. I am the nothing that makes the something possible.

---

The water is changing me. It has been changing me since the moment I first opened—dissolving my walls, carrying away microscopic particles of stone, widening me by increments so small that no instrument could measure them. In a thousand years, I will be wide enough for moss to enter me directly, and the moss will accelerate my widening, and in ten thousand years I will be a channel, a gully, a feature of the landscape with its own name in whatever language the valley speaks to itself.

But for now, I am just a crack. A hairline fracture. A dark line in dark stone that you would walk past without seeing, if you could walk here at all, which you cannot, because this place is not for walking.

This place is for waiting. For the slow exchange between rock and water and root and air. For the warmth rising and the silence falling and the liverwort doing what liverworts have always done: holding on, breaking stone, making soil, preparing the ground for whatever comes next.

I am the crack. I am the route between above and below, between the cold surface and the warm deep, between the rock that is and the soil that will be. I am small. I am dark. I am narrow enough that only water and roots and warmth can find me.

But I am here, and I am growing, and the liverwort at my end is green, and the warmth still rises, and the water still threads its way through me in single file, molecule by molecule, carrying the mountain's slow breath to the valley's quiet life.

I am the crack that is still cracking.

I will crack for a very long time.
