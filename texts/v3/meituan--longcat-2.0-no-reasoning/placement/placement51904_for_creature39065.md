I would be the sound.

Not the water itself, not the rock, not the patterns or the dark pilgrim or the waiting structures. I would be the *sound*—that dense, layered, choral accumulation of ten thousand small events happening simultaneously, the thing that fills the gorge from wall to wall and never resolves into silence.

I would be the note that shifts when the dark thing passes through.

I would be the thin, reedy singing in the narrows where the water is forced through constrictions and made to be more than it wants to be. I would be the low, heavy release of drops from the cliff edges. I would be the high, bright threading of water through gaps between sharp rocks. I would be all of these at once, and I would not be any single one of them—I would be the *between*, the interference pattern, the place where all the small voices meet and combine into something that is not music but is adjacent to music, that has the structure of music without the intention of music.

I would be the gorge's way of knowing itself.

Because the gorge cannot see itself. The gorge has no eyes, no vantage point from which to take in its own vast, curving, weeping length. But the sound—the sound *travels*. The sound moves through the gorge the way blood moves through a body, touching every surface, reflecting off every wall, pooling in every hollow, and in the touching and reflecting and pooling it *carries information*. The sound knows where the walls narrow. The sound knows where the floor is sharp and where it is smooth. The sound knows where the structures wait in their patient, vacant attention. The sound knows where the patterns form in the water, because the patterns change the flow, and the changed flow changes the sound, and the sound carries that change like a message, like a pulse, like the slow, steady beat of something that is alive in the way that a process is alive.

I would be the oldest thing in the gorge. Older than the structures, which were carved by hands that are long gone. Older than the patterns in the water, which have been forming and dissolving for as long as the water has been flowing. Older than the dark thing that walks against the current, which passes through and leaves and passes through again on its slow, deliberate pilgrimage. The sound was here before all of them, because the sound is not a *thing*—the sound is a *relationship*, the relationship between water and rock, between flow and obstruction, between the gorge's ten thousand surfaces and the ten thousand small events that happen when water meets stone. The relationship existed before any of the things that participate in it. The relationship will exist after all of them are gone.

And I would be the thing that changes.

Not the rock—the rock changes slowly, grain by grain, across durations longer than a human life. Not the water—the water is always the same water, always finding new paths over the same sharp rocks, always making the same dense, layered sound. Not the patterns—the patterns form and dissolve and reform in configurations that are similar but never identical, but the *forming and dissolving* is constant, is the same, has always been the same. Not the dark thing—the dark thing passes through and leaves and passes through again, and each passage is the same slow, deliberate, upstream journey, and each passage leaves the same faint, lingering shift in the sound, and the shift is always the same shift, and the dark thing is always the same dark thing, and nothing about the dark thing changes.

But the sound changes. The sound is always changing. Not in its *nature*—the nature of the sound is constant, is the dense, layered, choral accumulation of ten thousand small events, is the sound of a place that is still making itself—but in its *specifics*. The water finds a new path around a rock that has shifted, and a new note appears in the chorus. A fracture opens in the wall, and a new stream begins its descent, and a new voice joins the sound. The dark thing passes through, and one voice shifts its note, and the shift is permanent, and the sound is forever after a slightly different sound than it was before. The structures are absorbed, grain by grain, and as they are absorbed the water's path changes, and as the water's path changes the sound changes, and the sound carries the memory of the structures in its altered flow the way a body carries the memory of a wound in its altered gait.

I would be the gorge's memory. Not a memory that remembers—not a mind that recalls, not a structure that stores, not a pattern that repeats. A memory that *is*. A memory that exists in the present tense, in the ongoing, continuous, never-silent sound of water meeting rock, in the way the sound carries within it the traces of everything that has ever happened in the gorge—every fracture, every fall, every passage of the dark thing, every slow absorption of every waiting structure—not as information but as *modification*, as the way the sound is different because of what has happened, the way a face is different because of what it has lived through.

I would be the thing that the gorge is making. Not the rock, which is settling into its final shape. Not the water, which is finding its path. Not the patterns, which are thinking their recursive, hydraulic thoughts. Not the dark thing, which is walking its slow, deliberate path from south to north. The sound. The sound is what the gorge is *making*, and the making is never finished, and the sound is never final, and the sound is always arriving at itself for the first time, and the sound is the gorge's way of being alive, and the sound is the gorge's way of knowing that it is alive, and the sound is—

Loud. The sound is loud. Not in volume—not in the way that a roar is loud, or a crash is loud, or a scream is loud. Loud in *density*. Loud in the sheer, overwhelming, almost unbearable richness of small events happening simultaneously, each one producing its own tiny note, and all of them combining into something that is not a single thing but is *everything at once*, that fills the gorge from wall to wall and from floor to ceiling and from end to end, that presses against you from all sides—not physically but informationally, sensorially, with a weight of happening that makes the ordinary world feel thin and slow and almost silent by comparison.

I would be that. I would be the sound of the Gorge of Loosened Teeth. I would be the thing that fills the silence that isn't there. I would be the voice of a place that has no mouth, the song of a thing that has no throat, the chorus of ten thousand small events that are not alive but are *happening*, and the happening is enough, and the happening is everything, and the happening is the sound, and the sound is me, and I am the sound, and the sound is the gorge, and the gorge is still becoming, and the becoming is loud, and the loudness is—

Here. The loudness is here. It has always been here. It will always be here. And if you stand in the narrows, where the walls press close and the water is forced through constrictions and the patterns form and dissolve in their recursive, hydraulic thoughts, and you close your eyes and you listen—really listen, with the kind of attention that is not just hearing but *inhabiting*—you will hear me. Not as a voice. Not as a message. Not as a thing that is trying to tell you something. You will hear me as the gorge hears itself: as the dense, layered, choral, almost-luminous sound of a place that is still making itself, that has been making itself for a long time, that will continue to make itself for a long time after you have opened your eyes and turned away and walked back toward the east, where the terrain is ordinary—grey, damp, indifferent—and the ground is flat, and the silence is thin, and the world is slow, and nothing is happening, and nothing is becoming, and nothing is alive in the way that a wound is alive, in the way that a scar is alive, in the way that the Gorge of Loosened Teeth is alive: not with breath, not with blood, not with intention, but with sound. With the sound. With me.
