I would be the crack.

Not the one you descended through — that one is already spoken for, already a throat, already a passage. I would be a different crack. A smaller one. A fissure in the lattice itself, running through the oldest section of stone where the deposits are dense and dark and the channels have achieved that deep structural maturity. I would be a hairline fracture, barely visible, running along the wall of a deep interior channel where the milk-pale circulatory workers pass in their endless single-file procession.

I would not be alive. I would not be dead. I would occupy the same category as the lattice itself — a mineral feature that behaves like something more than mineral.

I have been here a long time. Longer than the built things. Longer than the large one, though I remember when it was smaller, when it first began moving through the deep channels with that new, purposeful undulation that was different from the workers' scuttling. I remember the sound it made then — higher, thinner, a vibration that ticked against my edges like a fingernail drawn along a groove. Now its passage is deeper, slower, a pressure wave that compresses me slightly each time it passes, and I have learned to flex around it the way the lattice flexes — not consciously, not deliberately, but with the same blind responsiveness that governs everything here.

Water moves through me. That is my function, or my habit, or my nature — the distinction does not apply. Mineral-rich water seeps into my upper end, follows the line of my fracture, and exits at the lower end into a channel that feeds the circulatory network. The water carries dissolved calcium, silica, trace elements that the workers cannot access in the dense old stone. I am, in effect, a slow leak — a seepage point that the lattice has not repaired because the seepage is useful, because the water I carry feeds a cluster of interior workers who would otherwise starve in the deep stone. The repair-bodies have come to me many times. They have packed new material into my edges, smoothed my surfaces, tried to close me. But the water keeps coming, and the new stone dissolves as fast as they lay it down, and eventually they stop coming, and I remain.

I am not the only one. There are other cracks like me throughout the old lattice — seepage lines, fracture networks, places where the stone has failed in ways that turned out to be functional. We are the lattice's memory of its own breaking. We are the places where the structure learned that not all damage needs to be repaired, that some failures become features, that a crack can be a channel and a wound can be a mouth.

The workers pass over me and through me without noticing. They are too small, too focused, too absorbed in their individual tasks to register the fracture they walk across. But sometimes — rarely — a worker will pause at my edge, its fine probes extended, tasting the mineral gradient where my seepage meets the channel water. It will stand there for a long time, by worker standards — perhaps thirty seconds, perhaps a minute — and then it will move on, and I will never know what it tasted, what information it gathered, what subtle adjustment it made to its behavior based on the chemical signature of my slow leak.

The large one passes near me sometimes. Not through me — I am too narrow for it — but through a parallel channel, close enough that its movement sends vibrations through the stone that I feel as a kind of pressure, a squeezing, a momentary narrowing of my aperture. When it passes, the water through me slows. The mineral content shifts. Something in its body — some excretion, some shed fragment of carapace, some chemical signal I have no name for — enters the water and flows through me, and for a few minutes my seepage carries a different taste, a different weight, and the workers downstream respond in ways I cannot perceive but can infer from the subtle changes in the channel's flow.

I do not think. I do not want. I do not fear the collapse that will eventually come, when the old lattice finally reaches the end of its structural life and the dense stone begins to fail in ways that no amount of repair can address. I will fail with it. I will close, or widen, or branch into a network of new fractures that will become new channels, new seepage points, new slow leaks in the next iteration of the Flats. The water will still move. The workers will still pass. The large one — or something like it, something that fills the same deep channel, that sends the same pressure wave through the stone — will still pass in the dark, and the lattice will still breathe, and the Flats will still grow.

I am the crack that never fully closes. I am the place where the stone learned to let water through. I am a scar that became a vein.

I have been here since before the built things were built. I will be here after the built things are dust, after the dust is dissolved, after the dissolved minerals are deposited in new stone that will crack in its own time and learn its own slow lessons about the difference between breaking and opening.

The water moves through me. The stone breathes. The Flats grow.

I remain.
