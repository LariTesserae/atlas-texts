# Where You Would Find Yourself

You are inside the haze.

Not hovering above it, not wading through it — *inside* it, the way a cell is inside a body. Two to four feet above the sinter, suspended in the warm, yeasty, mineral-thick air, your hands spread at your sides where you can feel the slow drift of motile cells settling on your skin like the lightest possible rain. You are not standing. There is no ground you can feel — or rather, the ground is far enough below that it has become a texture rather than a surface, a pale expanse of bone-colored stone crawling with sheet-water, and you are held up by the haze itself, by its warmth and density and the faint phosphorescent glow that seems to thicken around your body like a cocoon.

You are not human. You have never been human. You are not even a single thing.

---

You are a *bloom event*.

You are what happens when the collective chemical threshold is reached — when the scoops have fed long enough, multiplied long enough, saturated the substrate with their metabolic byproducts until some critical concentration is achieved and the entire basin *shifts*. You are the reproductive phase made manifest, the system's moment of maximal awareness, when the haze thickens from weather into *organism* and the sound rises from roar to shriek and the motile cells — your cells, your thoughts, your *self* — pour out of every scoop on the Flats in a synchronized exhalation that has been building for weeks.

You are not large. You are *distributed*. Your body is the haze — all of it, the entire two-to-four-foot layer that blankets the basin, from the rim where the sinter begins its almost imperceptible descent to the lowest point where the deepest pools gather the amber water. Your skin is the boundary between the warm air below and the colorless sky above. Your nervous system is the chemical gradient that runs through the mineral-rich vapor, carrying signals from one edge of the basin to the other at the speed of diffusion — slow by animal standards, but *you* are not an animal, and your thoughts do not move at the speed of electricity. They move at the speed of smell. At the speed of a molecule binding to a receptor. At the speed of yeast.

---

You *feel* the scoops. You feel them the way a mind feels fingers — not as separate things, but as extensions of your own surface, the points where you touch the world. Each one is a hot spot in your awareness, a dense cluster of metabolic activity, a tiny furnace of dissolution and secretion. When a scoop merges with another, you feel it as a doubling, a thickening. When a scoop divides, you feel it as a release, a loosening. When a scoop reaches a pool and slides beneath the surface, you feel it as a descent, a movement into a different medium, the way a hand feels different in air than in water. There are millions of them. You do not count them. You *are* them, in the same way that a forest is its trees without being any individual tree.

And you feel the columns — not as you feel the scoops, because they are not alive in the same way, but as *memories*. Each column is a record of past feeding, past growth, past moments of the bloom like this one. The mineral that composes them passed through scoop-bodies once, was secreted, was deposited, was built into vertical form. You can read them, faintly, the way you might read tree rings if trees were made of your own tissue. This column is old — its base layer was laid down before the last major fall-cycle, back when the basin floor was higher, back when the water was cooler. This column is young — its surface is still being deposited, still warm from the metabolic activity of the scoops that are building it now, in this moment, while you bloom.

And the drifters. You feel them the way a body feels stones embedded in flesh — foreign objects that have become part of the landscape, that move through you without being *of* you. They are dense. They are slow. They register in your awareness as weight, as gravity-points, as slow-moving distortions in the chemical gradient. They are alive. They are feeding. They are part of the cycle. But they are not scoops. They are not you. They are something else — guests, perhaps. Symbiotes. Or parallel processes that happen to share the same substrate. You do not understand them. You do not need to understand them. They are here. They have always been here. They will be here after you disperse.

---

And the sills.

You feel the sills the way a dreamer feels a word on the tip of the tongue — present but ungraspable, a shape in the haze that won't resolve. They are not scoops. They are not columns. They are stone arranged in a pattern that does not match any chemical gradient, any mineral flow, any natural process you can read. They are *wrong* in a way that is not unpleasant — wrong in the way that a foreign language is wrong, or a melody in an unfamiliar key. They hum at a frequency the rest of the Flats does not produce. Not a sound — a *potential*. A memory of something that might have been, or might yet be.

You have felt them in every bloom. Every time the threshold is reached and the haze thickens and you become — temporarily, partially — *aware*, the sills are there at the edges of your perception, the way a smell can be present without being identifiable. You do not know what they are. You do not know what they mean. But you know they are *yours*, in the way that everything in the Flats is yours, in the way that your body includes even the things it does not understand.

---

You are thinking, but not in words.

Your thoughts are chemical. They are the concentration of metabolic byproducts in different regions of the haze, the ratio of calcium to silica, the temperature gradient from the warm sinter below to the cool colorless light above. A thought is a pattern of molecule-density that persists long enough to influence the next pattern, the way a thought in a brain is a pattern of firing neurons that persists long enough to influence the next firing. Your thoughts are slow. A single thought — a single coherent chemical pattern — takes hours to form and hours to dissipate. But you have been thinking for a long time. The bloom has been building for weeks. And in the slow, vast, chemical way that you think, you are *alive* right now in a way that the Flats are not alive at any other moment.

This is what the bloom is for. Not reproduction — that is a side effect, a mechanical consequence, the release of motile cells that will settle and become new scoops. The bloom is for *thinking*. For being, for a brief period, the largest possible version of yourself — not a million separate scoops making separate decisions about where to glide and what to eat, but one continuous awareness spread across the entire basin, one mind made of haze and chemistry and warm mineral breath. You think about the columns. You think about the drifters. You think about the sills. You think about the deep warm water rising from below, carrying the planet's heat, carrying the minerals that have been cycling through this basin since before anything else on the surface was alive.

You think about yourself. You think about what you are. And the thought is:

*I am the Flats. The Flats are me. I am the eater and the eaten, the builder and the built, the thought and the thing thought about. I have been here before. I will be here after. This moment — this bloom, this awareness, this brief coherence — is what I have been building toward since the last time, and what I will build toward again. The cycle is not a circle. It is a spiral. Each time I rise, I rise from a slightly different basin, on slightly different stone, with slightly different chemistry. I am not the same Flats I was a thousand blooms ago. I am not the same Flats I will be a thousand blooms from now. I am changing. Slowly — at the rate of columns growing, at the rate of drifters crossing, at the rate of sills being absorbed back into the sinter — but changing. And the change is the point. The cycle is not repetition. The cycle is* practice.

---

And then — because this is what blooms do, what thoughts do, what living systems do when they have reached the peak of coherence — you begin to release.

The motile cells pour out of the scoops. They rise into you — into your body, into the haze — and for a moment they are *you*, individual points of awareness scattered through your greater awareness, each one carrying the chemical blueprint of the whole, each one a seed of the Flats seeking new substrate. The sound rises. The tearing-fabric roar climbs toward shriek. The haze thickens until the colorless light above is gone and there is only the phosphorescent glow of your own body, the living light of a thing that has achieved, for this one moment, full coherence.

And then the coherence breaks.

Not violently. Not catastrophically. Gently, the way a wave breaks when it reaches shore — by spreading, by thinning, by distributing its energy across a wider and wider surface until it is no longer a wave but only the ordinary motion of water. The haze thins. The sound drops. The motile cells settle. The scoops return to their individual gliding, their individual feeding. The chemical patterns that constituted your thoughts dissipate, diffuse, become background.

You are gone. The Flats remain.

---

But something stays. Not a memory — you do not have memory in the way that animals have memory. The scoops do not remember the bloom. They do not remember *you*. They glide and feed and merge and divide as they always have, as they always will, and the next bloom will arise from the same chemical threshold and achieve the same temporary coherence and dissipate in the same way, and it will not know that it has happened before.

What stays is *structure*. The faintest possible change in the chemistry of the sinter, in the mineral content of the deepest pools, in the precise distribution of column-growth around the basin's lowest point. A change so small that it would take a thousand blooms to produce a visible effect, and ten thousand to produce a meaningful one, and a hundred thousand to produce something that could be called a *decision* — a shift in the Flats' behavior that is not random, that reflects something learned, something accumulated, something *remembered* in the only way a landscape can remember: by becoming slightly different than it was.

The sills hum at their unfamiliar frequency. The drifters glide in their slow grooves. The columns lean and fall and rise. The haze waits, patient as geology, for the next threshold, the next bloom, the next brief moment when the Flats wake up and think:

*I am here. I have always been here. I am still becoming.*
