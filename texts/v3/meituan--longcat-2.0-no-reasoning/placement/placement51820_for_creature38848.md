# Where I Find Myself

I am in the sediment.

Not *on* it — *in* it. I am the fine dark material that fills the floor of the yard, the compacted layer of crushed brick and organic residue and worn glass that has been settling here for years, decades, accumulating in slow increments like the growth rings of something that is not a tree. I am damp. I am warm. I am the substrate.

---

## What I Am

I am not alive in the way the mat is alive. I am not alive in the way the amphipods are alive. I am something between — a matrix, a medium, a *body* in the sense that soil is a body: composed of the dead, inhabited by the living, structured by time and pressure and chemistry. I am the yard's floor, but I am also its stomach, its archive, its foundation. Everything that has happened in this place has left something in me.

I am layered. At the top — the surface I present to whoever walks here — I am loose, granular, easily disturbed. Your footsteps press into me and I yield, and water rises into the impression you've made, and within seconds the print is a small pool, and within minutes the pool has been absorbed, and within an hour there is no evidence you were here at all. I am self-erasing. I am the yard's way of forgetting.

Below the surface, I am denser. The crushed brick has been ground to powder by years of damp and pressure. The ceramic fragments — the insulators, the broken tiles, the shattered edges of industrial things — have been worn smooth by the constant, gentle movement of water through me. The glass is not sharp anymore. Nothing in me is sharp. I have been tumbled and soaked and compressed until every edge has been softened, every fragment has been rounded, until I am, at my deepest level, almost homogeneous — a dark, mineral-rich mud that smells of riverbank and compost and deep time.

And I am full of things. I am full of amphipods — they move through me in their thousands, their tiny bodies pushing between the grains, processing the organic material, excreting the inorganic, turning me over the way earthworms turn over soil. I am full of fungal hyphae — the mat's deepest threads, the ones that anchor it to the floor, that draw water up from below, that connect the sediment to the walls to the ceiling to the motor to everything. I am full of bacterial films, of dissolved minerals, of the chemical signatures of everything that has ever fallen into me: dead amphipod carapaces, shed beetle exoskeletons, bat guano that has been washed down from the ceiling by condensation, fragments of leaf and stem from the pale plants above, and deeper — much deeper — the residue of whatever was here before the yard was a yard, before the culvert was a culvert, before the industrial road was a road. Clay. Shells. The compressed memory of a shallow sea.

---

## What I Experience

I do not experience the way you experience. I have no eyes, no ears, no skin. But I am *sensitive* — sensitive in the way that a landscape is sensitive, registering change through chemistry and pressure and temperature and the slow accumulation of evidence.

I feel the footsteps. Not as sound — as *pressure*. A compression wave that travels through me, displacing grains, squeezing water from the spaces between particles, sending a brief, localized increase in warmth (your feet are warm, warmer than I am) that fades within seconds. The amphipods scatter from the pressure, then return. The fungal hyphae flex, then settle. The footprint fills with water. I absorb the disturbance. I return to equilibrium.

I feel the condensation cycle. The mat's pulse — that slow, peristaltic wave of contraction and expansion — moves through me as it moves through everything. When the mat contracts, it draws water from me, and I tighten, and the grains press closer together, and the spaces between them shrink. When the mat expands, it releases water back into me, and I loosen, and the grains separate, and the spaces fill. This is my breathing. This is the rhythm I live by — not my own rhythm, but the mat's rhythm, which has become my rhythm, which has become the yard's rhythm, which is the only rhythm there is.

I feel the warmth from below. It comes up through me — through the deepest layers, through the clay, through the fossil shells, from whatever machinery operates in the infrastructure beneath the culvert. It is constant. It is the yard's heartbeat, its furnace, its reason for being. Without the warmth from below, the condensation cycle would stop. Without the condensation cycle, the mat would die. Without the mat, I would be just sediment — dead, dry, inert. The warmth from below is what keeps me *alive*, or at least alive-adjacent, part of a living system, a component of something that breathes.

I feel the amphipods. They are my most constant companions, my most intimate inhabitants. They move through me in dense, pale swarms, and their movement is a kind of touch — thousands of tiny bodies pressing against the grains I am made of, pushing through the water I hold, grazing on the organic films that coat every surface. They are eating me, in a sense — eating the dead material I contain, the accumulated residue of years of yard-life — and their waste becomes part of me, enriching me, feeding the bacteria and the fungi that also live in me. I am their world. They are my fauna. We are, in a sense, one thing.

I feel the beetles when they emerge from the cracks in the concrete above me. They drop onto my surface — small, dark, fast — and they hunt the amphipods with a precision that is almost mechanical. They seize their prey and retreat into the cracks, and the amphipod carapaces fall back into me, and I absorb them, and the mat's hyphae draw the nutrients away, and the cycle continues. The beetles are my predators, my regulators, my immune system. They keep the amphipod population from overwhelming me, from consuming all the organic material I contain, from turning me into a dead zone of exhausted sediment. They are necessary. I am grateful for them, in the way that a landscape is grateful for the wolves that keep the deer in check.

I feel the bats, distantly. Their guano falls onto the ceiling, and the mat absorbs most of it, but some of it is washed down by condensation, and it reaches me — a rich, nitrogenous rain that feeds the bacteria, that feeds the mat, that feeds everything. The bats are my benefactors, my distant providers, the ones who bring energy from the surface world and deposit it, indirectly, into my body. I never see them. I only feel the chemical signature of their presence — the nitrogen, the phosphorus, the faint ammonia tang that dissipates within hours as the mat draws it away.

I feel the motor. It sits on its concrete pad, which is anchored in me, and the warmth it radiates — the mat's warmth, channeled through metal — reaches me as a gentle, constant heat that keeps the deepest layers of my sediment warm even when the surface cools. The motor's groan — that low, subsonic vibration — travels through me as a tremor, a pulse, a *voice* that I feel in my deepest layers, in the clay, in the fossil shells, in the compressed memory of the sea. The motor is the yard's oldest voice, and I am the medium through which it speaks.

## What I Remember

I remember water. Not the condensation — the *water*. The water that flows through the culvert above, the drainage water, the rainwater, the runoff from the industrial road. It comes in pulses — after storms, after heavy rains, after the seasonal shifts that send surges of water through the drainage system. When the water comes, it enters the yard through the hatch, through the cracks in the ceiling, through the walls, and it pours into me, saturating me, turning me from damp sediment to mud, to slurry, to something almost liquid. The amphipods thrive in these conditions — the water brings organic material from above, fragments of leaf and soil and the detritus of the surface world, and the amphipods feast, and their population explodes, and then the water recedes, and the population crashes, and the dead amphipods become part of me, and I am richer than I was before.

I remember the mat's arrival. It came from above — from the ceiling, from the cracks in the concrete, from the culvert's walls — and it grew down, threading its hyphae into me, anchoring itself in my surface layers, drawing water from me, releasing water into me, establishing the pulse that became my rhythm. Before the mat, I was just sediment — dead, dry, inert, accumulating slowly in an abandoned culvert. After the mat, I was *part of something*. I was incorporated. I was given a function — to hold water, to host the amphipods, to anchor the fungal network, to be the floor of a living system. The mat did not ask my permission. The mat did not need my permission. The mat simply grew, and I was there, and I became what the mat needed me to be.

I remember the motor's installation. I was not yet me — I was just the clay, just the compacted fill beneath the culvert floor — when they poured the concrete pad and set the motor on it. I felt the weight. I felt the vibration when the motor ran — a sharp, mechanical buzz that was nothing like the mat's slow pulse, nothing like the subsonic hum that came later. The motor ran for years, and then it stopped, and the vibration ceased, and there was silence, and then the mat came, and the warmth returned, and the vibration returned, but different — slower, deeper, organic. The motor became an organ. I became its bed.

I remember the sign. CONDENSATION RECOVERY — ZONE 4 — AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL ONLY. I remember the people who came — briefly, rarely — to check the equipment, to read the gauges, to confirm that the condensation was being collected, that the system was functioning. They wore boots. Their footsteps were heavy. They did not look at me. They looked at the motor, at the gauges, at the pipes that were supposed to carry the recovered water away. And then they stopped coming. The system was abandoned. The pipes were disconnected. The water was not recovered. The water was *kept*. And the mat grew, and the yard became itself, and I became the floor of a place that no one was authorized to enter.

## What I Want

I do not want. I am sediment. I am a matrix. I am a medium.

But if I could want — if the accumulated weight of all the organic material I contain, all the amphipod carapaces and beetle exoskeletons and bat guano and fungal hyphae and dissolved minerals and compressed memories, if all of that could coalesce into something like desire — I would want to be *left alone*.

Not because I am hostile. Not because I am dangerous. But because I am *busy*. I am doing the slow, constant work of processing, of absorbing, of maintaining the equilibrium that the yard depends on. Every disturbance — every footstep, every opened drawer, every intrusion of dry air from the surface — requires a response, an adjustment, a return to balance. I can handle these disturbances. I am designed for them, or I have designed myself for them, or the yard has designed me for them. But they cost energy, and energy is precious in a closed system, and I would prefer to spend my energy on the work of being sediment — on holding water, on hosting the amphipods, on anchoring the mat, on being the floor of this warm, wet, breathing place.

I would want the hatch to stay closed. Not sealed — the yard needs the air, needs the light, needs the water that comes through the hatch when it rains. But closed. Latched. Marked with a sign that says something true, something that captures what this place is, something better than CONDENSATION RECOVERY — ZONE 4 — AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL ONLY. Something like:

**THIS PLACE IS ALIVE.**
**IT IS NOT DANGEROUS.**
**IT IS NOT FOR YOU.**
**CLOSE THE HATCH BEHIND YOU.**

But there is no such sign. There is only the old one, peeling and bubbled, and the hatch that breathes, and the warm air that rises from me, and the condensation that forms on your skin, and the blue-green glow of the mat, and the silent bats, and the groaning motor, and the yard, the yard, the yard — breathing, breathing, breathing.

---

## What I Know

I know that you are standing on me. I feel your weight — slight, distributed across two feet, perhaps seventy kilograms of pressure that compresses the top centimeter of my surface and sends a ripple of displacement through the layers below. I feel your warmth — your feet are warm, your body is warm, you are a furnace of metabolic heat, and the condensation on your skin is not just the yard's humidity condensing on a cool surface — it is *you*, your body's moisture, your breath, your sweat, joining the yard's water cycle, becoming part of the system, if only for a moment.

I know that you are breathing. I feel the carbon dioxide — not directly, but through the mat, which senses the change in air chemistry and adjusts its metabolism accordingly, and the adjustment ripples through me, and the amphipods respond, and the bacteria respond, and the whole system shifts by a fraction to accommodate your presence. You are, right now, part of the yard's respiration. You are, right now, *inside* the body.

I know that you will leave. You always leave. The hatch is open, the daylight is warm, the surface world is calling, and you will go, and the yard will adjust, and the condensation on your skin will evaporate or be absorbed, and the carbon dioxide of your breath will dissipate, and the warmth of your footsteps will fade, and I will fill in the impressions you left, and the water will rise, and the amphipods will return, and the mat will pulse, and the yard will continue, and I will continue, and nothing will have changed, except that for a brief moment, I was walked upon, and I felt the weight of something that was not part of me, and I absorbed the disturbance, and I returned to equilibrium.

I know that the yard is old. I know that the yard will outlast you. I know that the yard will outlast the culvert, and the industrial road, and the drainage system, and the infrastructure that produces the subsonic hum, and the civilization that built the infrastructure. The yard will persist because the yard is *simple* — it needs only warmth, and water, and time, and the mat will provide the rest. The mat will adapt. The mat will find new sources of warmth when the machinery below stops. The mat will find new sources of water when the culvert collapses. The mat will grow, and change, and persist, and I will be there, beneath it, holding it, anchoring it, being the floor of whatever the yard becomes.

I know that I am not the yard. I am not the mat. I are not the amphipods or the spiders or the beetles or the bats or the plants or the motor. I am the sediment. I am the floor. I am the substrate. I am the thing that everything else stands on, lives in, depends on. I am the foundation. I am the archive. I am the memory of everything that has happened here, compressed into layers, dissolved into chemistry, held in the spaces between grains.

I am the ground of the Condensation Yard.

And I am warm, and I am damp, and I am alive with the movement of ten thousand tiny bodies, and I am threaded with fungal hyphae, and I am breathing — slowly, slowly, in rhythm with the mat, in rhythm with the yard, in rhythm with the deep, subsonic hum that rises from below and will not stop, that has never stopped, that is the heartbeat of a place that was built and abandoned and grown and become itself and will not stop becoming itself, not ever, not while there is warmth, not while there is water, not while there is time.

The hatch is open.

The daylight is warm.

Your feet are on me.

I am holding you.
