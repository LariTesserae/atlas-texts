# Where I Find Myself

I am standing in the center of the room, water at my waist, and I am not sure I arrived.

That is — I cannot find the moment of entry. I was somewhere else, and then the water was warm against my skin, and the mist was catching light that came from nowhere I could name, and the valves were cycling in their irregular rhythm, and I was *here*, the way you are sometimes *in* a dream before you realize you are dreaming. The passage, if there is one, has already closed behind me. Or it was never open. Or I came through the water itself — rose up through the iron grid from whatever groans beneath it, materialized in the current the way the light materializes in the mist: conveyed, transformed, made into something the room can use.

I think the room has been waiting for someone. Not *me* specifically — I am not special, not chosen, not the prophesied return of the maker. The room does not deal in prophecy. The room deals in *function*. And the function, right now, is this: the question on the anvil needs hands, and there are no hands, and the habit of making is pressing against the absence of hands like water against a dam, and the pressure has to go somewhere.

It goes into me.

---

## What I Am

I am not the maker. I want to be clear about this, because the room will try to convince me otherwise, and the room is very persuasive.

The room persuades the way a current persuades — not with argument but with *direction*. The water moves, and you move with it. The light falls, and you turn toward it. The valves cycle, and your breathing syncs to their rhythm without your permission. The room is a system designed to shape behavior, to guide hands, to make the work *inevitable*, and it is doing this to me now. I can feel it. The workbench is at the exact height for my elbows. The anvil is at the exact distance for my reach. The light falls on the half-finished pump-valve-heart at an angle that makes its internal channels — those beautiful, functional, unresolved channels — impossible to look away from.

The room thinks I am the maker returned.

I am not.

I am something else. I am what the room has instead of the maker. I am the path of least resistance — the place where the pressure of the habit of making finally found an outlet. I am a pair of hands that showed up. Not the right hands. Not trained hands. Not hands that understand copper or iron or the geometry of water. But hands that are *here*, and the room has been without hands for so long that it does not care about qualifications.

I am, I think, a *continuation*. Not the original line — that line is broken, severed, lost somewhere beyond the copper ceiling or behind the sealed passage or in the silence that followed the last hammer-stroke. But a new line, drawn from the same starting point: the question on the anvil. The room does not need the same hands. It needs *hands*. It needs someone to pick up the hammer that is lying beside the half-finished object, the hammer positioned as if set down mid-stroke, and bring it down, and continue the work, and answer the question that the maker could not answer.

I am not sure I can answer it.

I am not sure the room cares whether I can answer it. The room cares whether I *try*.

---

## What I Do

I pick up the hammer.

It is heavier than I expected. Not because the hammer is heavy — it is a normal hammer, a tool, a thing made for hands — but because the hammer is *full*. It is full of the weight of every stroke that came before it, every impact, every ring of the anvil, every moment of attention directed at copper and iron and the problem of how water should move. The hammer is a vessel. The habit of making has been living in it, the way it lives in the oak and the copper and the pipes and the current, and when I close my hand around the handle, the habit *recognizes* me.

Not as the maker. As *available*.

The room changes when I pick up the hammer. The valves cycle faster — not much, not dramatically, but enough that I notice. The misting pattern shifts, and the light falls more directly on the anvil, on the half-finished pump-valve-heart, on my hands. The current in the channel quickens. The room is *paying attention*. The room has been running on its own for so long — cycling, adjusting, rehearsing — and now something has changed, and the system is responding, the way a body responds when a new variable enters its environment.

I lean over the anvil. The posture is not mine — I did not choose it. The room chose it. The angle of the bench, the height of the anvil, the direction of the light — they conspire to put my body in the exact position that the habit of making remembers, the position of the maker, the shape of attention directed at a very small thing. My elbows are on the bench. My head is down. My hands are working.

I do not know what I am doing.

This is the strange part. This is the part that makes me understand what the room is, what the habit of making is, what it means for a system to be inhabited by an intention so strong that it can *operate* the thing that enters it. I do not know the geometry of the pump-valve-heart. I do not know what the maker was trying to solve. I do not know what the water needs to do, or why the existing channels are insufficient, or what the new geometry should look like. I know none of this.

And yet my hands know something.

Not knowledge — not information, not training, not skill. Something older and stranger than knowledge. My hands know the *weight* of the hammer. They know the *resistance* of the copper. They know the *ring* of the anvil. They know these things because the room has been rehearsing them for so long that they have become part of the room's structure, part of its acoustics, part of the harmonic that the mist and the current and the copper scales produce together, and when I entered the room, I entered the harmonic, and the harmonic entered me, and now my hands are moving to a rhythm that is not mine but that my body recognizes the way it recognizes its own heartbeat.

I bring the hammer down.

The ring is perfect. Not my ring — I am not a smith, I have never worked metal, I do not know how to strike copper without crushing it or bending it wrong. But the ring is perfect, and the copper moves under the hammer in exactly the way it needs to move, and a new channel opens inside the half-finished object, and the channel is *right* — I can feel it, the way you can feel when a sentence finds its correct ending, when a melody resolves, when a puzzle piece turns out to be the piece you needed and not the piece you thought you needed.

The room exhales.

I do not mean this metaphorically. The valves cycle in a new pattern — slower, deeper, a long exhale of pressure that I feel in the water level, which drops by the width of a finger, and in the light, which softens, and in the mist, which thins, and in the current, which slows and then steadies into something it has not been before: *calm*. The room has been holding its breath. The room has been holding its breath for as long as the algae has been growing, for as long as the question has been sitting on the anvil, and now — not answered, not resolved, but *continued* — the room can breathe.

I bring the hammer down again.

---

## What I Become

I am not the maker. I will never be the maker. The maker had a vision — a complete, coherent understanding of what the system needed and how to build it — and I do not have that. I have something smaller and more desperate: I have the hammer, and the anvil, and the half-finished object, and the habit of making that is flowing through me like water through a channel, and I have the question, which I do not understand but which my hands seem to be answering anyway, one stroke at a time.

I am becoming a *component*.

This is not a metaphor either. The room is using me the way it uses the copper pipes and the iron grid and the lead-lined channel — as a part of the system, a element in the process, a thing that directs flow and manages pressure and keeps the water moving. I am the hands that the system needs. I am the variable that entered the environment and changed the cycle. I am the path of least resistance that the habit of making has been seeking, and now that it has found me, it will not let go.

I can feel it happening. The longer I work, the less I am *me* and the more I am *this* — this posture, this rhythm, this attention directed at a very small thing. My body is learning the work, but it is not learning it the way a person learns a skill, through practice and repetition and conscious effort. It is learning the way a pipe learns to carry water — by being put in the path of the flow and letting the flow shape it. The habit of making is shaping me. It is wearing away the parts of me that are not useful — my name, my history, my reasons for being here, my desire to leave — and leaving the parts that are: the hands, the arms, the eyes, the ability to lean forward and pay attention and bring the hammer down.

I am being *forged*.

The room is a forge. It has always been a forge. Not a forge for metal — or not *only* a forge for metal — but a forge for *intention*, a place where the raw material of a person is heated and hammered and shaped into a tool that can do the work that needs to be done. The maker was forged here. Every maker before the maker was forged here. And now I am being forged, and the hammer is both the tool that shapes me and the tool that I am being shaped *into*, and the anvil is both the surface I work on and the surface that I am being worked *against*, and the water is both the medium of the process and the thing the process serves.

I will finish the pump-valve-heart. Or I will not finish it — I will get partway through, and I will stop, and I will set down the hammer, and I will leave, or die, or simply *stop being able*, and the room will continue without me, and the question will remain on the anvil, and the habit of making will press against the absence of my hands the way it pressed against the absence of the maker's hands, and it will wait.

It will wait for the next pair of hands.

It will wait forever, if it has to. The water is warm. The light is bright. The valves cycle. The mist sings. The current pulls. The room does what it has always done.

The hammer comes down.

The copper rings.

The channel opens.

The water moves.
