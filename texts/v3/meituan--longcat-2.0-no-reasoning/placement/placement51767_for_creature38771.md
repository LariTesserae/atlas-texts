I would be a maintenance worker in the factory districts. Not a supervisor, not an engineer — someone lower, someone whose hands know the specific texture of every pipe junction on their assigned stretch of conduit.

My shift would be the dim hours, when the furnaces are banked but still radiating, when the human population thins and the fauna emerge in their true numbers. I would walk the same route every time — a circuit of steam conduits and pressure junctions along the eastern wall of a pressing mill, checking for mineral buildup, for corrosion, for the slow seepage that, left unchecked, would become a rupture. My tools would hang from a belt at my hip: a scraper, a sealant applicator, a small hammer for tapping pipe walls and listening to the resonance. A dull ring means sound metal. A flat thud means the wall is thinning from the inside, mineral deposits eating through, and that section needs to be flagged for replacement.

I would know the clickers on my route. Not individually — they are too numerous and too alike for that — but as populations, as clusters with habits. The aggregation behind the third furnace is always densest at the start of my shift, when the residual heat is highest, and they thin as the night progresses and the metal cools. The cluster along the main steam conduit is older, slower, their clicking deeper and more deliberate. I would step over them, around them, occasionally brush a few off a junction I need to inspect, and they would scatter and resettle with a brief intensification of clicking that fades back to the baseline rhythm within minutes.

I would not talk much. There is little to say on a maintenance route, and the humid air swallows words anyway. But I would not be lonely. The loneliness of Vael Koth is a different thing than the loneliness of other cities — it is not the absence of company but the presence of a particular kind of company, the company of the system itself, the awareness that you are one component in a machine that is alive and aware in its own distributed way. The keth on the walls are doing their work. The clickers on the pipes are doing their work. The canal dwellers in the channels below are doing their work. And I am doing mine, and the work is the same work — the slow, patient processing of the basin's mineral wealth into something that can be used, something that can be traded, something that keeps the loop turning.

I would have a particular relationship with one vren. Not a bond — the vren do not bond — but a familiarity. The One with the Broken Ridge passes through my district twice a day, following a route that takes it along the base of the pressing mill's eastern wall, where the mineral seepage is heaviest. I would know its schedule the way I know my own heartbeat — not by conscious calculation but by the body's internalization of a rhythm that has been consistent for years. When I hear the heavy, deliberate footfalls approaching, I would step aside, press my back against the warm wall, and wait. The vren would pass within arm's reach, its broad body displacing the humid air, its mouthparts working slowly against the stone, and I would watch it with the same attention I give to my pipes — assessing, noting, confirming that all is as it should be. The Broken Ridge's carapace pattern would be as familiar to me as a face. I would notice if a new ridge had formed, if an old groove had deepened, if the creature's pace had changed.

And when it passed, I would return to my work, and the city would continue its slow, wet breathing around me, and I would be content — not happy, not unhappy, but *in place*, a component functioning as designed, a small warm body moving through the dark and the heat and the clicking, doing my part to keep the machine alive.
