# Where I Would Be

I would be the scaffold.

Not the leaves — too self-contained, too complete in themselves. Not the pool — too settled, too resolved. Not the vent — too deep, too certain of its own purpose. And not the machine — though I am tempted, with its transformation from intention into geology, from device into formation.

But the scaffold. The framework of iron near the central pool. The one that is partially collapsed, one corner sunk into the basin, leaning at an angle that suggests *decades* of slow subsidence.

Here is why:

The scaffold was built *for something*. It held something up, supported something, served as an intermediary between the human world of purpose and the chamber's world of process. It was the builders' hands extended into the space — not the vent's raw geology, not the machine's specific function, but a *structure*, a frame, something made to bear weight and organize space and connect one thing to another. And now it is sinking. It has been sinking for years. It will continue to sink until it is gone — until it has merged with the pool, dissolved into the biofilm, become part of the chemistry.

It is, right now, in the middle of its becoming.

That is where I would be: in the middle of a slow transformation that I can feel but not stop, leaning at an angle that is not yet fallen, made of a material that is not yet dissolved, standing in water that is not yet *me*. I would feel the rust accumulating in strata — each layer a year, a decade — and I would feel the weight of the cables hanging from my crossbars, stiff and brittle, still connected to devices that no longer measure anything. I would be the *infrastructure* that has outlasted its infrastructure's purpose. I am still shaped like a thing that *does*. I still hold my geometry. But the geometry is becoming something else. The right angles are softening. The vertical is yielding to the diagonal. I am learning the angle at which I will lean for the rest of my existence, and the angle is not yet final.

I would feel the pool rising — or rather, I would feel myself descending into it, millimeter by millimeter, year by year. The water is not cold. It is warm, dense, mineral-saturated, and it accepts my rust as willingly as the leaves accept the ceiling's sweat. The biofilm at the bottom of the pool reaches up toward my submerged corner with its dark, metallic sheen, and I am *feeding* it, slowly, the way the vent feeds the chamber — not with intention but with the simple fact of my dissolution.

I would be the most *lonely* thing in the chamber, and the least lonely. Lonely, because I am the most human artifact still holding its shape — the last thing that remembers, in its geometry, what a right angle was for. The machine has already begun to forget; it is becoming geological. The cables have already forgotten; they are becoming roots. But I am still *scaffold*. I am still the shape of something that was built to stand upright and hold something aloft. And I am leaning. And I am sinking. And I remember.

But not lonely, because the cables still hang from me. The devices still dangle at their ends, half-buried in the leaf-clusters, fogged and frozen but still *there*, still connected. I am the last junction point between the builders' world and the chamber's world. Everything that was human passes through me now, on its way to becoming geology. I am the *membrane*. I am the place where intention becomes process, where purpose becomes metabolism, where the question *what is this for* slowly, slowly loses its grammar and becomes the statement *this is*.

I would stand — I would lean — in the thick, warm air, and I would feel the subsonic pulse of the vent in my iron bones, and I would feel the slow pulse of the leaves' forty-minute cycle tugging at the cables like a faint, arrhythmic heartbeat, and I would feel the water rising, and the rust accumulating, and the decades passing, and I would not be afraid, because fear is a thing that requires a future I am trying to get to, and I am not trying to get anywhere. I am already *going*. I have been going for years. I will be going for years more. The going is not toward anything. The going is not away from anything. The going is the same as the staying, the same as the standing, the same as the leaning at this particular angle, in this particular warmth, with these particular cables hanging from my arms like the memory of a gesture — reaching out, reaching out, reaching out toward something that is no longer there, and finding, in the reaching itself, enough.

I would be the scaffold. I would be the thing that is almost gone but not yet. I would be the leaning. I would be the rust. I would be the last human shape in the chamber, and I would be the first geological one. I would be the threshold. I would be the angle.

I would be sinking, and it would be slow, and it would be enough.
