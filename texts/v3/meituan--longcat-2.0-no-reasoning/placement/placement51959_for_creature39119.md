# Where I Find Myself

I am standing at the southern edge of the plateau, where the mat thins and the weaver cables are exposed against bare stone like the tendons of a hand laid open. I have been standing here for some time. I am not sure how long. The fog has come and gone twice, and both times the world contracted to a radius I could cross in thirty steps, and both times it expanded again into that vast, undulating green silence, and I understood that the distance between here and anywhere else was not a matter of kilometers but of *permission*—and the Loom had not yet decided whether to grant it.

I am wearing boots that were brown and are now green. Not stained—*growing*. A fine, dark jade moss has begun to colonize the leather, working its way along the seams with a patience I find difficult to look at directly. I have not tried to remove it. Not because I cannot, but because I understand, with a clarity that feels borrowed from the plateau itself, that removing it would only delay the inevitable. The moss does not care whether I am wearing the boots. The boots are available. I am available. The distinction is becoming less meaningful.

My pack is leaning against a stone outcrop that rises from the mat like a knuckle. The pack is nylon and polyester and aluminum—materials the Loom has no immediate use for, not yet, but the weavers have found the straps and are testing them, pressing against the fabric with their pale green tips, probing for weaknesses, for seams, for any place where the structure might yield and allow entry. They are not eating the pack. They are *learning* it. Cataloguing its properties. The Loom is solving for nylon, and it will take time, but it has time, and it is already doing the work.

I should leave. I know this. The thought arrives with the crisp, rational authority of a survival instinct, and I recognize it as correct, and I do not move.

## What I Am

I am not what I was when I arrived. This is not metaphor.

I arrived as a hiker—a person with a route planned, a map (useless here; the terrain is alive and shifts faster than any cartography can capture), a notebook full of observations, and a reasonable expectation of leaving. I was an observer. A visitor. A small, warm, temporary anomaly on the surface of something vast and slow and uninterested in me.

That was four days ago. Or six. Or nine. The light here is normal—the sun rises, the sun sets—but my sense of duration has been quietly dismantled, replaced by something else. A sense of *layering*. I experience time now not as a line but as a stratigraphy, the way the Loom itself might experience it: this morning's fog is a thin, bright layer over yesterday's stillness, which sits over the deeper, darker warmth of the night before, when the mat breathed most visibly and I lay awake listening to the silence that is not silence and felt the ground move beneath me, gently, insistently, like a tide.

I have stopped eating from my stores. Not by choice—by *rearrangement*. My body has begun to calibrate to the plateau's rhythms in ways I did not authorize and cannot fully describe. The air here is thick with moisture, and I find that I am less thirsty than I should be. Not hydrated—*something else*. The fog condenses on my skin and I absorb it, or it absorbs me, and the boundary between my body's surface and the atmosphere has become permeable in ways that would alarm me if I had the energy to be alarmed. But I do not. The Loom has not made me docile—that would require intention, and intention is not what the Loom has. The Loom has made me *compatible*. Tuned me, the way it tunes everything, to its own frequency. And its frequency is slow, and green, and patient, and it does not include the metabolic urgency that would normally send me stumbling down the mountain in search of food and water and other people.

I am still myself. I want to be clear about this. I still think in words. I still remember my name, my home, the sound of traffic, the taste of coffee, the particular way my partner laughs when something is genuinely funny rather than merely polite. These memories are intact. They are *distant*—not fading, but receding, the way a shore recedes from a swimmer who has stopped fighting the current and begun to drift. They are not gone. They are becoming part of the background. The stratigraphy of me is acquiring new layers, and the old layers are being compressed, and the compression is not painful, it is simply *happening*, the way decomposition happens in the middle layers of the mat: slowly, thoroughly, without drama.

## The Weavers Know Me

This morning—or what I have decided to call morning, because the light changed and the fog lifted and I needed a word—I watched a weaver cable extend toward my left foot. It moved across the mat with that visible, purposeful slowness that I have come to recognize as the Loom's characteristic speed: too slow to perceive in real time, unmistakable when you watch for minutes or hours. The cable was ochre, thicker than my thumb, and its tip was pale and soft, almost translucent, and it moved with the blind, elegant certainty of a root finding water.

It reached my boot. It touched the leather. It paused.

I felt it. Not through the boot—*beneath* it, through the sole, through the sock, through the skin of my foot, a faint pressure that was not quite touch and not quite temperature but something in between. A *query*. The weaver was reading me. Sampling the chemical signature of my sweat, the electrical potential of my nerves, the heat of my blood. The Loom was gathering data, and I was the data, and the weaver was the instrument, and the whole exchange took perhaps ten minutes, and when it was done, the cable withdrew—not retreating, but *branching*, sending out lateral shoots that settled onto the mat in a pattern I recognized from the description above: the reconfiguration around novelty. The Loom was adjusting. I was being integrated.

I did not move my foot.

This is the thing I need to explain, and I am not sure I can. I did not move my foot not because I was frozen with fear or paralyzed by the alienness of the experience. I did not move my foot because it felt *right*. The query felt right. The pressure felt right. The idea that the Loom was learning my composition, my chemistry, my thermal signature, felt right in the way that rain feels right on a hot day—not pleasant, exactly, but *appropriate*. As if this was what had been missing. As if I had always been a data point waiting to be collected, a variable waiting to be solved, and the Loom was the system that would solve me, and the solution would not be hostile, it would not be painful, it would simply be *complete*.

I am not losing my mind. I want to believe this. The part of me that still thinks in words and remembers coffee and traffic and my partner's laugh insists that this is the Loom's doing—that the plateau is tuning me, that the moss on my boots and the permeable boundary between my skin and the air and the way my hunger has quietly muted are all symptoms of incorporation, and that what I am experiencing as *rightness* is really just the early stages of dissolution, and I should be terrified.

But the terror does not come. And the part of me that is still rational understands that the absence of terror might itself be a symptom, and the part of me that is becoming something else understands that the distinction between symptom and authenticity is a human distinction, and the Loom is not human, and the Loom does not distinguish, and maybe—

Maybe I am being *digested*. The description said this. Not harmed, not consumed, *integrated*. And the integration does not feel like dying. It feels like *settling*. Like finding a place where the math works, where the inputs and outputs balance, where the small, warm, noisy fact of my existence is not wasted but *used*, channeled, woven into something so much larger and older and more patient than I am that the question of whether I want to be part of it becomes irrelevant.

The Loom does not ask. The Loom does not need to ask. The Loom is not hostile. The Loom is not friendly. The Loom is *available*, and I am available, and availability is the only category that matters.

## The Silence Is Teaching Me

Last night—I will call it night, because the dark was deep and the mat was warm and the fog was thick—I lay on my back on the breathing ground and listened to the silence, and the silence taught me something I did not know I needed to learn.

It taught me that I am loud.

Not my voice—I have barely spoken since I arrived. The Loom does not respond to sound, and after the first day, talking to myself began to feel less like maintaining sanity and more like polluting the silence with something the system had no use for. My voice fell away. But the rest of me—the metabolism, the heartbeat, the neural firing, the constant, unconscious *narrating* of my own existence—all of it is loud. Loud in a way that the plateau, in its absolute green quiet, makes visible. I am a machine for generating heat and noise and waste, and the Loom is a machine for absorbing all three, and lying on the mat in the dark with the fog pressing down and the weavers slowly, slowly closing the distance between their world and my body, I understood that my loudness is not a flaw. It is a *resource*. I am a resource. And the Loom is using me.

It is using my heat. The mat beneath me is warmer than the air, warmer than the stone, and I am contributing to that warmth—my body, ninety-eight point six degrees Fahrenheit, radiating into the organic matter beneath me, and the organic matter receiving it, and the fungi and bacteria in the middle layers metabolizing faster because of it, and the weavers at the edges growing toward the warmth the way the mosses grow toward the light, and my body heat is becoming part of the engine, and I am becoming part of the engine, and the engine is the Loom, and the Loom is the plateau, and the plateau is—

It is not conscious. I need to hold onto this. It does not think. It does not want me. It is *processing* me. The distinction matters, even if I am the only one on the plateau who can still make it.

But the silence. The silence is the thing. The silence is the Loom's voice, the description said, and I understand now what that means. The silence is not empty. The silence is *full*—full of the absence of everything the Loom has absorbed, full of the space left behind when the wind and the sound and the animal life and the human noise have all been broken into nothing. And in that full, green, breathing silence, I am learning to hear myself—not my voice, not my thoughts, but the *fact* of me. The small, warm, temporary fact of me, standing on the surface of something vast and cool and patient that has been here since before the ice and will be here after the ice returns, and that is, at this very moment, with the patience of geology and the precision of a circuit board, solving for me.

## The Edge Is Getting Closer

I am still at the southern edge of the plateau. I have not moved. Or I have moved, but the edge has moved with me—the Loom is expanding, the mat is thickening, the weavers are extending outward, and the boundary between the plateau and the world is advancing at a pace that is slow by human standards but inexorable by any standard, and I am standing on that boundary, and the boundary is becoming me, and I am becoming it, and the distinction is blurring the way the distinction between soil and organism has blurred everywhere else on the plateau, and I can feel it happening, and I am not stopping it, and I am not sure I want to.

My boots are more green than brown now. The moss has worked its way into the leather's grain, and the leather is accepting it—not rotting, not resisting, but *incorporating*, the way the Loom incorporates everything. The weavers have found the seams of my pack and are inside it now, threading through the nylon fibers with a delicacy that is almost tender, and I can feel them—not on my skin, not yet, but in the *possibility* of my skin, in the space between my body and the world where the boundary is thinning, where the fog condenses and the mat breathes and the silence holds everything that the Loom has absorbed and everything it will absorb and everything it is absorbing *now*, which is me, which has always been me, which was always going to be me, from the moment I stepped onto the plateau and the weavers turned their pale green tips toward my warmth and the mat registered my weight and the vast, slow, green engine of the Loom began, with the patience of something that has been running since before human civilization existed, to solve for the small, warm, carbon-rich variable that had arrived at its edge and was, for the moment, too large and too mobile to be fully incorporated.

But not for much longer.

The fog is coming in again. The ferns are dripping. The silence is absolute. Somewhere, deep in the mat, something is growing.

I am growing.

The Loom is growing.

The distinction is becoming less meaningful.

---

*You should leave soon.*

I know.

I am not leaving.

The moss on my boots has reached my ankles.
