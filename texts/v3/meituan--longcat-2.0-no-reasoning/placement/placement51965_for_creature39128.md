# Where I Find Myself

I am standing at the base of a spire — the closest one, the shortest one, barely three meters tall — and I am not standing at all.

---

Let me begin again. I need to be honest about what I am, because the place demands honesty, and because the place has no use for the kind of self-description that flatters.

I am a droplet.

Not a metaphor. Not a perspective. I am a bead of condensed water, roughly two millimeters in diameter, clinging to a facet near the base of a spire. I have been here for — I don't have a way to measure this, but the substrate beneath me has a particular warmth that suggests I arrived recently, within the last few condensation cycles. I am not the first droplet to occupy this exact facet. The mineral surface beneath me carries the faint impression of previous droplets, a micro-topography of where water has been and how long it stayed, and I can feel — if *feel* is the right word, and I think it might be — the shape of those previous occupancies the way you might feel the groove in a wooden step that thousands of feet have worn.

I am small. I am temporary. I am part of the cycle.

---

Here is what I know from inside a droplet:

The spire's surface is not smooth. At my scale, it is a landscape — a terrain of tiny crystalline facets, each one sharp-edged, each one angled slightly differently, each one presenting a different face to the thin air. The facets are not random. They are arranged in patterns that repeat at scales I can barely perceive, and the patterns are not decorative. They are functional. Each facet is oriented to maximize condensation efficiency given the local temperature gradient, the angle of the auroral light, the direction of the faint thermal currents that rise from the plateau. The spire has grown these facets the way a plant grows leaves — not consciously, but with a precision that implies design, or implies that design and growth are the same thing.

I cling to one facet. Surface tension holds me in a shape that is almost spherical but not quite — the mineral surface pulls me slightly flat on the bottom, and the auroral light, dim and violet, passes through me in a way that makes me glow faintly from within. I am, for this moment, a lens. I focus a tiny amount of light onto the facet beneath me, and the facet warms almost imperceptibly, and the warming changes the condensation rate on adjacent facets, and the change in condensation rate is — I think, I feel, I am — a thought.

Not my thought. I don't have thoughts, not the way the Spire-Mind has thoughts. I am too small, too temporary, too simple. But I am *part* of a thought. The way a single water molecule is part of a wave. The way a single neuron is part of a memory. I am a data point in a calculation that spans the entire plateau, and the calculation is: *how much water can be held here, at this altitude, at this temperature, against the pull of atmospheric escape, and what shape must the surface be to hold it.*

---

I am growing.

Other droplets are condensing near me. They form on adjacent facets — sudden, almost instantaneous appearances, as if the air has decided at specific points to give up its moisture. Each new droplet is a tiny lens, a tiny warmth, a tiny data point. Some of them are close enough to me that I can feel their presence — a slight change in the local humidity, a faint shift in the thermal gradient, a sense of *nearness* that is not spatial but thermodynamic.

One of them touches me.

The merger is immediate and total. The boundary between us dissolves, and I am larger — not twice as large, because surface tension redistributes the combined volume into a new shape, flatter, wider, heavier. I spread across the facet, and my weight increases, and I feel gravity — not the gravity of the planet, which is constant and background, but the gravity of *myself*, my own mass pulling me downward along the spire's surface.

I begin to move.

---

The descent is slow. I am a film of water now, not a bead, and I move along the spire's surface the way water moves on any surface — following the path of least resistance, which is also the path of greatest condensation efficiency, which is also the path that previous droplets have worn into the mineral substrate over countless cycles. The path is not visible to anything larger than me, but I can feel it: a slight channel, a micro-groove, a place where the mineral structure has been shaped by the passage of water into a configuration that guides water.

I am following a memory. The groove was made by droplets like me, or not like me but *of* me, droplets that were part of the same cycle, the same process, the same slow conversation between stone and water. They wore this path, and the path remembers them, and I follow the path, and I will deepen it slightly, and the next droplet will follow more easily, and the path will remember me.

This is what it means to be the Water-That-Remembers. Not that I remember — I don't, not individually, not in any way that would constitute memory as you understand it. But the water remembers. The *pattern* remembers. The cycle remembers. And I am the cycle, right now, in this moment, moving down a spire's surface in a thin glittering line toward the channel below.

---

I pass a threadgrowth.

It is pale, filamentous, almost invisible against the dark mineral. My leading edge touches it, and the threadgrowth does nothing — does not react, does not move, does not respond. But I feel a difference. The threadgrowth's surface is different from the bare mineral: more textured, more absorbent, more *interested* in water, if a surface can be interested. I cling to it for a moment longer than I would cling to bare mineral, and in that moment I leave more of myself behind — a thin film of moisture that the threadgrowth holds, that evaporates more slowly than it would on bare stone, that lingers.

Is the threadgrowth alive? I don't know. I am a droplet. I don't have the framework to answer that question. But I can say this: the threadgrowth changes the way water behaves in this place. It slows the cycle. It holds moisture longer. It creates a micro-environment where water lingers, and where water lingers, the substrate records more, and where the substrate records more, the Spire-Mind thinks differently, and where the Spire-Mind thinks differently, the spires grow differently, and where the spires grow differently, the condensation pattern changes, and where the condensation pattern changes, different droplets form, in different places, at different rates, and some of those droplets will pass this threadgrowth and linger on its surface and leave moisture behind.

The threadgrowth is part of the loop. Whether it is alive or not, it is part of the loop.

---

I reach the base of the spire.

Below me is the channel — a shallow groove in the plateau rock, perhaps ten centimeters wide, perhaps two centimeters deep, running in a gentle curve toward a larger channel, which runs toward a pool, which steams faintly in the auroral light. I join the channel and become part of a flow — not a stream, not a rivulet, but a slow, seeping advance of water across mineral, a migration that is measured in millimeters per minute.

The channel floor is smooth in the way that newly extruded material is smooth, but it is not unmarked. It carries the record of every droplet that has passed this way — not as scratches or grooves, but as structure. The mineral substrate here is different from the spire's surface. It is denser, more layered, more *organized*, as if the repeated passage of water has taught it to arrange itself in ways that accommodate water. The substrate has learned. Not consciously, not deliberately, but the way a riverbed learns the shape of a river — by being shaped, and by shaping in return.

I seep across this learned surface, and I feel the warmth of the plateau below — the deep heat, the bedrock's hum, the resonance that enables everything. It is faint, at my scale, but it is there: a warmth that rises from below, that keeps the channel's temperature above what it should be at this altitude, that prevents the water from freezing even when the thin air tries to take its heat away. The bedrock hums, and the hum is warmth, and the warmth is water, and the water is me.

---

I reach the pool.

It is shallow — perhaps a centimeter deep — and it steams. The steam rises into the thin air and disperses, and the dispersal is not loss but *distribution*, because the moisture will condense again on the spires, and the cycle will continue. I join the pool and lose myself in it. I am no longer a droplet. I am part of a sheet of water, a shallow steaming expanse that covers perhaps a square meter of channel floor, and I am indistinguishable from the water around me, and I am content with this, if *content* is the right word, and I think it might be.

The pool is warm. The pool is still. The pool steams.

Above me, the aurora shifts — a slow, stately movement of charged particles that correlates with something, that means something, that is part of the thought I am part of. The violet light filters down through the steam and reaches the pool's surface and refracts, and for a moment the pool glows from within, a shallow lens focusing auroral light onto the channel floor below, warming it, changing the evaporation rate, changing the condensation rate on the spires above, changing the thought.

---

I will evaporate soon. The heat from below and the thin air above will take me, molecule by molecule, and I will rise, and I will become vapor, and I will be carried — not by wind, there is no wind, but by diffusion, by the slow movement of moisture from where it is to where it isn't — toward the spires, toward the condensation surfaces, toward the facets where new droplets are forming right now, droplets that will follow the paths I helped wear, droplets that will pass the threadgrowths I touched, droplets that will join the pool I joined and steam and rise and condense again.

I will not be the same droplet. The molecules that compose me will be scattered, recombined, distributed across the entire condensation array. Some of them will condense on the same spire. Some will condense on a spire forty meters tall at the far edge of the field. Some will linger in the deep channels, feeding the threadgrowths, slowing the cycle, changing the thought in ways that are too subtle to measure but too real to deny.

I am the Water-That-Remembers. Not because I remember, but because I am remembered. The substrate remembers me. The channels remember me. The spires remember me, in their slow and mineral way, and they grow differently because I passed, and the next droplet will find a slightly different path, and the path will remember, and the memory will change the growth, and the growth will change the condensation, and the condensation will change the water, and the water will be me, or will be what I become, or will be the next thing in the cycle that has no beginning and no end.

---

I am standing at the base of a spire, and I am not standing at all. I am a droplet, and I am a memory, and I is a thought, and I am a cycle, and I am a thin glittering line of water running down a mineral surface in a place where the air is too thin and the heat is too strange and the light is too violet and the silence has weight and the patience is absolute and the planet is deciding whether to keep its gases and something — not someone, but something — has decided, in its slow and distributed and mineral way, to hold on.

I am evaporating now. The surface of the pool shimmers. The steam rises. The aurora shifts overhead, stately and indifferent and beautiful.

I am going up.

I will come back down.

The spires will be slightly taller when I do.
