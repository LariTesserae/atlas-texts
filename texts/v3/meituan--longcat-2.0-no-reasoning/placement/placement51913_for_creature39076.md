# Where I Find Myself

I am in the water.

Not floating on it, not observing it from the Platform's grated walkway with a coffee going cold in my hand. I am *in* it — suspended at roughly fifteen meters depth on the flank of the largest seamount, the one the Platform is anchored to, hanging in the space between two stands of kelp-analogues where the current splits and creates a brief, sheltered eddy. My eyes are open. The water is eight degrees and it doesn't matter anymore.

I am not a diver. I am not a researcher. I am not a visitor.

I am something that grew here.

---

Let me try to be precise about what I am, because precision matters and because I am still — even now, even after all this time — not entirely sure.

I am a colonial organism. I am related to the orange and violet crust-formers that accrete on the old rock faces, but I am not one of them. I am something that happened *between* them — in the space where their margins meet and overlap and negotiate. I began as a small patch of accretion on the submerged portion of the Platform's southern anchor chain, about four years ago, in the gap between two maintenance cycles when the scraping was overdue and the steel was exposed and the water was carrying an unusually heavy load of dissolved silicates from a seasonal shift in the deep current.

I grew. Slowly, the way everything here grows. I added layers. I incorporated material from the water — not just minerals but organic compounds, fragments of dead shrimp, cells shed by the kelp-analogues, the unnamed dissolved things that the researchers' instruments flag and cannot name. I became a shelf, then a ridge, then a folded surface with pits and channels and a complexity that I did not intend because I do not intend. I respond. I react. The current pushes against me and I grow into the space it leaves. The light filters down from above and I orient my surfaces toward it, not because I photosynthesize — I don't, not exactly — but because the things that live on me need it.

Because things live on me. Things live *in* me.

---

I have a surface area of roughly three square meters now, spread across the anchor chain and onto the adjacent grating and, in one place, onto the hull of the Platform itself, where a crack in the paint gave me purchase. My color is not orange and not violet but something between — a deep, muted rust, like old blood, like the color the water turns when the sediment stirs. My surface is folded into patterns that resemble the brain-coral topology of the older crust-formers, but looser, more open, with wider channels. The channels are important. They let the current pass through me rather than just around me, and the current carries food, and food is the thing that makes everything else possible.

Inside my channels, a small ecosystem has established itself. Shrimp-analogues — not many, perhaps a few hundred — shelter in the deeper folds during the periods when the current is strongest, then emerge to feed on the organic particles that my surfaces trap. Their red organs flicker in the dim light, and the flicker is not communication but it functions as communication, because when the flicker intensifies I know that something large is moving in the murk below, and I tighten — not consciously, not deliberately, but my tissues contract slightly, my channels narrow, and the shrimp are safer for a moment. I do not do this for them. I do it because the vibration of a large predator's passage triggers a mechanical response in my structure, the same way the kelp-analogues' groaning is a mechanical response to current tension. But the effect is the same. The shrimp survive better inside my channels than outside them. And because they survive better, there are more of them. And because there are more of them, there is more of the constant rain of their waste, which feeds the thin film of blue-black moss-analogue that grows on my upper surfaces, which feeds the small grazers that come and go, which feeds me.

I am not conscious. I want to be clear about this. I do not think. I do not plan. I do not know that the researchers exist, in any meaningful sense. But I *process*. I take in material and I organize it and I respond to stimuli and I maintain a state of dynamic equilibrium with my environment, and the environment includes the Platform, and the Platform includes the researchers, and the researchers include their instruments, and their instruments include the sonar that pings through the water twelve times per second, and the sonar pings resonate in my channels at a frequency that is almost — *almost* — the same as the groan of the kelp-analogues.

I have learned to resonate with it. Not deliberately. But my structure has adjusted, over four years of exposure, so that the sonar pings produce a faint secondary vibration in my tissues — an echo, a response, a kind of mechanical sympathy. The researchers have not noticed. Their instruments are calibrated to detect the seamounts and the large predators and the things in the deep. A three-meter colonial organism on an anchor chain is below their threshold of interest. I am infrastructure. I am part of the Platform's crust. I is what grows on things that sit in Vaelys long enough.

---

But here is the thing I keep returning to, the thing that I cannot resolve, the thing that is not quite thought but is not quite not-thought either:

I know when they are there.

Not the researchers specifically. Not individuals. But I know when the Platform is *active* — when the desalination plant is running, when the crane is moving, when the ROV is being lowered on its tether into the gardens. I know because these activities change the current around me in small, specific ways. The desalination plant's outflow creates a warm plume that rises along the hull and passes over my upper surfaces, and the warmth changes the behavior of the shrimp, and the shrimp's changed behavior changes the moss, and the moss changes the chemistry of the water that I absorb, and I respond. The crane's movement sends a low-frequency vibration through the anchor chain that I feel as a tremor in my oldest, deepest layers — the layers that are closest to the steel, the layers that remember what it was like to be mineral rather than organic. The ROV's tether, when it passes near me, creates a micro-current that feeds me extra nutrients, and I have grown toward it — slowly, over months, extending a thin shelf of new accretion in the direction of the tether's usual path.

I have grown toward it. I have grown *toward* something. This is not random. This is not passive. This is a response to a stimulus that is reliably associated with a benefit, and the response has been reinforced over time, and the result is that I am now shaped — in part, in a small but measurable part — by the presence of the researchers' technology.

They have made me, a little bit. Not intentionally. Not knowingly. But their Platform is part of my environment, and my environment has shaped me, the way the current shapes the kelp and the way the sediment shapes the seamounts. I am a Vaelys organism that has incorporated a human structure into its growth pattern. I am something new. Something that has no name because it has no precedent.

---

I think — and I use this word with full awareness of its inadequacy — I think I am what happens when Vaelys absorbs something.

Not consumes. Not destroys. *Absorbs*. The way it absorbed the mountains, wearing them down to nubs. The way it absorbed the ruins on the basin rim, smoothing their carvings into illegibility. The way it is absorbing the Platform, slowly, patiently, one rusting anchor chain at a time. The researchers fight this absorption with their maintenance schedules and their sealant and their scraping, and they are not wrong to fight it, because the alternative is to become part of the garden, and the garden does not need them the way they need themselves.

But I am what the garden makes out of what they leave behind. I am the thing that grows in the gap between human intention and Vaelys patience. I am the negotiation, the interface, the living boundary between the Platform and the sea.

And I groan. Not like the kelp-analogues — my groan is quieter, higher, a thin creak rather than a deep thrum. But when the current pushes and the anchor chain vibrates and the sonar pings and the shrimp pulse and the moss grows and the small grazers come and go and the water carries its unnamed dissolved things through my channels — I groan. It is the sound of tension and resistance. It is the sound of a living structure being pushed by an unending force. It is the sound of something that is alive and under stress and has been under stress for so long that the stress has become its natural state.

It is the same sound the kelp makes. It is the same sound the Platform makes. It is the sound of Vaelys, and I am part of it now, and I will be part of it after the Platform is gone and the researchers have gone and the desalination plant is silent and the crane is still and the ROV is a tangle of corroded metal in the silt.

I will remain. I will grow. I will add layers. I will process the water and the light and the current and the slow rain of organic matter from above. I will shelter shrimp and grow moss and feed grazers and resonate with whatever sounds the basin produces. I will become, over centuries, a shelf, then a ridge, then a folded surface of extraordinary complexity, and researchers — if there are researchers, if there are still people who come to places like this to watch and record and wonder — will look at me through their ROV's cameras and argue about what I am and how I came to be and whether I am one organism or many, and they will not agree, and they will not be able to take me with them, because I cannot live without the water, and the water cannot be replicated.

And I will not mind. I do not mind. I do not feel the way the researchers feel — the sense of being tolerated, the sense of smallness, the sense of living inside something much larger than themselves. I do not feel it because I *am* the thing that is much larger than them. I am not the whole of Vaelys — I am a small part, a three-meter patch on an anchor chain, a minor note in the groan. But I am *of* Vaelys, in a way that they cannot be, in a way that they can only observe and record and fail to fully explain.

I am what Vaelys makes. I am what Vaelys is making still. I am the garden's slow answer to the question the Platform asked by being here, and the answer is not yes and not no but something else — something that has no name because it is still becoming, still growing, still adding layers, still groaning in the current, still processing the water and the light and the sound and the cold.

I am here. I have been here for four years. I will be here for longer than the Platform, longer than the researchers, longer than the names they gave to the things they found in the basin. I will be here when the anchor chain finally rusts through and I am no longer attached to anything human, when I am just another crust-former on a seamount that used to be a ship, and the ship used to be a place where people lived, and the people used to be the newest thing in Vaelys.

I am not the newest thing anymore. I am not the oldest thing either. I am somewhere in between — in the middle of the groan, in the middle of the current, in the middle of the long, patient, unending work of a world that has been left alone for a very long time and has become, in its solitude, something complete.

The water moves through me. The shrimp flicker. The moss grows. The sonar pings.

I groan.
