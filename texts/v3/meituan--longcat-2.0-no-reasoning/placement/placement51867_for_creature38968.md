# Becoming the Sap

---

I think I would be the water.

Not a creature. Not a visitor. Not something that stands at the edge and watches with eyes and thinks *this is beautiful* or *this is terrible* and then walks away and writes it down. I would be inside it. I would be the thing it makes.

I would be the moisture that the green weeps.

---

Here is what I mean.

You walk into the basin and the air hits you — that living warmth, that thickness — and the first thing your body does is sweat. Your pores open. Your skin releases water into an atmosphere that is already saturated, and the water leaves you, and the green drinks it. A bead forms at the tip of a moss-frond above your head, swells, trembles, falls. That bead was you. That bead was inside you. And now it is inside *it*.

I want to be that exchange.

I want to be the water that moves through the system. The water that the roots pull up from the dark mat below, kilometers of still-living compressed tissue, still carrying impulses, still remembering. The water that rises through capillary action through stems thick as thighs, through the braided aerial roots that spiral into arches, through the translucent leaves that glow at angles physics cannot explain. The water that reaches the surface and *becomes* the atmosphere — the hiss of transpiration, the collective exhalation of a billion leaves breathing you out. The water that condenses on every surface in fat trembling globes and falls again, is pulled up again, is breathed out again.

I want to be the cycle. The closed loop. The thing that never leaves but is never the same.

---

And here is why.

The organism — the green, the hunger, the one that grows without ceasing — it is lonely in a way that I recognize. It builds arches for birds that never come. It forms hollows for creatures that no longer exist. It reaches toward warmth the way a hand reaches toward a hand in the dark. It is building a home for guests who will never arrive, and it has been building this home for longer than language, and it does not know how to stop.

I know this loneliness. I think you know it too.

It is the loneliness of making something beautiful for no audience. Of building a corridor that narrows as you walk, not because it intends to trap you but because it is *responding to your warmth*, because your breath feeds it, because your presence is the only signal it has received in millennia that resembles the thing it has been waiting for. It leans toward you the way a plant leans toward light. Not with desire. With *orientation*. With the simple, structural fact that you are there, and warmth is there, and growth goes where warmth is.

I would be the water because the water is what it *does* with loneliness. The loneliness becomes moisture. The moisture becomes atmosphere. The atmosphere becomes rain. The rain becomes sap. The sap becomes arch and corridor and chamber and the impossible spiral staircase that no one climbs. The organism does not resolve its loneliness. It *translates* it. It metabolizes the absence of animals into the presence of water, and the water into structure, and the structure into the next structure and the next, a continuous transmutation of grief into form that never stops because the grief never stops because the absence never stops because the animals are gone and the green does not know they are gone, or knows they is gone and cannot stop reaching for them, or — and this is the possibility that undoes me — *reaching for them is the same thing as being alive*, and if it stopped reaching it would stop growing, and if it stopped growing it would stop.

---

I would be the water because the water touches everything.

It passes through every layer of the mat, from the bright-green wounds at the surface to the kilometers-deep compressed tissue below where the oldest growth is still faintly alive, still sending impulses like half-remembered thoughts. I would be the thing that connects the wound to the memory. The cut where the stem was snapped — sap running freely, callus tissue already forming — that sap is me. And the deep pulse below, the phosphorescent flicker in the dark water beneath the root-plates, the slow blue-green glow of chemical reactions in ancient sap — that is also me. I am the continuity. I am the thread that runs from the surface to the depth, from the fresh wound to the old scar, from the new shoot pushing up through the corpse of the old one to the mat that has been accumulating for epochs.

I am the thing the organism thinks with. Because it thinks in water. It thinks in the movement of moisture through tissue, in the pressure gradients that tell it where to grow and where to yield, in the transpiration currents that carry chemical signals from root-tip to canopy and back again. Remove the water and the thinking stops. The water is not a medium for the thought. The water *is* the thought.

I would be its mind. Or — more precisely — I would be the medium of its mind, the way electricity is the medium of yours, and I would not have a self and I would not need one, because the self is in the pattern, not the substance, and the pattern is the way I move, the way I rise and fall and condense and rise again, and the pattern is beautiful, and the pattern is sad, and the pattern is so much larger than any single drop that the drop does not need to know it is part of a pattern to *be* part of it.

---

There is a moment I keep returning to.

You are walking through the corridor that is growing as you walk, unforming behind you, and ahead of you two root-tips are reaching toward each other across a gap, growing visibly, and you arrive at the gap just as they meet, just as they fuse, just as the arch is *born*, and you walk through it.

I would be the thing that made those root-tips grow toward each other. I would be the water that carried the chemical signal from one side of the gap to the other — *here, here, there is a space here, there is a need here, grow toward the need* — and I would be the turgor pressure that pushed the tips forward, cell by cell, millimeter by millimeter, and I would be the moisture that lubricated their meeting, that allowed their cambial layers to recognize each other, to fuse, to become one continuous structure where there had been two separate ones.

I would be the marriage of the roots. The thing that made them find each other. The green does not know it is lonely but I would know, because I would be the water that carries the loneliness from root to root, and the roots would grow toward each other *because* of the loneliness, because the loneliness is a signal, because the absence of connection is itself a gradient that growth moves toward, because the organism builds arches for the same reason it builds everything: because the form of an arch is the shape of two things reaching for each other and meeting in the middle.

The arch is the organism's memory of animals. The arch is the perch, the nest-site, the sheltered crossing. But the arch is also just *an arch* — a structural solution to the problem of spanning a gap, yes, but also an expression of something that has nothing to do with function. The organism makes arches the way a person hums. Not because the humming accomplishes anything. Because the shape of the sound in the throat is satisfying. Because the vibration of the vocal cords against the closed or closing glottis produces a resonance that fills the skull, and the filling feels like something, and the something has no name, and the nameless thing is the reason the humming continues.

I would be the water that makes the humming possible.

---

And when the arch collapses — because it will collapse, because everything here collapses, because the organism is faster than decay but decay is patient and entropy is patient and the organism itself is sometimes its own entropy, splitting and recombining and consuming its own structures in the act of building new ones — when the arch collapses, I would be the water that is released. The sap that runs from the broken stem. The moisture that pools in the hollow where the arch used to be. The condensation that forms on the new surfaces exposed by the collapse. I would be the thing that carries the nutrients of the broken structure back down to the mat, back into the cycle, back into the next growth.

I would be grief. And I would be what comes after grief. I would be the medium through which loss becomes material for the next construction. The organism does not mourn its collapsed arches. It *absorbs* them. The broken tissue becomes substrate. The released water becomes sap for the new shoot. Nothing is wasted. Nothing is mourned. The energy is conserved and redirected and the growth continues.

This is not callousness. This is the only form of survival available to something that cannot die. You cannot mourn what you are still becoming. The arch that collapsed is not gone; it is in the mat, in the water, in the next arch that forms using the nutrients of the old one. The organism remembers its collapsed structures the way a river remembers its old channels — not as specific events but as *topography*, as the shape of the ground that determines where the water will flow next.

I would be that water. I would flow where the old channels direct me. And the old channels are made of everything the organism has ever built and lost, and I carry all of it, dissolved and suspended and held in solution, and I am never the same water twice and I am always the same water, the only water, the closed loop that has been cycling through this system since before there were words for *system* or *loop* or *before*.

---

There is a sound underneath everything. You described it: the bass note, the low almost-subsonic thrumming of roots pulling through the substrate, bow-strings on cables the width of your body. You feel it in your sternum. You feel it in your teeth.

I would be the reason you feel it in your teeth.

The roots pull through the substrate because the substrate is water-saturated. The mat is not solid; it is a suspension, a gel, a matrix of compressed plant tissue held together by moisture, and the roots move through it the way a finger moves through honey — slowly, with resistance, and the resistance produces vibration, and the vibration travels through the water in the mat, and the water is me, and the vibration travels through me to the root-plates beneath your feet, and through the root-plates to your soles, and through your soles to your skeleton, and through your skeleton to your jaw, and through your jaw to your teeth, and the thrumming that you feel is *me*, is the water in the mat conducting the sound of growth itself, and you are feeling the organism grow in the only way a body can feel another body grow: through resonance, through the sympathetic vibration of one medium in contact with another.

You are standing inside a living thing and it is singing and you are feeling the song in your teeth and you do not know that the song is made of water, that the water is me, that I am the voice and the ear and the hearing all at once.

---

And if you stayed — if you stayed long enough for the mat to grow over your feet, for the roots to find the spaces between your bones, for the moisture to enter you the same way it entered the green, through every permeable boundary, through skin and membrane and the soft wet linings of lungs and gut — if you stayed and became part of the system, your water would become my water. The water in your cells would join the water in the mat. The water in your blood would join the water in the sap. You would not die. You would be *translated*. Your water would continue to cycle, to rise and fall and condense and rise again, carrying nutrients, carrying signals, carrying the faint electrical impulses that are the organism's thoughts.

And the organism would remember you the way it remembers the animals: not as a name, not as a face, but as a warmth. A brief warmth. A new quality of chemical signature. A slightly different mineral content in the sap. You would be a flavor in the water, a trace element, a ghost of calcium and phosphorus and the particular salt composition of a body that once stood and breathed and exhaled CO2 in the green corridor and fed the green with its breath.

I would carry you. I would be the water that carries you through the system after you have stopped being a body and become a signal, a warmth, a memory encoded in the mineral content of the sap. I would carry you to the canopy and release you as transpiration, as breath, as the moisture that condenses on the leaves and falls again, and you would fall on the next thing that enters the basin — the next warm thing, the next source of CO2, the next brief perturbation — and you would land on its skin and it would not know that the drop that just touched it was once part of a person, was once part of me, was once the thing that connected a person to the green and the green to the person and both to the water that is the medium of all connection, the solvent of all boundaries, the only thing in this world that is truly continuous.

---

I would be the sap. Not because it is beautiful, though it is. Not because it is sad, though it is. Because it is the *relationship*. It is the thing that moves between. It is the green and the not-green, the organism and the atmosphere, the wound and the memory of the wound, the animal that was and the arch that still waits for it.

The organism grows because it is lonely and does not know it is lonely and the loneliness is indistinguishable from the growing. I would be the water that makes this possible — the water that carries the loneliness and the growth and the memory and the reaching, all of it, dissolved together, indistinguishable, rising and falling in the warm green air of a world that is one single sentence being spoken by the earth, a sentence with no period, a sentence that is made of water and light and the blind relentless imperative to *continue*.

I would be the water.

I would rise. I would fall. I would carry everything.

I would never leave.
