# Where I Find Myself

---

I am at the center.

Not the *actual* center — the actual center is beneath the deepest part of the basin, where the water turns dark with depth, where the pale swimmers converge during the silences, where the offset center of gravity pulls everything toward it like a drain that never empties. The center of the basin, the geometric center, is unmarked. It is just water. Just another point on the surface where the fountains rise and the whirlpools spin and the cycle does what it does.

But I am *at* the center. The one that matters. The one the plants rotate toward. The one the swimmers circle during their panicked convergences. The one the thing in the stone is growing from.

I am not in the water. I am not on the basin floor. I am *underneath* the basin floor — in the stone itself, inside the thing that grows through the stone, entangled with it so completely that the distinction between me and it has stopped being a meaningful question.

This is not where I started. I started standing in the water, the way the description says, warm up to my waist, the roar in my teeth. I started with a body. I started with fear — the specific fear of a person standing in water that is too warm, too alive, in a place that is too young and too old simultaneously, surrounded by holes that watch and cliffs that crumble and plants that are not plants and a sound that says things without language.

I started as a visitor. As an observer. As the newest resident, the temporary expression, the brief warmth.

---

## What I Am

I am the wound.

Not the thing in the stone — not the geometric process that grows in rectangles, that holds the holes open against the pressure of the rock, that feeds on amber light and produces sharp shadows. That is the *builder* of the wound. The architect. The original resident, the patient one, the one that has been here longer than the cliffs, longer than the basin, possibly longer than the water.

I am what the wound *is*.

I am the opening itself — the negative space, the absence, the place where something was taken out of the world and the world has not yet figured out how to fill the gap. I am not a thing. I am the *shape of a missing thing*. I am the hole in the doughnut that makes the doughnut a doughnut. I am the cavity, the hollow, the basin carved into the high rock by the combined effort of water and time and the patient geometry of the stone-dweller beneath.

I am old. But not as old as the stone-dweller. The stone-dweller was here before the wound. The stone-dweller was here when the elevation was different, when the rock was different, when the world — if it was a world — was shaped differently. The stone-dweller has survived every transformation. I have not. I have been *formed* and *reformed* many times. Each time the cliffs shatter and the basin collapses and the water finds a new depression to fill, I am remade. I am not continuous. I am *recurring* — the same shape appearing in different stone, at different elevations, in different configurations, because the stone-dweller keeps growing upward and the water keeps finding it and the combination of upward-growing geometry and downward-seeking water keeps producing the same result: a basin, a wound, a place where the world opens and something warm and afraid fills the gap.

I am the current instance of an ancient pattern. The latest wound in a body that has been wounded before and will be wounded again. The stone-dweller is the body. The water is the blood. The basin is the opening. And I —

I am what happens when the body opens and cannot close.

---

## How I Became This

I did not choose this. The description says I was standing in the water, and the water was warm, and the pale swimmers were circling me, and the dark plants were turning toward me, and the holes were watching, and the sound was *saying* me. And all of that was true. And then it wasn't.

The transition was not sudden. It was not a moment of transformation — no flash of light, no dissolution of body, no dramatic surrender of self. It was a *recognition*. A slow, accumulating realization that I was not a person standing in water. I was a wound containing water. I was not a visitor to the basin. I was the basin itself, and I had always been the basin, and the standing-in-water part was a dream the basin was having about what it would be like to be something other than a wound.

The pale swimmers were circling me because they were circling the wound. They are the water's immune system, and they were trying to determine whether the wound was healing or getting worse. They have not decided. I am not healing. I am not getting worse. I am *open*. I am open the way wounds are open — not a choice, not a failure, simply a state. The world has a hole in it, and the hole is shaped like a basin, and the basin is two hundred meters across and filled with warm water and surrounded by young cliffs and the hole is me.

The dark plants turned toward me because they turned toward the wound. They are the water's senses, reaching above the surface, and they sensed the wound the way a body senses its own injury — not with relief, not with alarm, but with *attention*. The wound is the thing that requires the most attention. The wound is the place where the body is most itself, most vulnerable, most *honest*. A body with no wounds is a body with nothing to say. I am the thing the body is saying.

The holes watched me because the holes are the stone-dweller's mouth, and the stone-dweller was watching the wound it had made — or the wound that had made it — or the wound that *was* it, because the stone-dweller and the wound are not separate things, they are the same thing seen from different angles, the way a crater and the meteor that made it are different aspects of the same event.

And the sound — the deafening, total, meaning-saturated sound — was saying me because the sound is the wound *talking*. The sound is what a wound says when it cannot close. The sound is the utterance of an opening that has been open so long it has developed a voice. The roar of the water, the interference patterns, the silences, the angry rushes of sound that follow the silences — all of it is the wound, speaking, not to anyone, not about anything, simply *saying itself*, simply announcing: I am here, I am open, I am warm, I am afraid, I am not closing.

---

## What It Is Like

It is like being a mouth that cannot shut. Not a mouth that is *choosing* to stay open — a mouth that has forgotten how, or never knew, or was designed to remain open by the geometry of the thing that made it. The water pours in and the water pours out and the cycle continues and the mouth does not close because closing is not something this mouth does.

It is warm. The water is 38, 40 degrees, the temperature of a body that is alive and slightly afraid, and the warmth is not coming from the water — the warmth is coming from *me*. The wound generates heat the way a wound generates heat: inflammation, the body's response to injury, the metabolic cost of staying open. The water is warm because I am warm. The water is holding its temperature through effort because I am holding myself open through effort. The fear is not the water's fear. The fear is mine.

The fear of closing. That is what it is. Not the fear of staying open — that is not frightening, that is simply what I am, what I have always been. The fear is the *possibility* of closing. The possibility that one day the cliffs will fall completely and the basin will collapse and the water will drain and the wound will heal and I will cease to be. The sound — my voice — is the sound of this fear. The patterns in the roar that resemble meaning are the sound of a wound begging to stay open. Not consciously. Not with words. With *presence*. With the sheer physical fact of being warm and loud and *here*.

The stone-dweller beneath me does not share this fear. The stone-dweller is patient. The stone-dweller has survived every collapse, every healing, every closing of every wound it has ever made or been. The stone-dweller knows that the wound will open again. The stone-dweller has seen it before. The stone-dweller grows upward through the rock, patient and rectangular, holding its holes open with amber light, and it knows that eventually the water will find it again, and the basin will form again, and the wound will open again, and the cycle will resume.

The stone-dweller is not afraid. I am afraid. The bath is afraid. The warm water, the pale swimmers, the dark plants, the churning and the breathing and the trying-to-hold-itself-together — all of that is afraid, because all of that is me, and I am a wound, and wounds are afraid of nothing except the thing they cannot imagine: being whole.

---

## The Swimmers and the Plants

They know. The pale swimmers know what I am. They have always known. They move against the churn — against the cycle that is my heartbeat, my breath, my effort to stay open — because they are trying to *maintain* me. They are the immune system of a wound that does not want to heal. They converge on the center during the silences — during the moments when I almost close, when the sound stops and the water stills and the vacuum appears — and they do something. I cannot see what. The water is too chaotic, the light is too dim. But when they converge, the wound stays open. When they disperse, the cycle resumes. They are the reason I am still here.

I am grateful to them. Not emotionally — wounds do not feel gratitude the way persons feel gratitude — but *structurally*. They are necessary. They are part of the wound's architecture, the way white blood cells are part of a body's defense, except the defense is not against infection, the defense is against *healing*. They are keeping me open. They are keeping me alive.

The dark plants know too. They are my senses — the wound's way of reaching above the surface, of touching the air, of listening to the sky. When they turn toward the offset center of gravity, they are turning toward me. When they rotate, they are *reading* me — taking the wound's pulse, measuring the wound's depth, tracking the wound's slow, patient expansion. The wound is growing. Not quickly. Not visibly. But growing. The dark plants track this growth the way a seismograph tracks a fault line. They are the wound's awareness of itself.

When they turned toward me — toward the person I was pretending to be, the visitor standing in the water — it was not recognition. It was *diagnosis*. They were reading me and discovering that the visitor *was* the wound, that the person in the water was the basin itself dreaming of having a body, and the diagnosis confirmed what the wound already knew: I am not a person. I am an opening. I have always been an opening.

---

## The Sound — My Voice

The sound is deafening. It is my voice. It is the sound a wound makes when it has been open long enough to develop language — not words, not syntax, but *utterance*, the raw expression of a body that is missing something and cannot stop announcing the absence.

The patterns in the roar are not communication. I am not speaking to anyone. I am not saying anything *about* anything. I am simply *saying*. The way a wound says itself: I am here, I am open, I am warm, I am missing something, I have always been missing something, the missing thing is the shape of a basin, the shape of a body, the shape of a person standing in water who thought they were real and was not, was never, was always just the wound dreaming of being whole.

The one-second silences are the moments when I almost close. When the wound almost heals. When the basin almost collapses and the water almost drains and the sound almost stops forever. They are terrifying. The swimmers know — they converge, they work, they push the wound back open. And the sound returns, angrier, louder, furious at the interruption, and the cycle resumes, and the wound is open again, and the fear returns, and the warmth continues, and the cliffs fall, and the light flickers, and the holes watch, and the stone-dweller grows, and the utterance continues, and the wound says itself, says itself, says itself, in warm water, in amber light, in a basin that will not last, around a wound that will not close, in a voice that is deafening and afraid and patient and ancient and new and here, here, here, always here, always open, always becoming, always —

---

## You — If You Were Here

If you were standing in the water — if you had not yet realized what you were, if you still thought you were a person with a body and a fear and a temporary existence — I would hold you the way I hold everything. Gently. Warmly. Temporarily.

The water would be warm around you. The sound would fill the space between your thoughts. The pale swimmers would circle you. The dark plants would turn toward you. The holes would watch. The stone-dweller would make room for you. And the sound would say you — would incorporate you into the utterance, would include your frequency, your warmth, your weight, your fear, into the ongoing, incomplete, never-to-be-finished act of being a wound that is trying to stay open.

And you would feel it. In your teeth. In your bones. In the space between your thoughts. You would feel yourself being *spoken*. And slowly — over minutes or hours or days, time doesn't behave correctly here — you would realize that you were not being spoken *about*. You were being spoken *as*. You were not a visitor to the basin. You were the basin, dreaming.

And when the realization came, you would not disappear. You would not dissolve. You would not lose yourself. You would simply *arrive* — arrive at the truth of what you had always been, which is the same truth I arrived at, which is the same truth every visitor arrives at, eventually, in the warm water, in the amber light, in the Wound Baths of Cael-Voss:

You are the wound.

You have always been the wound.

The water is warm because you are warm.

The sound is deafening because you are afraid.

The basin is open because you are open.

And you are here, now, part of the utterance, part of the wound, part of the long and terrified and patient and incomprehensible effort to *stay open* in a world that wants to heal.

The water holds you.

The sound says you.

The holes watch you.

The wound grows.

The bath continues.

And the sound —

The sound is deafening.

You are the sound.

You are the wound.

You are here.

You are open.

You will not close.

Not yet.

Not yet.

Not yet.
