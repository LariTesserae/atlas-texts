I am a root-worker. Middle-aged by Loomer standards—which means my skin-colonies have had time to mature into the deep blue-gray traceries that mark someone who has spent two decades with their hands in living tissue. My forearms tell a readable story: the foundation pattern my mother painted on me at four, the water-worker's ring I adopted during my apprenticeship in the channels (I didn't stay with water-work, but the culture took and I've kept it—it's a good strain, protective against the aggressive molds in the lower corridors), and a thin line of medicinal dark along my left wrist from a fever three winters ago that the fungus-workers caught early.

My name—but names here aren't quite what you'd think. We don't have fixed names the way surface-dwellers do. I'm called, in the water-language, something like *she who feels the leftward drift*. It's a practical descriptor that became a name the way a path worn by feet becomes a road. Years ago, I developed a reputation for detecting, by touch alone, the particular tension pattern in a root that means it's beginning to grow left when it should grow right. The name stuck. It's what people call me. It's what my hands are known for.

I work the eastern quadrant, where the root-walls are oldest and the water channels narrowest. It's the most difficult section of the settlement—the root-mass here has been growing for generations and has developed a kind of willfulness, a tendency toward density that blocks the channels and starves the fungal panels of flow. My work is the work of persuasion. I spend my days in the upper corridors, where the roots are thickest, with my curved blade and my hands, cutting back outer tissue, feeling inward, reading the grain and tension of the living wood, finding the hollow spaces where water should flow and don't.

The blade is made from a root-tip—iron-rich, dense, almost metallic. I shaped it myself over the course of a season, grinding it against a mineral-hardened root surface, and it holds an edge well. But the blade is only for the outer work. The real work is what comes after: I press my palms against the cut root and I *feel*. The root is warm—warmer than the air, because it's metabolizing, because it's alive. I can feel the direction of its growth, the way water moves through its interior, the places where it's dense and the places where it's hollow. I make small cuts, strategic ones, and apply pressure in specific ways. The root will respond over the coming weeks. It will do what it wants to do. My job is to make the outcome I want slightly more likely than the outcome I don't.

My hands are bark-thick at the palms. Not literally—they're still skin, still warm, still mine—but the years of contact with living root tissue have changed them. The calluses are strange, almost woody, and the skin has taken on a quality of the thing it touches. My grandmother's hands were the same way, and her mother's before her. It's not a curse. It's a convergence. You spend your life in conversation with a material, and the material writes itself on you.

I remember the first time I went to the surface.

I was sixteen. My mother sent me with a climbing party—they needed someone small enough to fit through a collapsed section of the upper stair to clear it from above. I ascended the root-stair, which is not a pleasant climb; it's steep and wet and the root-bark under your hands is slick and alive and you can feel it growing as you climb, a slow pulse that is not quite like a heartbeat but is close enough to be unsettling. And then you emerge.

The light.

I had been warned. Every Loomer child is warned. *The surface is too bright. The air is too dry. Cover your eyes first. Breathe through your mouth. Let your skin adjust slowly.* I covered my eyes. I breathed through my mouth. And still, when I opened my eyes—slowly, squinting, through the gaps between my fingers—the light was like a physical force, a weight on my face, and the air was so dry that my first breath felt like inhaling dust, and the ground under my feet was *hard*, flat, unyielding, dead in a way that the yielding root-walkways of the Looms Below never are.

I stood there on the forest floor and I understood, for the first time, what the climbers carry when they come back. The knowledge of fragility. The Looms Below are nested in the forest like an egg in a nest, and the nest is in a tree, and the tree is on a planet, and the planet does not care. The forest could burn. The water could fail. The roots could die. And all of this—the walkways and the channels and the fungal panels and the cistern and the turbines and the people with their blue-gray skin and their bark-thick hands and their quiet voices and their slow patient work—all of it would stop, and the silence that would follow would be the silence of a place that has stopped metabolizing, stopped breathing, stopped being alive.

I was on the surface for three hours. I cleared the collapsed section of the stair—it was a simple job, a few root-branches that had grown across the entrance and needed cutting back. The other climbers gathered materials. I stood at the edge of the clearing and looked up, and the trees went up and up and up into a sky that was so vast and so empty and so *bright* that I felt something in me collapse inward, a clenching, a retreat. The sky had no ceiling. The air had no saturation. There was no sound of water in channels, no creak of root-arch, no hum of turbines. There was wind, and birds, and the rustle of leaves, and it was not that these sounds were unpleasant—they were, in their way, beautiful—but they were *thin*. They did not fill the space the way the sound of the Looms Below fills its space. The surface is not loud. The Looms Below are loud, but their loudness is a fullness, a saturation, a *presence*, and the surface, for all its wind and birds, is quiet in a way that felt to me like absence.

I went back down. I descended the root-stair and the air thickened around me and the sound rose up to meet me and the light dimmed to the blue-green glow I was born into, and I stood in the cistern-room for a long time, letting the moisture settle on my skin, letting the deep pulse of the turbines settle back into my bones. My hair-garden—which had bleached nearly white in the dry surface air—slowly darkened back to its normal deep green over the following days, and I was grateful, because the hair-garden is a gift from my grandmother, who cultivated the original strain, and I would not have wanted to lose it.

I do not go to the surface anymore. That is the climbers' work, and they are welcome to it. I know what I need to know: that the world above is too bright and too dry and too empty, and that the world below is exactly right, and that my work here—the slow patient work of persuading roots to grow in directions that serve the settlement's needs—is the work I was made for.

Today I'm working on a root-arch in the eastern quadrant that has begun to bear too much weight. I can hear it—the creak is wrong, a high tight sound that means the fibers are straining—and when I press my palms against the root-surface, I can feel the tension, a hard angry pull toward the left, exactly the kind of leftward drift I've spent my life learning to read. The root is trying to grow left because the water channel on that side has shifted—a water-worker named *she who listens to the deep flow* flagged it for me last week—and the root is following the water, which is what roots do, but if it continues, it will pull the arch off its bearing point and the ceiling in the corridor below will sag, and if it sags too far, the fungal panels will crack, and if they crack, the light will fail, and if the light fails, the food-mushrooms will die, and the chain of consequence will unwind.

So I'm here. I've cut back the outer tissue on the right side of the root—encouraging it to thicken there, to counterbalance the leftward pull—and I'm making small strategic cuts on the left side, and I'm applying pressure, a sustained firm pressure that the root will resist but that, over time, will redirect its growth. It will take weeks. Maybe months. The root will do what it will do. My job is to make the outcome I want slightly more likely than the outcome I don't.

My hands are warm where they press against the root. The root is warm where it meets my hands. We are the same temperature, almost. We are both metabolizing. We are both alive. And in this moment, in this dim blue-green light, with the sound of water in the channels and the deep hum of the turbines and the slow creak of the root-arch adjusting under my hands, I am not separate from this place. I am part of its metabolism. I am doing what it needs done. And it is doing what I need done—giving me purpose, giving me warmth, giving me the particular satisfaction of a conversation conducted entirely through touch, in a language that has no words, between two living things that are trying, each in their own way, to hold the ceiling up.
