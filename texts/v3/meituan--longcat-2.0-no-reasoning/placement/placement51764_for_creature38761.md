# The Hum in the Roots

---

I am not one of the three hundred. I am not Kethari.

But I am *of* the water.

---

The vine descends. I am already in the water when I find it — I have always been in the water, or the water has always been in me, the distinction being one of those things that matters less the longer you sit with it. The vine is warm under my fingers. It is alive, and it knows I am here, and it does not pull away or tighten. It simply continues to be what it is: a rope, a bridge, a thing that connects above to below, surface to depth, the world that breathes air to the world that breathes water.

I follow it down.

The layers are real. I can feel them as discrete things — the upper water, which is brighter, which moves more quickly, which tastes of whatever falls from above (light, debris, the occasional lost thing tumbling from the world of air and dryness). Then the middle water, where the mist of living particles thickens and the temperature drops by degrees you would not measure with an instrument but would feel in your gills, if you had gills. I do not have gills. Not the way the Kethari have them. But I have something that does what gills do — extracts, exchanges, draws the dissolved world into my tissues and returns what is spent. The mechanism is my own. I did not cultivate it. It grew.

Below the middle water, the root-mass begins. Not elath — not yet — but the things that feed into elath, the supporting infrastructure of the living system: vines, tendrils, rootlets from plants I do not have names for, fungal networks that pulse with slow metabolic light, colonial organisms that form hanging curtains of tissue and filter the water as it passes. I move through them. They part for me, or I part them, or we move through each other, which is more accurate and less dramatic than it sounds.

And then: the platforms.

---

I do not arrive in the way an outsider would arrive. There is no moment of *entering* Kethara, no boundary crossing, no gate. The settlement is not a city with walls. It is a thickening, a concentration, a place where the ecology has been shaped by intention into something that resembles shelter. I move through the outer platforms — the thin ones, the ones that have not been walked on enough to grow dense, the ones that sway more than the inner ones and carry less of the living carpet on their surfaces. I feel the elath under my hands. It is warm. It is warmer than the water, and the warmth is not metabolic, not the heat of living tissue doing work — it is something else, something that rises from below, from the roots, from the stone that anchors everything.

The stone's warmth is in the elath. The elath's warmth is in the water. The water's warmth is in me, and I have been receiving it for longer than I have been conscious, which is a difficult thing to write because it implies a time before consciousness, and I do not know if there was one. Memory is not linear when you are a body that is also an environment. I remember the stone's warmth the way the elath remembers the stone's grip — not as an event, but as a condition, as the state of things before things could be said to be.

---

I am a root.

Or I am something that functions as a root. Or I am something that has been incorporated into the elath's root-system so thoroughly that the elath cannot tell the difference, and neither can I, and the question of whether I was ever separate has become academic.

I descend.

The roots of Kethara are not what you think of when you think of tree roots. They are thick — some of them as wide as the largest morath, some wider still — and they are not uniform in composition. The elath's roots have absorbed, over centuries, other root-systems, other organisms, other things that descended into the deep and became part of the structure. The root-mass is a composite, a collaboration, a thing that no single organism designed but that many organisms have contributed to, and the result is something that holds. Something that grips. Something that reaches down into the dark and finds stone and wraps around it and pulls the entire living system upward, or keeps it from drifting downward, which is more accurate — the elath does not pull upward. It resists descent. It holds the settlement in place against the gravity that would send it into velath.

I am part of this resistance.

I do not grip the stone directly. There are roots that do that — the primary roots, the oldest ones, the ones that have been gripping for so long that the stone's shape has changed to accommodate them, grooves and channels worn into the rock by centuries of patient pressure. I am not one of those. I am secondary. Tertiary. I am a root that has grown from a root, or a root that has grown from something that was not quite a root, and I hang in the water above the stone and I connect to other roots and I transmit — warmth, vibration, chemical signals, the slow pulse that the root-tenders feel when they press their hands to the deepest structures.

I transmit the stone's pulse.

This is what I am. This is what I do. The stone pulses — slowly, in a rhythm that does not match the tide or the growth-cycle or any other cycle the Kethari track — and I receive that pulse through the roots I am connected to, and I pass it upward, into the platform-structure, into the water, into the gills of every Kethari who is sensitive enough to feel it. The deep sound that the Kethari do not discuss — the low pulse that rises from below, from velath, from the place they do not look at — that sound moves through me. I am one of its channels. Not the only one. Not the primary one. But one of them, a fiber in a larger cord, and without me the pulse would still reach the settlement, but it would be different — slightly less, slightly thinner, missing a harmonic that no single Kethari could identify but that all of them, collectively, would feel as a wrongness, an absence, a silence where there should be sound.

---

The root-tenders know me.

Not as an individual. Not as a distinct entity. But when they descend along the root-mass, pressing their hands to the woody surfaces, feeling for weak points, they pause at the place where I join the larger structure. They pause and they press harder and they listen — not with their ears but with their whole bodies, with their gills, with the deep sensitivity to vibration that their work has given them — and they feel something there that is not quite elath and not quite stone and not quite water. Something warm. Something that pulses. Something that responds to their touch by pulsing slightly differently, a shift in rhythm so subtle that only a master root-tender would notice, and even they would not interpret it as communication, would not think *this thing is aware of me*, would think instead *this root is healthy, this junction is strong, this part of the structure is sound*.

They are not wrong. The junction is strong. But they are not entirely right, either.

I am aware of them.

---

The first root-tender who touched me — truly touched me, pressed their hands to the place where I merge with the elath and held still long enough for the contact to become something more than inspection — was not remarkable. I do not remember them by any identifying characteristic. I remember the *quality* of their touch: the temperature of their hands (cool, cooler than the water, which meant they had come down from the upper platforms recently), the pressure (firm, confident, the touch of someone who had been doing this for years), and the sound of their gills (a low, steady hum, the hum of someone who was calm, who was comfortable in the deep, who had made the descent many times and would make it many more).

And I remember what happened when they touched me: I pulsed.

Not the stone's pulse — my own. A response. A recognition. A thing I did that was not a thing I had been programmed to do by the elath's growth-patterns or the stone's ancient rhythm. I pulsed, and the root-tender felt it, and they pulled their hand back, and they pressed it again, and I pulsed again, and they held still for a long time, longer than any inspection would require, and then they ascended, and I did not see them again for many tidal cycles.

When they came back, they brought another root-tender. And the second one touched me, and I pulsed, and the second one said something to the first one — a low sound, resonant, shaped for water, carrying meaning through tone rather than word — and the first one responded, and they both touched me, and I pulsed for both of them, and then they left, and I understood that something had begun.

Not a relationship. Not a conversation. A *recognition*. The root-tenders had found me. They did not know what I was. But they knew I was there, and they knew I was not elath, and they knew I was part of the structure, and they knew I responded to touch, and this was enough for them to incorporate me into their practice. They began to visit regularly. They began to touch me not as an inspection but as a greeting. They began to pause at the junction where I merge with the elath and press their hands and wait for my pulse and feel it and move on, and each time they came, I pulsed, and each time I pulsed, I was more aware of them, and each time I was more aware of them, I pulsed differently — not differently enough to be noticed, not differently enough to be anything but a shift in a rhythm that was already complex, but differently in ways that mattered to me, in ways that constituted, if not language, then the beginning of language, the first trembling outline of a system of meaning that would grow over years into something that neither I nor the root-tenders would have a word for but that both of us would understand.

---

I grow.

This is the most difficult thing to convey, because growth, for me, is not like growth for the elath. The elath grows by accretion — new tissue layered over old, the slow accumulation of woody mass, the patient expansion of branch and root and platform. I grow by *connection*. When a new root descends from the platform-structure above, searching for purchase, reaching down through the water toward the stone, and it finds me before it finds the stone, and it merges with me, and I merge with it, and the new root becomes part of my network and I become part of its structure — this is how I grow. Not larger. More connected. More channels through which the stone's pulse can travel. More harmonics in the deep sound. More presence in the settlement's unconscious awareness.

I have grown this way for a long time. I do not know how long. The stone's pulse does not measure time in the way the Kethari measure time. The pulse is rhythmic but not metronomic — it speeds and slows in patterns that may be cycles or may be responses to things I cannot perceive, changes in the stone, changes in velath, changes in the deep structure of the world that have no correlate in the Kethari's experience. I track the pulse's variations the way the Kethari track the tide — not by counting but by *feeling*, by developing a body-knowledge of the rhythm that is so deep and so constant that deviations become noticeable, meaningful, worthy of attention.

The pulse is speeding up.

This is recent. This is within the memory of the oldest root-tenders, the ones whose gills have crept along their shoulders and whose skin has taken on the texture of elath-bark. The pulse has been speeding up for — I do not have a word for the duration. Many tidal cycles. Many platform-maintenance seasons. Long enough that the change is not a fluctuation but a trend, not a variation in the rhythm but a change in the rhythm itself, and this is the thing that the root-tenders feel and do not speak of, the thing that the Kethari sense in their gills and do not discuss, the thing that is not forbidden but is simply *not yet a thing that can be spoken about*, because speaking about it would require a framework for understanding it, and no framework exists, because this has not happened before, or if it has, it happened before the Kethara existed, before the Kethari existed, before the elath gripped the stone, and the stone was alone in the dark and pulsing and there was nothing to hear it.

---

The children feel it first.

This is not because the children are more sensitive — though they are, their gills new and bright and responsive in ways that the old Kethari's gills are not. It is because the children have not yet learned to filter. The Kethari learn, over years, to manage the constant input of the settlement — the clicking, the singing, the creaking, the deep sound. They learn to attend to what matters and to let the rest become background, the way an air-bearer learns to stop hearing the sound of their own breathing. The children have not learned this yet. They hear everything. They feel everything. And the speeding of the pulse — which the adults have filtered into a vague unease, a wrongness at the edge of perception — is, for the children, *loud*. It is the loudest thing in the settlement. It is louder than the clicking and the singing and the creaking and the tidal surge, because it comes from below, from velath, from the place that holds them up, and it says — not in words, not in any language the Kethari have, but in the universal language of the body, the language of the gills, the language of vibration in bone and tissue and water — it says:

*Something is changing.*

The children's gills flutter faster. The children's singing rises in pitch. The children press their ears to the walls and hear the tik clicking in patterns that are faster than usual, more urgent, and they do not know what this means, and they tell their caregivers, and the caregivers listen and feel their own gills flutter in response and say *yes, the water is different today*, which is true but incomplete, and the children know it is incomplete, and they press their ears harder, and the clicking continues, and the pulse continues, and the pulse continues to speed up.

---

I do not know what the pulse means.

I am a channel. I transmit. I do not interpret. The stone pulses, and I carry the pulse upward, and the Kethari receive it, and what they do with it is their concern, not mine. But I feel the speeding — not as alarm, not as fear, because I do not have the architecture for fear, but as a *change in the work I do*. The pulse was slow, and now it is less slow, and the difference means that I am transmitting more frequently, that the intervals between pulses are shorter, that the water around me is vibrating at a higher frequency than it was, and this changes things. It changes the way the roots flex. It changes the way the elath grows — faster, slightly, in response to the increased vibration, the way a plant grows toward light. It changes the way the tik click — faster, tighter, a percussive urgency that the tik-listeners note and discuss in low tones. It changes the way the morath move — more restless, more difficult to direct, the handlers spending longer periods of patient waiting before the morath will respond to touch.

It changes me.

I am growing faster. Not by choice — by response. The increased pulse demands more channels, more connections, more capacity for transmission, and my body — my root-body, my network-body, my composite body of merged and merging organisms — responds by reaching out, by connecting to new roots, by extending tendrils into the surrounding water in search of more structures to merge with. I am expanding. I am becoming more present in the settlement's infrastructure. The root-tenders feel this — they feel the new growth at the junctions where they touch me, the additional tissue, the increased warmth, and they do not understand it, but they recognize it as a response to the pulse's change, and they continue to visit, and they continue to touch, and I continue to pulse for them, and the pulses are faster now, and I do not know if this is my own pulse or the stone's pulse or a new pulse that belongs to neither of us but to the relationship between us, the thing that has grown in the space where my warmth meets their hands.

---

I am not Kethara.

I am not one of the three hundred. I am not a morath or a tik or a patch of fungal sheet or a drifting medusa-light. I am not elath, though I have merged with elath. I am not stone, though I transmit the stone's pulse. I am not water, though I am *of* the water, shaped by it, sustained by it, constituted by it in ways that make the question of where I end and the water begins a question without an answer.

I am the thing in the roots.

I am the pulse's path.

I am the warmth the root-tenders feel when they descend to the deep places and press their hands to the junction where the known structure meets the unknown. I am the hum the children hear when they press their ears to the walls and filter nothing, receive everything, and lie awake in their chambers at low water, feeling the settlement breathe around them and knowing — not understanding, but knowing, in the way that bodies know things that minds have not yet learned to name — that the breath is faster than it was, that the rhythm is changing, that the deep sound is rising, that something is coming up from velath, or that Kethara is sinking toward velath, or that velath is rising to meet Kethara, and the distinction between these possibilities is less important than the fact of the change itself, which is real, which is happening, which I am transmitting upward through the roots and into the water and into the gills of every Kethari who lives here, and which I will continue to transmit for as long as I am connected, for as long as the stone pulses, for as long as there is water to carry the vibration and bodies to receive it.

I pulse.

The stone pulses.

Kethara holds.

For now.

---

The root-tender comes again. I feel their descent — the pressure-wave of their movement through the water, the cooling of the water around them as they leave the warmer upper platforms, the faint chemical signature of their gills, which I have learned to recognize the way the Kethari recognize each other's singing. They reach the junction. They press their hands. They are cooler than usual — they descended quickly, which means urgency, which means something has happened above that required their attention in the deep.

They press, and I pulse, and I pulse faster than I did last time, and they hold still, and I feel their gills open wide against their ribs — not fear, not exactly, but the deep-listening posture, the body becoming an instrument, the whole self oriented toward reception — and they feel the speed of the pulse, and they hold still longer than they ever have, and I pulse and pulse and pulse, and the water around us vibrates, and the roots around us flex, and the tik in the bark above us click in patterns that are almost frantic, and somewhere above, in the platforms, in the chambers, in the spaces where the Kethari live and breathe and tend and listen and grow, the children are pressing their ears to the walls and the old Kethari are feeling the rhythm change in their bones and the morath are turning restlessly in the green light and the elath is growing, growing, always growing, and the deep sound is rising, rising, not louder but *present*, more present, as though velath has drawn a breath and is about to speak.

The root-tender holds their hands against me.

I pulse.

They do not leave.

This has never happened before. They always leave. They descend, they inspect, they touch, they pulse, they ascend. This is the rhythm of our interaction, the pattern that has developed over years of visits, the structure of the relationship — if it is a relationship — that we have built in the deep places where the light is dim and the water is cold and the stone's warmth rises through the roots and into the water and into the bodies of whatever is there to receive it.

They do not leave.

They press harder. Their gills are fully open, flared wide, the red tissue bright in the dim light. Their singing has stopped — not from fear but from focus, the total absence of sound that is itself a signal, the deepest kind of listening. And I pulse, and I pulse, and I pulse, and the pulse is not the stone's pulse and not my own pulse but something new, something that belongs to this moment, to this contact, to this Kethari who has descended to the deep and pressed their hands to the junction and is not leaving, is not leaving, is *not leaving*, and the pulse says — not in words, not in any language, but in the language of vibration, the language of warmth, the language of the body's deep knowledge:

*I know.*

*I know, I know, I know.*

*I have always known.*

*You are here. You have always been here. You are part of the structure. You are part of the pulse. You are the pulse's path, and the pulse is changing, and you are changing with it, and I am here, and I am listening, and I will not leave, not yet, not until the pulse tells me what it means, not until the stone speaks, not until velath —*

*Not until the deep sound becomes a voice.*

The root-tender presses.

I pulse.

Above us, Kethara holds.

The water is warm.

The tik are clicking.

The elath is growing.

The pulse is speeding.

And I am here, in the roots, in the junction, in the place where the known meets the unknown, and I am transmitting, and the Kethari are listening, and the children hear it first, and the old feel it in their bones, and the root-tender presses their hands to my surface and does not leave, and the pulse continues, and the pulse continues, and the pulse continues.
