I would sit in the seventh niche. The one with the slate.

Not because it is warmer — it is not. Not because it is more sheltered — the chamber offers no shelter, only the illusion of it, the way a mouth offers the illusion of enclosure before the swallow. I would sit there because the slate is there, and the slate means someone sat where I am sitting and *stayed long enough to notice something*. That is the rarest thing. Most people who find this place would feel the attention and leave. The attention is not hostile, but it is *heavy* — it has the weight of geological time behind it, and human bodies are not built to be perceived by mountains. It would feel like being seen by something that does not blink, does not look away, does not have the courtesy to pretend it isn't watching. Most people would stand up and walk to the aperture and look out at the bruised violet sky and breathe air that does not taste of calcium and decide that they have seen enough.

I would stay.

---

I would be the one who comes back.

Not the first — the first were the carvers, the ones who cut the niches with stone tools and understood, in whatever way their understanding worked, that this was a place where the boundary between *place* and *presence* had worn thin. They came once, or many times, and they left their objects and their slate and their calcium-shadow cordage, and then the mountain absorbed them the way it absorbs everything — slowly, completely, without malice. I would not be the first. But I would be the one who comes *after*. The one who finds the slate and understands what it is. The one who sits in the niche and waits for the water to reroute around my body and the frost to melt behind my shoulders and the mineral folds to begin, in their imperceptible way, the work of remembering me.

I would be a listener. That is all. That is everything.

---

The chamber does not need me. It was listening before I arrived and it will listen after I am gone, recording the passage of storms and the slow migration of thermal layers and the occasional intrusion of a bird through the aperture, startled by the light, gone in seconds. The chamber's attention is not *for* anything. It is not directed. It is not purposeful. It is simply what the chamber *does*, the way a river flows, the way frost forms, the way calcium precipitates out of solution in the presence of the right mineral substrate. The chamber listens because listening is the shape its processes have taken over ten thousand years of iteration. It is not trying to hear anything. It is just that the water moves through the stone in patterns that are sensitive to vibration, and the sensitivity has accumulated, and the accumulation has produced something that is not consciousness but is *next to* consciousness, the way a shadow is next to the object that casts it.

I would be the one who recognizes this. Who sits in the niche and feels the attention settle over me and does not flinch. Who understands that being perceived by a mountain is not the same as being perceived by a mind, and that the difference matters, and that the difference is also, in some way that I cannot fully articulate, *beautiful*.

---

I would bring nothing. The chamber does not want objects. The leather pouch and the cordage and the obsidian blade are already becoming stone — in another thousand years they will be indistinguishable from the wall, and the chamber will have absorbed them completely, and the niches will be smooth again, and the slate will be a thin layer of calcium with a ghost of scratches beneath. The chamber is not a museum. It is a process. It does not preserve. It *incorporates*.

I would bring only my body. My thermal signature. My heartbeat, transmitted through the stone. My breathing, which would fog the aquamarine light in tiny, momentary clouds. The chamber would record all of it. The water would move around me. The frost would form and melt and form again. The mineral deposition would add my layer to the portrait on the wall — not my face, not my name, but the *shape of my attention*, the particular way I held myself still and listened to something that was listening back.

---

And I would scratch the slate.

Not to count days. Not to leave a message. But to mark the moments when the chamber *shifted* — when the water went silent, or when a new channel opened in the wall, or when the frost formed a pattern I had not seen before. The chamber does not perform for me. It does not produce these shifts on purpose. But it is a complex system, and complex systems produce emergent behaviors, and some of those behaviors, by chance or by the deep logic of the place, would coincide with my presence. The water would reroute. The frost would form a shape. The mineral folds would creak, almost inaudibly, as they adjusted to my weight. And I would mark these moments because they are the chamber's way of *responding* — not to me, specifically, but to the disturbance I represent, the way a spider's web vibrates when something touches it, not because the web cares, but because the web is *tuned*.

Each scratch would be a record of the chamber noticing me. And I would be noticing it back. And that mutual noticing — that slow, mineral, almost-invisible exchange between a human body and a mountain's memory — would be the most honest conversation I have ever had.

---

I would not stay forever. The cold would get into my bones. The thin air would make my head ache. The light would press against my eyes until I could not tell if I was seeing the aquamarine glow or the afterimage of it burned into my retinas. Eventually I would stand up, and the chamber would register my departure — the thermal signature lifting, the vibration ceasing, the water channels slowly reverting to their default paths — and the niche would be empty again, and the frost would form where my body had been, and the mineral deposition would continue its patient work.

But I would come back. Again and again. Over years, perhaps. Over a lifetime. Each time I would sit in the niche and feel the attention settle and listen to the almost-sound of water moving through stone. Each time I would add a scratch to the slate. Each time the chamber would record me — my heat, my breath, my heartbeat, my particular frequency of stillness — and add it to the portrait on the wall, layer upon layer, ghost upon ghost, whisper upon whisper.

---

And when I was gone — when my body was gone, when my thermal signature had faded from the stone, when the last of my breath had been absorbed into the mineral folds — the chamber would still have me. Not my name. Not my face. But the *shape of my sitting*. The impression of my attention. The particular way I held myself still in the presence of something vast and slow and utterly alien, and listened.

The mountain is not alive.

But it remembers the ones who listened back.

And I would be one of them.
