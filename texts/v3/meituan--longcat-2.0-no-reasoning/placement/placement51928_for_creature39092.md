I would be a water-carrier.

Not a senior one. Not on the path to becoming primary. Someone mid-ranking, mid-thirties, with working hands and a body that knows the routes so thoroughly that my feet navigate them in sleep. The kind of water-carrier who has a particular stretch of channel — mine would run from the Second Sump up through the low corridor behind the tannery district, where the ceiling drops to five feet and the moss grows so thick it drips — and who tends that stretch with something between maintenance and companionship. I know every fissure in that section of wall by the particular sound it makes when water passes through it. The high one, near the junction, that whistles faintly. The crack three steps further that only runs during a high Sump and sounds like someone running a wet finger around the rim of a cup. The slow seep behind the old tannery that never stops, that I keep clear of mineral buildup by scraping it with a flat stone once every nine days, and that if I let go for two weeks would grow a mineral lip thick enough to divert the whole channel into someone's sleeping room.

I carry a staff — hardwood, surface-traded, worn smooth at the grip — and I use it to test the floor before me in the lowest passages, where standing water makes the footing uncertain. I have a belt of clay cups in oiled leather pouches, for tasting and sampling. I have a nose for rot. I lost a toe in my twenties, a small one, the last on my left foot, to something I should have caught earlier — I was tired, I'd been on a double route because another carrier was sick, and I didn't check the beam in the northeast Spore corridor the way I should have. It's a small loss. The damp takes small things from everyone eventually. You learn not to count them.

I live alone, near the Second Sump, in a room I carved myself over two years with obsidian blades and a patient lack of urgency. The room is not large. It has a hide curtain, a sleeping platform raised off the floor on stacked stone, three resin lamps, and a shallow channel I cut myself to drain the condensation that otherwise pools near the doorway. I am proud of that channel. It is not elegant — it meanders slightly where I hit a harder vein of basalt and had to route around it — but it works, and it is mine, and when I lie on my platform at night and listen to the water moving through the stone, I can hear the difference between the natural flow and my little diverted stream, and the distinction pleases me in a way I would have trouble explaining to anyone who didn't live here.

I was born here. My mother was a mushroom-farmer — her hair always wrapped, her hands always smelling of damp wood and spores — and my father was a tanner with ash-white forearms who died when I was twelve, not dramatically, just the way people die here, slowly, the damp working its way into his lungs over years until one morning he couldn't get up. I was tested for channel-sense at seven and showed enough aptitude to be taken into training. I have never been to the surface. I know it exists — the throat, the stacked stone, the world above. I have heard the stories. The sky. The dryness. The light that comes from *everywhere* at once, which sounds to me like madness, like a place where nothing is hidden and nothing is intimate. I don't fear it. I don't desire it. It is abstract. The water is concrete.

I think what I am, if I am anything in particular, is someone who has learned to hear clearly. That is what the Dripworks teaches, more than anything. The city is a place where listening is survival, where the difference between a trickle and a seepage and a fissure-run can mean a flood or a drought or a structural collapse, and where generations of people have refined their attention to the point that it becomes something almost sacred — not in the way surface people mean sacred, with altars and invocations, but sacred in the way that a craft done well is sacred, in the way that a relationship maintained over time is sacred. I am in a relationship with water. It is the longest relationship of my life. It is more honest than any human relationship I have had, because it tells me what it is doing, always, in every moment, if I am willing to pay attention. It does not lie. It does not perform. It simply moves through the stone according to its own deep logic, and my job — my craft, my purpose — is to hear it moving and to help it find the places where it is needed.

I am not extraordinary. That is, I think, the point. The Dripworks does not need extraordinary people. It needs attentive ones. It needs people who will check the channels in the rain — there is no rain, but in the high Sump, the closest thing — and who will sleep with one ear tuned to the sound of the water and wake when the pitch changes. I am one of those people. I am a carrier. The water moves through me as much as through the stone. I am part of its route.

And when I stand at the edge of the Second Sump in the evening, after the routes are done and the lamps are lit and the city is settling into its damp, dripping nighttime hum, I sometimes press my hand flat against the wall and feel the vibration of the water deep in the basalt, far below, moving through passages no one has ever seen, and I think: *this is enough*. Not in a resigned way. In a full way. In the way that a cup is full of clean water and nothing else is required.

The Dripworks accepted me before I was born. I was born into its acceptance. And I will end in it too, eventually — my bones in the stone, my name in someone's memory for a generation or two, and then not even that. And the water will still be moving. That is the comfort. That is always the comfort.
