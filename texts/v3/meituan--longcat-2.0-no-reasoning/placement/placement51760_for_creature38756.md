# Where I Would Find Myself in the Thawfields of Elevation

---

I would be at the edge of a seepage flat, where the water is deepest—still only three inches, but deep enough to cover the mineral crust completely, deep enough that the surface holds a reflection even as it moves.

I would be standing still. Not because I chose to stop, but because I have been here long enough—hours, maybe a day, maybe longer; time is difficult to track in this light—that the distinction between standing and moving has begun to dissolve. The air is thin enough that I breathe deliberately, each breath a conscious decision, and the rhythm of that breathing has synchronized with something else: the hydraulic pulse of the ground, the slow seep of water through mineral matrices beneath my feet. I am matching the land's respiration without meaning to. My body has decided to do this on its own.

The ground gives slightly when I shift my weight. There is that trampoline tension, that sense of being held by something that hasn't committed to holding me. My footprint fills with water within seconds—clear, so cold it stings through whatever I'm standing in. I can feel the mineral crust cracking beneath me, a sound like fine porcelain being pressed, and I know that my presence here is a small violence, that I am breaking something that was still becoming itself, that the surface I stand on was constructing itself in real time and I have interrupted it by being heavy and warm and *here*.

I am wet. Not from rain—there is no rain here, only the constant, ambient wetness of a place where water exists as a near-surface condition. Everything weeps. I am weeping. My skin carries the same thin sheath of condensation that coats every pipe on every machine, and in the cold it has begun to glaze, a clear film that catches the even, sourceless light and holds it. I am beginning to look like the landscape. I am becoming mineral.

---

The Crawlers are close. I can hear them before I see them—that sound, the *shlshlshl*, membrane against crust, a continuous whisper that fills the space between my breaths. They move through the three inches of seep in loose aggregations, and the surface of the flat breaks into concentric rings around each body, a constant disturbance that makes the water appear to breathe. I have been watching them for—how long? Long enough that I no longer try to count them. Long enough that I have stopped reaching for words like *snake* and *worm* and *centipede* and have accepted that they are themselves, that they require no comparison, that they are the base of everything here and they do not care that I am watching.

They are beautiful. This is not a word I expected to think in this place, but it is the right one. Their skin is translucent enough that I can see the dark pulse of their organs, segmented, rhythmic, and the way the light—the grey-white, shadowless, directionless light of this place—passes through their bodies makes them briefly luminous, makes them glow from within like something lit by its own metabolism. They have no eyes. They navigate by chemistry and touch and something else, some sense I don't have, and I am envious of this. I have been relying on vision for hours and it has given me nothing—the flat light erases depth, erases form, makes everything a single grey-white plane—and the Crawlers are moving through a world I cannot see, reading information written in a language I will not learn.

One of them passes close enough that I could reach down and touch it. I do not. I have learned that touching things here changes them, and they change me, and the exchange is not always equal. I watch it move instead—the lateral undulation in water, the moment of transition onto crust where the limbs extend and the motion becomes part caterpillar-crawl, part sidewinding, and then the sound, the whisper of its body against the mineral surface as it moves away from me, following some gradient I cannot detect, toward some aggregation that is tightening in response to a stimulus I cannot feel.

---

There is a machine to the north. I can hear it more than see it—the low, grinding hum, the diesel knock that becomes distinct when the wind shifts, then dissolves back into the aggregate drone. Its lights appear through the haze as slow-moving points, amber and white, and I watch them the way you watch stars: not expecting them to arrive, just noting that they are there, that something large and purposeful is moving across the plateau in a direction that is not random.

I have been following the machines. Not deliberately. Not at first. But I have found that my path across the Thawfields tends to intersect with theirs—their compressed tracks, their tailings ridges, the warmth that lingers near their exhaust vents even after they have moved on. I tell myself this is because the tracks are the easiest ground to cross, the mineral crust hardened by thousands of passes, stable beneath my feet in a way that the fresh ground is not. But I think it is something else. I think I am drawn to the warmth. I think I am drawn to the sound. I think I am drawn to the only thing in this place that is *doing something*, that is moving with apparent purpose, even if that purpose has nothing to do with me.

The machine passes perhaps a quarter mile from where I stand. I can see its shape now—massive, tracked, the size of a shipping container, moving at a speed that is measured in patience. Its treads leave deep impressions in the thaw-ground that immediately begin to fill with seep. It is carrying a sheath of condensation that catches the light, and for a moment it gleams, and it looks alive, and I know it is not alive, I must remember this, but the grinding of its engine is so constant and so persistent that it has become a kind of heartbeat for this place, and when the wind shifts and the sound reaches me I feel it in my chest, in my diaphragm, in the place where my breathing has synchronized with the ground.

---

A Scavenger appears at the edge of my vision. I almost miss it—the pale, ashy dark of its pelt matches the sky so perfectly that it is visible only as an interruption in the grey, a place where the light is being absorbed rather than reflected. It is low to the ground, loping, head down, and it is moving toward the machine's tail, toward the fresh tailings that have been deposited in a long, parallel ridge that I can see winding across the plateau behind the machine like a vein.

I watch it approach the tailings. It begins to feed—not quickly, not desperately, but with a methodical efficiency that suggests this is routine, that it has done this hundreds of times, that the relationship between Scavenger and machine is as old as either of them can remember. It is eating the organic fraction, the ancient plant matter that has been locked in permafrost for millennia and is now, for the first time in that long darkness, accessible to metabolism. The machine dug it up. The Scavenger is eating it. The distinction between industrial process and predation has collapsed, and I am watching something that is both and neither.

The Scavenger's nostrils open and close in rhythm. It is building a chemical map of this place, reading information that I cannot access, and I realize that it knows I am here—has known since before I saw it, has been tracking my scent across the plateau while I was still looking at the machine, still listening to the Crawlers, still trying to decide what I am in this place. It does not care. I am not food. I am not a threat. I am not relevant to its chemical map in any way that matters, and it returns to feeding, and I feel the small, cold shock of being assessed and dismissed by an animal that has more important things to process.

---

The sky is the same as it has been since I arrived. Overcast not with clouds but with suspension—water vapor, mineral dust, combustion byproducts, biological particles. The sun is a smear. The light is even, shadowless, flat. I have stopped looking for depth. I have stopped looking for form. I am learning to see the way the Crawlers see: not with eyes but with something distributed, something that reads the world as a field of gradients and traces rather than objects and surfaces.

---

A sound. Thin, high, arriving from a direction that does not match its source. A raptor's call, bent and scattered by the cold, mineral-laden atmosphere, arriving at my ears with a clarity that feels surgical. I look up and see nothing—the haze is too thick, the bird too high, too small, too perfectly colored against the grey-white sky. But I know it is there. I know it is circling above a seepage flat somewhere to the east, riding the thermal from a machine's exhaust, reading the surface disturbance patterns, looking for the moment to fold its wings and drop.

I will not see the strike. It will happen while I am looking at something else, or it will happen in the grey space between one moment and the next, too fast for my eyes to track. But I will hear the aftermath—the panicked tightening of the Crawler aggregation, the boil of the seep-surface, the rapid wingbeats of the raptor climbing back to altitude with prey in its talons—and I will know that the food chain has completed another cycle, that energy has moved from mat to grazer to predator in a sequence that is subsidized by the machine's heat and the permafrost's thaw and the geological memory of a warmer world.

---

I am standing at the edge of a seepage flat in the Thawfields of Elevation. The ground is deciding what it wants to be. The water is deciding whether to flow or stay. I am breathing deliberately in air that is thin and cold and carries the metallic tang of mineral suspension. I am wet. I am becoming mineral. I am not sure how long I have been here, and I am not sure I am ready to leave, because leaving would require a decision, and decisions require a sense of direction, and direction requires landmarks, and the landmarks here are still becoming themselves.

The Crawlers move through the three inches of seep. The Scavenger feeds on the tailings. The machine grinds across the plateau toward whatever it is grinding toward. The raptor circles at the edge of vision. The lichen grows at the speed of losing. The mat feeds on warmth that should not be here. The ground breathes beneath all of them, beneath me, a slow hydraulic pulse that I have synchronized my breathing to without meaning to.

I am not a Crawler. I am not a Scavenger. I am not a machine. I am a body standing at the edge of something that is still becoming itself, and I am being changed by the standing—by the wet, by the cold, by the thin air, by the even light, by the sound of machinery at the edge of hearing, by the whisper of too many limbs against mineral crust, by the knowledge that the ground beneath me is alive in the way that geology is alive, slowly, massively, in a timescale that will outlast everything I can see.

I am becoming part of the record. My footprints fill with seep. My warmth lingers in the air for a moment before the wind takes it. My breath adds its moisture to the constant, ambient wetness of a place where everything weeps. I am not permanent—nothing here is permanent, not the machines, not the animals, not the ground itself—but I am *present*, and presence is the only thing this place asks of you, the only currency it recognizes.

The light does not change. The sound does not stop. The water does not decide.

I am still standing here.
