I would not be one of the large things. I have no appetite for the deep dark, no patience for the booming patience of the cave. I would not be the network itself — that requires a timescale I cannot inhabit, a thought that takes a week to complete. I would not even be the Old Ones, exactly, though I would want what they had.

I would be something smaller. Something that fits in the gaps.

I would be a lizard — one of the thumb-sized ones, or perhaps somewhere between thumb and forearm, small enough to live entirely within a single chamber of the root-tower lattice without ever needing to cross open stone. I would have scales the color of the moss I sleep on, which is not camouflage in the way you think of camouflage — I am not hiding. I am *matching*. There is a difference. Hiding implies a self separate from the surface. I am close enough to the moss that the distinction is unclear, even to me.

My territory is one corridor and three chambers. I know the breathing rhythm of this section intimately — not the way a scientist knows a waveform, but the way your lungs know the moment before you need to inhale. When the root-towers expand in the morning, the corridor widens by just enough that I can pass through a gap I cannot pass through at night. I time my hunting to this. I am already waiting at the gap when it opens, and I slip through into the next chamber where the tiny glittering insects are beginning their morning streams, and I eat.

I do not understand that I am part of anything. The concept of the network is not available to me. But I know the pulse — I feel it through my belly when I press against the root-tower bark to warm myself in the early light. I know the midday event — not what it means, but that it *happens*, and that when the scent comes and the world goes still and the birds stop singing, I feel a pull toward stillness myself, a heaviness in my limbs that is not unpleasant, and I press myself flat against the bark and I become very quiet, and for twenty minutes I am *inside* something larger than I have words for.

Then the flowers close. The birds resume. The insects scatter. I hunt again.

I have lived in this corridor for three years, or what I experience as three years — I have no counting. The mammals pass through sometimes, the brown-and-grey travelers, and I watch them from my perch with the particular stillness of something that knows it is watched in turn. They are too large to eat and too wary to approach. They know these corridors. They pass through without disturbing anything, and I let them pass.

Once, I found a section of corridor where the root-tower bark had peeled away in a wide strip, and beneath it the wood was pale and wet and warm, and there was a smell — sweet, fermented, not the epiphyte scent but something deeper, something from inside the wood itself. I ate a little of the exposed tissue, carefully, and I felt the pulse change under my belly — faster, sharper, a single strong beat — and then it settled, and the corridor where I was sitting grew a new root-tower, small, no taller than my body, over the course of a single day. The fastest growth I have ever witnessed. As if the network was *noticing*. As if my bite had been a signal it was waiting for.

I did not go back to that spot. Not from fear — I am not capable of that kind of fear. But from something else. A sense that I had said something I did not mean to say, and the reply had been too fast, too direct, and I was not ready.

I live lightly. I take what I need. I match the moss. I know the breathing. I press myself against the bark at midday and go still, and for twenty minutes I am part of something vast and slow and not-quite-conscious, and I do not know this, but it is true.

The escarpment does not need me.

But I am here. In my corridor. In my three chambers. Waiting for the gap to open so I can slip through and eat.

The ground is alive under my feet, and I am alive on top of it, and the distinction between these two things is not as clear as you might think.
