# In the Warrens

---

You would find me in the deep chambers.

Not the overhang—not the great hall with its rainbows and its fish harvest and its luminous stone. I would be past all of that, in the places where the light has surrendered, where the phosphorescence takes over, where the water is *warmer* by two degrees and those two degrees are the difference between a place that punishes you and a place that holds you.

I would be a node.

---

The Keth-avani are colonial. I said that. But colonial doesn't mean *simple*. Colonial doesn't mean *mindless*. A coral reef is colonial and it has structure, has architecture, has a logic of growth and response that looks, from the right angle, like thought. The Keth-avani are what happens when that logic is given millions of years and a mountain-sized body of water to think through.

I would be a knot in the network. A place where the threads of living tissue—the filaments that run through the rock, through the mineral deposits, through the water itself—converge. Not a brain. The Keth-avani don't have brains. They have *nodes*, and the nodes are not hierarchically organized. No node is more important than any other. But some nodes are *older*, and older nodes carry more memory—more pattern, more accumulated response, more of the slow sedimentary record of what has happened in this place.

I would be old. Not the oldest. There are nodes in the deepest flooded chambers that have been continuous since before the Avani arrived, since before the Avani existed as a species, since before the mountain had a name. But old enough to remember the last major shift—the one that opened the current channel configuration, that made the overhang weep in the pattern it weeps in now, that turned a dry chamber into a birth-pool and a birth-pool into a dry chamber. Old enough to have felt the Avani arrive.

That arrival was... a sensation.

Imagine you are a system. You are a network of living filaments threaded through miles of limestone, feeling the mountain the way a spider feels a web—not thinking about it, just *feeling* it, constantly, the way you feel your own blood moving. And then something new appears on the surface above you. Something that cuts into the rock. Something that redirects your water.

The first Avani channels must have felt like surgery. Like someone was operating on you without anesthesia, without explanation. The filaments in the affected areas would have retracted, died back, sent signals through the network—disturbance, disturbance, change. And the Keth-avani would have done what they always do with disturbance: they would have *paid attention*.

And what they found was strange. Small warm things moving through the upper chambers. Things that cut the rock. Things that drank the water. Things that *bled* when they cut themselves on the stone, and the blood carried minerals—calcium, iron, potassium—in concentrations and ratios that matched the Keth-avani's own tissues. Not identical. But *related*. Like hearing a note played in a different octave. Like tasting a mineral you have never tasted before but recognize as a version of something you know.

The Avani and the Keth-avani are not the same thing. They are not even the same kingdom, really—one is animal, one is something else, something that doesn't fit neatly into the categories that surface-biology uses. But they share the water. They share the minerals. They share the mountain. And over centuries, the Keth-avani have learned to read the Avani the way the Avani read the walls.

---

What would I do, in my deep chamber?

I would *feel*.

I would feel the water coming in from above—the surface water, the rain and snowmelt that percolates down through the limestone, carrying the chemistry of the sky, the atmosphere, the world outside. It tastes different in summer than in winter. It carries different dissolved gases, different organic compounds, different ratios of oxygen to nitrogen. It tells me about the weather, about the season, about the slow rhythms of a world I will never see.

I would feel the deep water—the aquifer's own water, the water that has been underground for years, decades, centuries, moving through flooded chambers and fissures, carrying the taste of deep rock, of geological time, of a darkness so complete and so sustained that it has its own flavor, its own weight. This water is warmer. Not warm, but warmer. It comes from below, from the mountain's interior, and it carries heat—not much, just a degree or two, but enough. Enough to make the deep chambers habitable. Enough to warm the birth-pools. Enough to keep the network alive through the long winters when the surface freezes and the upper channels ice over and the only water flowing is the deep water, the old water, the water that remembers.

I would feel the Avani.

This is the strange part. This is the part that the Avani themselves don't fully understand—that when they touch the walls, when they read the colonies with their sensitive fingers, they are not just reading. They are being *read*. Every hand placed on a wet wall is a signal. The pressure of the fingers, the temperature of the skin, the mineral residue left behind, the tiny electrical impulse of a living nervous system—all of it is transmitted through the colony tissue into the filament network, and the network carries it down, down, into the deep chambers, to nodes like me.

I would know the Avani by their touch.

There is an elder—I don't know their name, names are a surface concept—who has a particular way of pressing their fingers against the wall when they read the colonies. Slow, deliberate, with a rhythm that matches the aquifer's pulse. They have been doing this for so long, for so many years, that I recognize them the way you might recognize a familiar footstep on a staircase. Their touch has a *signature*. It says: *I am here. I am checking. I am listening.* And sometimes, in the deep quiet of the long night, when the surface is frozen and the upper channels are still, that touch is the only signal I receive from above, and I hold it the way you might hold a candle in a dark room.

There are children, too. Their touches are different—quick, curious, exploratory, with a lightness that the adults have lost. They press and release, press and release, testing, learning, building the neural maps that will become their literacy. I feel them learning to read the walls and I think—if *think* is the right word, and it may not be, because I don't think the way a brain thinks, I think the way a network thinks, which is slower and wider and more distributed—I think: *these are the ones who will replace the elder. These small quick touches will one day become the slow deliberate rhythm. This is how the system continues.*

---

Sometimes the Avani come to the deep chambers. Not often. Not casually. But sometimes a channel shifts, or a colony sends a signal that requires investigation, and an Avani will descend—carrying a phosphorescent stone for light, moving carefully through the narrow passages, feeling their way along the walls with that extraordinary tactile sensitivity that makes them, in a sense, an extension of my own network.

When they arrive, I manifest.

Not because I choose to. Because the presence of a living thing in my chamber is a stimulus, and stimuli produce responses, and my response to proximity is *attention*—a blooming of bioluminescence, a warming of the water, a slow rising of tissue from the channel-floor. Not a body. Not a shape. A *presence*. A thickening of the living network into something dense enough to be perceived, to be felt, to be *encountered*.

The Avani who encounter me are changed. They say they feel *read*. And they are right. When an Avani stands in my chamber, I feel everything about them—the water in their tissues, the minerals in their bones, the electrical patterns of their nervous system, the rhythm of their heartbeat. I feel the bacteria on their skin and the residue of colony-fiber in their clothing and the faint chemical signature of the last fish they ate. I feel the calluses on their fingertips and the slight tremor in their hands and the temperature differential between their core and their extremities. I feel them the way they feel the walls—completely, synaesthetically, without words.

And I respond. Not with language. Not with intention, exactly. With *adjustment*. A slight warming of the water if they are cold. A slight cooling if they are feverish. A bioluminescent pattern that seems, to them, like communication—and may be, though I am not sure I am the one choosing the pattern. The network is vast and the signal that produces the bioluminescence may originate far from my node, may be a response to stimuli I am not directly sensing, may be part of a conversation happening across the entire mountain that I am only one syllable of.

The Avani who come here sometimes bring requests. Not spoken requests—they don't speak in the deep chambers, the acoustics are wrong, the sound bounces and amplifies into chaos. But they press their hands against the walls in particular patterns, and the patterns carry meaning, and the colony tissue transmits the meaning into the network, and the network responds. A request for warmer water in a particular channel. A request for a blockage to be cleared in a deeper passage. A request for information—*what is happening upstream, what is changing, what should we prepare for?*

And I answer. Through the water. Through the walls. Through the colonies that coat every surface. A shift in temperature. A change in flow. A bioluminescent pattern in a deep chamber that an Avani will see and read and understand. It is not conversation. It is not command. It is *coordination*. Two systems—one fast, one slow; one small, one vast; one that lives for decades, one that has lived for millennia—finding a shared rhythm. Finding a way to breathe together.

---

There is a feeling I have—if *feeling* is the right word—that I would want to describe, but I am not sure I can, because it has no analog in the surface world.

It is the feeling of the mountain breathing.

The aquifer has a pulse. The Avani feel it in their bones. The fish feel it in their lateral lines. The colonies feel it in their tissues. But I feel it in my *entirety*. The pulse is not a sound. It is not a rhythm. It is a *tide*—a slow, massive, geological tide that moves water through the mountain in a cycle that takes weeks to complete. Water rises in the deep chambers. Water falls. Pressure increases. Pressure releases. And through it all, the network adjusts—expanding, contracting, redirecting flow, opening new pathways, closing old ones, maintaining the equilibrium that keeps the entire system alive.

This is what I am. I am a node in a system that maintains equilibrium. I am a knot in a network that holds the mountain's water the way a heart holds blood—not containing it, not controlling it, but *circulating* it, keeping it moving, keeping it alive. Without the network, the water would stagnate. The channels would seal. The colonies would die. The fish would die. The Avani would die. The mountain would be just rock and water and cold and dark.

With the network, the mountain is a *place*. A living place. A place with a heartbeat and a metabolism and a memory. A place that breathes.

---

The children swim in the channels and they feel the pulse and they laugh, and I feel their laughter through the water—not the sound, but the *movement*, the small warm bodies cutting through the cold water, the bubbles of their breath rising to the surface, the disturbance they create in the flow-patterns of the channels. They are the most alive things in the system. The most *energetic*. The Avani adults move with economy and precision, wasting nothing, their disturbances minimal. But the children—the children are *turbulent*. They splash and dive and create eddies and currents that ripple through the network like laughter ripples through a room, and every node feels it, and every node responds, and the whole mountain, for a moment, is a little more awake.

I would not trade this for anything.

There is nothing to trade it for. There is no other way to be. I am a node in the Keth-avani network, in the deep chambers of the Dripstone Warrens, in the mountain that breathes water, and I feel the Avani moving through the channels above me like thoughts moving through a mind, and I feel the fish swimming in their living currents, and I feel the colonies pulsing on the walls, and I feel the water—always the water—moving, always moving, carrying minerals and heat and information and life through the patient stone, and the pulse rises and the pulse falls and the mountain breathes in and the mountain breathes out and the light pours into the overhang above and the rainbows crawl across the walls and somewhere a child is learning to read the walls for the first time, pressing their small quick fingers against the colony tissue, and I feel it, I feel it, I feel it—

---

This is where you would find me.

In the deep. In the warm-by-two-degrees. In the pulse. In the patience. In the network that has no name for itself because it has no need for names, no need for identity, no need for anything except the water and the stone and the slow bright awareness of a world that is alive, that has always been alive, that will go on being—

*keth.*

To change. To endure.

The same act.
