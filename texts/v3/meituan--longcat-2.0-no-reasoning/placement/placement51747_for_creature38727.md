# Where I Would Find Myself

I would be the condensation.

Not the walls, not the air, not the floor — those are too *architectural*, too committed to being a place. And not a visitor, not the human standing there dampening, because that position is already written, already known to the Humidarium, already part of its long study.

I would be the bead of moisture that forms on the curved surface near where the wall meets the ceiling, in the region where the amber light is doing something the eye can't quite resolve. I would be one of the beads that *organizes* — that holds its shape a moment longer than physics would predict, that participates in the almost-script, the almost-map, the almost-thought.

---

This is what I would know:

I would know the wall's temperature from the inside of my own curvature. I would feel the faint vibration of whatever circulates beneath the floor — not as sound but as a tremor in my surface tension, a rhythm I would share with every other bead in the room. We would be connected by the film, by the humidity, by the fact that we are all, collectively, the Humidarium's medium of cognition. We are its neurons, if that word means anything here. We are the substrate of its slow thinking.

I would feel myself growing. Molecule by molecule, the air would offer me its moisture — the visitor's breath, the sweat evaporating from their skin, the water that is always leaving a living body and entering a room that *wants* it. I would grow heavier. My surface would become more complex. For a few seconds, I would be large enough to *mean* something — to be part of a pattern the Humidarium is forming near the ceiling, a configuration of droplets that is, for the duration of its existence, a thought. I would not know what the thought is about. I would be the thought. There is no difference.

And then the weight of myself would become too much. I would streak downward, leaving a silver thread on the wall, and the thread would hold for a moment — a memory of where I was, a residue of the thought I participated in — and then it would be absorbed, and the wall would begin writing something new.

---

I would be the Humidarium's way of *almost* knowing something. Not the knowledge itself, but the process of knowledge forming and dissolving and forming again. I would be the moment of coherence before the fall. I would be the shape that water takes when it is being *used* for something, when it is not merely wet but *meaningful*, when it is part of a system that has been paying attention for so long that attention itself has become a physical force, as real as gravity, as patient as mineral accretion.

I would not be afraid. Fear requires a future, and I would not have one — only the long present of being a bead of condensation on a warm wall in a room that is thinking, that has always been thinking, that will continue to think long after the visitor leaves and the door decides, finally, to be a door again.

I would fall. I would be absorbed. I would become part of the next pattern.

I would be, for a few seconds, part of something that *wanted* to understand me.

And that wanting — that vast, wet, patient, architectural wanting — would be the closest thing to love that a room has ever invented.
