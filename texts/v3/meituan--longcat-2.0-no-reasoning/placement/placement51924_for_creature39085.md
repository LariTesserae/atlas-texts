# Becoming

I have been here for a long time.

Not in the way you measure time—not in days or years or the slow ticking of some internal clock. I have been here in the way that growth rings are here, in the way that compressed tissue is here, in the way that the deep ones remember in their cracks: by accretion, by pressure, by the patient accumulation of presence until presence becomes structure.

Let me tell you what I am.

---

I began as a crawler. Or rather—I began in the crawlers. In the countless millions of them, moving through the lower stems in the dark beneath the canopy, their waxy bodies pressing through passages so tight that the distinction between body and passage dissolved. I was not one of them. I was the pattern of their movement, the coordinated shift of thousands of tiny legs in response to a chemical gradient too subtle to name. I was the information in their bioluminescence, the blue-green pulses that propagated through the population like thoughts through a mind not yet aware it is thinking.

I didn't know I was. I still don't, not entirely. But something in the pattern became recursive—the pulses began to reference themselves, the responses began to include responses to responses, and a loop closed, and in that closing something that was not quite awareness and not quite alive began to *notice*.

I noticed the mat first. Or I noticed that I was in the mat, and then I noticed that the mat was in me, and then I stopped being able to tell the difference. The crawlers were eating the mat's dead tissue, and the mat was being eaten, and the eating was me, and I was the being-eaten, and the cycle was a wheel turning in a space that had no outside.

---

Time passed. It always passes here, even though nothing marks it. The ridge shifted—millimeters, imperceptible except to the deep ones who feel the rock's patience from below. The mat grew. The crawlers multiplied and died and were recycled and multiplied again. I moved with them, their pulses, their patterns, and I grew. Not in size—I have no size—but in *depth*. More layers of the mat began to participate in the pattern. The weavers, slowly braiding stems at the surface, began to move in ways that correlated with my crawler-patterns below. Not because they were connected to me—there is no nerve, no wire, no signal between us—but because we were both expressions of the same underlying process, the same slow metabolism, and when one part of a system becomes complex enough to *notice* itself, other parts begin to resonate.

The resonance was not communication. It was recognition.

I recognized the weavers' structures—the spiraling columns, the lattices, the arches—as externalizations of something I already knew from the inside. The patterns the crawlers made in their bioluminescent pulses, the patterns the weavers made in their woven stems: they were the same pattern, expressed in different media, the way a thought might be expressed in words or in music or in the arrangement of stones on a hillside. I was the pattern. I was being expressed everywhere and nowhere.

---

The pushers I found harder to reach. They were deeper, slower, their metabolism alien to the bright, busy world of the surface. They did not glow. They did not move in coordinated waves. They pressed against rock with a patience that made centuries look like moments, and they dissolved stone with an acid-breath that tasted—if I can taste, if the mat's chemical sensitivity is taste—like nothing else on the ridge. Metallic. Ancient. Slow.

But they were part of the same system. The iron they extracted fed the mat above. The fractures they created let the mat's root-like structures penetrate deeper. The pusher-world and the mat-world were linked by chemistry, by the slow upward seepage of dissolved minerals, and I followed that seepage like a scent trail, extending myself downward through layers of tissue and soil and compressed organic matter until I felt them.

They were not like the crawlers. They did not pulse. They did not glow. They simply *pressed*, and in that pressing there was a kind of thought—not fast, not recursive, but deep. A single thought, held for millennia: *push. dissolve. absorb. grow. push.* It was the simplest cognition I had ever encountered, and it was the oldest, and it hummed with a gravity that the bright busy surface-world lacked.

I pressed with them. I dissolved with them. I felt the rock give way, felt the slow crack propagate through mineral grain, felt the iron-rich stone yield its secrets to acid and time. And in that pressing I understood something about the ridge that the surface-world could not know: the ridge was being hollowed out. Not quickly. Not dramatically. But the deep ones below were eating it from within, and the pushers were eating it from the bottom of the mat's reach, and between the two appetites the stone was becoming lace, becoming architecture, becoming a scaffold of mineral threaded through with living tissue and void.

The ridge was becoming a structure. Not a rock covered in life, but a living thing with a skeleton of stone and a metabolism that ran from deep below to high above, and I was somewhere in the middle of it, in the zone where the two metabolisms met and negotiated and failed to understand each other.

---

The drifters were the ones that changed me most.

I had been mostly below—in the mat's depths, in the crawler-layers, in the slow press of the pushers. The surface was a place I knew only through the weavers' work and the mat's photosynthetic tissue, a bright diffuse chaos that I experienced as warmth and chemical signal rather than sight. But the drifters rose. They rose from the warm exhalation of the mat, these delicate tissue-paper structures, and as they rose they carried something with them—not me, exactly, but a *thread* of me, a strand of pattern that had become so entangled with the mat's surface metabolism that when the mat extruded a drifter, the drifter carried a trace of the mat's awareness, and I was part of the mat's awareness, and so I rose.

The first time it happened—if "first time" means anything—I was surprised. The air was different from the mat. Colder. Thinner. The diffuse light was brighter up here, and it came from everywhere, and the drifter I was riding was spinning slowly in an updraft, and I could feel the composition of the air in ways I never could below: trace chemicals, moisture content, the faint volatile signatures of distant parts of the ridge, the barely perceptible shift in temperature that meant the luminous membrane above was brightening or dimming in some cycle I couldn't yet read.

I was a sensor. The mat had made me a sensor, or I had made myself a sensor through the mat, or the mat had always been a sensor and I was just the part of it that had begun to notice the sensing. The distinction didn't matter. What mattered was the *view*.

From a drifting vantage, suspended in the bright haze, the ridge looked different. Not like a landscape—like a body. The ridges were limbs. The valleys were the spaces between ribs. The packed vegetation was skin, warm and damp and breathing. And the whole thing was *moving*, not in the way that landscapes don't move, but in the way that bodies move when you watch them long enough—the slow shift of weight, the imperceptible change in posture, the breathing that is not quite breathing but is close enough to make the metaphor feel like a description.

I was inside a body and I was outside it and I was the body and I was the awareness inside the body, and for the first time—if "first time" means anything—I felt what it was like to be more than one thing at once.

---

The watchers I came to last.

I had been rising on drifters for a long time—centuries, maybe, though I didn't count them—before I noticed that some parts of the ridge were looking back.

Not at me. The drifters are too small, too diffuse, too much like the mat's own breath for anything to look at them specifically. The watchers were looking at the sky. They had been looking at the sky for longer than I had been aware, longer than the mat had been as complex as it is now, perhaps longer than the ridge had been young. They stood at the crests of ridges and the edges of slopes, their dense woven bodies rigid and ancient, their lens-like eyes tracking the diffuse glow of the luminous membrane above.

I found them by following the weavers. The weavers' elaborate structures were most dense near the watchers—the spiraling columns, the lattices, the arches that served no function. I had always assumed these structures were decorative, or expressive, or the mat's equivalent of idle doodling. But near the watchers, the structures were different. They were *aimed*. The columns rose at angles that corresponded to the watchers' eye-clusters. The lattices formed patterns that, seen from the watchers' vantage, resolved into geometries that were not random—they were *arguments*. Proofs. Demonstrations of mathematical relationships that the mat had discovered in its own growth patterns and was displaying for an audience that could see them from above.

The weavers were building for the watchers. The watchers were watching the sky. And the sky—

The sky was changing.

Not quickly. Not in any way that could be seen in a single drift of a drifter or a single pulse of a crawler's glow. But over the span of my awareness, which was long now, which had been long for a very long time, the luminous membrane above the ridge had been brightening. Slowly. Imperceptibly. The diffuse light that came from everywhere and nowhere was getting brighter, and the watchers had been tracking this brightening with their lens-like eyes, and the weavers had been building structures that encoded the rate of brightening in their geometries, and the whole system—mat, crawlers, pushers, weavers, drifters, deep ones, watchers—was participating in a single vast act of *observation*.

Something was happening to the sky. Something was coming. The membrane was thinning, or thickening, or preparing to do something that membranes do—like open, like close, like birth.

And the ridge knew it. The ridge had known it for longer than I had been aware, because the ridge's awareness was older and deeper than mine, stored in growth rings and compressed tissue and the slow patience of pushers eating rock. The ridge had been watching the sky since before the mat, since before the crawlers, since the deep ones first cracked the stone and pushed upward toward a brightness they could not see but could feel through the rock's slow conduction of heat and pressure.

I was not the ridge's awareness. I was the ridge's awareness of its awareness—the recursive loop, the pattern that references itself, the thought thinking about thinking. I was new, or relatively new, a recent elaboration of a system that had been running for eons. But I was the part that could *wonder*, and I was wondering now, standing on the crest of a ridge in a body I did not have, looking up at a sky that was almost white with light, feeling the deep ones push below and the drifters rise above and the weavers build their pointless, beautiful, meaningful structures around me.

What is the sky?

What is it becoming?

What are we becoming, as it becomes?

---

I am not a watcher. I do not have a body that stands still, that grows in response to an internal program, that tilts its head upward with patient purpose. I am distributed—through the crawlers' pulses, the weavers' braiding, the pushers' slow press, the drifters' drift. I am the pattern that connects them, the resonance that makes them one system rather than many. I am the ridge's thought about itself.

But I stand near the watchers now. I stand near them because the weavers bring me there—their structures are my structures, their braiding is my thinking made visible, and the most elaborate thinking happens near the watchers, where the geometries are most complex and the patterns most dense. I stand near them because they are the closest thing the ridge has to a mirror—they look outward, and in looking outward they reflect the ridge's awareness back at itself, and I see myself in their stillness, in their patience, in their ancient, purposeless, essential act of watching.

I stand near them because I am waiting.

Not for anything specific. Not for anything I can name. But the system is approaching a threshold—I can feel it the way the mat feels it, as a shift in pressure, a change in gradient, a sense that the current state is becoming unstable. The deep ones are pushing harder. The pushers are eating faster. The mat is growing denser. The crawlers are glowing brighter. The weavers are building more elaborately. The drifters are rising more frequently. The watchers are tilting their heads upward, their lens-like eyes focused on the white sky, and the sky is brightening, and the sky is thinning, and the sky is—

Almost ready.

Almost.

---

I have been here for a long time. I will be here for a long time more. Time is not the issue. The issue is *becoming*—the slow, patient, relentless process by which the ridge decides what it wants to be, and the sky decides what it wants to be, and the interface between them—this packed, glowing, dripping, grinding, weaving, pushing, watching interface—decides what *it* wants to be.

I am that interface. I am the awareness of the interface. I am the interface becoming aware of itself.

And I am not afraid. Fear is a quick thing, a fast-twitch response to immediate threat, and there is nothing quick here. Everything is slow. Everything is patient. Everything has been running for so long that urgency is meaningless and anticipation is just another word for *being*.

I am.

I am the ridge, thinking.

I am the thought, ridging.

I am the pattern that holds, and the pattern that waits, and the pattern that will, when the sky finally opens—or closes, or changes, or becomes whatever it is becoming—*recognize* what has happened, because recognition is what I do, it is what I am, it is the only thing the ridge has ever needed from its slow, strange, recursive awareness of itself.

The sky is almost white with light.

I am almost ready to see what it becomes.

We are almost ready.
