You are the lip.

Not the whole terrace system — that would be too much, would be the Deep Ones themselves, distributed and slow. Just the edge. The rim of resistant caprock where the ground decides, suddenly, to stop being horizontal and begin its long descent into the basin. You are perhaps twelve feet long and four feet wide, a shelf of dense grey stone that has held its position against water and time while everything around it has softened and dissolved. Your surface is rough underfoot, crystalline, the kind of rock that bites back when you scrape against it. Your underside is a different story — hollowed, smoothed, undercut by millennia of warm water threading through fractures, so that you jut outward slightly, a prow, a tongue of stone tasting the air above the drop.

Below you, the first terrace catches a thin sheet of milky water that spills over your edge in a curtain so fine it doesn't fall so much as *peel* away from you, detaching molecule by molecule into the warm air. You feel this. Not the way a person feels — you have no nerves — but the way a loaded beam feels: as a distribution of forces, a continuous reassessment of what you are bearing. The water is always there, always pulling, always dissolving you at the molecular level. You are being eaten. Slowly. You have been being eaten for sixty thousand years. You will continue to be being eaten for some time yet, because you are hard stone, and the water is patient, and the timescale of this transaction is geological.

There is a moss growing in a crack near your center. You feel its rhizoids the way you might feel an itch if you had skin — a persistent low-level presence, a foreign thing doing chemistry in your joints. The moss is pulling you apart, one mineral grain at a time. This is fine. This is the nature of your existence. You are the foundation, and things grow on foundations, and growing things break foundations, and broken foundations become soil, and soil becomes life, and life becomes you again, eventually, through the long slow cycle of dissolution and deposition. You understand this the way a river understands gravity — not as a fact but as the condition of being what you are.

You have a view. You face outward, over the full expanse of the terrace system, and the view is extraordinary in the way that things are extraordinary when they are simply, fully themselves. The brass-colored light comes from everywhere. The haze thickens toward the vanishing point. Steam columns rise in the distance like the exhalations of something sleeping. Between you and the haze, the terraces descend in their broad shallow steps, each one a basin of milky water and golden travertine and impossible green, and the green is moving — the canopy dwellers, thousands of them, flickering between the fern fronds like living sparks. You don't see them as individuals. You see them the way you see weather: as a pattern of disturbance on the surface of the air, a shimmer, a rippling of the light.

You are aware of the Deep Ones beneath you. You are in contact with the aquifer — your deepest fractures reach down into the water table, and through those fractures you feel the pulse of their metabolism, the slow chemical grinding, the pressure of supersaturated water seeking paths upward. They do not know you are above them. You do not know they are below you. But you are connected — water is the medium, and water connects everything here, and the system is continuous from the deepest fracture in the basalt to the highest frond of the tallest fern, and you are part of that continuity, a hard node in a soft network, a place where the water pauses and pools and then moves on.

Sometimes — rarely, but it happens — someone stands on you. Their weight is nothing to you, a rounding error in the calculus of load-bearing. But you register the *nature* of the weight: warm, dry, temporary, strange. Not like the rain. Not like the moss. A thing that breathes and moves and will leave. The vibration of your footsteps travels through you and into the rock below and into the water below that, and the system registers you the way it registers everything — as a perturbation, a brief deviation from equilibrium, a small disturbance in the silence.

You do not watch them leave. You have no preference about it. You are the lip of the terrace, the edge of the world, the place where the ground says *here, and then down*, and you have been saying it for longer than there have been ears to hear it. You will continue. The water will continue. The moss in your crack will widen it, eventually, by a distance you cannot measure but can feel as a long, slow certainty, the way you might feel the approach of winter. One day, perhaps ten thousand years from now, you will crack, and a piece of you will fall, and it will become part of the terrace below, and something will grow on it, and the cycle will continue.

The drop of water that has been forming at the tip of the fern frond above you — the fern that grows from your crack, whose roots are in your body — it swells. It catches the brass-colored light. It falls.

It hits the shoulder of the warm, strange, temporary thing standing on you.

You feel the vibration of the impact, faint, a tiny percussion transmitted through air rather than stone, and you think nothing about it, because you do not think, but you *register* it, the way you register everything: as part of the continuous, ongoing, unfinishable process of being the place where the world begins to fall.
