I found myself at the threshold, where the outside world began to soften and weep.

The first thing I noticed was the water—not falling, not flowing, but *seeping*. It emerged from the soil in countless tiny veins, as if the ground itself were perspiring. The elevation was so low that the horizon had collapsed into a flat seam between grey-green sky and darker green earth, and everything felt pressed down, settled, like a landscape that had been slowly sinking for millennia and had finally come to rest in its own damp lap.

I stepped forward, and the ground rose around my foot. Water, dark as tea from tannins, pooled in the hollows of roots and stones. Between these pools, the earth was carpeted in a thick, springy mat of moss and liverworts, punctuated by buttress roots that spread outward and downward like the foundations of something vast.

The trees were not trees. They were structures—colossal, slow-growing, their trunks a dense fibrous composite layered like cathedral walls. Their canopies interwove into a continuous ceiling, a living roof through which light filtered in scattered, normal-quality beams, dappling the floor below. It was the light of a cloudy afternoon, steady and diffuse, casting no harsh shadows.

And then I saw the order.

The great plant-structures were arranged in clusters, like neighborhoods, with open spaces between them that functioned as plazas. The density was sparse—deliberate emptiness gave each structure room to breathe. Some clusters were connected by arched walkways formed from fused branches; others stood alone, isolated in clearings where the ground was a sheet of still water. Beneath the canopy, at root level, networks of tunnels and chambers were lit by a bioluminescence that pulsed faintly, like the breathing of a sleeping animal.

The technology was beyond modern. It was intrinsic, woven into the plants' cells—computational, their vascular systems carrying not only sap but data, their leaves acting as antennae. The plant-buildings were not static; they were dynamic, constantly growing, repairing, reconfiguring. I could hear it: a low, continuous hum, the sound of a million tiny processes, punctuated by sharper clicks and creaks as branches shifted and roots groaned.

The fauna was abundant. Insects clouded the air, their wings catching light like flecks of gold. Amphibians clung to mossy walls, translucent bodies, jewel-like eyes. Birds moved through the canopy in flashes of color. And stranger things—creatures that were part plant, part animal, covered in leaves or ending in tendrils, moving with purpose, tending to the plant-buildings, pruning, watering, as if they were caretakers of a living city.

The dynamic was turbulent. A wall sprouted a new window overnight; a tower collapsed into compost, replaced by new growth rising with visible speed. The ground shifted, roots breaking the surface, pools draining and reforming. The air was alive with movement—leaves rustling, branches swaying, creatures scurrying, water dripping. The sound was a symphony of wetness and motion, loud and immersive.

And yet, for all its turbulence, there was a deep, ancient calm. The erosion had worn away everything but the essential. The water, warmth, light, and life were part of a single, self-sustaining system, a closed loop running since before memory. The rules were simple: everything connected, everything alive, everything in motion.

I was in a place that should not exist—a strange, loud, wet, warm, ancient, small, sparse, urban, beyond-modern, normal-lit, abundant, weird, turbulent miracle.

A grove that dreamed it was a metropolis.

And in dreaming, had become one.
