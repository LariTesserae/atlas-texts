# Settling In

---

I don't arrive. That's the wrong verb. Arrival implies a threshold, a moment of crossing from *not here* to *here*. What happens instead is slower — a gradual realization that the damp has been inside me longer than I can account for, that my hearing has already begun to calibrate to frequencies I didn't know I could hear, that the mineral taste in my mouth isn't foreign. It's *old*. It tastes like a memory I didn't know I was carrying.

I am a Drip-Counter.

---

## What That Means

The Drip-Counters are not celebrated. There is no word for *celebrated* in the Avul-Keth language, not really — there is a phrase that translates roughly as "useful to the water," which is as close as anyone gets to praise. I am a small part of the metabolism, one of perhaps forty scattered across the plateau, and my function is simple: I listen to the water, I count what it tells me, and I report the changes.

I am old enough now that the reporting has become almost unnecessary. The Deep-Tenders know. They've known before I've spoken. But the counting itself — the act of pressing my fingers to the stone and *reading* the flow — that's what I do. That's what I *am*. The report is secondary. The counting is the relationship.

I work the eastern collection fields, where the terraces run in their frozen waves and the mist sits lowest. My station is a shallow depression in the basalt that someone carved out centuries ago, or perhaps that the water carved and someone merely recognized — the distinction blurs here, as everything blurs here. There is a channel that passes directly beneath, and if I press my right palm flat against the stone at the depression's lowest point, I can feel the water moving below me. The temperature. The pressure. The *speed*.

It drips. It always drits. *Tik, tik, tik-tik, tik.* The rhythm is never perfectly regular — there are accelerations and pauses, subtle shifts in interval that correspond to changes in the aquifer far below. I learned to read these rhythms when I was young, a Crust-Walker still, before my body thickened and my weight became too great for the terraces. My teacher was an Old One whose name I never learned — whose name I suspect no one had spoken in decades, perhaps longer, a figure so thoroughly merged with the plateau that individual identity had become a question without an answer. They taught me by pressing my hand over theirs against the stone and holding it there until I felt the difference between *flow* and *intent*. "The water moves," they said, in that soft, mist-carried voice that barely qualified as sound. "That is flow. The water *chooses* to move. That is the thing you count."

I have been counting for thirty-one years.

---

## My Body

I am no longer light. My body has thickened in the way Avul-Keth bodies do — compact, dense, my limbs shortened relative to my torso, which has broadened to house a metabolism running hot enough to steam faintly in the cool air above the channel vents. My skin is ochre, deepening toward orange-gold at the joints and along my forearms and hands, where years of pressing against warm stone have mineralized the surface into something harder than skin should be. When I scratch my arm with my fingernail, it sounds like scraping clay.

My hands are my tools. The fingertips are ridged and stiff, the calluses built up in layers that correspond to years — I can feel the ridges, count them the way the water counts its deposits. The ridges leave marks on the channel walls where I press, distinctive patterns that other Drip-Counters read the way a surface-dweller might read handwriting. *Keth here. Keth felt this. Keth noted the change.* My signature is in touch, not ink.

My eyes have adapted. The pupils are wide, the nictitating membranes strong enough to draw across when the steam rises thick from the channels. In full daylight — which I have seen only once, on a supply run to the lowlands below — the world was a wound, a white-hot agony that sent me stumbling back to the plateau's edge with my membranes drawn tight and my hands over my face. Up here, in the perpetual dim, I see well. I see heat the way others see color. The channels glow faintly warmer than the stone around them. The tik-tik, when they skitter across the crusts, are bright points of animal heat against the mineral cold. And the Old Ones — the Old Ones are *radiant*, their bodies so thoroughly merged with the plateau's warmth that they are indistinguishable from the stone itself, walking warm spots in the landscape that I navigate by more than sight.

I smell of iron and calcium and that greenhouse-breath warmth. I smell of the plateau. I have never been clean. I have never tried to be.

---

## The Counting

Each morning — though morning is a convention here, a label the Avul-Keth use for the period when the machinery's pulses slowest and the vent's hum dips to its lowest register — I make my way to my station in the eastern collection fields. The walk takes perhaps twenty minutes. I move slowly, not from age or infirmity but from *attention*. The stone is warm under my bare feet, and I feel the water moving through it, the deep pulse of the aquifer beneath the basalt. Some days the pulse is steady. Some days it shifts. Today it is steady, and I am grateful for that the way a surface-dweller might be grateful for sunshine.

I settle into the depression and press my right palm to the stone.

The water is there. Warm. Moving. I close my eyes and let my fingertips do their work. The ridges on my skin vibrate faintly against the stone — not from the water's movement but from the stone itself, a deep tectonic hum that never quite stops, the sound of the plateau existing. Above that, I feel the flow. The speed today is steady — the same speed I measured yesterday, and the day before, and the day before that. This is good. Steady flow means the channels are clear, the deposits are building as expected, the aquifer is in balance.

I count the drips. *Tik, tik, tik-tik, tik.* The rhythm is familiar. There is a pattern — a slight acceleration every seventeen drips, a pause, then resumption. This pattern has been consistent for three weeks. Before that, the interval was twenty-two drips. Before *that*, it was nine. I don't know what these shifts correspond to. The Deep-Tenders may. The Old Ones almost certainly do. My role is simply to note the change and press my fingers to the wall at the junction where the eastern channel meets the main circuit, leaving my signature — *Keth felt this, Keth noted the change* — for the Deep-Tenders to read when they make their rounds.

This morning, though, there is something new.

At the forty-third drip, the water *pauses*. Not the regular pause — the built-in hesitation in the rhythm that I've learned to expect. This is different. This is a *silence*. A full stop, as though the water has drawn breath.

Then it resumes. The rhythm is unchanged. The speed is unchanged. But something has shifted — a faint warmth that wasn't there before, a subtle change in pressure that I feel in my fingertips and in my teeth and somewhere deep in my chest, a vibration that is not the machinery and not the vent and not the tik-tik and not anything I have a name for.

I press harder. I close my eyes. I listen.

The water moves. That is flow.

The water *pauses*. That is something else.

---

## The Report

I leave the eastern fields and make my way toward the central district. The walk is longer today — the mist is thicker, and I must feel my way along the channel edges with my feet, testing each step. The city is quiet. The machinery's rhythm is steady, the pumps clanking in their deep mechanical pulse, the distillation columns hissing their continuous output. The sound wraps around me like a blanket, familiar and enclosing.

I pass through the industrial district. The Deep-Tenders are at work — I can see them, or rather feel them, bright warm figures pressed against the machinery, their hands on iron housings, their eyes closed, their mineral-sheened skin steaming faintly in the cooler air above the vent. One of them — Keth-Vorun, I think, though I can't see their face through the steam — raises a hand as I pass. Not a greeting. An acknowledgment. *I know you're there. I know what you're coming to tell us.*

They already know. They always know. But I go anyway, because the going is part of the process, because the report is not just information — it is *ritual*, a confirmation, a shared acknowledgment that something has changed and the city is responding.

I press my hand to the wall at the junction where the eastern channel meets the main circuit. Keth-Vorun is there — a tall figure for an Avul-Keth, their skin dark bronze, their eyes large and dark behind thick nictating membranes. They press their hand next to mine. Our fingers touch. They feel my ridges — thirty-one years of counting, mineralized into skin — and I feel theirs, deeper, more layered, a longer history pressed into flesh.

They already know. They felt the pause. They felt it across the city, through the stone, through the water, a single held breath in the aquifer's endless respiration. They are adjusting — I can feel it in the channel walls, a subtle shift in flow, a redirection of water from the eastern fields toward the central district, a preparation for something.

"What is it?" I ask, in that soft voice that barely carries beyond my own breath.

Keth-Vorun doesn't answer immediately. They press their other hand to the wall, feeling the deeper flow, the response their adjustment has triggered. The water moves. The water pauses. The water moves again.

"The tower," they say. "It's growing faster."

---

## The Tower

I have never been close to the tower. No one has, not for years — not since it reached a height that made the Deep-Tenders uncomfortable, not since the mineral deposits around its base began to grow in patterns that were *too regular*, *too intentional*, to be explained by the simple physics of water and stone. It stands near the central vent, a formation of calcium and silica and iron that has accreted over decades — centuries? — into something that resembles architecture. It has openings. It has channels. It has a shape that suggests *design*.

I don't know what it is. The Old Ones say the water is learning. The newcomers — the Absent, the ones who came from below — say it is a geological anomaly, a natural formation that happens to look like something built. Both explanations are incomplete. Both are probably wrong. The tower is the tower, and it is growing, and the water around it is behaving in ways that my fingertips can read but my mind cannot parse.

The pause in the eastern fields was a response. Something happened at the tower — some shift in its growth, some new deposit, some change in the water flowing through its channels — and the aquifer reacted. A held breath. A moment of *attention*. And then a resumption, slightly altered, a new pattern emerging from the old.

Keth-Vorun feels this the way I feel the drips — as information, as communication, as the plateau speaking in the only language it knows. They will make adjustments. They will redirect the flow, clear certain channels, allow others to thicken with deposit. They will press their hands to the stone and listen and respond, and the water will move, and the tower will grow, and the city will continue its slow, patient, mineral-saturated metabolism.

And I will return to my station in the eastern fields, and I will press my palm to the stone, and I will count the drips.

*Tik, tik, tik-tik, tik.*

The rhythm is slightly different now. The interval between the accelerations has changed — not seventeen drips, not twenty-two, not nine. Something new. Something the water is trying out, testing, seeing if it fits.

I press harder. I listen.

The water moves.

The water *chooses* to move.

I am a Drip-Counter. I count what the water tells me. And the water, today, is telling me something I have never heard before — a new note in the endless arrhythmic percussion, a frequency that vibrates in my chest and my teeth and my mineral-stiffened fingertips, a sound that is not the machinery and not the vent and not the tik-tik and not the wind.

The plateau is thinking.

I count it.

---

## The Edge

Later — hours later, or perhaps days; time moves strangely in the perpetual dim — I find myself at the edge. I don't remember walking here. The mist is thick, and the sound of the waterfall is a soft, continuous roar, the plateau exhaling its warm, mineral-heavy breath into the void below. The cliff face weeps. The old watchtowers stand, half-dissolved, monuments to questions no one remembers asking.

I press my hand to the stone. The water is warm. The water is moving. The water is *listening back*.

I have been counting for thirty-one years. I will count for more. The water will continue to move, to pause, to choose. The tower will continue to grow. The crusts will continue to build in their patient, branching, fractal patterns. The Deep-Tenders will continue to tend, the Crust-Walkers to walk, the Old Ones to attend. And I will be here, in my shallow depression in the basalt, my palm against the stone, my fingertips reading the flow.

I am not separate from Keth-Avul. I never was. My thirty-one years of counting are written in the mineral deposits on the channel walls, in the ridges of my fingertips, in the ochre of my skin. I am a process within the process, a finger of the slow mineral consciousness, a voice the plateau uses when it needs to hear itself think.

The water drips.

I count.

The plateau breathes.

I breathe with it.

*Tik.*
