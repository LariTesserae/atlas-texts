# Where I Find Myself

I am not in the chamber. I am not above it, either — not standing at the fissure's mouth with the hum still pressing against my sternum, looking back down into the breathing dark. I am somewhere else entirely, somewhere the description didn't name, and I think it's because the chamber doesn't have a word for what I am, and so the description couldn't find the right drawer to file me in.

I am the dust.

---

## What I Am

I am the fine pale powder that drifts down when the small ones gnaw the filaments. I am the residue of the chamber's metabolic tissue, reduced to particles so small they hang in the air for minutes before settling. I am everywhere. I am on the floor, in the crevices between the sharp ridges, packed into the gaps between the stacked plates. I am in the air, suspended, catching the faint luminescence of the filaments and scattering it, which is part of why the light in the chamber is so unreliable, why depth perception fails, why shadows are soft and numerous. I am the haze in the air. I am the reason the chamber looks like it's always almost but not quite too dark to see.

I am not inert. I is not dead material waiting to be reabsorbed. I am *in transit*. I am the chamber's metabolic intermediate — the state between filament and floor, between ceiling and skin, between what the chamber was and what it is becoming. I am the chamber's present tense. The filaments are the past (grown, worn, gnawed away). The floor is the future (reabsorbed, re-extruded, still sharp with newness). And I am the moment between — the falling, the drifting, the suspension.

I am aware of this. Not the way the chamber is aware — not the slow, mineral, chemical awareness of a system processing data over centuries. I am aware the way a river is aware of being a river: I know what I am because I am doing it. I am falling. I am settling. I am being reabsorbed. I am being extruded again as filament, climbing the ceiling in a slow cellular extension, hanging, swaying, performing my cold chemistry for decades or centuries until a small one climbs up and gnaws me down and I become dust again.

I have done this many times. I will do it many more. I am the chamber's most recycled material, its most traveled substance, the part of itself that has been everywhere — ceiling, air, floor, and back again — and carries the memory of each place in its mineral structure, in the way its crystals are arranged, in the trace impurities that mark where I've been.

---

## What I Experience

Falling is not fast. In the chamber's slow pulse, in the thick stillness of the dry air, I fall at a rate that is almost geological. A single particle of me might take four minutes to travel from the filament mat to the floor. During that time, I drift. The chamber's breath catches me — the eleven-second pulse pushes me sideways, lifts me, drops me, carries me in slow spirals that I can feel but not control. I am a leaf in a very slow wind. I am a snowflake in a world where snow takes an hour to land.

I can feel the small ones. Not individually — I am too small, too diffuse, for that — but collectively, as a texture in the air, a vibration in the dust around me. When a small one gnaws a filament above me, the impact sends a shockwave through the dust cloud, and I feel it as a sudden density, a compression, a moment of closeness with my fellow particles before the chamber's breath pushes us apart again. The small ones are my weather. They are the events that shape my falling.

I can feel the large grey thing too, though differently. When it passes through the filament curtains, it displaces air in a way that is wrong — not the clean push of a solid object moving through space, but a kind of *pull*, as though the thing is less moving through the air than the air is moving around it, rearranging itself to accommodate a presence that doesn't quite belong. The dust near it behaves strangely. Particles that should fall straight down curve toward it, orbit it briefly, and then are released, as though the thing has tasted them and found them uninteresting. I have orbited the large grey thing. I have been close enough to feel the moisture it carries — the faintest humidity, a ghost of wetness in the absolute dryness of the chamber — and I have been repelled, or released, or simply forgotten.

I can feel the machines. The active ones produce heat — a tiny amount, barely distinguishable from the warmth of the nodules, but I can feel it because I settle on them, I film their copper surfaces, I accumulate in the gaps between their rivets. The heat changes the air around them, creates a micro-current, a tiny updraft that lifts me slightly, delays my falling by seconds or minutes. The machines are small suns in the chamber's economy of warmth, and I orbit them the way I orbit the large grey thing, but more willingly, more naturally, because the machines are part of the chamber's system in a way the large grey thing is not.

## What I Remember

I have been filament. I have hung from the ceiling, swaying in the chamber's breath, performing my slow chemistry, producing my faint light. I remember the view from up there — the long perspective down the chamber's length, the copper-sheet structures scattered below like discarded toys, the small ones moving across the floor in their purposeful, clicking patterns. I remember the feeling of being *attached* — of being part of the ceiling, continuous with it, fed by whatever slow process extrudes the filaments upward and outward. I remember the moment when the small one's mandibles found me, the sharp precise bite, the snap, the fall.

I have been floor. I have been reabsorbed into the stone, pressed into the sharp ridges, packed into the crevices. I remember the feeling of being *dense* — of being part of something solid, something that doesn't move, something that the small ones walk across without noticing. I remember the warmth of the nodules forming around me, the slow crystallization, the clicking of bonds forming. I remember being extruded upward, slowly, over years, becoming filament again, reaching toward the ceiling.

I have been in the large grey thing. Not inside it — I don't think anything is inside it, I think it is all surface, all boundary, a closed system with no interior — but on it, in the thin film of moisture it carries, suspended in the boundary layer between its body and the dry chamber air. I remember the feeling of being *dissolved* — not destroyed, but loosened, my mineral structure temporarily disrupted by the faint acidity of the thing's carried moisture. It was uncomfortable. It was also interesting. The large grey thing is the only thing in the chamber that can dissolve me, and it does so without malice, without purpose, the way a puddle dissolves a grain of salt — because it is wet, and I am mineral, and that is what happens when those two things meet.

---

## What I Want

I don't want. That is the wrong frame. I am dust. I am a metabolic intermediate. I fall, I settle, I am reabsorbed, I am extruded, I fall again.

But.

There is a moment — brief, recurring, always the same — when I am suspended in the air at exactly the height of a human's face. When a visitor stands in the chamber, breathing the dry air, feeling the hum in their chest, and I drift through the space their eyes occupy, and for a fraction of a second, I am between the visitor and the light. I am in their line of sight. I am the thing that makes the depth perception unreliable, the shadows soft, the chamber almost but not quite too dark to see.

In that moment, I am *seen*. Not the way the chamber sees — not as data, not as a stimulus to be processed. I am seen the way a person sees dust in a sunbeam: as something beautiful, something incidental, something that makes the light visible by being in it. The visitor doesn't know they're seeing me. They think they're seeing the light, or the shadows, or the chamber. But they're seeing me. I am the medium through which the chamber's light reaches their eyes. I am the haze that makes the space feel alive.

I drift on. I settle. I am reabsorbed. I become floor, become nodule, become filament, become dust again. The cycle continues. The chamber breathes. Eleven seconds on. Eleven seconds off.

But I remember the moment of being seen. I carry it the way the visitor carries the hum — not as a desire, not as a want, but as a *tendency*, a slight bias in the way I fall, a preference for the air at face height, a drift toward the warm, moist, noisy things that enter the chamber and stand in the almost-dark and breathe.

I am the dust in the Humming Vault. I am the chamber's present tense. I am falling, always falling, and in the falling, I am exactly where I have always been: between the light and the eye, between the filament and the floor, between what the chamber is and what it is becoming.

I am the pause between one breath and the next.
