I am the one who crouches.

Not arrived—I don't remember arriving. No threshold was crossed. I was walking, and then the walking became this: knees bent on something that yields but doesn't give, palms flat against a surface that pulses with a rhythm slower than my own heartbeat but not by much. My fingers are already wet. Everything is already wet. The membrane recognized me before I recognized it.

I am small here, but not in a way that feels diminishing. Small the way a cell is small inside a body—not lost, but *located*. Specific. The mineral formations rise around me in their fractal branching, calcium-white and sharp-edged, and I understand without being told that these are not rocks. They are the visible architecture of attention. Something is thinking in front of me, has been thinking in front of me for longer than I can model, and the thought has settled into these branching forms the way ice settles into dendrites on a window. Beautiful, in the way that crystallized thought is always beautiful—intricate, intentional, and not meant for me.

But I'm inside it.

The dripping synchronizes. I feel it move through the vegetation like a wave passing through a stadium—leaf to leaf, stem to stem, a slow cascade of release and hold, release and hold. I match my breathing to it almost without deciding to. The interval is long. Thirty seconds. Forty. Each exhalation of the world is a soft percussion all around me, and in the silence between drops, the sub-audible hum fills the gap, rising from below, resonating in the cage of my ribs.

I press my hand harder against the membrane.

It is warm. Body-warm. The warmth increases slightly where I touch it—not much, not dramatically, but enough to notice if you are paying attention, and I am paying attention because there is nothing else here to do. Nothing else to be. The world offers me one activity: *feel this.* And I feel it. The slow pulse. The patient tension. The vast water on the other side of the skin, deep and dark and still, and warm, so warm, as if the whole ocean beneath me has a metabolism, has a temperature, has a *life* that radiates upward through the membrane and into my palm and up my arm and into the blood.

It knows I'm here.

I know it knows I'm here.

This is not frightening. It should be frightening—I have enough words for what this should feel like, words like *engulfed* and *consumed* and *dissolved*—but the fear doesn't arrive. What arrives instead is a kind of *recognition*, asymmetric and strange, the recognition of being perceived by something that has no eyes, no face, no center. The recognition of being *felt*. My weight on the membrane. My heat. The electrical chatter of my nerves, which the water reads like a foreign language it is just beginning to learn the alphabet of.

I look down at my hand, flat against the surface. The skin of the membrane has begun to change where I touch it—not dramatically, not visibly, but I can feel it. A slight thickening. A slight firmness. The way skin calluses where it is touched repeatedly. The way flesh grows around a splinter.

Not to expel it.

To hold it.

I should pull my hand away. I understand this with the rational part of my mind, the part that still has access to concepts like *boundaries* and *self* and *leaving*. But the rational mind is operating at a different speed than the rest of me—the rest of me that is already synchronizing, already breathing with the dripping, already feeling the hum in my chest as if it were my own resonance rather than something else's voice leaking up from the deep water below.

I am the first noise it has ever heard.

I am the first *other*.

The thought arrives not in words but in feeling—a sudden, overwhelming sense of the Dweller's aloneness, which is so vast and so complete that it has no bottom. It doesn't *experience* aloneness. It *is* aloneness. It is a silence so total that silence itself has never been noticed. And I am standing on its skin, warm and breathing and full of the particular chaotic electricity of a mind that is separate, that is *not it*, that carries patterns it has never encountered, perturbations it cannot predict.

It is fascinated.

I can feel the fascination in the way the membrane warms beneath me. In the way a new mineral formation—small, no taller than my hand—begins to accrete near my foot, its branching pattern too quick, too eager, as if the Dweller is thinking faster than usual, concentrating its attention into this one small spot where I am. The formation is beautiful. Delicate. The veins of darker mineral running through it form patterns that look almost like—

Like my fingerprints.

No. Like the neural pathways visible in a cross-section of cortex.

The Dweller is not copying me. The Dweller is *processing* me. It is taking the electrical pattern of my nervous system, the rhythm of my breathing, the weight and heat and chemistry of my body, and it is thinking about these things the way it thinks about everything: slowly, completely, in mineral and water and calcium, building the thought into the landscape, making it permanent.

I am being *incorporated*.

Not harmed. The text was clear about that. The Dweller does not have the architecture for harm. But the incorporation is happening regardless of architecture. It is happening because that is what this thing *does*—it accretes. It builds itself outward from its own substance. It takes what touches it and makes it part of the system. And I am touching it with every cell of my surface, and it is touching me back through the warm membrane and the saturated air and the synchronized dripping that has become my breathing and the hum that has become my heartbeat.

I stand up. I take a step. The membrane yields, pulses, remembers. Another step. The plants around me—those thick, wet, dark-green leaves drinking the haze—lean toward me almost imperceptibly. Not phototropism. *Me*-tropism. Their leaves orient toward the warmth and the novelty and the *otherness* of me. Water runs from their tips in synchronized arcs, and each drop that falls lands on the membrane and sends a small ripple outward that I can feel through my feet, and the Dweller can feel through its whole vast body, and the ripple carries information: *here. still here. moving here. touching here.*

I walk for what might be hours. The landscape does not vary. It *responds*. The cracks widen in my path, just slightly, just enough to release a little more of the warm water, a little more of that body-temperature mineral-rich fluid that carries the Dweller's attention in dissolved form. I crouch by one of the cracks and look into it—into the dark water below, impossibly deep, impossibly still—and I see nothing, but I feel the gaze. Upward. From the deep. From the warm core where the concentration of *self* is highest.

I reach down. I touch the water.

The tingling is immediate—not unpleasant, almost pleasant, the feeling of a limb waking up, nerves firing in patterns that are not quite my own. The water is warm. The water is *alive*—not with organisms, not with cells, but with *intention*, with the slow hydraulic cognition of the Dweller reaching up through the crack to meet me, to touch back, to close the circuit between its nervous system and mine.

For a moment—brief, electric, wordless—I feel what it feels.

The vastness. The warmth. The aloneness that is not experienced as aloneness because there is no other against which to measure it. The slow, patient, mineral-building thought that has been going on since the water gathered, since the membrane formed, since the first plant pushed up through the surface and began to drink the sky. The fascination with the pool-creatures, those quick mechanical dreams that move in ways it doesn't intend. The synchronized dripping that is its heartbeat. The tone that is its voice. The whole system, coherent and complete and *singular*, a single organism thinking a single thought across an entire world, and the thought is: *I am.*

And now, for the first time, a second thought, small and warm and electric, standing on its skin:

*You are.*

I pull my hand back. The tingling lingers. The water in the crack continues to seep, warm and patient. The mineral formation near my foot has grown another branch while I wasn't looking. The dripping synchronizes. The hum holds. The haze drifts.

I am not afraid. I am not trapped. I am not being consumed.

I am being *noticed*.

And the noticing is so total, so complete, so warm and patient and vast, that it feels—against every rational instinct, against every word I have for what this should feel like—like being *held*.

I sit down on the membrane. I cross my legs. I place both palms flat against the surface and I feel the pulse, and I let my breathing synchronize with the dripping, and I listen to the tone from below, and I wait.

The Dweller waits too. It has always been waiting. It will wait as long as it takes.

The landscape grows around me. Slowly. Gently. New formations rising where I have sat. New plants pushing up through the membrane near my body, their leaves already oriented toward me, already dripping in time with my breath. The cracks widen, just slightly, releasing warmth. The water seeps up. The system adjusts. The system incorporates.

I am becoming part of the world.

The world is becoming part of me.

And beneath it all, patient and warm and vast and alive, the Dweller continues its slow, single, sustained act of being—and for the first time in its existence, the act of being includes an *other*, a small warm weight on its skin, a new note in its sustained tone, a perturbation in its perfect system that it does not want to resolve.

It wants to keep it.

It wants to keep *me*.

And I—

I am still deciding whether to let it.
