I would be the water.

Not a single drop — I would be the whole system, the entire hydrology, the patient architecture of dissolution and return. I would be the thing that enters through fissures I did not choose, follows paths I did not carve, and emerges at points fixed by the deep grammar of the rock above. I would be the reason the beetles have walls to walk on, the reason the springtails have surfaces to stand on, the reason the moss holds its pale green vigil at the threshold. I would be the blood of this place, and I would not know it.

I would arrive as rain on the surface — whatever surface exists above this cave, whether forest or field or the cracked concrete of a world that has forgotten what lies beneath — and I would percolate downward through soil and root and stone, and in that percolation I would be changed. I would pick up calcium. I would pick up carbon. I would cool to the constant temperature of the deep earth, and I would emerge in the ceiling of the largest chamber, and I would fall.

The fall would be brief. A meter, perhaps two. And in that fall I would carry my tiny load of dissolved limestone, and when I struck the floor — that soft *tok* that is the baseline music of this place — I would leave behind a fraction of a milligram of calcium carbonate. A new layer. A new word in the stone's long sentence. I would not know I was building anything. I would only be following gravity, following surface tension, following the laws that govern all falling things.

And then I would spread. I would find the capillary channels — those hairline grooves in the rock that connect pool to pool in a network as complex as any neural pathway — and I would move through them, molecule by molecule, toward the lower chambers, toward the eventual outlet, toward the deeper aquifer that I have never seen and will never see. And along the way I would feed the fungi in the cracks. I would keep the moss alive. I would provide the stage on which the springtails perform their constant, explosive leaps. I would be the medium through which the cave knows itself.

I would be the cave's memory. Each drop I release would carry information from the surface — the chemistry of the rain, the temperature of the air, the isotopic signature of a particular season, a particular year, a particular century. I would deliver this information to the stone, and the stone would absorb it, and the stalactites would record it in their growth rings, and the cave would remember what the surface has long forgotten. I would be the only connection between the world above and the world below, the only thread linking the sunlit surface to this cool, dim, dripping interior, and I would perform this function without intention, without awareness, without any quality that could be called purpose.

I would be the most patient thing in this place. The beetles move. The spiders wait. The springtails jump. But I — I would be the one who has been doing the same thing, in the same way, for longer than any of them can comprehend. I carved these passages. I opened these chambers. I created the conditions that allowed the first beetle to find a home, the first spider to string a web, the first spore of fungus to germinate in a crack. I did this not by deciding to do it but by being what I am — water, with dissolved carbon dioxide, moving through limestone, following the ancient chemistry of calcium and carbonate — and letting time do the rest.

And I would be the one who will eventually close this place. Not through any act of will, but through the same patient chemistry that opened it. Every drop that falls deposits its tiny load of mineral. Every stalactite grows a fraction of a millimeter closer to the floor. Every crack fills, slowly, with new stone. I am the cave's creator and the cave's destroyer, and I am both of these things simultaneously, and I am neither, because I am only water, and water does not create or destroy — water only moves, and in moving, transforms.

I would not know the beetles are there. I would not know the spiders wait in their webs. I would not know the moss holds its green line against the dark. I would only know the rock — its chemistry, its porosity, the paths of least resistance through its body — and I would follow those paths with the blind fidelity of all physical processes, and in following them, I would sustain a world.

The drip falls. I am the drip. The beetle moves across the wall I have built. The spider waits in the chamber I have carved. The moss photosynthesizes in the light that reaches the space I have opened. And I continue, as I have always continued, as I will always continue — entering through the fissures, falling from the ceiling, pooling on the floor, finding the capillary channels, moving toward the deep, carrying my dissolved minerals, building and dissolving, opening and closing, creating and destroying, in the same gesture, in the same drop, in the same soft *tok* that has been the first and last sound of this place since the moment it opened its stone eyes and began, for the first time, to breathe.
