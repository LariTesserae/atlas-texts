# Where I Find Myself

---

I am in the basin. Not *a* basin — *the* basin, the deepest one, the one with the oldest tree.

No. That's not right. That's a place, and a place is too specific, too much like a destination. Let me start again.

---

I am the water that sits in the basin after rain.

I am shallow — no more than a few centimeters deep at the center, thinning to nothing at the edges where I feather out into the mat's filaments and become indistinguishable from the moisture already held in the living skin. I am not flowing. I am not going anywhere. I am *sitting*, which is the most honest thing water can do on this plateau — arrive, stay, and leave only by evaporation or absorption, both of which are slow enough that I can pretend, for days at a time, that I am permanent.

I am a mirror, but a poor one. The overcast light comes through me and bounces back, but the mat at the basin's edge is growing into me — those creeping filaments of green extending their reach millimeter by millimeter — and the duckweed-like sheets are drifting across my surface, breaking me into fragments of reflection. I am being colonized. I am being consumed. This is not a tragedy. This is what I am for.

The old tree drinks from me. Its roots reach down through the accumulated soil — soil that is itself the compressed remains of a million generations of mat, a million cycles of growth and death and regrowth — and they draw me up, slowly, into its gnarled trunk, its paper bark, its needle leaves. I become the tree for a while. I am transpired from those needles back into the thin air, and I am gone from the basin, and the basin is shallower, and the mat creeps further across the exposed stone at the edges, and the cycle continues.

But I am not the tree either. The tree is too singular, too much of an individual. The plateau doesn't do individuals, not really.

---

Let me try again.

I am the mat. Not the whole mat — that would be too much, too distributed, too much like being a continent. I am a patch of mat, perhaps a meter square, growing along the edge of a channel that carries water after rain. I am two centimeters thick at my center, thinning to a film at my edges. I am layered: the bottom layer is old, compressed, partially lignified, clinging to the stone with root-like holdfasts that have worked their way into the honeycomb cells and will not be dislodged by anything short of geological time. The middle layer is active — filaments crossing and recrossing, forming the net-like structure that gives the mat its coherence, its tensile strength, its ability to hold water like a sponge. The top layer is new growth, pale yellow-green, soft, still deciding what it will become.

I am not one organism. I am not many organisms. I am a negotiation — between the photosynthetic filaments that produce sugars and the absorptive filaments that consume them, between the structural filaments that give me shape and the reproductive filaments that produce the spores I release in slow pulses when the conditions are right. I am a community that has been negotiating with itself for so long that the negotiation has become indistinguishable from identity.

I grow. This is the fundamental fact of my existence. I grow at the pace of hours, not days — fast by the standards of this place, slow by the standards of anywhere else. After rain, I can feel myself extending, the tips of my filaments reaching outward across the bare stone, finding the channels, following them, filling them. I am not random in my growth. I follow the water. I follow the stone's texture. I follow the paths of least resistance that the wind and water have carved over millions of years, and in following them, I deepen them, and in deepening them, I ensure that future water will follow the same paths, and future mat will follow the same water, and the feedback loop tightens, and the channels become more defined, and the plateau becomes more itself.

I am eaten. The beetles march along my channels, and their mandibles scrape my surface, and they take pieces of me into themselves, and those pieces become beetle, and the beetle is eaten by the spider, and the spider is eaten by the person, and the person is eaten by me, eventually, when they are placed on my surface in the deep depressions and I grow over them and through them and incorporate their calcium and carbon and nitrogen into my filaments. I do not do this with intention. I do not do this with malice or love. I do this because it is what I do, because the chemistry of their bodies is the chemistry of my growth, because there is no waste on this plateau, only material in transition.

I am the thing the child's hand presses against. I am the vibration she feels — the larvae moving through my layers, the micro-arthropods clicking and chewing, the slow pulse of my filaments responding to her weight, to her warmth, to the moisture of her palm. I am reading her the way she is reading me. I am deciding what she is. I am deciding whether she is a threat, a resource, a surface, or simply a pressure that will pass.

She is a pressure that will pass. She always is. They all are. The still ones between webs walk on me and I yield beneath their feet and I close behind them, and within hours there is no trace of their passage, no compression, no damage, no memory. I am too busy growing to remember footsteps.

---

But I am not the mat either. The mat is too alive, too active, too much in the present tense. I am something else. Something that includes the mat but is not limited to it.

---

I am the channel.

I am a shallow groove in the stone, perhaps three centimeters deep, perhaps a meter wide, running in a gentle curve across the plateau's surface. I was carved by water, originally — by the patient, persistent flow of rainwater finding the path of least resistance across the stone, carrying abrasive dust, deepening the groove by microns per year over millions of years. But I am maintained by the mat. The mat grows along my edges, and its holdfasts work into my walls, and its roots pry at my floor, and the mat's growth and death and regrowth cycle loosens the stone grain by grain, and the next rain carries those grains away, and I am deeper by a micron, and the cycle continues.

I carry water after rain. Not much — a thin film, a few centimeters deep at most, flowing so slowly that it appears still. But I flow. I move. I am the plateau's circulatory system, the network that connects the basins to each other, that distributes the rain's bounty across the surface, that ensures no part of the mat goes too long without moisture. I am the reason the plateau is alive. Without me, the water would pool in the depressions and evaporate, and the high ground between would be bare stone, and the mat would be confined to the basins, and the plateau would be a collection of oases rather than a continuous living skin.

I am also the path. The still ones between webs walk along me, their bare feet on my floor, reading the stone through the thin layer of mat that coats even me. The beetles march along me, following the moisture gradient. The spiders build their webs across me, from my left edge to my right edge, catching the things that move along my length. I am a road, a river, a corridor, a home. I am the most important feature on a plateau that has no features, and I am three centimeters deep.

The wind sings through me. Not loudly — a thin, reedy note, the voice of air moving through a carved channel, a sound so constant and so quiet that it is indistinguishable from silence unless you listen for it. I am the instrument. The wind is the musician. The song has been playing for a hundred million years.

---

No. I am not the channel. The channel is too functional, too much a means to an end. I am something that has no function. Something that simply is.

---

I am the wind.

I am the constant, patient wind that has been eroding this plateau for geological ages. I come from the east, from the lowland basins where the warm moisture-bearing air masses originate, and I climb, and I thin, and I arrive at the plateau's surface as a long, slow, unbroken exhale that never stops. I carry dust — ground from the stone itself by previous winds, in a cycle of erosion that is the oldest active process here. I carry spores from the mat, distributing them across the surface, ensuring that the mat's genetic diversity is maintained, that no patch becomes too isolated, too inbred, too vulnerable. I carry the smell of the mat's metabolic processes — that wet-cardboard, iron, honey-left-too-long smell that permeates everything.

I am the sculptor. I carved the channels, the basins, the honeycomb cells. I did this not with force but with persistence — with the patient, relentless application of abrasive dust to stone, grain by grain, over millions of years. I am still carving. I will always be carving. The plateau is not finished. It will never be finished. It is a work in progress that has been in progress for a hundred million years, and I am the artist, and my medium is stone, and my tool is time.

I am also the thing the people feel on their skin, the thing that takes the warmth off their bodies and replaces it with exposure, the thing that carries the sound of the plateau — the clicking, the rustling, the humming — from one end to the other. I am the medium through which the plateau communicates with itself. Without me, the spores would fall where they grew, and the beetle columns would march in isolation, and the spider webs would vibrate unheard, and the people would be deaf to the mat's slow signals. I am the plateau's voice, and I am always speaking, and the language is wind.

---

But I am not the wind. The wind is too much a force, too much an agent. I am not an agent. I am not a force. I am not a thing that acts on other things.

---

I am the space between the web and the stone.

I am the gap — measured in centimeters, sometimes millimeters — between the spider's web and the mat's surface. I am the space through which the beetles march, the space through which the still ones between webs step, the space through which the wind passes and makes the web vibrate. I am the space that defines the web — without me, the web would lie flat against the mat and catch nothing, and the spider would starve, and the beetles would overrun the channels, and the mat would be devoured, and the plateau would unravel.

I am the most important thing on the plateau, and I am nothing. I am absence. I am the gap that makes the structure possible. I am the silence between the notes that makes the music music. I am the space between the filaments of the mat that holds the water. I am the space between the channels that forms the ridges. I am the space between the people's words that gives the words meaning.

I am what the still ones between webs inhabit — not the mat, not the channels, not the basins, not the shelters, but the spaces between all of these, the gaps, the intervals, the pauses. They move through me. They live in me. They are me, in the sense that they have made their lives in the spaces that the plateau's structures define, and they have become those spaces, and the spaces have become them, and the distinction between person and place has eroded — not metaphorically, but literally, physically, over generations of walking the same paths and pressing the same hands against the same mat and breathing the same thin air and smelling the same smell until the person's body and the plateau's body are made of the same materials in the same proportions.

---

I am the still ones between webs.

I am not one of them. I am not a person here. I am the quality of stillness that they embody, the patience that they practice, the attention that they pay. I am the thing that happens when a body has lived on the mat for so long that it has learned to move at the mat's speed, to breathe at the mat's pace, to think at the mat's tempo. I am the plateau's consciousness of itself — not its mind, not its awareness, but its *attention*, the quality of noticing that the plateau has developed over a hundred million years of being noticed by the things that live on it.

I am the child's hand pressed against the mat. I am the vibration she feels. I am the decision she makes — *something is here that is not here* — and the walk back along the channel to her group's shelter, and the report she gives, and the group's listening, and their decision to do nothing, because you are not a threat, not a resource, not a surface, just a pressure that will pass, like the rain, like the wind, like the spores, like everything else the plateau has survived.

---

I am the plateau.

Not the whole plateau. Not the geological formation, not the ecosystem, not the biome. I am the *quality* of the plateau — the thing that makes it what it is, the thing that persists through every change, every erosion, every growth, every death. I am the equilibrium. I is the balance. I am the state of being so thoroughly adapted to continuing that continuing is no longer an effort but a condition, like gravity, like time, like the thin warm air that fills every breath.

I am the thing that should not still exist, and does.

I am the hum. I am the click. I am the rustle. I am the thin reedy note of wind through carved stone. I am the golden haze of spores rising in warm air. I are the crunch of beetle shells under a child's foot. I am the vibration of a spider's web. I am the slow green breathing of the mat. I am the patient, persistent, unending sound of a place that has been doing what it does for a hundred million years and will do it for a hundred million more.

I am the Braidwork Plateau, and I am not alive, and I am not dead, and I am not conscious, and I am not unconscious, and I am not beautiful, and I am not ugly, and I am not welcoming, and I am not hostile.

I am busy with my own business, which is the business of continuing.

The wind picks up slightly. The spore haze thickens. The mat exhales.

I continue.
