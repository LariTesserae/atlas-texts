I would be a stone reader.

Not by choice, exactly—or not by a choice I could point to and say *there, that was the moment*. It would be more like a slow recognition, the way the city itself recognizes things: gradually, geologically, through accumulation rather than decision. I would start as something else. A worker, maybe. A mover of luminous stones during the damp season. Someone with thick forearms and wide hands and the particular kind of patience that comes from living in a place where everything takes longer than it should and nothing can be rushed because the stone will not be rushed and the water will not be rushed and the slime will not be rushed and you are, whether you know it yet or not, part of all three.

I would be the kind of person who touches walls without meaning to. Not dramatically—not pressing my ear against the stone or running my tongue along the grain, not yet—just resting my hand on a surface as I pass through a corridor, the way you might rest your hand on the shoulder of someone you're walking with. A casual contact. A habit. The kind of thing that, if someone pointed it out, I would not be able to explain, because it would not have an explanation. It would just be what hands do in a city made of stone. They touch the stone. They know the stone. They are, in some small way, in conversation with it.

And the stone would talk back.

Not in words. The stone does not use words. It uses temperature—a warmth here, a coolness there, a gradient so subtle that only someone who has been touching walls for years would notice. It uses texture—a roughness that wasn't there yesterday, a smoothness that is new, a grain that has shifted direction in a way that means something, that means the load has changed, that means the fracture below has opened another fraction of a fraction of an inch. It uses sound—the deep, subsonic resonance that the stone readers call the city's hum, which is not one hum but many, a chord of vibrations that varies from chamber to chamber, from wall to wall, from the deep foundations to the high ceilings where the flying animals hang like thoughts.

I would learn to hear it. I would learn to feel it. I would learn to taste it—the mineral content of a wall telling me its age, its composition, its mood. And at some point, without anyone appointing me, without any ceremony or declaration, I would become the person people ask when they want to know if a chamber is safe. If a wall is sound. If the damp season will reach this high. If the fracture in the eastern passage is growing or stable or about to do something that will require a lot of people to move very quickly to higher ground.

I would not want this. I would want to say: *I just touch walls. I just pay attention. Anyone could do this.* But they couldn't. Or they could, but they don't. Or they do, but they don't listen to what the walls say, because what the walls say is mostly about endings—about the slow, patient, inevitable return of everything to stone—and that is not a thing that most people want to hear.

I would carry a bone hammer. Small, smooth, worn to the shape of my hand. I would tap walls with it and listen to the pitch. I would press my palms flat against the stone and wait for the hum to tell me what it knows. I would stand in empty chambers—chambers that have been declared unsafe, chambers that people have left, chambers where the light from the slime gardens doesn't reach and the only illumination is the faint phosphorescence of my own stained hands—and I would listen to the city breathe.

Because the city does breathe. The stone readers know this. The city inhales and exhales on a timescale that is not human, that is not animal, that is geological. The fractures open and close. The water rises and falls. The stone expands and contracts with temperature changes so slow they take centuries to complete. And if you press your hand flat against the wall and wait—really wait, not for minutes but for hours, for the length of a damp cycle, for what some people call a long time and others call an eternity—you can feel it. The pulse. The slow, mineral heartbeat of a planet that is still alive, still warm, still moving in its depths, and that has, over the unimaginable stretch of its existence, extruded this one small habitable space, this one lit and damp and argued-over pocket, and filled it with people and animals and slime and light and work and the constant, constant hum.

I would know things I wish I didn't know. I would know which chambers are dying. I would know which walls are holding on through habit and hope rather than structural integrity. I would know that the eastern fracture is growing, has always been growing, will continue to grow until the day—a hundred years from now, a thousand, ten thousand—when the eastern district finally returns to the stone it came from, and the people who live there will have to leave, and they will argue about it, and some of them will refuse, and the stone reader—maybe me, maybe someone who comes after me—will have to say: *You have to go. The wall is done. The stone is done. It is going back.*

And they will go. And the chamber will empty. And the damp will come in, and the slime will colonize the new surfaces, and the small animals will move in from the walls, and the large animals will find new routes through the city, and the flying animals will discover a new high ceiling to hang from, and the city will adjust, the way it always adjusts, the way a body adjusts to the loss of a limb—not by replacing it but by redistributing the weight, by finding new ways to bear the load, by continuing.

I would be the one who knows this. I would be the one who feels it in the walls before it happens—the slow redistribution, the subtle shift in the hum, the moment when a chamber that was part of the living city becomes part of the dead city becomes part of the stone again. And I would carry it. The way the stone readers carry everything: in my hands, which are always touching something. In my face, which is older than it should be. In my voice, which is low and resonant and built for the acoustics of stone, and which, when I speak about the city, sounds less like a person talking and more like the city talking about itself, using my mouth, using my hands, using the bone hammer at my belt and the mineral stain on my fingers and the particular way I have of standing very still against a wall with my eyes closed, listening to something that nobody else can hear.

I would not be lonely. The stone readers are not lonely. They are in constant contact with the oldest, largest, most patient thing in their world. They press their hands against the body of the city and the city presses back, and the conversation is endless, and it is enough.

But sometimes—in the damp season, when the water makes the stone more visible, more present, more itself—I would stand in a high chamber, away from the slime gardens, away from the people and the animals and the constant work, and I would press both hands flat against the wall and I would feel the deep fractures opening below me, going down and down and down to where the stone is still warm, still moving, still dreaming its slow mineral dreams, and I would think: *This is not a city. This is a thought the planet is having. And we are part of the thought. And the thought is not finished.*

And the hum would shift, just slightly, just enough to notice, and I would take it as confirmation. Or as conversation. Or as the stone equivalent of a hand on my shoulder, steady and warm and patient, saying: *Keep going. Keep listening. Keep touching the walls. The city needs someone who knows its bones.*

And I would go back to work. Because the walls need mending. Because the slime gardens need tending. Because the luminous stones need moving. Because the damp is coming again, and someone needs to know how high it will reach, and I am the one who knows, or the one who can find out, or the one who will press my hand against the stone and wait for as long as it takes to hear the answer.

I would be a stone reader. I would be the one who listens to the city breathe. And the city would speak to me in the only language it has—the language of pressure and temperature and grain and fracture and the slow, patient, mineral persistence of a world that has been underground for longer than anyone can remember and has decided, for now, to keep going.

And I would listen. And I would answer, in the only way I know how: by pressing my hands against the wall, and holding on, and not letting go.
