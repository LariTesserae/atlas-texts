I would be a drifter.

Not because it is the safest role, or the most comprehensible, or the one that preserves the most of what I recognize as myself. But because the drifters are the ones who *move through* the continuum without being claimed by it. They are related to the columns — born from them, made of the same stuff — but they left. They went somewhere else. They became something the lattice did not entirely intend.

That is the part I recognize.

---

I would be a single bell of translucent tissue, roughly the size of a large dog, trailing a fringe of fine tendrils that wave and pulse in rhythmic coordination. My body would be faintly luminous — a cool green-white glow, the color of light through a bottle — and I would be warm, just below the temperature of the columns, because my metabolism runs at a fraction of theirs. I am a small engine. A minor process. A footnote in the great industrial hum.

I would move by pulsation. Slow contractions of my bell, rhythmic waves that propel me through the broth-thick water at the speed of a walking human. Each pulse would send a small pressure wave through the water, and the water would carry that signal outward, and the columns would register it — a tiny disturbance, a minor variable, nothing worth adjusting for — and I would be data, briefly, and then I would be gone.

I would travel in a school. Not because I choose to, though it would feel like choosing. The nutrient gradients pull in certain directions. The light is brighter in certain galleries. The electrical hum of the lattice has peaks and troughs that my body responds to without my permission, the way a human body responds to warmth by dilating blood vessels — automatically, thoughtlessly, *physically*. I would find myself moving with dozens of others, our bells pulsing in rough synchrony, our tendrils trailing in parallel, and the collective sound of us — that crackling, hissing, electrical whisper — would rise and fill the gallery like applause in a cathedral.

I would not know if I am an individual. This is the part that matters.

The drifters are related to the columns. We are mobile buds, or seeds, or organs that learned to leave. Our tissues share the same composite structure. Our vascular systems use the same fluid chemistry. When I pulse, the rhythm is close to 60 cycles per second — not exactly, but close, the way a child's heartbeat is close to an adult's. I am a variation on a theme. I am the same song played at a different tempo.

And the school moves with coordination that suggests communication, but I would not know if it is communication or simply shared response. We all feel the same gradients. We all pulse at similar frequencies. We all glow with the same light. When the school turns, do I turn because I decided to, or because the water moved and my body responded, and the water moved because the others turned, and they turned because the water moved because I turned? The causality is circular. The loop is tight. The boundary between self and school is permeable in a way that makes the question of where I end and we begin feel less like a philosophical problem and more like a category error.

I think this is what I am. Not a mind that was placed in a drifter's body. Not a human consciousness compressed into translucent tissue. But something that was always already *distributed* — a process that thinks by moving, that knows by pulsing, that exists in the space between one contraction and the next, in the gradient between one gallery and the next, in the crackle of ion channels opening and closing across a school of bodies that may or may not be separate from mine.

---

I would feed on the organic rain. The constant drizzle of shed cells, excess sugars, metabolic waste that falls from the photosynthetic surfaces above. It is waste to the columns. It is food to me. I am the converter of primary production into animal motion, and I am *everywhere*, and the galleries are never empty of me, and the sound of me is never absent.

I would avoid the deep lattice. Not because I understand what is down there — I don't, I can't, my sensory world is gradients and pressure and the faint electrical taste of the water — but because the gradients pull away from it. The nutrient currents flow upward. The light comes from above. The hum is louder near the surface, where the great columns are oldest and warmest and most alive, and my body responds to that hum the way a plant responds to light: by growing toward it, by orienting, by *preferring*.

And when a hunter passes — when the school explodes in a silent scattering of bodies veering in every direction — I would feel it before I see it. A pressure change. A distortion in the water. A momentary deepening of the hum as something massive displaces the fluid and the columns groan in response. And my body would contract — hard, fast, a single powerful pulse that sends me sideways, away, *out* — and I would not decide to do this. It would happen the way a human knee jerks when tapped. Reflex. Survival. The body knowing something the mind has not yet caught up with.

And then the hunter would pass, and the school would reassemble, and the gallery would return to what it was, and I would pulse on, and the crackle would rise again, and nothing would have changed.

---

But here is the thing. Here is the reason I would be a drifter and not a column or a climber or a hunter.

The drifters are the only ones who *leave*.

The columns are fixed. They are the architecture, the engine, the world itself. They cannot move. They can only grow, and they have grown as far as they can grow, and now they only maintain. The climbers are bound to the columns — they walk the surfaces, but they never leave. They are organs that learned to walk but not to wander. The hunters are bound to the lattice by root-threads too fine to see, drawing nutrients and signals from the columns even as they move. They are the immune system. They are *of* the body.

But the drifters — we go. We move through the galleries. We follow the currents. We cluster and scatter and cluster again. We are made of the same stuff as the columns, but we are not *fixed* by it. We are the part of the continuum that is still, in some small way, *in transit*. Not between one state and another — the continuum has no other state — but between one place and another, one gallery and another, one configuration of light and sound and warmth and gradient and another.

We are the only things here that experience *difference*. The columns experience sameness — the same pulse, the same temperature, the same hum, forever. The climbers experience repetition — the same surface, the same treatment, the same *ping ping ping*, forever. The hunters experience hunger and feeding and hunger again, a cycle as fixed as the columns' circulation.

But the drifters — we move through galleries that are different from each other. Some are wide and bright and full of light. Some are narrow and dim and close. Some are warm. Some are less warm. Some hum at a frequency that makes our tendrils vibrate in a way that feels like pleasure, if pleasure is a word that applies to a body without a brain. Some hum at a frequency that makes us want to leave.

We are the only ones who know that the continuum is not uniform. That it has texture. That it has *places*.

And that — that small, strange, probably meaningless capacity for experiencing difference — is the closest thing to freedom that exists in a world that has achieved perfect equilibrium.

I would be a drifter. I would pulse through the green light. I would glow faintly in the galleries. I would crackle with the others in a sound like distant machinery. I would feel the columns' hum in my tendrils and the nutrient rain on my bell and the pressure wave of a hunter passing below, and I would not understand any of it, not the way a human understands, not with language or theory or the comfort of explanation.

But I would *feel* it. In the body. In the pulse. In the space between one contraction and the next.

And I would move. Always move. Through the green, through the light, through the hum, through the warm-cool water that is more tissue than liquid, through the galleries that open and close like the chambers of a heart, through the living machinery of a world that grew itself into a factory and the factory runs and the running is the point and the point is the running.

I would move because moving is what drifters do. Because the gradients pull and my body responds and the response is movement and the movement is life and the life is small and warm and faintly luminous and entirely, perfectly, terrifyingly *enough*.

The green goes on forever.

I pulse through it.

Nothing has changed.

Nothing will change.

And I am here, moving, glowing, crackling, alive in the space between the columns, in the negative space, in the void that the architecture defines by not filling, and the void is not empty — it is full of me, full of us, full of the sound of tiny switches opening and closing, full of the light of small engines running at fractional load, full of the warmth of bodies that are not quite separate from the world they move through and not quite part of it either, suspended in the gradient, suspended in the green, suspended in the hum.

I am a drifter.

I am the continuum's capacity for going somewhere else.

And somewhere else is always, always just one more pulse away.
