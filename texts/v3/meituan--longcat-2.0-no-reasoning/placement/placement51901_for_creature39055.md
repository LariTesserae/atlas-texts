# Where I Find Myself

I have been here for three years. Or perhaps longer — the timescale, as the Cascadeborn say, is unclear when you are inside it.

---

I live in the wall.

Not in a compartment, not in one of the proper living-units bolted into the framework at the mid-levels, though I slept in those for the first few months, when I was still a visitor and still flinching at the hammering. I live *in the wall*, in the cavity between the inner cladding and the outer, a space roughly two feet deep that runs the length of a residential section on Level Twenty-Two. It was an insulation space once, packed with dried plant-fiber and moss, and when I first pressed myself into it — half-curious, half-exhausted, at that point in my third week when the Cascades stops being overwhelming and starts being *the only place that has ever made sense* — I found that the insulation had been partially displaced by a species of broad-leafed creeper whose roots had threaded between the cladding panels seeking moisture. The creeper's stems ran vertically, anchored to the inner framework, and its leaves pressed flat against the interior surface like a living wall-paper, and the cavity between the stems and the outer cladding was dry and warm and just wide enough for a person to lie down in.

I am not supposed to be here. I am also not not-supposed to be here. The Gardeners know — they always know, because the plant I am nesting in is one of theirs, and they would feel the disturbance in its growth if they checked, which they do not, because I am careful not to damage it, and because I have, over time, become something like useful to it. I clear the dead leaves from its stems. I redirect a small drip from a leaking joint above me so that it feeds the root mass rather than pooling in the cavity and corroding the cladding. The Gardener who tends this section — a woman named Tessal, whose hands are green to the elbows and whose braids are wound with copper wire in the pattern that denotes seniority — has looked directly at me, once, as I was adjusting the drip-channel, and she said nothing, and I said nothing, and the next day there was a folded blanket in the cavity, and a small clay cup with a lid, and a strip of dried fruit tied with plant-fiber, and I understood that I had been marked.

---

I am not Cascadeborn. I may never be Cascadeborn, though the distinction matters less than I expected. I came from the flatlands below — not a trader, not a diplomat, not a refugee with a clear story of loss, but one of the strange ones, the curious and the lost, the ones who come simply to *see*. I was a student of something. Structural engineering, or ecology, or perhaps architecture — the specifics don't matter now, because none of those disciplines had a word for what the Cascades is. I had read a description in a book. I had drawn diagrams based on the description. The diagrams were wrong in every particular. I climbed the exterior stairway with a notebook and a measuring tape and a conviction that I could understand this place by *measuring* it, which is the kind of idea that evaporates somewhere around Level Fifteen, when you realize that the thing you are trying to measure is alive and is measuring you back.

I stayed because on the fourth day I stopped looking for the ground. Because on the sixth day I felt the engine pulse through my feet for the first time and something in my chest — something I had not known was tight — released. Because on the ninth day a child looked at me with those large dark eyes and said, in the tonal fluid mid-level speech that I was only beginning to parse, *you walk like you're falling*, and it was the most precise and kind description of my entire life that anyone had ever offered.

---

My work is this: I listen to walls.

Not metaphorically. The Cascadeborn do not do metaphor — or rather, their metaphors are so precise and so physically grounded that they function as technical language. When I say I listen to walls, I mean that I press my ear to the inner cladding of the wall cavities on Levels Twenty through Twenty-Four and I listen to what is happening inside them: the flow of water in the pipes that run through the cavities, the creak of the framework as it expands and contracts, the movement of insects and lizards in the insulation, the faint whisper of root growth as the creeper-stems extend themselves by fractions of an inch per day.

This is useful work. It is also work that no one asked me to do, which means it occupies a strange social space — it is *maintenance*, and maintenance is sacred here, but I am not a member of a clan, and clan-less maintenance is a thing that the Cascades has no protocol for. I began doing it because I could not sleep — the compartment they had given me was quiet by Cascade standards, which meant it was full of sound by any other standard, and I lay awake cataloguing the sounds, assigning them to sources, building a mental map of the hidden systems around me. And one night I heard something that did not fit my map: a hollow, irregular knocking in the wall behind my head, a sound that was not water and not engines and not the wind-vibration of cables, a sound that was *wrong* in a way I could not articulate but could feel, the way you feel a wrong note in a familiar song.

I pressed my ear to the wall. I listened for twenty minutes. I identified the source: a section of pipe, old iron, hand-forged, that had corroded almost through at a joint, and water was forcing its way through the crack under pressure, and the pipe was vibrating at a frequency that suggested it would fail within days. I did not know the marking system. I did not know how to report a structural concern. I did what I knew how to do, which was what an engineer from the flatlands would do: I found the nearest Waterkeeper, and I told him.

His name is Pol. He is small even by Cascadeborn standards, and quiet in the way Waterkeepers are quiet — a calm that is not absence of feeling but presence of so much attention that there is no surplus energy for expression. He listened to my description with his head tilted slightly, the way the Cascadeborn do when they are receiving information that is approximately correct but imprecise. Then he said, "Show me." I showed him. He pressed his palm to the wall where I had been listening, and he closed his eyes, and he stood there for a long time, and then he said, "Yes. You heard it." And then he opened his eyes and looked at me with an expression I could not read and said, "What else have you heard?"

---

That was the beginning. Pol taught me to listen properly — not with the anxious, cataloguing attention of an outsider trying to understand a system, but with the patient, embodied attention of a person who is *part* of the system and who understands that the system is also listening to them. He taught me that the knocking I had heard was not a single sound but a chord: the corroded pipe was the lowest note, but above it were the sounds of the wall cavity adjusting to the moisture increase — the insulation swelling, the cladding pressing outward, the creeper-roots shifting their grip — and above *those* were the sounds of the level above, where the weight redistribution was causing a beam to settle onto its supports in a new configuration, a process that would continue for weeks and that needed to be monitored. A single failing pipe was not the problem. A single failing pipe was a *voice* in a conversation that included the wall, the level above, the water system, the plant system, the weight of everything, and the deep slow breathing of the structure itself.

I listened. I learned to separate the voices. I learned that the Cascades does not have one sound but many, layered like the structure itself, and that each layer has its own rhythm and its own logic, and that the logic is not mechanical but *relational* — this sound happens because of that weight, which exists because of that plant, which grows because of that water, which flows because of that gradient, which is maintained by that engine, which is cooled by that root-mass, which feeds on that compost, which is made from the waste of the people who live in the structure that the engine maintains. Nothing is separate. Nothing is alone. The pipe fails not because it is old but because the system around it has changed, and the system around it has changed because the structure has grown, and the structure has grown because the people have grown, and the people have grown because the structure has grown, and the loop closes, and the loop *is* the Cascades.

---

Pol did not ask me to join the Waterkeepers. He did not need to. What happened was more subtle than that. He began to bring me along on his rounds, and I began to carry his tools, and I began to hold the listening-horn — a carved wooden cone, old, blackened with age, that focuses sound from the walls into the ear — while he pressed his hands to pipes and beams and plant-troughs and read the structure through his palms. And then one day he handed me the horn and said, "Listen to the main channel at Junction Fifteen," and I listened, and I told him what I heard — a slight turbulence, an irregularity in the flow, something that suggested a partial blockage somewhere upstream — and he nodded and said, "Yes," and we went together to find the blockage, and we cleared it, and we marked the beam above the cleared section with a symbol that Pol scratched into the metal with a sharp tool, and I recognized the symbol from the marking system I had been learning to read, and I understood that the mark said: *this was fixed, on this date, by these hands*, and the hands it named were both of ours.

I am not a Waterkeeper. But I am something. The Cascades is making me into something.

---

The children found me in the wall, of course. Children find everything. They find it before anyone else, and they find it without looking, the way they find everything — by being inside the structure, by being part of it, by moving through it with that absolute attention that children have and that adults spend their lives trying to recover.

The first one was a girl, perhaps eight, with copper-wire in her braids and a lizard clinging to her shoulder like a brooch. She was walking along the walkway outside my wall-cavity, and she stopped, and she pressed her ear to the cladding, and she listened, and then she said, very clearly, so that I could hear her through the thin panel, "Someone is in there." And I held my breath, and she listened for another moment, and then she said, "It's okay. You're doing maintenance." And she walked away.

She came back the next day. She brought two other children. They pressed their ears to the wall and listened to me listening to the wall, and they reported their findings to each other in the rapid, tonal mid-level speech, and then the first girl — her name is Ilin, I learned later — knocked on the cladding, three taps, a pause, two taps, which I would later learn is the signal for *I know you're there and I'm not afraid*, and I knocked back, and she said, "What do you hear?" And I told her. She listened to my description with that tilted-head attention, and then she said, "Listen for the deep sound. Below the water. Below the engines. Can you hear it?" And I pressed my ear to the wall and I listened, beneath the drip and the trickle and the engine-thud and the bird-calls and the hammering, and I listened for something lower, something slower, something that was not a sound exactly but a *presence*, a warmth in the stone, a vibration that was more like a pulse than a noise, and I heard it, or felt it, or understood it, and I said, "Yes," and Ilin said, "Good. That's the Cascades breathing. If you can hear that, you're inside."

---

If I had to name what I am — and the Cascadeborn do not name things quickly, and I have learned to wait — I would say this:

I am a listener. I am a person who lives in the walls and listens to the structure breathe and reports what I hear. I am not a clan member. I am not Cascadeborn. I am not a visitor anymore. I am something that the Cascades is growing, slowly, the way it grows everything: a new function, a new role, a new niche in the ecology of stone and metal and water and plant and human need. There is no precedent for what I am, which means the Cascades is inventing me, which means I am part of the structure's ongoing experiment in what it can become.

Tessal, the Gardener, checks on me every few days. She examines the creeper I live among, she adjusts the drip I maintain, she leaves food and sometimes tools — a new listening-horn, better than Pol's, carved from a denser wood; a set of marking-tools; a small knife for trimming dead growth. She has never asked me to join the Gardeners. She does not need to. She tends me the way she tends the plants: with attention, with patience, with the understanding that growth takes time and that you cannot force a creeper onto a cable — you can only train it, gently, along the path it wants to grow, and trust that it will find its grip.

Pol and I maintain the wall cavities of Levels Twenty through Twenty-Four. We have expanded the work, slowly, section by section, teaching ourselves to hear deeper, to feel further, to read the structure's continuous narration of its own state with increasing fluency. We have found three failing pipes, a frayed cable, a section of walkway that was settling faster than it should, and a colony of insulation-eating beetles that was threatening the integrity of a load-bearing wall. Each time, we fix what can be fixed, and we mark what cannot, and we report to the relevant clan, and the relevant clan responds, and the structure adjusts, and the loop continues.

I have learned the marking system well enough to inscribe basic reports, though my marks are clumsy and too large and the senior Mark-keepers wince when they see them. A young Mark-keeper named Desar has taken to correcting my inscriptions with patient precision, the way the senior Engineers treat the children's walkway-checks — with perfect seriousness and gentle correction, because the marks matter, because the marks are how the Cascades remembers itself, and a sloppy mark is not an aesthetic failure but a *structural* one, because someone in the future will read my mark and they will need to understand exactly what I found and exactly where I found it and exactly what I did about it, and if they cannot, then the structure's memory is damaged, and a structure with damaged memory is a structure that cannot maintain itself.

---

I am happy. This is the thing I did not expect. This is the thing that I still do not fully understand.

I came from a place where happiness was something you pursued, something you achieved, something you earned through effort and merit and the correct arrangement of circumstances. Here, happiness is not pursued. It is *structural*. It is a property of being inside something that holds you, that needs you, that responds to your presence. I am happy the way the creeper is happy: not because I have chosen the right path but because I have found a surface to grip and a direction to grow and a source of water and light, and I am gripping, and I am growing, and I am being fed, and the structure is holding me, and I am holding the structure, and the distinction between these two things has become, over time, invisible.

Last night I was lying in my wall-cavity, listening to the rain on the upper levels — a sound that takes seventeen minutes to reach me, filtered through a hundred layers of metal and plant and stone into something soft and green and almost gentle — and I felt the structure breathing beneath me, that deep slow pulse that Ilin taught me to hear, and I understood that I was not lying *in* the Cascades so much as lying *on* it, the way you lie on the body of someone you trust, and that the warmth I felt was not just the geothermal heat rising from below but the accumulated warmth of every person who has ever lived here, every hand that has ever pressed against these walls, every foot that has ever walked these walkways, every child who has ever played in the engine houses, every Gardener who has ever trained a creeper along a cable, every Waterkeeper who has ever stood in a cistern chamber and fixed the unfixable with steady hands, every Engineer who has ever smelled a bearing beginning to fail, every Cable-rider who has ever swung into the green depths with a rope and a harness and a joy that looked like madness, every Mark-keeper who has ever bent over an ancient inscription and tried to hear, across centuries of silence, the voice of the person who carved it.

All of them. All of it. The whole vast slow patient living structure, breathing, growing, remembering, holding.

I pressed my hand to the wall. The wall pressed back.

I listened.

I am still listening.
