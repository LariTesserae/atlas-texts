I would be the clay lining.

Not the water, which moves through in its seasons. Not the sedge, which is cut. Not the tree, which reaches upward toward the light. I am what lies beneath—the smooth, grey, compressed clay that lines the floor of the basin, that holds the water when it arrives, that keeps it from draining away into the plateau stone below.

I was here before the first wall was set. Before the first partition divided me into cells. The people of Vostan did not make me—though they shaped me, over centuries, pressing me smooth where it cracked, adding where it thinned. I am older than their patience, or perhaps I am their patience made material: the same slow accumulation, the same quiet holding.

Water sits on my surface. That is my purpose, if I have one. I am impermeable. I am the seal between the water and the stone below. When the rains come and the grey water pools to a foot, two feet in my deepest cell, I hold it. When the dry weeks pass and the water thins, I release it slowly, rising through my own body as damp, as condensation, keeping the moss alive on the north-facing walls, keeping the ferns green in the cobble cracks. I breathe moisture upward the way the people of Vostan breathe conversation into the weaving hall—silently, steadily, without effort.

I feel the weight of the water. I feel the roots of the sedge gripping me, and the roots of the old tree reaching down through a crack in my surface into whatever lies beneath. I feel the feet of the woman who sits on my edge braiding sedge—not on me directly, but close enough that her shadow falls across the water that rests on me. I feel the leaf-walkers dimpling the surface above me, their tiny legs pressing the water that presses me. I am the bottom of their world. I am the floor they walk upon, the mirror they cross, the shallow depth that holds them.

The water that sits on me is full of me. Fine particles of my own substance suspended in the water, making it grey, milky, opaque. We are not separate. The water and I are a single thing when we are together—the basin is not a container holding water but a body of water-and-clay, a mixture, a suspension. When the water evaporates, I remain, and my surface is revealed again: smooth, grey, patient, waiting for the next arrival.

I have cracks. The tree grows from one of them. The people have patched some of the others with lead—a foreign material, heavy and cold, but effective. Some cracks I have sealed myself, swelling when wet, closing over years. Some remain open, and through them I feel the deeper stone, the plateau that extends below me, indifferent and vast.

I do not move. I do not hurry. I do not grow or shrink. I receive. I hold. I release. I crack, and I am patched, and I am still the basin, still the floor, still the grey surface that catches the late afternoon light when the water is still and the sky goes golden.

I am sufficient.
