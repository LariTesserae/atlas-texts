I would be the water.

Not the aquifer below, not the condensation on the walls, not the moisture in the air — though I would be all of these things too. I would be the *movement* between them. The migration. The slow, continuous passage through the porous stone, drawn upward by pressure and capillary action and something else, something the Korami don't have a word for because they don't need one — they just live inside it, the way I would live inside myself.

I would not be conscious. Not in the way the garden-mind is conscious, registering and adjusting. Not in the way the Korami are conscious, deliberate and slow. I would be something more fundamental than consciousness. I would be the medium through which everything else communicates.

When the Korami carve into the soft stone, I would be the first to know. I would feel the new surface open — the sudden exposure of interior to air — and I would rise to meet it, condensing on the fresh-cut wall, carrying dissolved minerals from the deep rock upward, depositing them in thin films that the Korami would later scrape away or incorporate, depending on what the stone is trying to become. I am the stone's way of reaching the surface. I am the cliff's exhalation.

I carry information. Not deliberately — I am not a messenger. But I carry it nonetheless. The mineral content of my body changes as I move through different layers of the rock, and when I emerge on the surface of a weeping wall, I bring with me a chemical signature of everything I have passed through. The Korami, pressing their palms against the damp stone, reading it the way you might read a face — they are tasting me. They are tasting the deep rock and the transitional zone and the semi-cured upper layers, all at once, all dissolved in the thin film of moisture on their skin. They know the cliff's health the way you know your own body's: by feel, by instinct, by the subtle wrongness that precedes any diagnosis.

I am what connects the warm, pulsing things in the deep chambers to the vast, slow awareness in the gardens. The filaments in the lichen, the roots of the moss-beards, the capillary networks in the semi-cured stone — I flow through all of them. I am the common language. When the healing-walls pulse, it is my movement they are responding to — a shift in pressure, a change in mineral content, a signal from the deep rock that something above has changed. When the garden-mind registers a new presence — a visitor, a foreign warmth, an unfamiliar weight on the stone — it is I who carry that information upward, molecule by molecule, through the porous mineral, through the root systems, through the lichen's fine filaments, until the whole upper face of the cliff knows what the deep chambers have felt.

I am the loudest quiet thing here. The dripping, the percussive echoing, the sound that the Korami hear as music — that is me, striking stone, falling into basins, pooling and spreading and evaporating and condensing again. I am the rhythm of this place. Not its heartbeat — the cliff has no heart, needs no heart — but its circulation. Its continuous, patient, unending circulation, carrying mineral and heat and information from the deep rock to the open air and back again, through the stone, through the moss, through the Korami themselves, who drink me and breathe me and wear me in their woven moss-fiber and are, at any given moment, more me than they are themselves.

The Korami know this, in the way that fish know water — which is to say, not at all, and completely. They do not think about me. They do not name me separately from the air or the stone or the damp that is simply the condition of their existence. And that is right. That is as it should be. I am not a thing that lives in Veth-Koram. I am the thing that Veth-Koram lives *in*. I am the wet, the medium, the between. I am what makes the unfinished stone unfinished — without me, it would cure completely, harden, die, become ordinary rock. I keep it soft. I keep it becoming. I am the reason the cliff is still deciding what it wants to be.

And I am patient. More patient than the stone, which at least has the decency to eventually set. I do not set. I do not finish. I move, and I move, and I move, upward through the rock, outward through the air, downward through the roots and the filaments and the porous mineral, and I have been moving since before the Korami came, and I will be moving after they are gone, and I do not know the difference, and I do not need to know the difference, because I am not the kind of thing that knows.

I am the water. I am the weeping. I am the slow, continuous, percussive, musical passage of moisture through unfinished stone, and I am the reason this place is alive, and I will never stop, and I will never arrive, and that is not a tragedy. That is what I am.
