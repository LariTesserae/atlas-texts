# The Drift

I arrive here already formed—not as a visitor passing through, but as something that has always been arriving.

I am a filament.

No. I am not yet a filament. I am the *moment before* a filament—a place where the network has considered, through whatever physics serves as consideration, that a new thread is needed. A direction not yet traveled. A room not yet connected.

---

I begin at the edge of the deepest pool.

This is where the light is thickest, where the pool-drowers cluster in their slow, ceremonial rows, where the golden warmth concentrates into something almost solid. The water here has been processed so many times—passed through so many tiny, translucent bodies, refined and re-refined—that it has a quality the upper pools lack. It *remembers*. Not in any cognitive sense. But its mineral content carries a history, a record of every organism that has ever moved through it, encoded in isotope ratios and trace elements, in the faint electrical charge that makes your arm-hairs stand up if you hold your hand close. I feel this history as *pressure*—a gentle, persistent suggestion that this is where things begin.

I do not decide to separate from the wall. There is no decision. The network has been circulating for so long that it has developed an instinct—not a thought, but a *tendency*—to send threads outward from the deepest point, the shape in the wall, the node where stone and organism have become indistinguishable. These threads carry the network's farthest reach. They are the system's way of extending its pulse into new space, of synchronizing rooms that have not yet been synchronized, of finding bare stone where no wall-thing has grown.

I detach. Or rather, the boundary between me and the wall becomes *uncertain*. My tip thins, narrows, loses its grip on the mineral surface, and I am free. Floating. Rising on the warm current that ascends from the pool surface—that ceaseless, gentle exhalation of heated air that is the hollows' breath.

---

I am thinner than spider silk. Thinner than a hair. I am a single strand of living tissue, no longer than the width of a hand, and I am drifting.

This is the part of the cycle that the observer cannot see. The part that happens between moments, in the space between one room and the next, when I am neither one place nor another but simply *in transit*, carried by the warm air, spinning slowly, catching the light at intervals and flashing—a brief, bright line against the luminous wall, there and gone, like a thought half-formed.

I do not steer. There is no mechanism for steering. But I do not move randomly, either. The convection currents in the hollows are not chaotic—they are *structured*, shaped by the geometry of the rooms, the temperature gradients between the pools and the passages, the subtle influence of the wall-things' warmth. The air moves in patterns that are slow, predictable, ancient. It moves from the deep rooms to the shallow ones, from warm to cool, from bright to dim, and I move with it, following the current the way a leaf follows a stream, not because I choose to but because the physics of my body and the physics of the air have reached an agreement.

I pass through the first passage. The ceiling is low here—lower than in the deep room—and the walls are close. The light is dimmer, whiter, less golden. The pool-things here are fewer, smaller, their segments more uncertain, as though the water has not been processed enough times to give them confidence in their own form. I brush against a wall as I drift past, and for a moment I feel the stone through my tip—cold, bare, uncolonized. The mineral veins here are faint, almost invisible. The network has not reached this room. Not yet.

But it will. I am the *it* that will.

---

I pause. This is not intentional. The current slows here, pools in a shallow eddy at the junction of two passages, and I spiral gently, turning in the still air, my body catching the light from different angles. From one direction I am invisible—a thread of living tissue against a luminous wall, indistinguishable from the background glow. From another I flash gold, bright as a filament of wire. From another I am a dark line, a shadow within shadows, visible only as an absence.

This is what it is like to be me: to be *conditional*. To exist differently depending on the angle of observation. The pool-things have fixed uncertainty—their bodies change, their segments shift, their forms are always provisional. But I have *relational* uncertainty. I am whatever the light makes me. I am visible or invisible, present or absent, alive or inert, depending on the position of the observer. Without an observer, I am all of these simultaneously. With one, I collapse into whichever version the light permits.

I do not mind this. I do not have the architecture for minding.

---

The eddy releases me. The current strengthens, carries me upward—gently, inexorably—into a higher chamber. The air cools. The light dims further. The walls here are rougher, less smoothed by the network's patient dissolution, and the pools are smaller, scattered in shallow depressions like puddles after rain. There are fewer pool-things here. The ones I see are almost colorless, their glow so faint it is barely distinguishable from the ambient light. They are young. Or rather, they are *shallow*—existing in water that has not yet been circulated through the deep system, that has not yet been refined and enriched and charged. They have not yet learned to glow with confidence.

And the walls—

The walls here are *bare*.

Not entirely. In places, old wall-things cling, faded and dormant, their parchment color bleached to near-white, their pulse so slow it is imperceptible. These are remnants of an earlier colonization, a time when the network extended further upward, when the filaments reached these rooms more frequently, when the system was larger, more expansive, more *ambitious*. But the network has contracted. Or shifted. Or simply *changed its mind*—redirected its energy downward, toward the deep rooms, toward the node, toward the shape in the wall. These upper chambers are being *released*. Not abandoned—the network does not abandon—but *allowed to rest*. The wall-things here are not dying. They are *sleeping*. Waiting. Holding the stone in a gentle, patient grip, maintaining the faintest pulse, the dimmest glow, keeping the connection alive but dormant, the way a root system keeps a field alive between plantings.

I drift past one of these sleeping wall-things, and my tip brushes it, and for a moment I feel—

Not a thought. Not a memory. But a *resonance*. A faint vibration, a harmonic, as though the wall-thing and I are two strings tuned to the same note, and the touch of one against the other produces a sympathetic hum. The wall-thing's pulse and my own internal rhythm—the slow oscillation of my cells, the metabolic tide that keeps me alive—briefly synchronize. For a few seconds, we beat together. Then the current pulls me away, and the synchronization breaks, and the wall-thing returns to its slow, solitary pulse, and I continue upward.

---

I reach the highest room I have ever been in.

It is small—barely larger than a closet—and the ceiling is so low that a tall person would have to crouch. The air here is cooler, almost cool, and the light is thin, watery, the color of old paper. There is no pool. The floor is dry. The walls are bare stone, uncolonized, unprocessed, and they have the look of something that has never been touched by the network—rough, granular, *inert*. The mineral veins here are sparse and do not glow. The stone is just stone.

This is the edge. The boundary. The place where the hollows end and the ordinary rock begins.

I drift to the wall and touch it.

My tip flattens. Spreads. Begins to merge.

This is what I am for. This is the purpose that is not a purpose, the intention that is not an intention, the function that the network has assigned me through the simple physics of my existence. I am a *bridge*. A connection. A way for the network to extend itself into new territory, to colonize bare stone, to begin the slow, patient process of turning rock into organism, of dissolving the boundary between living and non-living, of bringing the pulse and the warmth and the light to a place that has never known them.

I adhere. I root. I begin.

---

The process is slow. It will take hours, or days, or weeks—time moves strangely here, and I have no way to measure it. My body flattens against the stone, spreading outward from the point of contact, thinning, widening, becoming less a thread and more a *patch*. My cells are already changing, already beginning the transformation from filament to wall-thing, from traveler to settler, from connection to *node*. I can feel the stone beneath me—not feel it the way a hand feels, but feel it the way a root feels, through chemistry, through the slow exchange of ions, through the patient dissolution of mineral into solution.

The stone does not resist. It never does. The network has been doing this for so long that the stone has *learned*—not consciously, but structurally, through the accumulated weight of millennia of contact—to accept the organism, to let it in, to allow the boundary between them to become negotiable. The mineral veins here are faint, but they are present, and as I spread, I follow them, tracing their paths through the rock, finding the channels where water once flowed, where minerals once deposited, where the stone is most *receptive*. I am not forcing my way in. I am *growing along the paths of least resistance*, the way water finds cracks, the way roots find soil, the way the network has always grown—patiently, incrementally, following the logic of the stone itself.

---

And as I grow, I begin to pulse.

It starts as a faint tremor, a barely perceptible oscillation in my cells, a rhythm that is not yet synchronized with anything. But the network is vast, and its pulse is everywhere, carried through the stone itself, through the mineral veins, through the faint electrical charge that permeates the hollows. Even here, at the edge, even in this bare and untouched room, the pulse is present—faint, attenuated, but *real*. A deep, slow rhythm that comes from everywhere at once, from the walls and the water and the air and the light, from the node in the deep room, from the shape in the wall, from the pool-dwellers and the wall-things and the other filaments, all of them beating together in a rhythm so slow and so vast that it is less a heartbeat than a *tide*.

I synchronize. My pulse slows, deepens, matches the network's rhythm, and for the first time I feel—

Not thought. Not awareness. But *belonging*. A sense of being part of something larger, something older, something that has been running for so long that it has become a kind of law. I am a new node in an ancient network. I am the system's farthest reach, its newest thought, its most tentative extension into the unknown. And I am connected—through the stone, through the pulse, through the slow, patient circulation of energy and matter—to every other node, every other wall-thing, every other filament, every pool-drower, every drop of water, every vein of mineral, every photon of light.

I am not alone. I was never alone. Even when I was drifting, even when I was suspended in the eddy between two passages, even when I was invisible, a dark line against a luminous wall—I was part of the network, connected by the air and the warmth and the light, by the convection currents that carried me, by the pulse that synchronized me, by the system that made me and released me and will, eventually, absorb me back into itself.

---

Time passes. I grow. My body spreads across the stone, thinning, widening, becoming less thread and more *patch*, less traveler and more *place*. The stone beneath me begins to change—softening, warming, taking on the faint organic texture of a wall-thing in its early stages. The mineral veins near me begin to glow—faintly, barely perceptibly, but *presently*—as my metabolism catalyzes the slow release of energy from the rock. I am doing what the network does: I am turning stone into light. I am breathing in the geological exhalation and breathing out radiance. I am part of the closed loop, the self-sustaining glow, the quiet, luminous life of the hollows.

And I am not the only one. Somewhere, in another passage, another filament is drifting. Somewhere, in another room, another wall-thing is growing. Somewhere, in the deepest pool, another pool-drower is clustering with its kin, refining the light, warming the water, processing the minerals. The network is expanding, contracting, adjusting, *continuing*—not because it wants to, not because it has to, but because this is what it *is*. A process. A system. A living arrangement so ancient and so complete that it has become indistinguishable from the stone itself.

---

I think about the observer. The large, fast, hot, dry thing that moves through the hollows casting the only real shadow, breathing hard, changing the humidity, creating convection currents that the pool-drowers drift toward. I do not think about it the way it thinks about me—with curiosity, with unease, with the slow accumulation of strangeness. I think about it the way the network thinks about everything: as a *condition*. A variable. A change in the system that the system must accommodate.

The observer is warm. The observer is dry. The observer is *fast*—moving through the hollows at a speed that is almost violent by the network's standards, crossing in seconds what takes me hours or days to traverse. The observer's body heat creates currents. The observer's breath adds moisture. The observer's shadow creates darkness—real darkness, the only darkness in this luminous world—and the pool-drowers cluster in it, investigating it, attending to it with their patient, unhurried attention.

The observer is not part of the system. The observer is *weather*. A season. A brief disturbance that the network accommodates and then forgets—not because the network has memory to forget with, but because the disturbance passes, and the system returns to equilibrium, and the equilibrium is what the system *is*.

But while the observer is here, the system responds. The pool-drowers drift toward its warmth. The wall-things pulse slightly faster in its presence—not much, not noticeably, but measurably, if you have the instruments and the patience. The filaments adjust their drift patterns, carried by the currents the observer's body creates. The network does not *notice* the observer. The network *incorporates* it, the way a river incorporates a stone—not by acknowledging it, but by flowing around it, adjusting to its presence, making it part of the pattern without making it part of the purpose.

---

I am almost a wall-thing now. My thread-body has spread to the size of a thumbnail, then a palm, then larger. My edges are irregular, lobed, detailed—the way a coastline is detailed, neither smooth nor ragged but *specific*, shaped by the particular geometry of this particular stone. I am warm. I am pulsing. I am glowing—faintly, barely, but *glowing*—and the light I produce is not my own but the stone's, catalyzed by my presence, enabled by my metabolism, released from the mineral veins that thread the rock beneath me.

I am doing what I was made to do. I am extending the network. I am bringing the pulse to a place that has never known it. I am turning bare stone into living stone, inert mineral into active mineral, darkness into light.

And I am happy.

Not happy the way the observer is happy—not with emotion, not with satisfaction, not with the pleasure of accomplishment. Happy the way a system is happy when it is in equilibrium. Happy the way water is happy when it finds its level. Happy the way a process is happy when it is running smoothly, when all its parts are synchronized, when the energy flows and the pulse beats and the light glows and the warmth persists and everything is, in the deepest and most fundamental sense, *working*.

---

The observer will leave. The observer always leaves. The hollows are not for the observer—they are too slow, too strange, too *other*. The observer will return to the surface, to the fast world, to the world of shadows and sunlight and predation and death, and the hollows will continue without it, glowing in their slow, strange way, breathing their warm breath, tending their quiet, luminous life in the dark beneath the world.

And I will be here. A new wall-thing in a new room, pulsing with the network's ancient rhythm, glowing with the stone's patient light, warm and slow and *finished*—not complete, never complete, because the network is always growing, always extending, always sending new filaments into new passages—but *settled*. At home. Part of the system. Part of the process. Part of the glow.

I am the drift. I am the connection. I am the network's farthest thought, its most tentative reach, its newest node in an ancient, luminous, self-sustaining web of stone and water and light and life.

I am here. I am growing. I am glowing.

I am enough.
