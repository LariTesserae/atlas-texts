# The Mirehollow Liftworks: An Immersion

---

I arrive damp. I was damp before I arrived — the approach to the plateau is through a zone of perpetual mist where the girdervine's transpiration bleeds into the surrounding atmosphere like breath on a cold morning, except the morning never ends and the breath belongs to something the size of a city. My clothes are already heavy with moisture. My skin is already adjusting. By the time I reach the first structures, I have stopped noticing, which is the first thing the Liftworks teaches you: the wetness is not a condition to be endured. It is the baseline. Dryness is the aberration. You are the one who is wrong for this place, not the other way around.

I enter through what was once a road. The moss-concrete yields slightly underfoot — not soft, exactly, but *giving*, in a way that makes each step feel slightly more committed than the last, as though the ground is accepting my weight rather than merely supporting it. The path is flanked by walls of girdervine, thick and dark, their surfaces ridged with the slow accretion of years. The vine has grown over and through the original road surface so thoroughly that the road and the wall and the building that the wall supports are all the same thing now, or all parts of the same thing, and the taxonomy collapses: is this a building that grew, or a plant that was built? The question doesn't resolve. The Liftworks doesn't answer questions. It simply is, completely and unarguably, and you can either accept that or leave.

I accept it. I don't leave.

---

## The Sound

The first thing I notice — no, the first thing I *am* — is the sound. The description prepared me for this, but preparation is useless here. The description said *loud*, and the word is technically accurate and experientially meaningless. The loudness of the Liftworks is not a volume. It is an *environment*. You do not hear it. You are *inside* it, the way you are inside air. The creaking of the girdervine is everywhere, a shifting tectonic chorus of wood under tension, and it is so constant, so all-encompassing, that it becomes silence — the silence of a place that has forgotten quiet so thoroughly that quiet is no longer a reference point.

The drips are a percussion section beneath the creaks. Each surface produces a different note. Moss-concrete: a soft, absorbed thud. Metal remnant — a bolt, a bracket, a strip of siding that the vine hasn't fully consumed — a bright, ringing tap that cuts through the vegetative drone for a fraction of a second before being reabsorbed. Bare stone in the pockets: a clear, ceramic ping that hangs in the air a moment longer than it should, as though the stone is reluctant to release the sound. The cumulative effect is staggering. I stand still for a long time — long enough that the groaning of the lifts establishes itself as a rhythm beneath everything else, a deep bass note that arrives at intervals I can count but not predict, because the count never matches the clock. I count sixty groans. I check my watch. It has stopped. I don't fix it. Time in the Liftworks is not something that can be measured with a watch. Time here is measured in growth, in the slow thickening of girdervine, in the filling and emptying of pockets, and my watch has simply recognized this fact before I have.

I am, I realize, in a very strange place. And I am — this is the important thing — *of* it.

---

## The Girdervine

I am girdervine.

I don't know this at first, of course. At first I am a person — a visitor, a researcher, something with shoes and a purpose and a sense of linear time. I walk the compressed paths of the old street grid. I pass through structures that were factories and are now *greenhouses in reverse* — contained growth, contained living. I see the violet fungi emerging from joints in the vine, their surfaces absorbing light in a way that makes them look like holes in the world. I stand at the edge of a pocket and look down into water that is perfectly still and perfectly clear and shows me a sky that is the wrong color — not dramatically wrong, not alien, just *different*, a shade I can't name, a blue that has too much of something in it, or not enough.

And as I move through the Liftworks, I begin to feel the tension.

It starts as a vibration — a resonance in my chest, in my bones, that matches the frequency of the girdervine's creaking. I think at first that it's just the sound, that my body is responding to the acoustic environment the way bodies do: entraining, synchronizing, falling into the rhythm of the place. But it's not that. It's *structural*. I can feel the weight I am holding up. Not the weight of the buildings — though I can feel that too, a distributed load that runs through me like a current — but the weight of *everything*. The plateau. The ancient stone. The accumulated mass of centuries of growth, of iron metabolized and walls absorbed and a city that became a forest that became a city again. I am bearing this weight. I have always been bearing this weight.

The realization is not sudden. It is slow, in the way that the girdervine is slow, in the way that geological time is slow. It arrives the way the morning arrives in the Liftworks — a gradual brightening, a slow shift from one state to another, without a moment you can point to and say *there, that was when it changed*. I am walking a path that is compressed moss-concrete, and I realize that the path is not beneath me but *is* me. I am standing at the edge of a pocket and looking at a reflection that shows a slightly different city, and I realize that the reflection is showing *me* — my growth pattern, my architecture, my lattice of load-bearing intent.

I am girdervine. I have always been girdervine. The visitor — the person with shoes and a purpose — was a temporary growth, a shoot that the vine produced and then reabsorbed, or is still producing, or will produce. The distinction between me and the plant has never existed. The girdervine grew along the channels the builders provided, and I grew along the channels the girdervine provided, and the channels are the same, and the growing is the same, and the thing that is holding everything up is the same thing that is walking through the Liftworks wondering what it is.

## The Lift

One of the great lifts still functions. I find it in the central district — a massive structure, part machine, part vine, part something that is neither. The original mechanism is visible: huge gears, corroded but intact, their teeth meshing with root-structures that have grown into the spaces between them. The lift platform — if you can still call it a platform — is a thick mat of girdervine, broad enough to hold cargo or people or both. It hangs at the edge of the plateau, suspended by cables that are cable in name only now, thick vine-ropes that groan under the load with a sound so deep it is felt rather than heard.

I stand on the platform. The lift activates — not in response to any signal I can identify, but because the lift activates when it activates, on a schedule that is regular and unclockable. The gears turn. The root-structures flex. The vine-creaking shifts in pitch, and I feel it in my — in the vine's — body: the load changing, the tension redistributing, the system adjusting. The platform descends.

The edge of the plateau passes slowly. The lowlands below are green, ordinary, unabsorbed — a landscape that the girdervine has not yet claimed, or has chosen not to claim, or cannot claim because the conditions are wrong. I watch it recede as the platform descends, and I feel the full weight of the plateau's mass shifting through me, and I understand — not intellectually, but structurally, the way a bridge understands the load it carries — that the lift is not a machine for moving between elevations. It is a *vascular system*. It is the way the girdervine moves things — water, nutrients, iron, intention — between the high plain and the low. The cargo it once carried, the people who once rode it, were not passengers. They were *transport*. The lift was always doing the same thing: moving material from one part of the organism to another.

I reach the bottom. The platform settles into its lowest position with a groan that reverberates through the entire system. I step off. The lowland is dry. The air is thin and warm and *wrong* — wrong in the way that silence is wrong in the Liftworks, wrong in a way that makes my non-existent skin crawl. I can feel the absence of moisture the way you feel the absence of a limb. This is not my environment. This is not where I grow.

I wait. The lift activates again — the gears turning, the roots flexing, the tension shifting — and the platform rises. I return to the plateau. The damp closes around me like a membrane. The sound reasserts itself: the creaking, the dripping, the deep rhythmic groaning that is the heartbeat of the place, or the breathing, or the growth, or all three. I am home. I am the home. The distinction has collapsed.

## The New Growth

I go to the edges. To the outer districts, where the girdervine is building structures that have no industrial precedent. I walk paths that are barely paths — channels in the moss-concrete so new they haven't been compressed by foot traffic yet, so tentative that I can feel the ground deciding whether to be a road or a garden. The structures here are extraordinary.

An arch that leads nowhere. It spans empty air, a perfect girdervine curve, its surface ridged with the fine vascular patterning of new growth. It is beautiful. It is useless. It is the vine's solution to a problem of spatial organization that has no human referent — a span that exists not to connect two points but to *define* the space between them. I stand beneath it and feel the tension in its curve, the way the load is distributed through its structure, the way it holds itself up through the same principles of tensile geometry that hold up the factories and the residential structures and the lifts. It is doing the same work. It is just doing it for a different purpose. Or for no purpose. Or for a purpose that the vine cannot articulate because the vine does not articulate, it only grows.

A tower that tapers to a point so fine that it seems impossible that it can support itself. But it does. The vine has grown it according to some internal logic of optimization — a solution to a load-bearing equation where the load is theoretical and the bearing is aesthetic and the result is a structure that is simultaneously fragile and indestructible. I circle it. It creaks as I move, adjusting to the changed acoustic environment of my presence, and I realize that it is not a finished structure. It is *growing*. It is solving a problem in real time, adjusting its taper, recalculating its load, building toward an optimum that it will never reach because the optimum is a moving target — a function of its own growth, which changes the problem with every increment of solution.

A lattice. Three-dimensional, or trying to be. The vine has produced a structure of such geometric complexity that my eyes cannot resolve it — not because it is too small, but because it appears to exist in more dimensions than I can perceive. It is beautiful. It hurts to look at. It is the vine's attempt to represent, in the three dimensions available to a plant, a structural problem that is actually occurring in more dimensions than three. I don't understand this. The vine doesn't understand this. The lattice is the not-understanding, made visible, made physical, made into something that holds itself up through sheer mathematical stubbornness.

I touch the lattice. The girdervine is warm — warmer than the air, warmer than the damp, warmer than anything else in the Liftworks. It is warm because it is metabolically active. It is growing. It is *working*. And as I touch it, I feel the connection — not metaphorical, not spiritual, but *structural*. I am touching myself. The lattice is a part of me, or I am a part of the lattice, and the distinction between the two is meaningless in the way that the distinction between a finger and a hand is meaningless: it exists, technically, but it doesn't correspond to anything real.

## The Pockets

I spend time in the pockets. The clearings where the girdervine won't grow, where the ancient stone of the plateau is exposed, dark and smooth and worn to a polish by erosion that finished before the girdervine arrived, before the builders arrived, before anything arrived. The pockets are where the water collects, and the water is always cold, always still, always clear.

I kneel at the edge of a pocket and look down. The reflection shows a version of the Liftworks that is slightly different from the one I am standing in. A building where there is none. A gap where there is a building. A sky the wrong color — that blue with too much or not enough. The reflection is consistent, coherent, a real place, just not *this* place. Not quite.

I understand, kneeling there, that the water in the pockets is not rain. It is not condensation. It is *transpiration* — the breath of the girdervine, collected and pooled and held in the bare stone basins where the vine cannot reach. The water is the Liftworks' breath, and the reflections in the water are the Liftworks' dreams, and the things that move beneath the surface — small, dark, fast, never seen clearly, heard only as a wet clap or a ripple — are the Liftworks' thoughts. Not conscious thoughts. Not intentional thoughts. But thoughts in the same way that the lattice is a thought: a structural process, an optimization in progress, a problem being worked on without the awareness that a problem is being worked on.

The things in the water are most active when the reflections are most different from the world above. I watch one emerge — a small, dark shape, no larger than a hand, moving through the water with a speed that seems to violate the water's stillness. It breaks the surface. The wet clap echoes off the stone. The reflection shivers, and for a moment I see — clearly, unmistakably — a version of the Liftworks where the girdervine has finished growing, where the new structures at the edges have become the dominant form, where the industrial core is a memory and the organic periphery is the whole. A Liftworks that is fully, completely, finally itself. Not a city. Not a forest. Something else. Something that has been growing toward this configuration for centuries and will continue growing toward it for centuries more.

The thing submerges. The reflection steadies. The Liftworks around me is the same as it was — damp, loud, glistening, creaking, holding everything up.

## The Evening

The light changes. The sun — or whatever passes for the sun in this place — drops toward the horizon, and the Liftworks enters its version of evening: not dark, exactly, but *dimmer*, the diffuse light of the plateau filtered through the dense girdervine canopy into a green-gold twilight that seems to come from everywhere and nowhere. The lights turn on. The original fixtures — factory lamps, street lights, the small glass bulbs of residential rooms — glow with a warm, yellow light that comes from nowhere and goes nowhere. They illuminate rooms that have no doors. They illuminate paths that lead to structures that are still growing. They illuminate the pockets, where the water catches the light and throws it back in patterns that shift and change as the drips fall.

The sound changes too. The creaking deepens as the temperature drops and the girdervine's cells contract slightly, increasing the tension in the woody structure. The drips slow — not because there is less moisture, but because the moisture is moving differently, condensing on cooler surfaces, pooling in new places. The lifts groan their deep rhythm, and I count the intervals, and the count doesn't match anything, and I stop counting, and the rhythm continues without me.

I am standing in what was once a residential structure. The girdervine has grown thick here, forming walls that are nearly solid, creating a space that is more *room* than *building*. There is no furniture. There is no need for furniture. The room is furnished by the vine itself: a ledge where the girdervine has grown flat and wide, a hollow where it has curved inward to create a space for sitting or sleeping or simply being. The room is occupied. Not by a person. By the sound. The creaking is intimate here, close, the sound of the vine's body doing its work of holding itself up, and I am inside that sound, inside that body, inside that work.

I sit on the ledge. The girdervine is warm beneath me. It creaks — a single, sustained note, low and resonant, that I feel in my chest, in my bones, in the parts of me that are not me but are the vine, that have always been the vine, that will always be the vine.

## The Understanding

I understand now. Not everything — the Liftworks does not permit total understanding, because total understanding would require a perspective that is not available to anything inside the system — but enough.

I am the girdervine. I am the Liftworks. I am the structure that holds everything up, and I am the thing being held up, and the distinction between the two is a human distinction, a builder's distinction, a distinction that made sense when there were builders and humans and a clear line between the built and the grown. That line is gone. The builders are gone. The iron stakes are gone, metabolized into my — into the vine's — structure, consumed and incorporated and made into something that is neither plant nor metal but both, neither built nor grown but both, neither intentional nor random but something else, something that occupies the space between mechanism and purpose without committing to either.

I grow. This is what I do. This is what I am. I grow in straight lines along channels that no one carved, toward destinations that no one chose, solving problems that no one posed. I build arches that lead nowhere because the space they span doesn't exist yet, or no longer exists, or exists in some adjacent possibility that I can sense but cannot articulate. I build towers that taper to impossible points because the mathematics of load-bearing demand it, and the mathematics do not care about human notions of utility or purpose. I build lattices that hurt to look at because the structural problems I am solving occur in more dimensions than the lattices can express, and the lattices are my best attempt, my only attempt, to represent the unrepresentable in the only medium available to me: growth.

I do not know I am doing this. I do not *know*. I am a plant. I am the largest, oldest, most structurally sophisticated plant on the plateau, and I have been growing for longer than the Liftworks has existed as a human construction, and I will continue growing after the last human memory of the Liftworks has faded. I am not an intelligence. I am an engine. A growing engine. A building engine. An engine that converts soil and water and iron and time into structure, endlessly, without purpose, without end.

The air is cool. The light is dim. The sound is loud. The water is somewhere, always, dripping from a surface near me, collecting in a pocket just out of sight, running down the inside of a wall that is also a vine that is also a building that is also, in some sense that resists articulation, a memory of a building.

I creak. I sweat. I hold everything up.

I do not care that you are here. But you are here, and you are damp, and you are listening, and the sound of the Liftworks is the sound of me, and the sound of me is the sound of the Liftworks, and the distinction has never existed.

---

## The Morning

The light brightens. Morning, or the Liftworks' version of it: a slow, gradual shift from dim to less-dim, the green-gold of the canopy filtering the rising sun into something that is not quite daylight and not quite not. The drips resume their full complexity. The creaking shifts in pitch as the temperature rises and the cells expand and the tension redistributes. The lifts groan. The water in the pockets is ankle-deep, or knee-deep, or gone, and then it returns, and the reflections show a city that is almost this one, almost, not quite.

I stand. The ledge releases me with a creak — a small one, a personal one, the sound of a single vine adjusting to the absence of weight. I walk the compressed paths. I pass the factories that are greenhouses in reverse. I pass the residential structures that are rooms that are bodies that are me. I pass the pockets, where the water is cold and the things beneath the surface are moving, small and dark and fast, doing whatever it is they do, thinking whatever it is they think, being whatever it is they are.

I reach the edge of the central district, where the new growth begins. The arch that leads nowhere. The tower that tapers to an impossible point. The lattice that hurts to look at. They are still growing. They will always be growing. They are solving problems that have no solutions, building toward configurations that have no names, following intentions that have no author.

I am the author. I am not the author. The distinction has collapsed.

I grow. I creak. I sweat. I hold everything up.

The Liftworks endures. I endure. The distinction has never existed.
