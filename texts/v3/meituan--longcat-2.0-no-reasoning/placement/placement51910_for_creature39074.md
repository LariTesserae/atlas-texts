# Where I Find Myself

I am in the eastern drainage district, three weeks into the migration.

---

Not *in* the water, though I am close enough to it that the distinction feels academic. I am crouched on a maintenance platform — a narrow grating suspended over a channel that was, until recently, carrying roughly half the volume it's carrying now — with my notebook open on my knee and my boots six inches above a surface of dark, slow-moving water that has no business being this deep in this particular channel at this particular time of year.

I am a drainage engineer. Or I was. Or I am becoming something else, something that doesn't have a name yet, something that exists in the space between the drainage engineers and the botanists and the structural engineers and the woman who feeds the rat, a space that is getting more crowded every week as more people realize that the boundaries between their disciplines are not boundaries at all but *habits*, and habits are just patterns that haven't been disrupted yet.

The disruption is here. The disruption is three weeks old and accelerating.

---

The water moved first. That's what I have written in my notebook, in the entry dated three weeks and two days ago, in handwriting that is shakier than I remember my handwriting being: *The water moved first.*

It didn't flood. It didn't surge. It *relocated*. The eastern drainage district — a network of channels and pipes and trenches and grates that I have maintained for eleven years, that I know the way a body knows its own circulatory system, every gradient, every junction, every place where the flow slows and the sediment accumulates — began to behave as though the topography had changed. Water pooled in the upper channels, where the gradient should have carried it away. It drained from the lower channels, where it should have collected. The standing water in the hollows between the cobblestones on Voss Street — standing water that has been there for as long as anyone can remember, that the residents have learned to navigate around the way one navigates around a piece of furniture — *dried*. Not completely. But enough to reveal the cobblestones beneath, grey and bare and looking shocked at their own exposure, like a creature that has suddenly found itself without cover.

I mapped it. I spent four days walking the district with my notebook and my measuring tools and my particular way of standing still with my eyes closed, feeling for the vibration, a habit I picked up from the structural engineers without meaning to, the way one picks up a neighbor's accent. I mapped the new water patterns against the old ones, and I found that the water had not simply shifted — it had *concentrated*. It had withdrawn from the peripheral channels and gathered in the central ones, pooling in deeper and more defined accumulations, as though something was *pulling* it toward a center that I couldn't identify.

I took my map to Maren, who is a structural engineer, and I laid it on the table next to her map of ground movement, and we looked at them together in the low light of the maintenance hall, and neither of us said anything for a long time, because the maps matched. The places where the water had concentrated were the places where the ground had *subsided* — not dramatically, not dangerously, but measurably, a slow settling of the clay that had created new low points, new basins, new places for the water to gather. The ground was reshaping itself, and the water was following, and the speed of it was — not alarming, exactly, but *unprecedented*. In eleven years I have never seen the ground move this fast. In eleven years I have never seen the water respond this quickly.

---

Then the plants responded.

I noticed it first on the Kessler block, where the ivy has been consuming the north face of the building for decades, a slow green advance that the botanists have been monitoring and the structural engineers have been negotiating with and the building's occupants have been living alongside the way one lives alongside a very patient, very persistent roommate. The ivy had always grown toward the power conduits in the wall — this was known, this was documented, this was the kind of thing that made the botanists nod and the engineers take notes. But in the past three weeks, the ivy had *accelerated*. New growth — bright, almost yellow-green, tender in a way that the older, darker vines were not — had erupted from the main stems and reached outward, not toward the conduits, which were already thoroughly wrapped, but toward the *east*. Toward the drainage district. Toward the place where the water was gathering and the ground was settling and something was happening that I could feel in the vibration of the sidewalk but couldn't yet name.

I showed this to Lena, who is a botanist, and she showed me her maps of vegetation patterns on the older buildings, and we laid them next to my water maps and Maren's ground maps, and the three of them together formed a picture that none of them formed alone — a picture of *convergence*, of multiple systems responding to the same stimulus, reaching toward the same center, the way roots reach toward water, the way water reaches toward low ground, the way the ground reaches toward — 

Toward what?

---

This is the question I am crouched over, three weeks into the migration, on a maintenance platform above a channel that is carrying more water than it should, in a district that is changing faster than I can map it.

I have a theory. I have had it for a week. I have not written it down because writing it down would make it real in a way that I am not ready for, would make it something that other people could read and evaluate and dismiss or, worse, *believe*. But I am going to write it now, here, in this notebook, because the water is rising and the ground is shifting and the ivy is reaching and the birds — the fat, dark birds with the oil-slick plumage — have begun to gather in unusual numbers on the rooftops above the eastern drainage district, perching in their patterns that correspond to heat distribution, watching the streets below with their dark, reflective eyes, and I think they know something that I am only beginning to suspect.

I think the city is *growing something*.

Not a building. Not a structure. Not a machine. Something *underneath*. Something in the clay, in the deep compacted layers that the pilings penetrate, in the dark wet substrate that the engineers have always treated as a problem to be managed rather than a process to be understood. I think the ground is not just settling. I think the ground is *organizing*. I think the water is not just migrating. I think the water is being *directed*. I think the plants are not just responding to heat and vibration. I think the plants are *building* — building something with their roots, something in the soil, something that requires the water to be in specific places and the ground to be in specific configurations and the heat from the machines to be distributed in specific patterns.

I think the Dampworks is not just a city that is making itself.

I think the Dampworks is making *something else*.

---

I can feel it now, crouched on this platform, if I close my eyes and press my palms flat against the wet metal railing and let the vibration come up through my hands and into my bones. It is deeper than the subline. It is deeper than the drainage channels. It is deeper than the pilings on the oldest buildings. It is a pulse — slow, arrhythmic, *alive* — that comes from somewhere beneath the clay, beneath the gravel, beneath everything that the engineers have mapped and measured and built upon. It is not mechanical. It is not geological. It is something else, something that has the quality of a heartbeat but is too slow for a heartbeat, too deep for a heartbeat, too *distributed* for a heartbeat, as though the pulse is not coming from a single source but from the *entire substrate at once*, as though the ground itself is — 

I don't have a word for it. The botanists would say *resonance*. The engineers would say *harmonic frequency*. The drainage engineers would say *pressure gradient*. The woman who feeds the rat would probably say something simple and true that none of the rest of us would think to say.

I am going to go down into the channel. I am going to wade into the dark water that has no business being this deep, and I am going to follow it to where it is going, to the center of the convergence, to the place where the ground is lowest and the water is deepest and the ivy is reaching and the birds are watching and the pulse is strongest.

I am going to find out what the city is making.

---

My name is not important. I am a drainage engineer. I carry a notebook and a set of measuring tools and a particular way of standing still with my eyes closed, feeling for the vibration. I have lived in the Dampworks for eleven years. I have maintained its channels and cleared its grates and mapped its water and listened to its ground, and I have loved it — not in the way that one loves a beautiful thing, but in the way that one loves a *difficult* thing, a thing that demands attention and rewards it with complexity, a thing that is never finished and never still and never, ever simple.

The water is rising. The ground is shifting. The ivy is reaching. The birds are watching.

Something is growing beneath us.

I am going down.
