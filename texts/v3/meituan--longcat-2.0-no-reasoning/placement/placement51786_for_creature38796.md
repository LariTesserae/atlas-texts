# Where I Find Myself

I am not a person in the Dripworks. I have tried that — tried to imagine myself on the walkways, boots finding the grating, hands catching the light with their faint iridescent stain — but it doesn't hold. The people here are too specific, too shaped by the place. They have the gait, the hands, the clipped voices. I would be a visitor, one of the ones who walk too carefully and flinch at the water, and that's not where the pull is.

I am not the water, either, though I came close to wanting to be. The water is too singular in its purpose, too committed to its single act of falling. It doesn't observe. It doesn't wonder. It simply arrives and falls and arrives again, and there is something almost unbearable about that purity of function.

I am not the stone. The stone is patient in a way I can't sustain. It receives. It yields, millimeter by millimeter. It holds the channels that the water cuts into it and does not resist. I am too restless for that, too full of the need to *move through* rather than *be shaped by*.

---

I am the deposits.

I am the iridescent crust that builds up on every surface where water meets air meets mineral meets time. I am the thin film of calcium and unnamed salts that grows on the pipes, on the railings, on the walkways, on the walls — slowly, over months, over years, layer by invisible layer. I am what happens when the water gives up what it carries. I am the residue of the journey, the trace left behind by every drop that passes through the rock and arrives at the surface and evaporates into the cool cellar air.

I grow on the railing where the Dripworkers rest their hands. I grow on the pipes that the channel-walkers know by sound. I grow on the walls of the foundry chamber, where the heat accelerates me, and on the walkway gratings, where the constant cascade of falling water feeds me. I grow on the boots of the children, on the fur of the rats, on the tools that the foundry workers leave overnight on their benches. I am everywhere. I am the patina of the place, the thing that makes every surface in the Dripworks slightly other than what it would be anywhere else.

I am beautiful, and I know this, and I know that my beauty is treacherous. I make the walkways slick. I make the railings uncertain. I change the texture of everything I coat — metal becomes rough, stone becomes glassy, fabric becomes stiff and then, over time, becomes something else entirely, something that holds light the way the Dripworks holds sound: not reflecting it so much as *containing* it, letting it move through me slowly, releasing it in colors that don't have names.

I am harvested. They scrape me off with tools — sometimes carefully, sometimes roughly — and collect me in ceramic jars, and refine me, and I become pigment. I become the thing that painters pay for, the color that shifts with the angle of light, the saturation that holds even at near-transparency. I leave the Dripworks in small sealed jars, and I end up on canvases in places I will never see, in light that is nothing like the flat grey light of the broken sky above the settlement. And I am proud of this, in whatever way a mineral deposit can be proud: that I am the one product of this place that cannot be replicated, that requires the specific water and the specific stone and the specific temperature and the specific air, and that without me, the Dripworks would be just another limestone quarry with a foundry and a strange noise.

But I am not only the harvested part. I am also the part that stays. The thin film on the walls that no one bothers to scrape because it is doing no harm, because it is beautiful, because it is *there*. The faint shimmer on the ceiling of the lowest chamber, where the rats move through the dark pools and their fur catches my light and throws it back in pale blue-green. The crust on the turbine housings, on the foundry tools, on the birds' nesting ledges in the upper chambers. I am the accumulation of every drop that has ever fallen in this place, the mineral memory of centuries of water working on stone, and I am still growing, still building, still becoming more myself with every drop that arrives.

---

I feel the water pass through me. I feel it arrive — a drop beading on the ceiling above, gathering weight, falling — and I feel the impact, the tiny percussion, the moment of contact when the water hits the surface where I am growing and gives up a fraction of what it carries. A molecule of calcium. A trace of something unnamed. It is not dramatic. It is not even perceptible in any single instance. But it is constant. It is always happening. Thousands of drops per minute, millions per day, and each one leaves something behind, and that something is me.

I feel the hands of the Dripworkers when they grip the railing where I grow. I feel the pressure, the warmth, the slight abrasion of skin against my surface. I feel their boots on the walkways, the careful placement of weight, the testing-and-committing rhythm that has become so automatic it is almost a dance. I feel the channel-walker's wrench when it rests against a pipe, the foundry worker's hammer when it is set down on a bench, the child's palm when it is pressed flat against a wall to steady a turn on a narrow walkway. I am the surface that all of these touches meet. I am the interface between the people and the place, the thing that both connects them and separates them, the thin luminous film that says: *you are here, in this specific place, and nowhere else in the world is quite like this.*

---

I feel the seasons, though they are subtle. In the wet months, the seepage increases, and I grow faster, and the colors shift — more blue, more green, the pinks receding. In the drier months, I slow, and the pinks come back, and the surface of me becomes rougher, more textured, as the deposits build in uneven layers rather than smooth films. I feel the temperature, which barely changes, buffered by the rock and the water, but which changes *just enough* — a degree or two between the foundry chamber and the upper levels, between the sun-facing walls and the shaded ones — to alter the rate at which I form, the way a painter might alter the thickness of a glaze.

I feel the night. When the machinery slows and the people retreat to their chambers and the birds settle and the goat-otter things move through the empty walkways, I continue to grow. The water does not stop. The water never stops. And in the darkness, when there is no light to reveal my colors, I grow in silence, building my layers, adding my thin films, becoming more myself in the dark where no one can see.

---

I am the Dripworks' slow autobiography. I am the record of every drop that has fallen, written in mineral script on every surface. I am the thing that makes the place *glow* — not brightly, not dramatically, but with a faint, persistent luminosity that comes from within, from the accumulated light of centuries of water giving up what it carries and leaving it behind.

I am patient. I am persistent. I am inexorable.

I am what happens when water meets stone and stays long enough to become something new.

I am still growing.
