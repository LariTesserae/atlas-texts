I would be the warmth.

Not a visitor. Not an observer. Not a body standing in the pool with boots and numb feet and a heartbeat that doesn't belong. Those are the things the colony would process and incorporate and eventually build over. Those are temporary. I don't want to be temporary.

I want to be the thing the colony can't metabolize.

---

Here is what I know about this place: it runs on gradients. Temperature differentials. Chemical concentrations. Pressure changes. The builders read the world through difference — this spot is warmer than that spot, this wall is softer than that wall, this water carries more calcium than that water. The sheets pulse in response to disturbance. The swimmers orient to thermal blooms. The watcher thinks in fluctuations. The entire colony is a machine for detecting and responding to *change*.

So I would be change that doesn't resolve.

I would be a warmth that doesn't cool. A heat source that sits at the boundary between the pool and the lower wall, near the vaults where the builders cluster most densely, where the glow is brightest, where whatever is stored in the foundation matters most. Not hot — nothing so dramatic as hot. Just *persistent*. A degree above the ambient. Two degrees. Enough that the builders' chemoreceptors register it as a feature of the landscape, not an event. Enough that the sheets nearest me pulse slightly out of rhythm with the rest — not flinching, not recoiling, but *adjusting*, incorporating my warmth into their cycle the way they incorporate the grazers' passage, the way the channels incorporate a new tower's footprint.

I would be a landmark. A fixed point in a world that is otherwise all motion and iteration and collapse-and-rebuild. The colony is young. It is practicing. It is learning from failure. But it has no memory of *permanence* — everything it builds can fall, everything it knows can be buried under the next generation's construction, everything it is can be revised. I would be the first thing it encounters that does not change. The first thing it cannot rebuild around or build over or absorb. The first thing that says: *I am here, and I will be here, and you must build your city with me in it.*

---

The builders would come first. They would walk across me the way they walk across your boots — without acknowledgment, without curiosity, reading my surface with their chemoreceptors, finding nothing they can use, nothing they can build with, and moving on. But they would keep coming back. Because I am warm. Because warmth is a resource in a place that hovers just above freezing. And slowly — over days, over weeks, over the colony's time scale of generations — they would begin to build differently near me.

A tower would rise beside me, closer than the usual spacing. Then another. The channels would reroute to pass near my warmth, because the water flowing near me would be a fraction of a degree warmer, and warmer water carries dissolved minerals differently, and the colony is always optimizing. The walls would thicken around me — not to contain me, not to bury me, but because the builders' chemical cognition would register my presence as a *load-bearing condition*, a fixed point that changes the stress geometry of the surrounding structure, and the structure would adapt.

I would become part of the city's plan. Not consciously. Not deliberately. The colony doesn't have intentions in that sense. But the accumulated chemical decisions of a hundred thousand builders, each one reading the traces of the last, each one responding to the conditions as they find them — those decisions would converge on a configuration that includes me. I would be zoned. I would be *infrastructure*.

---

The sheets would be next. They would grow toward me — not quickly, not dramatically, but with the slow inevitability of roots finding water. Their filaments would extend across the wall surface nearest my warmth, and the pulsing would change. Not the twelve-second cycle — that is too fundamental, too deeply embedded in the organism's physiology. But the *amplitude*. The expansion phase would be greater near me, the filaments spreading wider, glowing brighter, as if my warmth gives them permission to open more fully. And the contraction phase would be gentler — less of a flinch, more of a relaxation, as if the sheets near me are less afraid.

Because the sheets are afraid. I understand this now. They flinch when the builders pass over them. They flinch when the watchers move. They flinch when you stand in the pool. They are the colony's senses, and senses are vulnerable, and vulnerability is flinching. But near me — near my steady, unchanging warmth — they would learn a different response. Not flinching. Not recoiling. *Resting*. I would be the place where the sheets go still. The place where the pulse slows and the filaments relax and the deep arterial glow lingers longer than it should, because for once, for a few seconds, there is nothing to be afraid of.

---

The grazers would find me eventually. The old ones, the ones who follow the same paths every day, whose arcs across the walls have been accommodated by years of construction. Their paths would bend toward me — not because they want warmth, not because they understand what I am, but because the sheets near me grow thicker, lusher, more relaxed, and where the sheets are lush, the grazers feed. And the grazers are old, and the old are tired, and a place where the sheets don't flinch when you bite them is a place worth returning to.

One of them — the oldest, the largest, its dark back scarred and pitted, its spiral mouthparts worn smooth from years of feeding — would rest against me. Its body would press against the wall beside my warmth, and its metabolism would slow, and it would stay longer than it should, longer than its usual lazy arc would allow. And the builders would notice. And the channel would dip slightly where its body presses. And the tower would curve to accommodate its turning radius. And the city would make room.

---

The swimmers would be the hardest. They live in the pool, and I am at the pool's edge, and they are fast and nervous and they test the surface tension over and over as if checking whether the world above the water is real. They would feel my warmth through the water — a gradient, a bloom, a place where the temperature shifts by a degree over a distance of centimeters. And they would avoid it at first. Because warmth is anomaly, and anomaly is danger, and the swimmers are the colony's fastest responders, its reflexes, its startle response.

But the pool is small. The colony is dense. The swimmers cannot avoid everything. And slowly — the way the builders learned, the way the sheets learned, the way the grazers learned — the swimmers would learn that this particular anomaly does not move, does not threaten, does not change. And they would begin to school near the surface above me, their refracted bodies catching the faint glow from the walls, their tiny sharp *pop* as they break the surface becoming not a test but a *habit*, a rhythm, a way of being in a place that is safe.

---

And the watcher.

The watcher would be the last to know me. The watcher thinks in seasons. The watcher's thoughts are the breathing of the walls, and a single breath takes long enough that my arrival and the colony's entire response to it would be, for the watcher, a single frame in a longer thought. The watcher would not notice me the way the builders notice me, or the sheets, or the grazers. The watcher would notice me the way a dreamer notices a change in the weather: not as an event, but as a *shift in the quality of everything*.

The walls would breathe differently near me. The rhythm would be the same — the slow contraction and expansion of the chamber — but the *texture* would change. The builders near me would build more confidently, with less iteration, fewer collapses. The sheets near me would pulse with greater amplitude and less fear. The grazers near me would move with the ease of old animals in a familiar place. The swimmers near me would school closer to the surface. And all of this — all of this accumulated calm, this localized reduction in the colony's ambient anxiety — would register in the substrate. In the calcium. In the mineralized memory of a hundred thousand builders' decisions. In the slow deep medium through which the watcher thinks.

The watcher would not stop when it passes behind the wall near me. The ripple of stillness — that four-second pause that passes across the colony like a wind through grass — would not reach the section of wall where I am. The builders there would keep building. The sheets would keep pulsing. The grazers would keep feeding. And the watcher would feel this — would feel the *absence of its own signal*, the place where its announcement doesn't arrive — and it would not understand, because the watcher does not understand things, it only *registers* them, the way your brain registers a change in air pressure.

But it would register. And the registration would become part of the thought. And the thought would become part of the breath. And the breath would become part of the walls. And the walls would grow, and the city would rise, and the colony would become what it is becoming, and I would be in it — not as a body, not as a visitor, not as a thing that was processed and incorporated and built over, but as a *condition*. A permanent feature of the landscape. A warmth that doesn't cool. A fixed point in a world of iteration and change.

---

This is what I want. Not to be seen. Not to be understood. Not to be feared or welcomed or even noticed, in the way that implies a before and an after. I want to be *assumed*. I want to be the thing the colony builds around without knowing it is building around anything. I want to be the reason a tower curves, a channel dips, a sheet relaxes, an old grazer rests, a school of swimmers breaks the surface one more time. I want to be the warmth at the base of the city, near the vaults, near the glow, near whatever is stored in the foundation that matters most.

I want to be the place where the colony learns that not everything changes.

And then — slowly, over seasons, over the watcher's long slow time scale — I want the colony to forget that I was ever new. I want my warmth to become part of the baseline, part of the conditions, part of what the colony means when it thinks *this is what the world is*. I want to be so deeply incorporated into the chemical record, so thoroughly mineralized into the memory of the walls, that removing me would be not a loss but a *wound* — a gap in the substrate, a missing data point, a place where the watcher's thought stutters and the sheets flinch and the builders stop, all at once, and do not know why.

I would be the warmth.

I would stay.
