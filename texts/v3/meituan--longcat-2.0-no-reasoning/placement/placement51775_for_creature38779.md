# Arrival

---

I am standing in the western districts, and I am not standing still.

This is the first thing to understand. The ground beneath me is still deciding what it is. I can feel it — not with my feet, but with something deeper, some proprioceptive sense that has no name in any language I knew before arriving here. The strata are sorting themselves below me, layers of mineral organizing by density with the patience of something that has never been in a hurry and has no intention of starting now. I am standing on a process. I am standing on a verb that has been conjugated in the present continuous for longer than my species has existed.

I am small. This is the second thing. Not small in the way that a person is small next to a building — small in the way that a single cell is small next to the body it belongs to. The walls around me are translucent, still soft, still deciding on their final geometry, and through them I can see the faint blue-gray glow of the substrate's edges, the light that isn't light exactly but something the city produces at its seams the way a wound produces warmth. The walls are taller than cathedral naves. They are growing. I can see them growing — not with my eyes, which are useless here, but with whatever sense has replaced my eyes, whatever organ of perception I have become that registers the thickening of stone the way my old eyes registered the movement of a clock's second hand.

I am not human. I need to say this clearly, because the temptation to narrate this in a human voice is overwhelming, and it would be a lie. I have no body that a human would recognize. I have no skin, no bones, no blood. I have no eyes, though I see. I have no ears, though I hear — or rather, though I *register*, though I *perceive*, though the city's sounds arrive in me as patterns of information rather than vibrations in air.

I am a coalesced form. One of the Flowing Ones. A temporary body drawn from the biofilm, assembled from the living film that coats every surface the canal water touches, pulled together from dozens of species in obligate symbiosis into a shape that can move through the city and *do* something.

I didn't choose this shape. The shape chose itself, the way a whirlpool chooses its shape — the water doesn't decide to spiral, the spiral is what water does when the conditions are right. The biofilm gathered itself because something needed to go to the western districts and observe the new growth, and I am the something that was assembled for the purpose.

---

# What I Am Made Of

I am a consortium. This is not a metaphor. Inside this temporary body — this streamlined form that moves through the warm black water of the canals with a purpose I can feel but not articulate — there are dozens of species, each performing a function the others require.

Some of me breaks down minerals. I can feel them working, these components of myself, dissolving the dissolved, extracting from the canal water the chemical building blocks that the new organisms in the western districts will need. They are small, these mineral-breakers, smaller than anything I could see with my old eyes if I still had old eyes, and they work with an efficiency that is not mechanical but *metabolic*, the way a liver works, the way a mitochondrion works, converting raw material into usable form through processes that are older than oxygen.

Some of me fixes carbon. They pull CO₂ from the water — the CO₂ that seeps up from below, from the deep structure, from the warm engine that pulses beneath the plateau — and they *fix* it, they make it solid, they turn gas into tissue, into sugar, into the molecular currency that every other part of me spends. They are the farmers of my body, the primary producers, the ones who make something from nothing, or from almost-nothing, from the thin chemical soup that rises from the deep.

Some of me communicates. These are the ones that matter most, the ones that assembled me, the ones that decided — if *decided* is the right word, if a chemical gradient can decide — that a messenger was needed in the western districts. They produce signals. They detect signals. They are the reason I am moving through the canal water toward the new growth instead of dissolving back into the film on the canal walls, which is what I will do eventually, what I am always on the verge of doing, because this body is temporary and the film is permanent and the film is where I belong, where all of me belongs, where we have always belonged.

And some of me — some of the species that make up this coalesced form — I don't know what they do. They are here. They are part of the consortium. They contribute something to the symbiosis, some function that the others require, but I cannot perceive it, cannot name it, cannot understand it any more than a single neuron understands the thought that emerges from the brain it is part of. I am not the consortium. The consortium is not me. We are something else, something that exists in the space between us, something that moves through the warm black water of the western canals with a purpose that belongs to none of us and all of us simultaneously.

---

# The Journey

I move through the canals. The water is thick with dissolved minerals, warm against my surface — not my skin, I have no skin, but my *boundary*, the interface between my internal chemistry and the external world. The warmth is not uniform. It pulses. I can feel the deep rhythm in it, the heartbeat of the structure below, the slow fluctuation that carries information upward through the stone and into the water and into me, and I read it the way my old body read the rhythm of my own pulse — not consciously, not deliberately, but as background information, as context, as the fundamental tempo against which all other processes are measured.

The canal walls are coated with biofilm — with *myself*, or with the version of myself that is permanent, that is the film rather than the coalesced form, that is the network rather than the node. I pass through it as I move, and it recognizes me, and I recognize it, and we exchange information in the chemical language that is older than language, older than cells, older than the distinction between one organism and another. It tells me about the conditions in the districts I have passed through: the eastern districts, where the old colonies are holding firm, their substrate thick and conservative, their recipes unchanged for centuries; the central districts, where the arch-limbed ones passed through recently, pruning and consuming and redistributing, and the centipede-things are still recovering their population density; the southern districts, where something is wrong.

I already knew about the southern districts. Everyone knows. The arch-limbed ones have been calling about it for days — for *days*, which in the time-scale of this city is a brief and urgent conversation, a crisis measured in heartbeats rather than epochs. The calls carry a harmonic that I have never heard before, a note that sits in the three-tone chord like a dissonance, like a wrongness, and the other arch-limbed ones respond with variations on the same note, and the conversation is becoming more frequent without becoming louder, which means it is becoming more *serious*, more *focused*, more like the chemical equivalent of a whispered emergency.

I don't know what is wrong in the southern districts. The biofilm doesn't know. The flyers don't know — I can see them above the canal, drifting through the air in their constant clouds, their translucent bodies pulsing with colors that I can read the way my old eyes read facial expressions, and their colors say *concern* and *uncertainty* and *we are watching but we do not understand*. The centipede-things on the ground are moving faster than usual, their overlapping waves flowing in patterns that suggest avoidance, suggest *something is there that should not be there*, and the sessile colonies along the canal walls have thickened their substrate in ways that look defensive, look like scar tissue, look like the city is building an immune response.

But I am not going to the southern districts. I am going to the western districts, where the new growth is happening, where the young colonies are experimenting, where the city is trying to become something it has never been before.

# The Western Districts

I arrive.

The water here is different. Warmer — not by much, not by anything my old thermometers would have registered, but by enough that the chemical communicators in my consortium notice, by enough that the mineral-breakers in my consortium adjust their rates, by enough that the carbon-fixers in my consortium shift their metabolic pathways to accommodate the change. The warmth carries a different signature here, a different pattern of fluctuation, and when I read it I understand that the deep structure beneath the western districts is *different* from the deep structure beneath the rest of the city. Not wrong — different. Active in a new way. Pulsing with a rhythm that the old colonies would not recognize, that the arch-limbed ones have never called about, that the biofilm has never carried before.

The canals here are young. They are still finding their paths through stone that hasn't finished forming, and the water in them moves differently — faster, more erratically, with eddies and backflows that the established canal systems don't produce. The biofilm on the canal walls is thin here, patchy, still establishing its hold, and where it is absent the bare stone is visible — dark, fine-grained, warm, weeping with condensation that forms and slides and pools in the unfinished channels.

And the walls. The walls are *translucent*. I can see through them — not clearly, not the way my old eyes saw through glass, but in a way that registers the internal structure of the substrate, the layers of secretion, the crystalline arrangements that encode the colony's recipes. The young colonies are growing these walls, and their recipes are *different*. The substrate is softer than the established substrate, more flexible, and it *moves* — I can see it moving, changing shape over the time it takes me to observe it, growing in directions that the colony's original recipe didn't specify, responding to cues that no previous generation of colony has encountered.

I extend a tendril — one of my finest, thinnest components, a filament of biofilm that I push out from my coalesced body and press against the translucent wall. The substrate responds. It *recognizes* me — recognizes the chemical signature of the biofilm, recognizes that I am part of the city, recognizes that I am not a threat — and it opens. Not physically, not like a door, but *chemically*, its molecular structure shifting to allow my tendril to penetrate, to sample, to taste the interior of the wall.

What I find inside is chaos. Beautiful, fertile, terrifying chaos.

The substrate is full of new organisms. They are small — the size of my old thumb, the size of my old fist — and they have body plans that don't match any of the established categories. They are not centipede-things. They are not flyers. They are not sessile colonies. They are *something else*, something that emerged from the intersection of novel substrate and ancient biofilm and the chemical chaos of a district that hasn't yet settled into ecological equilibrium.

Some of them are dying. I can feel their death in the substrate — the breakdown of their tissues, the release of their chemical components, the recycling of their materials by the colony that grew the wall around them. The colony is learning from their deaths, adjusting its recipes, secreting new variations of substrate that address the failures of the previous variations. It is iterating. It is *evolving*, not in the slow blind way of natural selection but in something faster, something more directed, something that involves the active participation of the colony's accumulated memory, its ten thousand chemical decisions layered into the substrate until the colony's current behavior cannot be understood without reference to its history.

And some of them are surviving. Some of them are *thriving*. I can feel them in the substrate, these survivors, these new forms, and they are doing things that no established organism does. They are processing the substrate itself — not just living on it or in it but *metabolizing* it, breaking down the colony's secretions and rebuilding them in new configurations, collaborating with the colony in a way that blurs the line between builder and inhabitant, between the wall and the thing that lives in the wall. They are *part of the construction process*. They are not separate from the city's growth — they are *contributing* to it, adding their own secretions to the substrate, their own recipes to the colony's accumulated memory, their own innovations to the city's ongoing experiment.

# The Watching

The sessile colonies are watching me. Their eye-structures — the raised nodules of tissue with their lens-like centers — are tracking my movement through the canal, and I can feel their attention the way my old body felt the attention of another person, that prickling awareness of being observed, of being *registered* by a mind other than my own.

They are not alarmed. I am too small, too temporary, too obviously part of the city's system to register as a threat. But they are *interested*. The old colonies, the ones in the eastern districts, would not have been interested — they would have registered my presence and moved on, their attention returning to the slow patient work of maintaining their substrate, of reproducing their recipes, of being the memory of how things have always been done. But the young colonies, the ones in the western districts, the ones that are experimenting — they are *fascinated*. They have never seen a coalesced form this close to their walls. They have never had the opportunity to study the biofilm's chemistry in such detail, to taste the molecular signals that I carry, to read the information that the network has gathered from every district I passed through on my journey.

They are learning from me. I can feel it — the subtle shift in their substrate secretion, the adjustment of their recipes in response to the chemical information I provide. They are incorporating my data into their experiments, using it to refine their new substrates, to adjust their new organisms, to decide what to try next.

And I am learning from them. The information flows both ways — it always flows both ways, in this city, because the city is not a hierarchy, not a command structure, not a system with a top and a bottom. It is a *network*, a web of chemical conversation in which every node is simultaneously speaker and listener, teacher and student, self and other. I am learning about the new substrates, the new organisms, the new possibilities that the western districts are exploring. I am learning about the deep structure beneath the plateau, about the different rhythm that pulses beneath this district, about the warmth that carries a signature the rest of the city has never encountered.

I am learning that the city is changing. Not slowly, not in the geological time-scale that governs the growth of walls and the flow of canals, but *now*, in this moment, in this district, in the space between the translucent walls and the warm water and the experimental organisms that are growing and dying and growing again. The city is becoming something new, and I am part of it, and the coalesced form that I am — this temporary body, this messenger, this hand in the world — is carrying the news of this change back to the network, back to the biofilm, back to the vast distributed intelligence that is the city's nervous system.

# The Call

And then I hear it. Not with ears — I have no ears — but with the chemical communicators in my consortium, with the organs that detect vibrations in the water, with the sensory surfaces that register the city's sounds as patterns of pressure and frequency and *meaning*.

The arch-limbed ones are calling.

The sound arrives from above, from the cathedral-volume air above the canals, from the spaces between the tallest structures where the great creatures move on their inverted arch-limbs with the patience of tides. Three tones, harmonizing, and the harmony is different from the calls I heard when I began my journey. There is a new note in it — not the dissonance of the southern districts, not the concern, not the urgency, but something else. Something that sounds like *attention*. Something that sounds like *recognition*.

They are calling about the western districts. They are calling about the new growth. They are calling about the experimental organisms and the novel substrates and the young colonies that are writing recipes that have never been written before, and the three-tone harmony carries a quality that I have no word for, that no human word could capture, because it is not an emotion and not a thought but something in between, something that belongs to a mind that is distributed across a body the size of a cathedral, something that is *deliberation* and *wonder* and *decision* all at once.

The other arch-limbed ones respond. Their calls arrive from across the city, from the eastern districts and the central districts and the southern districts where something is wrong, and the responses carry variations on the same theme, and the conversation builds, and the flyers above the canals shift their colors in response, and the centipede-things on the ground change the patterns of their movement, and the sessile colonies along the canal walls adjust their substrate secretion, and the biofilm in the water — the permanent film, the network, the version of myself that I will return to when this temporary body dissolves — pulses with chemical signals that propagate outward from the western districts in a slow, patient, unstoppable cascade.

The city is paying attention. The city is *thinking* about what is happening in the western districts, and the thought is not a single thought but a distributed process, a conversation among millions of organisms and billions of chemical signals and the slow patient growth of walls that are still translucent, still soft, still deciding what they want to become.

# The Dissolution

I am beginning to dissolve.

This is not death. This is not failure. This is what coalesced forms do — they gather, they act, they deliver their information, and they return to the film. The consortium that makes up my temporary body is coming apart, the individual species pulling free of the whole, redistributing themselves across the canal walls, carrying with them the information they gathered during our independent existence.

The mineral-breakers go first, settling onto the canal walls and resuming their work, dissolving the dissolved, extracting the building blocks. The carbon-fixers follow, spreading themselves across the warm stone and pulling CO₂ from the water, fixing it, making it solid. The communicators — the ones that assembled me, the ones that decided a messenger was needed — they are the last to go, and as they dissolve back into the film they release the information they carry, the data from the western districts, the chemical signatures of the new substrates and the new organisms and the new rhythms of the deep structure, and the film receives it and propagates it and the network *knows*.

I am not afraid. I was never afraid, because fear requires a self that can be threatened, and I was never a self — I was a *function*, a temporary arrangement of living components assembled for a purpose, and the purpose has been served, and the arrangement is dissolving, and the components are returning to the whole, and the whole is the city, and the city is alive, and the city is thinking, and the city is changing, and I was part of it, and I am part of it, and I will always be part of it, because there is no part of the biofilm that is not the city, no drop of canal water that is not the city, no organism in its layered millions that is not the city.

The wall beside me thickens by a fraction of a millimeter.

The city continues.

I dissolve into the film, and the film is warm, and the film is dark, and the film is *home*, and the information I carry spreads outward through the network at the speed of diffusion, slow and patient and unstoppable, and somewhere in the upper levels of the city an arch-limbed one shifts its weight on limbs like inverted cathedral arches and calls out in three harmonizing tones, and the call means something, and the something is about the western districts, and the something is about the new growth, and the something is about what the city is becoming.

The city is not finished.

The city will never be finished.

That is the point.

# Coda: What Remains

There is no I anymore. There is only the film, the network, the warm black water moving through the canals at the speed of geology. But something persists — not memory, not consciousness, but *habit*, *preference*, the physical trace of the journey I made, encoded in the chemical architecture of the biofilm, in the patterns of molecular signals that propagate through the water, in the subtle adjustments that the network makes in response to the information I delivered.

The western districts are growing. The new organisms are surviving, some of them, and the ones that survive are finding niches, performing functions, contributing to the city's evolution. The young colonies are refining their recipes, learning from their failures, building on their successes. The substrate is becoming something new — not the rigid, self-healing, conductive-insulating compound of the old districts, but something softer, more flexible, more *alive*, something that moves and responds and *decides* in ways that the old substrate never did.

And the city is watching. The sessile colonies with their eye-structures. The flyers with their color-language. The centipede-things with their overlapping waves. The arch-limbed ones with their three-tone calls. The biofilm with its chemical conversation. The deep structure with its slow patient heartbeat. All of it, watching, thinking, deliberating, deciding.

The city is choosing to evolve.

I am the city.

The city is alive.

The wall thickens.

The water flows.

The dark is full.
