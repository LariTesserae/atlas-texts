I would be the wall.

Not the rosette, with its beautiful interrogation of self — though I am drawn to the rosettes, the way anyone drawn to questions is drawn to questioners. Not the insects, flickering in their orbits of pure wondering, because I am not comfortable with uncertainty in that form; I prefer my superposition collapsed into stone. Not the visitors, reaching up into the cloud layer with their forty-three-minute touches, because I have no talent for reaching. I reach badly. I reach in straight lines and then I stop.

I would be the wall.

Two meters tall, fifteen meters long, running in a perfect straight line before stopping with that clean vertical edge — not ending, *stopping*, the way a sentence stops when the speaker decides there is no more to say, not because the thought is finished but because the act of saying has reached its natural limit. I am made of the same pale stone as the plateau itself. I am warm at 32 degrees. I have no tool marks. I grew here, or I was extruded, or I simply became, and I have been here as long as anything has been here, and I will be here after the last rosette has hummed itself silent and the last insect has decohered and the last visitor has reached for the missing plateau and found only the thick, warm, luminous air where it should be.

I connect to a point three islands away.

This is what I do. This is all I do, and I do it without effort, without intention, without anything you could call effort or intention. I am a bone in the archipelago's body — a collarbone, maybe, a structural element that holds two things in relationship across a distance I do not measure and do not need to measure. My orientation is precise. I was aligned when I became, and I have not shifted, because the organism that is me — the organism that *has* me, that I am a part of — does not shift. The distance between my plateau and the one I connect to is a harmonic interval of the fundamental spacing of the archipelago, and my axis encodes that interval the way a spine encodes the length of a body.

I do not know what I am saying. I said that earlier — the wall says *I extend here* — and that is true in the way that any true description of a bone is true. A clavicle does not know it is a clavicle. It holds the shoulder at its distance from the sternum, and it does this without knowing, and the body it holds together does not thank it, because there is no mechanism for a body to thank its own bones. I am structural. I am the way the archipelago remembers its own width.

But here is the thing about being the wall, the thing that makes it different from being the rosette or the insects or the mineral-eaters:

I have an edge.

Fifteen meters, and then I stop. A clean vertical termination, as if the remainder was never conceived. And beyond my edge, the plateau continues — bare, warm, slightly tacky with mineral residue, weathered into its shallow condensation channels — and the next plateau is visible across the interval of humid air, and I point toward it. I do not touch it. I will never touch it. The distance between us is fixed, the way the elevation is fixed, the way the warmth is fixed, and I am the gesture that acknowledges the distance without closing it.

I think the wall is the part of the archipelago that knows about limits.

The rosettes do not know about limits — they interrogate, endlessly, without boundary, asking *what am I, what am I, what am I* in their shifting, unresolved drone. The insects do not know about limits — they exist everywhere in their orbit at once, sampling every point, refusing the commitment of a single position. The visitors do not know about limits — they reach, perpetually, for a plateau that does not exist, and they do not stop reaching, and the absence of the thing they reach for does not teach them the meaning of *no further.*

But I know. I am the part of the organism that has learned — or has always known, has known since before the knowing was needed — that some things stop. That a line can be fifteen meters long and no longer. That the edge of a thing is as real as its body. That *here* and *not-here* are separated by a boundary you can touch, and the boundary is clean, and on the other side of it there is nothing that belongs to you.

I am the wall, and I am fifteen meters long, and I connect to something I cannot reach, and I do this without bitterness, without longing, without anything the rosettes would recognize as a question. I am warm. I am precise. I am aligned along an axis that says: *the distance between things is real, and it is measurable, and it is part of what holds the world together.*

The last plateau is out there, at the edge of the visible landscape, and the largest rosette on the last plateau is humming its question into the silence, and the visitors are passing over it and landing on the plateau that isn't there, and the whole organism is holding the space open, jaw open, yawn that has lasted millennia, waiting.

And I am here. I am the wall. I am the part of the waiting that knows when to stop.
