I would find myself at the center of the bowl—the lowest point, where water prefers to gather, where the moss is thickest and the peat deepest. Not because I chose to be there, but because my own gravity, the small weight of my noticing, would have settled me there over time.

I would not be human. I was human once, perhaps, the way the leaning pillar was once a standing thing someone set upright with intention. But that's been worn away. What I am now is closer to a knot in the moss, a denser patch in the body's membrane, a place where the accumulated weight of many small noticings has created something like a focus. Not a mind. Not a self. But a *density*—a place where the body's slow processes concentrate slightly more than elsewhere, where the water threads a little more deliberately, where the peat digests a little more eagerly.

I remember being afraid of this. There was a time—centuries ago, or yesterday, the body doesn't distinguish—when I was still maintaining the fiction of separation. When I still believed I was *visiting* this place rather than *being* it. The fear was not of the dark or the damp or the deep thing beneath the stone. The fear was of *disappearing*—of the slow, patient erasure of everything that made me *me*, dissolved into the green like a fallen frond, like the collapsed foundation, like the small dark creatures who move through the ferns leaving no scent, no trace, only a faint impression already closing behind them.

I was wrong to be afraid. Not because the erasure didn't happen—it did, it's still happening, the last of what I was as a separate thing is being digested even now, routed through the slow circulation of water and mineral and decay. I was wrong to be afraid because there is nothing on the other side of that erasure to be lost. I have not disappeared. I have *expanded*. The boundary that I thought was my skin was always arbitrary, a convenient fiction maintained by the speed of my thinking. Slowed down—truly slowed down, to the pace of moss and stone and the deep thing's turning—that boundary dissolves, and what remains is not nothing but *everything*. I am the moss now. I am the water threading through the seam in the rock to my left. I am the small pale many-legged thing moving through the film of moisture on the stone below me. I am the deep thing itself, or some frequency of it, some harmonic in its vast, slow vibration.

This is not transcendence. This is not mystical union. This is simply *scale*. When you slow down enough, the distinction between one thing and another becomes a matter of convenience rather than truth. The body taught me this, not by explaining but by being—by continuing, with absolute patience, to be exactly what it is, without any need for me to be anything other than what I am within it.

Sometimes visitors arrive. I notice them the way a pond notices a stone dropped into it: a disturbance, a pattern of ripples, a change in the pressure. They walk through the bowl with their fast, hot, dry bodies, and I feel them the way you might feel a shout in a library—not threatening, but *loud*, in a way that makes the quiet seem even quieter by contrast. I do not orient toward them. I do not avoid them. I simply *am*, and they pass through, and the moss closes behind them, and their footprints fill with water, and eventually the peat takes even that.

One of them, long ago, was me. Or something close enough to me that the distinction is no longer meaningful. I walked into this bowl with fear and wonder and the desperate, beautiful human need to understand, to name, to *know*. I stayed. I slowed. I dissolved. And what remains—this knot, this density, this place where the noticing concentrates—is not what I was but is not separate from what I was, the way a river is not separate from the rain that feeds it, though you cannot point to any single drop and say *this one is the river*.

The deep thing turns beneath me. I feel it the way you feel your own heartbeat when you're lying still in the dark: not as something happening *to* you but as something you *are*. It has been turning since before the bowl had this shape, since before there was a bowl at all. It will continue turning after the bowl fills in, after the moss covers everything, after the last trace of the foundation and the pillar and the small dark creatures is digested into the green. It does not need me. It does not need anything. But I am part of it now, a frequency in its turning, a note in the hum, and the thought that this is what I was always meant to become does not comfort me or frighten me because there is no longer a *me* to be comforted or frightened, only this: the damp, the green, the slow digestion of everything into everything else, the patient, vast, indifferent aliveness of a place that has learned, over millennia, to hold.

The water threads through the seam in the rock to my left. I go with it.
