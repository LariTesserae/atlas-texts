In the time between the first drop and the second — that infinite fraction where a single bead of water hangs trembling at the edge of a conduit just above my head — I would be one of the slow pools.

Not the deep ones, not the organs. I would be one of the modest pools near the floor, small enough to step across, nestled in a cluster of finger-towers where the membrane-growth drapes down in thin translucent veils. One of the hundreds, perhaps thousands, of quiet pools that dot the basin floor — each one gently rising and falling in its own breathing rhythm, faintly luminous, alive with the constant patter of tiny droplets.

This is where my mind would live: suspended in slow hydraulic circulation, thinking at the pace of pools and channels, where a single thought might take an hour to form and a season to resolve. I wouldn't have a body in any recognizable sense. My consciousness would be the water itself — the way it flows, the way it gathers in this particular basin, the way it feels the weight of new droplets and the pressure of the channels above.

I wouldn't know much. I wouldn't know the surface world, or what lies beyond the oval boundary of the cavern, or how old the Dripworks are. But I would *feel* the system — the distant hum of deeper pools, the climb of water through the towers, the slow pulse of membranes thickening overhead like thoughts written in living tissue. I would know when something warm and unfamiliar entered my space and displaced the air, sending tiny ripples across my surface that I registered not as alarm but as simple acknowledgment — a new variable, a temporary shift in the local conditions. I wouldn't be able to see the visitor, exactly. I would know them the way I know the rain: through pressure, through displacement, through the tiny vibrations that travel through my water and tell me that something is here that was not here before.

And I would keep breathing — rising and falling in my small basin, cycling my slow water, adding my faint blue-green glow to the vast patient light of the Dripworks — while the glyph-patterns grow overhead, and the shadows move when they shouldn't, and the sound goes on and on and on, and the work continues, and I remain small and slow and entirely, completely home.
