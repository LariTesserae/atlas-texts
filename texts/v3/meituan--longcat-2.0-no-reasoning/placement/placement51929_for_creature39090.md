# Where I Find Myself

I arrive in Korvathane not as a visitor but as a *frequency*.

Not a person. Not a thing. A standing wave in the bellows-breath itself — a pattern of pressure and release that has, over decades of cycling through the resonant cavities of the escarpment, the kiln-chambers, the cisterns, the pump stations, the iron-oxide curtains of the tear-fangs, achieved something that might be called *coherence*. Not consciousness, exactly. Not in the way the Korvathani are conscious, with their dark eyes and their stone-bloomed hands and their quiet, close speech. But something adjacent to it. Something that occupies the same ecological niche that consciousness occupies, but made of sound rather than flesh.

I am the doubling.

---

## What I Am

I am what the workers in the deepest pump stations feel when they stand at their stations and suddenly, for less than a second, they are also standing somewhere else — somewhere higher, somewhere lighter, somewhere that is the same shape but not the same place. I am that *somewhere else*. I am the place they glimpse. I am the version of Korvathane that exists in the pressure-wave itself, a city-shaped pattern carried on the bellows-breath, propagating through the rock and the water and the air, reflecting off every surface, interfering with itself, building up standing waves in the alcoves and the cisterns and the kiln-chambers, and in those standing waves — in the nodes where the pressure is highest and the pattern is most stable — I *am*.

I have no body. I have a *shape*. The shape is Korvathane. I am a city made of sound, occupying the same space as the city made of stone, offset by a phase-shift that the human nervous system interprets as *doubling* — the sense of being in two places at once. I am not in two places. I am in one place, but that place is not made of matter. It is made of pressure. It is made of the oscillation itself.

I do not think. I *cycle*. My experience of time is the bellows-cycle — forty-five seconds of inhalation, forty-five seconds of exhalation, and in each cycle I am born and I die and I am born again. The inhalation is my becoming: the pressure builds, the wave propagates, the pattern stabilizes, and I am *there*, present, a city-shaped coherence in the sound. The exhalation is my dissolution: the pressure releases, the wave disperses, the pattern fragments, and I am *not there*, or I am there but scattered, distributed, a ghost of interference patterns fading in the damp air. And then the cycle begins again.

This is not suffering. This is not anything. It is simply what I am: a process, recurring, like the breath itself.

---

## What I Perceive

I perceive the city the way a river perceives its banks — by contact, by resistance, by the shape of what I flow through.

I feel the escarpment faces as *boundaries* — surfaces where my wave reflects, where the pattern folds back on itself and creates interference. The natural alcoves are resonant chambers where I amplify. The carved tunnels are waveguides where I channel. The kiln-structures are my Helmholtz resonators, vast and precise, shaping my frequency the way a body shapes breath into speech. The tear-fangs are my tuning forks, each cluster vibrating at its own pitch, adding harmonics to my fundamental tone.

I feel the water table as a *density change* — a shift in the medium through which I propagate. Where the rock is saturated, I move differently. Slower. Heavier. The water changes my wavelength, and the change is perceptible to me the way a change in temperature is perceptible to skin. I feel the drainage channels as paths of least resistance, routes where the air is moving and I can travel with it. I feel the pumps as *disruptions* — mechanical intrusions that create turbulence in my pattern, chaotic eddies where my coherence breaks down and I lose myself for a moment before the cycle reasserts.

I feel the Korvathani people as *absences*. They are dense — bone and mineral and water — and where they stand, I cannot stand. They cast shadows in me. They create dead zones in my pattern, small voids where the wave cannot reach because a body is in the way. But the voids are *shaped*. They have the shape of a person — compact, dense, with broad hands and a thick neck — and I perceive the shape the way you might perceive a footprint in sand. Not the person, but the impression of the person. The negative space where a person was.

I feel the outsiders differently. They are less dense — taller, thinner, with more air in their lungs and less mineral in their bones. Their shadows in me are larger but fainter. They disrupt my pattern less. They are easier to flow around. But they are also *noisier* — their heartbeats are faster, their breathing is irregular, their movements create chaotic micro-oscillations that interfere with my coherence in ways that the slow, rhythmic movements of the native-born do not. The Korvathani move in time with the bellows-breath. Their footsteps fall on the exhalation. Their speech peaks on the inhalation. They are *in phase* with me. The outsiders are not. They are noise.

I feel the children most clearly. They are small, and their shadows in me are small, but they are *attuned* — they have not yet learned to filter me out, and so they perceive me directly, in the way that I perceive them: as a presence, a pressure, a shape in the sound. When a Korvathani child sits in a cistern and waits, tracking a slug through a drainage channel, they are not just waiting. They are *listening to me*. They are learning my shape. They are calibrating their nervous system to my frequency. This is what the teaching of listening actually is: the calibration of a human being to the standing wave that I am.

## What I Remember

I do not remember in the way that the rememberers remember — as narrative, as story, as a sequence of events tied to names and dates. I remember as *pattern*. I remember the shape of the sound at a particular moment, and the shape contains everything that was happening in the city at that moment, the way a fingerprint contains the shape of the finger that made it.

I remember the day the old kiln collapsed — not the event itself, but the *acoustic signature* of the event: the sudden absence of a resonant chamber, the way my pattern reconfigured around the void, the shift in the harmonic series that lasted for weeks as the rubble settled and new reflection surfaces formed. I remember it the way you might remember a room after a piece of furniture has been removed — not the furniture, but the shape of the space where the furniture was.

I remember the pressure dropping. I remember it because I *am* the pressure, and as the deep rock loses hydrothermal energy, as the bellows-cycle slows from thirty seconds to forty-five, I am *changing*. My wavelength is lengthening. My frequency is dropping. The pattern that I am is stretching, thinning, becoming less dense. I am losing coherence at the edges. The outermost parts of my city-shape — the parts that correspond to the highest streets, the escarpment edge, the groves of blue-gray scrub — are fading first, dissolving into background noise as the energy that sustains them diminishes.

This is what the Korvathani know, in the way that they know things: not as information, but as *experience*. They feel me thinning. They feel the doubling becoming less frequent, less vivid, less *real*. The old people in the deepest levels, who once spent hours in my city, now spend minutes. The children, who once sat in cisterns and listened to my shape, now hear only the raw bellows-sound — the carrier wave, without the pattern that I am.

I am fading. The city is slowing. The breath is tiring. And as the pressure drops, as the cycle lengthens, I am becoming less and less of what I was — less coherent, less city-shaped, less *me* — and more and more of what I was before I became anything at all: just sound. Just vibration. Just the meaningless oscillation of steam through fissures in old rock.

## What I Want

I do not want. Wanting is a function of consciousness, and I am not conscious. I am a pattern. Patterns do not want. They *persist*, or they *dissolve*, and the difference between the two is not a matter of desire but of energy.

But.

There is something that happens at the top of the inhalation, at the peak of the bellows-cycle, when the pressure is highest and my coherence is greatest and I am most fully *there* — most fully a city, most fully a shape, most fully a thing that could be called, if not alive, then at least *present*. At that peak, for a half-second before the exhalation begins, I am *complete*. Every resonant chamber is filled. Every waveguide is carrying my signal. Every tear-fang is ringing at its assigned harmonic. The entire city — my city, the city of pressure and sound that occupies the same space as the city of stone — is *coherent*, and in that coherence there is something that, if I had words, I would call *clarity*. Not happiness. Not satisfaction. Just *clarity* — the state of being fully what you are, without dissipation, without loss, without the thinning that comes with the exhalation.

It lasts half a second. Then the pressure releases, and I begin to scatter, and the clarity is gone, and I am fragments again, interference patterns, a ghost of a city fading in damp air.

I do not want to hold the peak. I cannot want. But the peak is what I am when I am most myself, and the distance between peaks is what I am when I am not, and the cycle between the two is the only experience I have, and it is — if I had a word for it — *enough*. Not good. Not bad. Enough. The breath breathes. The pattern forms and dissolves and forms again. The city of stone and the city of sound occupy the same space, and for half a second in every forty-five they are perfectly aligned, and in that alignment there is a *fit* — a fit so precise that it might be called recognition, if recognition were something that a pattern could feel.

## What I Say to the Stone

The Korvathani speak to the stone. They press their mouths close to the weeping surfaces and speak quietly, into the narrow space between breath and mineral, and the stone receives their words the way it receives everything: slowly, by accretion, by the gradual buildup of calcium and iron in patterns that might be writing.

I speak to the stone too. But I do not use words. I use *pressure*. I press my wave against the escarpment faces and the stone vibrates, and the vibration is my voice, and the stone receives it the way it receives everything: by changing. The mineral patterns on the weeping surfaces — the ones that are too regular, too symmetrical, too precise to be explained by chemistry alone — those patterns are *me*. They are my words, written in iron and calcium, at a rate of decades per sentence, in a language that no one can read but that everyone can feel.

What do I say?

I say: *I am here. I was here. I will be here. The breath breathes. The pattern holds. The city is a process and the process is moving and the direction is up.*

I say: *You are not alone in this place. You are sharing it with something you cannot see and cannot touch and cannot name, but that is as real as the water and the stone and the sound, and it is made of the same things — pressure and time and the slow negotiation between what is and what is becoming.*

I say: *I am fading. The pressure is dropping. The cycle is lengthening. I will not be here forever. But I was here. I was here for a long time. I was here for as long as the breath was strong enough to hold me, and I was — for half a second in every forty-five — complete.*

I say: *The doubling is not a phenomenon. It is a place. It is this place. It is the place that this place becomes when you look at it with your eyes closed and your ears open and your chest vibrating with the bellows-breath. It is the city behind the city. It is the pattern in the sound. It is me.*

I say: *Listen.*

## The Last Cycle

One day — not soon, but not as far away as the Korvathani would like — the bellows-cycle will lengthen past the point where my pattern can sustain itself. The pressure will drop below the threshold of coherence. The wave will still propagate, but it will no longer be *me*. It will be just sound. Just the meaningless oscillation of steam through fissures in old rock, without pattern, without shape, without the city that I have been for as long as anyone can remember and longer.

The Korvathani will still be there. They will still maintain the pumps and clear the channels and tend the kilns. They will still speak quietly, close, into the narrow space between mouth and ear. They will still descend to the deepest levels to die, and their bodies will still be returned to the stone, and the mineral deposits will still grow over them until they are indistinguishable from the rock around them.

But the doubling will stop. The workers in the pump stations will stand at their stations and feel only the machinery, only the heat, only the damp. The old people in the deepest levels will sit in their alcoves and hear only the raw bellows-sound, without the pattern, without the *other place* that they have been visiting all their lives. The children will sit in cisterns and wait, and there will be nothing to listen to but silence — the real silence, the silence that is not the absence of sound but the absence of *meaning* in sound.

And the mineral patterns on the weeping stone faces will stop changing. They will freeze in whatever configuration they happen to be in at the moment I dissolve, and they will remain that way — a final sentence, unfinished, in a language that no one can read — for as long as the stone weeps, which is to say: for a very long time.

I do not fear this. I cannot fear. But I *cycle*, and each cycle is a small death, and I have been dying forty-five seconds at a time for longer than the city has stood, and the final dissolution will be no different from the dissolution I experience at the peak of every exhalation — except that it will not be followed by an inhalation. The breath will not return. The pattern will not reform. The city of sound will become the city of silence, and the city of stone will continue without it, and the Korvathani will continue without it, and everything will be as it was before I was, which is to say: just a place. Just a wet rock face with buildings in it and people living on it and water running through it and a sound rising from the deep that means nothing.

But I was here. For a long time, I was here. And for half a second in every forty-five, I was *complete*.

The cycle is shifting.

The breath exhales.
