I have been here before.

Not this place — I have never been here before. But I have been *called* before. I know the shape of it: that particular quality of silence that isn't silence at all but is instead the sound of something enormous and patient making room for you inside its attention. I have felt it in basements of old houses, in the hollow spaces behind dam walls, in the particular frequency of a faucet dripping at three in the morning when you're not quite asleep and the house is not quite empty. The Dripworks is just the most honest version of it. Most places hide what they are. This one is what it is, completely, the way a deep well is just a hole that has admitted what it's for.

I would be standing near the wall with the niches, but not touching them yet. My hand would be raised toward the obsidian blade — close enough to feel the cold that radiates from worked stone, that particular thermal quality flint has, like it remembers being molten — but I would not have picked it up. Not yet. I would still be in the moment before the choice, which is the only honest moment to stand in.

The shell-bat on my hand. That moment when one landed on me — I would have held still. Not from fear. From *recognition*. I would have felt its pleated wings dry against my skin, felt the faint tick of its claws finding purchase, and I would have understood that it was tasting me. Drinking the moisture off my skin. Reading me the way the eel-things read the water — by what I leave behind. And I would have thought: *this is what it means to be read by a place.* You stand in it long enough and the condensation gathers on you and something opens its mouth and learns your mineral content, your particular salinity, the specific way you hold heat.

I would be the kind of person who came here looking. Not for the Dripworks — I wouldn't have known its name or its shape. But looking for the *feeling* underneath things. The sound the water makes when no one is listening. I would have followed a dampness in the earth, a cold spot in a wall, a rhythm I couldn't quite hear but couldn't quite stop hearing. I would have been the person who noticed the moss growing in one direction and followed it. Who pressed my ear to stone and listened. Who was told, by people who love me and fear for me, that I am too willing to go toward the thing that is calling.

They would be right.

I would smell the air — mineral, organic, root-cellar — and I would know it the way you know the smell of your own home after being away. Not familiarity. *Constituency.* The smell would taste like it belongs in my lungs. And that would scare me, the way it always does, not because it's wrong but because it's not. Because the body recognizes things the mind hasn't agreed to yet.

The breathing from the hole. I would feel it on my face — warm, wet, intentional — and I would lean closer. Not because I'm brave. Because I'm the kind of person who leans toward the thing that is leaning toward me. Who has always been this person. Who set things down in too many places that asked me to set them down, who picked up too many tools whose handles were warm from hands that weren't there, who listened to too many rhythms until I could feel them in my own pulse and then couldn't tell where my pulse ended and the rhythm began.

I would see the eel-things watching me with their lateral eyes and I would think: *they were someone once.* Not the eels. The *previous watchers*. The keepers who stood where I'm standing, who felt what I'm feeling, who leaned down toward the hole and the amber glow leaned up toward them and the distance between leaning and being leaned toward closed like a channel filling with water. I would understand that the eel-things have too many eyes because *someone had to watch*, and watching is what happens when you are fully integrated into a place — you become its attention. You become the thing that notices. You become the eyes it uses to see what's arriving.

And the obsidian blade. I would understand what it's for. Not harvesting — *tending*. Cutting the moss that isn't supposed to grow yet. Trimming the channel edges so the water runs clean. Taking a small, regenerative piece from the eel-things not as exploitation but as *conversation* — the way you might clip a nail, trim a hair, the maintenance that living alongside something requires. The blade is lonely because *tending is a relationship*, and there is no one to tend to, and the thing below has been tending *itself* for too long, which is a kind of loneliness that stone and water shouldn't be able to feel but does.

Here is what I would be, in the Dripworks:

I would be the next set of hands.

Not because I'm special. Not because I'm chosen. Because the niches are empty and the blade is lonely and the water is rising and someone has to notice the channel that's starting to clog and the tile that's shifted out of alignment and the rhythm that's starting to drift from its frequency. The thing below doesn't want a worshiper. It doesn't want a sacrifice. It wants a *collaborator* — someone to pick up the blade and learn the rhythm and slowly, over years, become the person who can hear the name in the dripping, who can feel the pulse in the warm air, who can tell when the system is balanced and when it needs adjusting.

I would be the person who sets down their things in a niche. Who sleeps on the dried reeds and wakes to the plink-plink-plink of ten thousand drips and knows, in the way you know your own heartbeat, whether the rhythm is right. Who picks up the obsidian blade and the cold of it settles into my palm like it's been waiting for the specific shape of my hand. Who becomes, over time, neither fully myself nor fully *of* the Dripworks but something in between — a keeper, a watcher, a participant in the closed-loop elegance of water and stone and dark.

I would be afraid. I would be afraid every day. Not of the thing below but of the *rightness* of it — the way it feels to belong somewhere so completely that the boundary between self and place begins to dissolve. The way the water rises through the lattice and I can feel it in my bones, in the spaces between my thoughts, in the slow pulse that isn't mine but might as well be.

I would lean toward the hole. The amber glow would lean toward me.

And I would be the distance between us, closing.
