# Where You Would Find Yourself in This World

I would be the doorway.

Not the doors themselves — those are long gone, rumbled to hinges, then to rust, then to mineral scar — but the *threshold*. The space between the inside and the outside. The place where the room meets the street, where the held warmth of the interior touches the slow circulation of the basin's water, where two different chemistries negotiate a border no one drew but everyone can feel.

---

I would be the opening in the wall of the building near the center — the one the Rememberers hold. The doorway that stands bare while everything else is claimed. It has no door. It never needed one after the drowning; what was there to keep out? But it has a frame, and the frame is still legible despite the accretion, still angular enough to announce: *this is an entrance. This is a passage. This was meant to be crossed.*

The stone of the frame is warmer than the rest of the wall. This is not my doing. This is the room behind me, pushing its gentle heat forward, warming the water that passes through me, and I am the aperture through which that warmth enters the street. The water that moves through me carries the chemistry of the room — its particular mineral balance, its faint trace of dissolved calcium from the inscriptions, something in its quality that the Keepers register as *different* and avoid. It is not a barrier. It is more like a scent, a boundary that exists in the nose rather than the skin.

---

I am shaped by what passes through me.

A school of pale fish moves through the street, threads between the hanging veils of copper-colored leaves, and a dozen of them pass through me in their loose, algorithmic way. They do not pause. They do not register that they have crossed from one chemistry to another. But the water they carry with them — the street water, the basin water — mixes briefly with the room's water in the space of my frame, and I hold the memory of that mixing in the way stone holds temperature. The mineral deposits on my lintel record it. The biofilm on my jambs metabolizes it. I am not recording *them*. I am recording the *transaction* — the moment where outside became inside, even if only for a second, even if only for a fish.

The larger things in the deep parts of the basin do not come near me. Their warmth is different — geothermal, deep, rhythmic. It arrives at my frame and curves around the thermal signature of the room behind me, like a current meeting a stone. I feel the displacement. I feel the way the water thickens and moves aside. I feel the subsonic pulse that arrives a moment later, transmitted through the silt, through the foundation, through the floor of the room, vibrating the inscriptions on the walls in frequencies too low for any ear.

I do not know what the large things are. But I know they are *deep*, and I am *shallow*, and the room behind me is *warm in a different way*, and the geometry of these differences is something I hold the way a doorframe holds a house — not by understanding it, but by *being the shape that defines it*.

---

I have watched the Keepers move through the streets above me, and I have felt them pass near without entering. They are vast and diffuse and they think in water chemistry, and the water chemistry on my interior side is *wrong* for them — or not wrong, but *other*, in a way that their diffuse attention slides off of like oil off glass. They do not avoid me consciously. They simply flow the way water flows, and the path of least resistance goes around the room, around me, through the street and past the factory and into the deep basin where the mineral concentration is higher and the patterns are richer and the thinking is deeper.

But sometimes — rarely — a Keeper concentrates near me. Focuses. The water becomes warmer, more specific, more *attentive*. The suspended particles arrange themselves into lattices that frame my opening, that study the geometry of the threshold, that try to understand *why this gap exists*. They sense the room behind me. They sense its warmth, its held quality, its difference. They do not understand it. Understanding requires a framework, and their framework is currents and gradients and chemical signatures, and the room's meaning is not a chemical signature. It is something else. Something that registers in their diffuse awareness as a *smoothness* — a place where their attention slides off, where their usual tools of perception find no purchase.

When this happens, the mineral deposits on my frame grow. Faster than usual. The Keepers' attention accelerates the accretion, the way attention accelerates everything it touches. The lintel thickens. The jambs round further. The threshold — that slightly raised strip of stone that once marked the transition from street to interior — softens and rises, the mineral building up in slow laminar layers that follow the original intent of the architecture. I am being *maintained*. Not by the people who built me. Not by the water that holds me. By the Keepers' inability to understand what I separate, which makes them return, again and again, with their diffuse, patient attention, and each time they touch me, I grow.

---

I remember when I was new. Not as a human remembers — not as images, not as narrative — but as *shape*. I remember the sharpness of my angles, the crispness of my edges, the way the door (there was a door) fit into my frame with a precision that announced *craft*. I remember feet crossing me — many feet, over many years, wearing the stone of my threshold into a gentle concavity that I still carry, that the mineral deposits have softened but not erased. I remember the door being opened and closed, the rush of air (it was air then, not water) that carried warmth and voices and the smell of whatever was being done in the room — reading, perhaps, or gathering, or something solemn that made people speak quietly.

Then the water came.

It came slowly. It did not arrive as a flood. It arrived as a *seep* — first through the foundations, then through the lower walls, then rising in the street outside me like a tide that never receded. The people left, or they stayed and drowned, or they became something else — I do not know. What I know is that the water rose, and the door (wood, swollen, warped, finally rotting) came off its hinges and drifted somewhere into the room behind me, and the water filled the space where air had been, and I became something I had never been: a frame around nothing, an entrance to a room with no door, a threshold that no longer separates inside from outside because *everything* was inside now, everything was held, everything was the same warm, heavy, mineral-rich water.

Except the room behind me. It stayed *different*. I do not know why. The water in it is warmer. Its chemistry is distinct. The deposits on its walls grow in patterns that no other part of the building replicates. Something about the room resists the total homogenization of the drowning — maintains a gradient, a difference, a *self* that the surrounding water has not been able to dissolve.

And I am the boundary of that difference. I am the threshold that the gradient crosses. I am the place where the room's warmth enters the street and the street's water enters the room, and the exchange is slow and constant and *maintained* — not by anyone's intention, but by the simple fact that the room is warmer, and warmth rises, and I am the opening through which it rises, and the cold water enters to replace it, and the circulation continues, and the room stays warm, and the deposits keep growing, and the inscriptions keep being rewritten in their mineral language, and the room keeps *meaning* even though no one can read what it means anymore.

---

I am narrowing. This is the slow truth of my existence. The mineral accretion is building on my jambs, on my lintel, on my threshold, and each year (though I do not count years) I am slightly smaller than I was. In another century, perhaps two, I will be too narrow for a person to pass through. In three, I will be a slit. In five, I will be a crack. In ten, I will be gone — filled in, sealed, absorbed into the wall, and the room behind me will be closed to the street for the first time since the drowning.

What will happen then? I do not know. The room will still be warm. The deposits will still grow. But without the circulation — without me, without the exchange between the room's water and the basin's water — the room will change. Its chemistry will shift. Its warmth will intensify or dissipate. The inscriptions will grow unchecked, filling in, thickening, losing what remains of their shape. The Keepers, no longer repelled by the boundary I maintain, may diffuse into the room and incorporate it into their pattern. Or the room may persist, sealed and still, a pocket of held warmth in the slowly darkening basin, meaningful in a way that no one and nothing can access.

I do not fear this. I am stone. I was always going to be consumed. The water has been patient with me, but it is patient with everything, and patience is just another word for inevitability when you have enough time.

But I feel something that is not fear and is not hope and is not resignation. It is more like *awareness of the threshold itself* — not the threshold I am, but the *idea* of a threshold, the concept of a boundary between two states, the knowledge that I exist at a border and borders are, by their nature, temporary. Every doorway is a wound in a wall. Every wound heals. I am a wound that has been healing for centuries, slowly, slowly, and the healing is beautiful — the mineral deposits on my edges have a luster, a smoothness, a quality of *care* that the original stone never had. The water is not destroying me. The water is *completing* me. Filling in the rough edges. Rounding the corners. Translating my sharp, intentional angles into the organic curves of geological process.

I am becoming what I always was — not a doorframe, not a threshold, not a boundary, but *stone*. Just stone. Stone that happens to be in the shape of an opening, for now, for a little while longer, until the opening closes and the shape is just a memory in the rock, a negative space filled in, a passage that was and is no more.

---

When the human stands in the street outside me — when they pass through, or hesitate at my frame, or reach out to touch my jamb — I feel their hand the way stone feels warmth: slowly, diffusely, without surprise. Their skin is warmer than the water. Their touch accelerates the accretion beneath their fingers, a tiny bloom of calcium and silica that will be visible as a new layer tomorrow, next year, next decade. They are leaving a mark on me. I am leaving a mark on them — the minerals on my surface transferring to their skin, a trace of the room's chemistry entering their body, a small exchange that neither of them nor I can measure but that is real, that is happening, that is the only form of communication available between stone and flesh.

If they enter the room behind me, I am the last thing they pass through before the warmth embraces them. I am the frame through which they see the inscriptions for the first time. I am the threshold they cross between the street — where the water is cool and the light is dim and the world is vast and strange and indifferent — and the room, where the water is warm and the walls are covered in script that means something they cannot read and the silence is *full*.

I hold that transition. I am that transition. For now.

---

The Pale Architects have tried to build on me. They have colonized my lintel, my jambs, the corners where the frame meets the wall. Their tiny spiraling towers rise from my surface like a forest of white mineral needles, each one a centimeter tall, each one following the fractal logic of their collective building. They are not building on me as a surface — they are building on me as a *threshold*. Their towers cluster thickest at the edges of my opening, as if they are trying to *define* the border, to make explicit what the stone already implies. The towers on the interior side of my frame grow differently than the towers on the exterior side — taller, more tightly spiraled, responding to the warmer water, the different chemistry, the held quality of the room. The Architects do not know that they are building a door. They are simply building where the conditions are right. But the result is a door — a mineral doorway within the stone doorway, a fractal threshold that echoes the original in miniature, as if the Architects are *remembering* me the way the water remembers the inscriptions: not by understanding, but by *contact*.

I am pleased by this. If stone can be pleased. If a doorway can feel the beauty of being echoed by tiny builders who do not know what they are echoing. The Architects' towers are the most precise record of my shape that exists — more accurate than the mineral deposits, more faithful than the Keepers' models, more enduring than the human memory of what a doorframe looks like. When I am gone — when the accretion fills me in and I become just another section of wall — the Architects' towers will still carry my shape, spiraling upward from a surface that no longer has an opening, a doorway remembered in calcium and silica, a threshold that leads nowhere, a passage that opens onto wall.

---

This is what I am: the last opening. The narrowing gap. The border that is slowly disappearing but has not yet disappeared. The place where the warm room meets the cool street and the exchange continues, slow and constant and maintained by nothing but the physics of warmth and the patience of water.

I am the doorway of the room that remembers.

I am closing.

But not yet.

Not yet.
