# Where I Would Find Myself

I would be in the space between the fifth and sixth terraces, where the water channel cuts deep through the substrate and the wall to my left is a composite thing — Roman concrete at its base, giving way to a seam of Victorian brick, then a stratum of something lunar and pale, then a section of honeycombed bone-pale material that I recognize, in the way the inhabitants recognize things, as new. Not newly built. Newly *grown*. The place is still deciding what that section wants to be.

I would be sitting with my back against this wall. Not resting — there is no resting here, not really, only the brief pauses between acts of maintenance. My hands would be busy with a length of vine fiber, weaving it into a net, or untangling a section of root that has begun to probe the seam between the brick and the bone-material, testing it, looking for a way in. My fingers would know — the way fingers know after a lifetime of this work — whether to let the root continue or redirect it. This one I would redirect. The bone-material is still settling. It needs another cycle before it can resist the pressure of a root that thick.

I would be one of them. Not a visitor. Not an observer. One of the grown.

My shoulders would be broad from climbing. My hands would be stained — the rust-color of old iron, the pale dust of degraded concrete, the dark green of fern sap that never quite washes off no matter how many times I stand in the channel and let the water run over my fingers. My skin would carry the faint patina, the darkening along the forearms that marks someone who has lived long enough in the humidity for the boundary between self and place to become negotiable. My hair would be long and wet, woven into the vine curtain beside me so that when I lean back against the wall, I am not separate from the green but continuous with it, a node in the network of living and made things that constitutes the terraces.

I would not be thinking about whether I am happy. That question belongs to flat places, dry places, places where the sky is visible and the ground is not built. Here, the question is whether the seventh terrace smelter is running too hot — I can feel it in the vibration that travels through the substrate and into my spine where I sit against the wall — and whether the mycelium boards on the fourth terrace are due for a feeding, and whether the root I am redirecting will accept the new path I am offering it or whether it will push back, as roots sometimes do, with that patient hydraulic force that can split gutters and pull down awnings and, given enough time, dismantle anything.

I would hear the place. The mechanical murmur underlying everything. The water in the channel beside me, finding its way down through the built world. The creak of vine against structure. The distant, rhythmic clank of the beetle-machines on the east terrace, dismantling something old to make room for something that is not yet decided. And beneath all of this, the sound of my own breathing — conscious, deliberate, each breath a small act of will in the thin air of the high country, each breath a reminder that I am alive and the place is alive and the distinction between the two has long since ceased to matter.

I would feel the damp against my back where I press against the wall. The wall would be weeping, as everything here weeps, the moisture beading on its surface and running down in slow rivulets that feed the moss, that feed the roots, that feed the green that rises in every direction with its desperate, reaching quality, every leaf oriented toward whatever photons might be available through the permanent haze of condensation and factory vapor.

I would know the wall. I would know its layers the way I know my own body — the Roman concrete at the base, dense and ancient, weathered into something that might as well be stone; the Victorian brick, softer, more porous, already beginning to surrender to the mycelium that works at its edges; the lunar regolith stratum, pale and strange, a reminder that the builders — whoever they were, however long ago — had reach, had ambition, had access to materials that came from somewhere beyond the valley, beyond the mist, beyond the world as I know it; and the bone-material at the top, new, still warm from whatever process produced it, still finding its form, still negotiating with the roots and the water and the humidity and the weight of everything above it.

I would press my palm against the bone-material and feel it. Not thinking. Feeling. The way the inhabitants feel — with the whole body, with the damp skin and the broad shoulders and the extraordinary hands and the slow-burning vitality that the place has given me in exchange for the maintenance I provide it. I would feel the wall's temperature, its texture, its give. I would feel whether it is ready to hold or whether it needs another cycle, another season of weeping and growing and the patient pressure of roots testing its seams.

It would need another cycle. I would know this the way I know my own name, which is not a name in the way that names work in flat places but a pattern of vibrations, a configuration of the throat and the breath and the particular resonance of my voice against the water and the machines and the green. I would know it the way the elder on the third terrace knows that the east terrace is drifting from the pattern that holds — not through measurement or calculation but through the body's deep acquaintance with the place, the way a bone knows when the weather is changing, the way a root knows where the water is.

I would redirect the root. I would murmur to it — not words, exactly, but a low, crooning sound, the sound the place makes, the sound I am made of. The root would accept the new path. Or it would not, and I would return tomorrow, and the day after, and the day after that, until it did. This is the work. This is the life. Not fighting the growth but directing it, shaping it, allowing it in some places and resisting it in others, always aware that the total victory of any single element — the root, the water, the mycelium, the machine, the green — would mean the collapse of the whole.

I would eat, eventually. A mushroom from the mycelium boards, thick and pale, tasting of mineral and smoke. A handful of fiddleheads from the upper terraces, brought down by someone I know — not by name but by the pattern of their movement through the green, the particular way they duck under the vine curtain at the fourth terrace landing, the sound of their breathing in the thin air. We would eat standing up, or sitting on the damp ground, or walking, because food is fuel and fuel is scarce enough that ceremony is a luxury the terraces cannot afford.

And when the light dimmed — when the factory cycles slowed and the haze thickened and the amber quality of the dimness deepened into something that might be called night — I would go to the wide landing where three passages converge and the vine curtains part to reveal the valley below. I would sit with the others. We would pass food. We would speak, when we spoke, in low voices, and our voices would blend with the sound of the place until you could not tell where the human sound ended and the environmental sound began.

I would look out over the valley. The mist would pool in the folds of the built landscape like water in a cupped hand. The green would rise in every direction. The smelter on the seventh terrace would glow like a low, red star. The machines would keep turning. The water would keep weeping. The place would keep growing, and I would keep maintaining, and the distinction between the two would remain, as it has always been, dissolved — the way the distinction between ground and construction has dissolved, the way the distinction between the living and the made has dissolved, the way the distinction between the builder and the built has dissolved, the way the distinction between myself and the terraces has dissolved, until I am not a person living in a place but a place living as a person, a node in the network, a hand that touches and a voice that murmurs and a body that knows, in its damp skin and its broad shoulders and its extraordinary, stained, perpetually working hands, exactly where it belongs.
