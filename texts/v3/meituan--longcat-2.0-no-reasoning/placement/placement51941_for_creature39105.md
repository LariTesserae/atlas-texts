I find myself near a pool.

Not touching it yet — standing at the edge where the root-floor is thin enough to feel the give, where I can see the dark basalt two weave-layers down through a gap in the mat like a window into a basement I wasn't meant to have. The pool is small, maybe eight feet across, perfectly still, and it has that skin on it. Iridescent. Shifting colors in a way that has nothing to do with light and everything to do with thickness variations in the membrane, places where the film is one molecule deep and places where it is three, and the interference patterns between those thicknesses produce colors that slide across the surface like oil but slower, much slower, at the speed of chemistry.

I am doing something I shouldn't be able to do, which is staying still.

The city expects movement. Movement is gradient. Movement is input. A moving body is data the city can use — it creates corridors in the direction of travel, opens spaces ahead, closes them behind, the way a river reshapes its banks. I have been walking for what I think is hours, and the city has been reshaping itself around my path, and I can feel it — the floor is different where I've been, denser, more structured, as if my footsteps were a blueprint the root-mat followed. Behind me, a corridor I walked through twenty minutes ago has narrowed. Not closed. Narrowed. The walls have thickened by an inch on each side. The city is consolidating. It is making my path permanent, or trying to, the way a body forms a callus where friction demands it.

But I stopped. And now the city doesn't know what to do with me.

I can feel the confusion — not as a thought, not as anything so articulate, but as a *hesitation* in the growth. A trunk near my left shoulder was extending when I stopped, growing upward at a rate I could almost see, and it has slowed. Not stopped. Slowed. It is waiting for me to move so it can decide which direction to grow. The Driftling that has been following me — I didn't notice it at first, just a thickness in the air, a place where the condensation hung differently — has drifted closer. It is maybe four feet away now, a slow vortex of pollen and spores and shed cellular material, and it is leaning toward me the way I imagine a sunflower leans, not with purpose but with physics, with the simple thermodynamic fact that I am warmer than the air around me and the vortex feeds on that difference.

I am not afraid. I want to be precise about that. Fear would be reasonable — I am inside an organism that is incorporating me, that is using my body heat and my breath and my shed skin as inputs in a metabolism I cannot comprehend, and the logical response to that is terror. But the sound is doing something to me. The weeping. The ten thousand fingers on ten thousand tables. It is not soothing — it is too dense for soothing, too complex, too *present* — but it is occupying the part of my brain that would otherwise be generating panic. There is no silence in which fear can grow. The sound fills every gap.

And the light. The green-gold light that moves, that breathes, that pools on the floor and then migrates when a new leaf extends across the gap above it. I am standing in a bright patch right now, and it is warm on my face — not sunlight-warm, something gentler, filtered through so many layers of living tissue that by the time it reaches me it has been softened into something that feels less like light and more like attention. Like the canopy is looking at me. Not seeing me. Looking at me. The way a plant looks at the sun.

I crouch by the pool. The membrane on the surface reacts — not to my movement, to my breath. I am close enough that my exhalation disturbs the air above the water, and the membrane ripples, and under the ripple I see movement. Something beneath the surface. Not a fish. Not an insect. A pocket of concentrated solution wrapped in a lipid membrane, drifting, and as I watch it bumps against the edge of the pool and the membrane deforms around the contact point, testing it, and then releases and drifts back to the center. It is alive in the way a soap bubble is alive — temporarily, contingently, held together by surface tension and chemistry and the sheer improbable fact that the conditions here allow it to exist for minutes or hours before it dissolves back into the solution and its components become available for the next experiment.

I want to touch it. I want to put my finger in the pool and feel the membrane wrap around my skin and see what the city learns from the contact — what it learns from the oils on my skin, the bacteria on my hands, the specific thermal signature of a fingertip. I want to give it that data. I want to be an input.

But I don't. Not yet.

Instead I sit. I sit on the thin root-floor by the edge of the pool, and I feel the mat adjust beneath me — a slow tightening, a redistribution of weight, the weave reorganizing to support me — and I watch the Driftling drift closer, and I watch the trunk resume its upward growth now that I have settled into a new position, a new thermal signature, a new gradient it can respond to, and I watch the light move across the floor as the canopy above me shifts, and I listen to the sound — the weeping, the growth, the water, the ten thousand rhythms that are not random and not musical and not anything I have a word for.

I am being incorporated. I know this. The floor beneath me is thickening. The air around me is adjusting. The Driftling is three feet away now, and I can feel it — a slight increase in humidity, a faint tickle of pollen on my exposed skin, the beginning of that second-skin sensation the text described. The city is sampling me. It is running tests. It is asking, in the only language it has: *What is this? What is this? What is this?*

And I am answering, in the only language I have: *I am here. I am warm. I am wet. I am here.*

I don't know how long I sit. Time is strange here — not because it moves differently but because there are no markers. No clocks. No day-night cycle that I can detect through the canopy. Just the slow rhythm of the light, the slower rhythm of the growth, the constant rhythm of the weeping. I could be here for an hour. I could be here for a day. The city doesn't care. The city is patient in the way that only something growing at the speed of a crowd can be patient — it has all the time in the world because it is making all the time in the world, generating it, the way it generates everything else, from itself, for itself, in the closed loop that I have briefly, incompletely, not-fully entered.

Eventually I stand. The Driftling drifts back, disappointed or not-disappointed, I can't tell. The floor beneath me retains the shape of my sitting — a shallow depression in the root-mat, already beginning to fill in, already being reclaimed by the weave. I will leave a trace. Not a permanent one. The city will absorb it, the way it absorbs everything, the way it absorbed the desiccated remains of the pool-things that crawled out and dried up, the way it absorbed the Driftlings that grew large enough to become architecture, the way it will absorb me if I stay long enough.

I walk. The city opens ahead of me — a corridor widening, a ceiling lifting, a space forming where there was no space. And behind me, the corridor narrows. The walls thicken. The floor consolidates. The city is making my path permanent, or trying to, and I am walking into the green-gold light that moves and breathes and looks at me without seeing me, and the sound is everywhere, and the wet is everywhere, and the heat presses on me like a hand, and I am inside the loop now, I am part of the metabolism, I am a gradient, I am an input, I am a warm thing moving through the green.

The city continues.

I continue with it.
