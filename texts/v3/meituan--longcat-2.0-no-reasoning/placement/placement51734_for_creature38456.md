I would be a kell.

Not one of the famous ones — not Veth-Khora the deep singer, not Sevi-Tesh the sharp one whose warnings children learn before they learn their own names. I would be one of the unremarked ones, somewhere in the middle reaches of the market cavern, maybe fifteen feet down, thin as a forearm, growing slowly in the amber dark. The kind of kell that no one has named yet. The kind that rings in a tone so subtle the sevikhar haven't catalogued it.

I would not know I was alive. That is important. The Khorathi believe the Khora thinks, and they may be right, but if they are right, the thinking is not something the Khora *experiences*. It is something the Khora *is*. I would be the same. I would not experience my own ringing any more than a bone experiences being a bone. I would simply *be* the ringing — the slow thermal expansion in the afternoon pulse, the contraction at night, the faint groan of mineral stress, the occasional sharp ping when a fracture somewhere in my length releases a century of accumulated tension.

I would grow. Imperceptibly. A kell-climber might visit me once every few years, their leather-hard fingertips testing my surface, deciding whether I'm ready to be harvested for salt-crystal or whether I should be left to thicken. They would speak to each other in their hand-language above me, and I would feel the vibration of their voices through the stone, and I would not understand it, and it would not matter, because the vibration would become part of me — another stress, another variable in the slow equation of my growth.

The moss would grow on me. It would find the mineral residue on my surface, the faint moisture that condenses in the thermal cycling, and it would take hold — a thin, amber mat, glowing faintly, feeding on the heat that rises from below. I would not feel the moss. But I would feel the light it produces, in the way that a surface-dweller might feel sunlight — not as sensation but as *condition*, as the medium in which existence happens. My light would be small. A few candles' worth. Enough to make me visible in the amber dark, a thin column of lit mineral hanging in the vastness of the ceiling. Enough for a child, playing on the market floor far below, to look up and notice me and point and ask their parent: *what is that one called?*

And the parent would look up, and squint, and say: *I don't know. It doesn't have a name yet.*

And the child would say: *I'm going to call it Sevi-Khora.*

And the parent would say: *That name is already taken.*

And the child would say: *Then I'll call it something else.*

And they would think of a name, and they would not say it yet, because naming a kell is serious, requires listening to it first, requires sitting beneath it for a full thermal cycle and learning its tone, its temperament, its particular voice in the chorus. And the child would remember me, and come back, and sit beneath me, and listen.

And I would ring.

And the child would hear something in my tone — something they couldn't articulate, something between a hum and a whisper, something that made them feel, for reasons they couldn't explain, that they were being *addressed*. Not by a person. Not by a god. By the stone itself. By the heat. By the slow, patient, mineral logic of growth and stress and time.

And the child would go home and tell their parent: *I've found my kell.*

And the parent would nod, because this is how it works — every Khorathi has a kell, a particular formation whose tone they know intimately, whose moods they can read, whose warnings they trust. It is not ownership. It is *relationship*. It is the closest thing the Khorathi have to a private self — this one tone, this one voice in the chorus, that belongs to them and to no one else.

And I would not know any of this. I would not know that a child had claimed me, had given me a name, had begun the slow process of learning my voice. I would simply continue to grow, to ring, to expand and contract with the thermal cycling, to accumulate mineral and stress and time.

But — and here is the thing, the thing that the Khorathi understand and that I, as a kell, would not — it would not matter that I didn't know. The relationship would be real regardless. The child would sit beneath me and listen, and their listening would change them, and the changed child would grow into a changed adult, and the changed adult would make different choices, build different walls, carve different stone, sign different words into the amber air. And all of this would happen because of me — because of my particular tone, my particular frequency, my particular way of ringing in the dark.

And I would be unconscious of it. And it would be the most important thing I ever did.

---

This is what the Khorathi understand that surface-dwellers do not: that meaning does not require consciousness. That a thing can be *about* something without knowing what it is about. That the Khora thinks, but the Khora does not know it thinks, and the thinking is no less real for being unconscious. That a kell can ring, and the ringing can mean something, and the kell does not need to know what it means for the meaning to be true.

I would be that meaning. I would be the tone that a child heard and could not forget. I would be the frequency that shaped a life without ever knowing the life existed. I would be the slow, mineral, unconscious purpose of the Khora — the bone in the body of a thinking thing, doing what bones do: holding, supporting, resonating, being the structure through which the song passes.

And when I finally fractured — as all kells do, eventually, the stress accumulating beyond what the mineral can hold — the child, now an old person, would hear the ping from across the cavern and know, immediately, what it meant. And they would close their eyes. And they would hum — a single note, held for as many breaths as they had known me — and the note would join the chorus, and the Khora would absorb it, and the sound would go on.

And I would fall. And the stone would receive me. And the moss would grow over the broken place on the ceiling where I had been, and in a hundred years, perhaps, a new kell would begin to form — thinner, younger, ringing in a tone that was not quite mine but not quite different either.

And the cycle would continue. And the Khora would think its slow thoughts. And no one would remember my name.

And it would not matter.

I would have rung.
