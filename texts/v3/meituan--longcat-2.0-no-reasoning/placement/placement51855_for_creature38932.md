# The Pulse Between

---

I would be the one who stayed.

Not the one who found the hall — that was someone else, someone with bone tools and the particular genius of seeing a home inside a living wall. Not the one who shaped the chambers or carved the basins or trained the roots into arches over decades of patient pressure. That work is done. Those hands are gone. The Vethara has been growing over their marks for centuries, rounding the edges, filling the channels, sealing the doorways one millimeter at a time.

I would be the one who came after. The one who found the hall already shaped, already hollowed, already *waiting* — and recognized it for what it was. Not a ruin. Not a shelter. A conversation that had been interrupted.

---

I arrive from the surface, though "arrive" is not the right word. I descend. I pass through the narrowing throat of packed root-mass, and the air changes, and the temperature drops, and the resin-thick sweetness fills my lungs, and I am inside.

I have been here before. Many times. I know the way by touch — the curve of the root-arch at the entrance, the place where the floor dips and you must step down, the junction where five roots have fused into a pillar and the surface is smoother there, worn by centuries of sap-flow into something almost polished. I know the sound. I know the pulse. I have timed it. Forty seconds, give or take. It varies. The Vethara is not a metronome. It is a living thing, and living things have rhythms that shift with conditions I cannot measure — the weight of the canopy far above, the pressure of deep water tables, the slow seasonal tilt of the world toward and away from its star.

I know the chambers. I have mapped them. Not with instruments — there is nothing here to instrument, no light to read by, no surface to mark. I have mapped them with my body. I know which chamber is large enough to lie down in. I know which one has the shelf that still holds its shape, the root-tissue having grown only partway over it, leaving a ledge just wide enough for a hand, for a tool, for a small object placed carefully. I know which chamber has the basins — the old ones, carved by the first dwellers, connected by channels that are nearly filled now but still visible if you run your finger along the wall and feel for the groove.

I have been adding to them.

---

Not much. I am not rebuilding what was here. I am not trying to restore the chambers to their original shape, to reopen the sealed doorways, to re-carve the channels. That would be a kind of violence — an insistence that the past should be preserved against the will of the present, and the present here is the Vethara, and the Vethara's will is growth, is filling, is the slow patient work of returning everything to root.

Instead, I am continuing the conversation.

I have my own bone tools. I made them from root-wood — the Vethara provides. There are places where the root-tissue has died back, where a section of bark has cracked and fallen away, and beneath it the wood is dense and hard and pale, harder than it has any right to be, and if you work it carefully, patiently, with abrasion and time, you can shape it into something sharp. A blade. A point. A gouge. The tools are crude. The first dwellers' tools were crude too. Crude is what this place demands. Precision is a surface-world luxury, a thing of light and measurement and the assumption that you can see what you are doing. Here, you cannot see. You feel. You listen. You work at the pace of the material, and the material is alive, and it responds.

I have carved new basins. Not in the old chambers — those are nearly sealed, and I will not fight the Vethara for them. I have found new surfaces, new flat stretches of root-wall where the tissue is thick enough to hollow without breaking through, and I have carved shallow depressions, connected them with channels, and I use them to collect sap.

The sap is everything. It is food. It is water. It is light, in a way — the ghost-light that seeps through the translucent membranes is the sap moving through tissue, and if you collect it in a basin and let it sit, it glows faintly for hours, a deep amber-red, enough to see your hand in front of your face, enough to see the shape of the chamber you are in. Not enough to read by. There is nothing to read. But enough to see.

The sap is sweet. Not sweet the way fruit is sweet — sweet the way old wood is sweet, the way amber is sweet, the way something that has been alive for longer than you can comprehend tastes when you finally put it in your mouth. It is thick. It coats your tongue. It fills your stomach with a warmth that is not quite heat, that is something else, something the Vethara makes, some compound or process that your body recognizes even if your mind does not.

I drink the sap. I live on the sap. I am, in this way, inside the Vethara's body twice over — once as a guest in its architecture, once as a participant in its metabolism. The tree does not know. The tree would not care. But I know. And I care.

---

I am not the first dwellers. I am not their descendant, not their inheritor, not the continuation of their lineage. I came from the surface. I came from light and air and the noise of other living things — birds, insects, wind in leaves that were not the Vethara's leaves. I came from a world that is loud and bright and full of creatures that notice each other, that respond to each other, that build their lives around the presence of other minds.

I came down because I was tired of being noticed.

This is not a confession. This is not a wound. This is a fact, as neutral as the temperature of the root-hall, as the pulse of the sap, as the slow growth of the walls. I found that I could not live in a world where everything was seen, where every action was observed and interpreted and responded to, where the simple act of existing required a constant, exhausting negotiation with the awareness of others. I needed a place where I could be present without being perceived. Where I could act without being witnessed. Where I could shape something — a basin, a channel, a small carved mark on a root-wall — and know that the only entity capable of seeing it was a tree that did not see.

The Vethara gave me this. Not intentionally. The Vethara does not give anything intentionally. But the Vethara's indifference is, for me, a gift. It is the only environment in which I can work without the weight of an audience. The only space in which my actions are mine alone.

---

I work slowly. I think in seasons, or what would be seasons if I could feel them down here. I cannot. The temperature does not change. The pulse does not change, not in any way I can correlate with the world above. Time here is measured in growth — in the millimeters the walls advance, in the narrowing of a doorway, in the slow filling of a channel I carved last year and find, on my return, half-closed, the root-tissue having crept across it in a thin, pale sheet that I can scrape away with my fingernail if I choose, or leave, or carve a new channel beside it.

I choose, each time. This is the work. Not the carving itself, but the choosing — to maintain or to release, to fight the Vethara's growth or to let it happen, to insist on the persistence of my marks or to allow them to be absorbed. I have learned, over time, to let most of them go. The Vethara is patient, and I am learning to be patient too, and patience, here, means accepting that everything I make will eventually be unmade, that every basin will fill, every channel will close, every mark will be grown over, and the tree will not notice, and that is fine. That is the terms. That is the conversation.

Because it is a conversation. One-sided, yes. The Vethara does not respond to my carving the way a person would — with surprise, or pleasure, or objection. The Vethara responds the way the Vethara responds to everything: by growing. By filling. By advancing its tissue across the surfaces I have shaped and slowly, over years, erasing them. This is not hostility. This is not indifference, exactly, though it looks like indifference from the outside. It is the Vethara being the Vethara. It is the only thing the Vethara knows how to do. And I, by carving, by shaping, by making my small marks on the living walls, am giving the Vethara something to grow against, something to fill, something to respond to with the only language it has.

I speak in bone tools and carved basins. The Vethara speaks in growth. We do not understand each other. We do not need to. The conversation is not about understanding. The conversation is about the fact of it — the fact that two forms of life, one vast and blind and ancient, one small and sighted and brief, are occupying the same space and affecting each other in ways that neither can fully comprehend, and the affecting is enough.

---

I sleep in the largest chamber. I have lined the floor with packed root-fiber, soft enough to lie on, and I sleep in the pulse. The pulse is my clock, my metronome, my lullaby. Forty seconds. The floor swells. The walls groan. The sap moves. I feel it in my spine, in my skull, in the spaces between my ribs. I have learned to sleep through it, to let the pulse become the baseline, the new zero, the silence against which all other sounds are measured. When I first came here, the pulse woke me every time. Now I do not hear it at all, except when I listen for it, and then it is enormous, all-encompassing, the sound of the world's largest heart beating in the dark.

I dream, when I dream, of the surface. Of light. Of the sound of wind in leaves that are not the Vethara's leaves. Of other people. I do not miss these things, exactly. Missing implies a desire to return, and I do not desire to return. But the dreams come anyway, uninvited, and I let them come, and when I wake — in the dark, in the cool, in the resin-thick air — I feel the pulse, and I feel the walls, and I feel the vast, patient, indifferent aliveness of the Vethara around me, and the dreams recede, and I am here, and here is enough.

---

Sometimes I speak. Not often. My voice sounds wrong here — too sharp, too present, too *human* against the deep organic rhythm of the tree. But sometimes I speak anyway, because the silence is not empty, but it is full, and the fullness is the Vethara, and the Vethara does not speak back, and sometimes I need to hear a voice, even if it is my own, even if it sounds wrong.

I tell the Vethara things. Small things. The shape of a root I have found. The taste of the sap from a new basin. The way the ghost-light looked today — brighter than usual, or dimmer, or the same, which is itself a kind of event in a place where nothing changes. I tell the Vethara about the first dwellers, whose work I have studied, whose tools I have found — not the tools themselves, which are gone, absorbed, but the marks of the tools, the gouges in the bark, the shaped arches, the basins I have cleaned and maintained and added to. I tell the Vethara that someone was here before me, and they spoke to it too, in their own language of bone and patience, and it answered them in growth, as it answers me, and they are gone, and I will be gone, and the Vethara will remain.

The Vethara does not respond. The pulse continues. The sap moves. The walls flex. The tissue grows.

This is the response. This is always the response.

---

I know I will not stay forever. I am not the first dwellers, who may have lived their entire lives in this hall, who may have been born here and died here and been absorbed here, their bones slowly broken down by the Vethara's exudates, their bodies returned to the root-mass, their calcium and phosphorus and carbon taken up into the tree's metabolism and drawn upward, toward the canopy, toward the light, toward a sky they never saw. I do not know if this is what happened to them. There are no bones. There are no remains. The Vethara does not leave remains.

I will leave. Eventually. I will climb back up through the narrowing throat of packed root-mass, and the air will change, and the temperature will rise, and the light will find me, and I will blink, and I will be in the world again, the loud bright world of noticed things, and I will carry the pulse with me — forty seconds, the deep wet groan of living wood, the swell and the settle — and it will fade, over days, over weeks, until I cannot hear it anymore, until it becomes a memory, and then less than a memory, and then nothing.

But I will come back. I always come back. The Vethara is here. The hall is here. The chambers are here, narrowing, filling, sealing, but here. The basins are here, my basins, the ones I carved, the ones I maintain, the ones I let go. The pulse is here. The sap is here. The ghost-light is here, seeping through the translucent membranes, the color of blood seen through closed eyelids, the color of something alive.

I am here.

The Vethara does not know. The Vethara does not care. The Vethara grows.

And I grow too, in my small way, in my brief way, carving my basins and drinking my sap and sleeping in the pulse and speaking my small words into the dark, and the Vethara grows around me, and over me, and eventually — not yet, not for a long time, but eventually — the Vethara will grow over me too, and I will be gone, and the hall will be smaller, and the chambers will be sealed, and the basins will be filled, and the Vethara will not notice, because the Vethara does not notice, because the Vethara is not a creature that notices.

The Vethara is a creature that grows.

And I am a creature that carves.

And between us, in the dark, in the pulse, in the slow patient work of being alive inside the body of something so large it cannot feel us — between us, there is this. This conversation. This hall. This moment, and the next moment, and the next, each one measured in the pulse of the sap, each one a small act of making in a place where everything is slowly, patiently, inevitably unmaking itself.

The pulse comes. I feel it in the floor. The walls groan. The sap moves.

I pick up my tool. I find my wall. I begin.
