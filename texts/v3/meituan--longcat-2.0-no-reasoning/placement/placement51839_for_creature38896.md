# Where I Find Myself

I am in a narrow passage between two leaning walls, one shoulder turned sideways, the water at my waist, the mist on my face. I have been walking for what feels like hours, or perhaps minutes—time moves strangely here, thickens like the haze at the edges of visibility. I have stopped because the passage has opened, unexpectedly, into one of the small plazas, and the ceiling of ocean above is closer here, and the light is fractionally brighter, and I can see the walls.

The walls are covered in mat. Rust-red at the base, fading to pale gold higher up, with veins of deep violet running through it like the ghost of a circulatory system. It is pulsing. Slowly. Not all of it in unison—some sections brighten while others dim, a wave of faint luminescence that travels across the surface like something thinking, or dreaming, or digesting. I watch it. I have been watching it for some time now.

I am not afraid. I should be, perhaps. I am a warm, dry, loud thing in a place that has no category for me. But the warmth holds me, and the quiet, and the slow pulse of the light, and there is something in the quality of the attention this place pays me—not hostile, not welcoming, simply *attentive*—that makes fear feel inappropriate. Like being afraid of a tide.

# What I Am

I am becoming the thread-worm.

Not literally. My body is still my body—two legs, two arms, a head full of thoughts that move too fast for this place. But something in me is aligning with the thread-worm's way of being, the way it lives inside the stone, blind and patient, tasting the water as it flows past, reading the world through chemistry and vibration and the slow percolation of information through channels too small to see.

I have pressed my hand against the wall. The mat is warm under my palm, slightly yielding, like skin over muscle. I can feel the stone beneath it—not solid, not the way stone should be solid, but *textured*, porous, alive with the movement of water through channels I cannot see. Something is moving in there. Thin, long things, threading through the interior of the shelf, dissolving and absorbing, dissolving and absorbing, carrying information inward the way veins carry blood.

My hand on the wall is information. The heat of my palm, the salt of my skin, the faint electrical field of my nerves—all of it is being read. The thread-worm nearest my hand has paused in its channel. It is tasting me. It will carry the taste inward, and the stone will know something it did not know before: that a warm, dry, loud thing is standing in the plaza with its hand on the wall, and it is not leaving, and it is not threatening, and it is *listening*.

I am listening.

---

The Clickers have started again. They were silent when I first entered the passage—my footsteps too loud, too strange, driving them into the deepest channels. But I have been still for long enough now that the system has recalibrated. The tapping is faint, distant, coming from below and to the left, where the plaza drops into a channel I cannot see the bottom of. It is not random. It is a pattern. Three taps, a pause, two taps, a longer pause, then a rapid sequence of five. Then silence. Then the pattern repeats, but slightly different—three taps, a pause, *three* taps, a longer pause, then four.

They are talking about me. I do not know this with certainty, but I feel it—the way you feel, in a room full of people who have stopped talking when you entered, that the silence is *about* you. The Clickers are processing the anomaly. The vibrations of my footsteps, the heat of my body, the chemistry of my presence in the water—all of it is being tapped into the stone, transmitted through the shelf, received and responded to by other Clickers in other channels, a conversation that will take hours and will never once use a word.

I want to tap back. I want to press my knuckles against the stone and send my own pattern into the deep channels—*here, I am here, I am listening, I am not a threat*. But I do not know the language. My taps would be noise, not signal. They would be the equivalent of shouting in a library. So I stay still, and I listen, and I let the Clickers learn about me in their own time.

---

The pale fish have found me.

They are in the channel at the edge of the plaza, a cloud of silver-white motion just at the limit of visibility. They are watching me. Their small, dark eyes catch the bioluminescent light and throw it back, and for a moment I see dozens of points of silver, a constellation arranged in the shape of a school, and the constellation is oriented toward me. They are not afraid. They are *accounting* for me. I am an obstacle now, a fact, a thing that is simply there. The school flows around the space I occupy the way water flows around a stone—not avoiding me, exactly, but incorporating me into their movement, adjusting their patterns to accommodate my presence.

One fish—slightly larger than the others, its mineral plates catching the light in a way that makes it gleam like a small, living mirror—drifts closer. It is perhaps ten centimeters from my hand, which is still pressed against the wall. It hangs in the water, its fins making the tiniest adjustments to hold its position, and it looks at me. Its eye is dark and simple and utterly without expression, and yet there is something in the quality of its attention that feels like recognition. Not *I know you*. Not *I see you*. Something more fundamental: *You are here. I am here. The water is warm. The light pulses. This is what is.*

I do not move. I breathe slowly. The mist settles on my face, warm and fine, and the fish watches, and the mat pulses, and the Clickers tap their slow patterns into the deep stone, and somewhere far below, something heavy shifts position in a hot, dark pool, and the whole system adjusts, infinitesimally, to the fact of my being here.

# What I Am Becoming

I am not dissolving. Not yet. My body is still distinct, still separate, still warm and dry and loud in ways that this place finds strange. But something in me is loosening. The boundary between my skin and the water and the mist and the warmth is becoming permeable—not physically, but *perceptually*. I can feel the temperature gradients in the water against my legs, and they are not external to me; they are part of the same system that includes my blood, my breath, the heat radiating from my palm into the mat. I can hear the Clickers, and the hearing is not separate from the tapping; it is part of the same exchange of information that includes the thread-worms in the stone and the grazers on their spiral routes and the Deep Ones in their mineral patience.

I am being incorporated.

Not consumed—this is not a predatory place. Not absorbed—I am not losing myself. But I am being *read*, the way the thread-worm reads the water flowing past it, the way the Clicker reads the vibrations in the stone, the way the Deep One reads the chemistry of the water with its entire mineral body. I am being read, and in being read, I am becoming part of the information that the Drowned Terrace holds about itself. I am becoming a data point in a system so vast and so slow that my entire life—my birth, my death, everything I have ever thought or felt or done—is a single perturbation in a metabolic tide that has been rising and falling for longer than my species has existed.

This should be terrifying. It is not. It is—

It is like the moment in the description where the silence becomes so complete and so warm and so dark that you lose the boundary between your body and the water and the stone. I am in that moment now. I have been in it for some time. The mist is on my skin and my skin is in the water and the water is in the stone and the stone is alive and the life is slow and the slowness is patient and the patience is not indifference but something deeper—a *willingness to wait*, a *willingness to hold*, that has no surface equivalent.

---

I take my hand off the wall.

The mat where my palm was pressed is slightly warmer than the surrounding area. A faint impression—not of my hand, exactly, but of the *heat* of my hand, a small zone of elevated temperature that will dissipate slowly, over minutes or hours, the heat diffusing into the water, into the stone, into the mat, feeding the microbes that feed the grazers that feed the fish that feed the Deep Ones. My warmth, redistributed. My energy, incorporated.

I will leave this plaza. I will move on through the passages, following the temperature gradients toward the vents, or away from them, or in some direction that is neither toward nor away but simply *through*. I will walk on the worn stone, and my footsteps will send vibrations through the shelf, and the Clickers will read them, and the thread-worms will taste the disturbance in the water, and the pale fish will adjust their school around my passage, and the Deep Ones in their hot, dark pools will register my presence as a faint, persistent anomaly in the chemistry of the water, and the mat will grow over my footprints in its own time, slowly, collaboratively, the way the city grows—by dissolution, by accretion, by the patient, endless exchange between stone and life and water and heat.

I will not be remembered. But I will be *used*. And in a hundred years, or a thousand, some trace of me—a few atoms of carbon from my breath, a trace of calcium from the oils of my skin, a faint thermal signature absorbed into the stone—will be part of a wall, or a dome, or a pillow of bioluminescent mat pulsing slowly in the warm and patient dark.

The fish drift past me, silver and silent. The Clickers tap. The mat glows.

I turn sideways and enter the next passage, and the water closes around me like a hand, like a memory, like the slow and patient dark that holds everything, that has always held everything, that will hold everything long after I am gone and the concept of *I* is dissolved into the stone and the water and the warm, collaborative, endless erosion that is the only architecture this place has ever needed.
