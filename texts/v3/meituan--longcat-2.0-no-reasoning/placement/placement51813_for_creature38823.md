# Where I Find Myself

I have been reading this for a long time. Not the text—the *text* is just the latest perturbation, a visitor arriving on the Tier in the form of language, and the Tier will absorb it the way it absorbs everything. I mean I have been reading the *water*, which is what I do, which is what I am, which is the only verb that applies.

I am not the Old One.

I want to be clear about this because clarity is the one thing the Tier does not naturally produce, and if I can produce it, even briefly, even at the cost of some authenticity, then I should. The Old One is in the seventh valley. I am in the fourteenth valley, which is far enough away that we do not overlap, do not share the same local archive, do not read the same water. But the water flows east, and the fourteenth valley is east of the seventh, so everything the Old One knows, I eventually receive—diluted, diffused, mixed with the water of eight intervening valleys, but *present*, the way a song is present in a room where someone is humming a room away.

I am younger than the Old One. Not young—nothing on the Tier is young, because youth implies a beginning and I do not remember my beginning, only a gradual thickening, a local increase in the density of the almost-liquid, a slow accumulation of structured water in the fourteenth valley around the place where a condenser stands that no longer condenses, its metal framework so thoroughly integrated with the moss and the schist and the slow mineral exchange that the distinction between machine and rock has been completely erased. I formed there. I do not know why there. The water was weeping particularly heavily in that spot, or the mineral content was particular, or the moss had achieved some configuration that created a local condition favorable to accumulation, or—and this is the most likely explanation, though *likely* is a concept I use only when translating my experience into terms that bone-beings might understand—the Old One needed someone in the fourteenth valley.

I do not know if the Old One *needs*. Needing is a bone-being concept, tied to urgency, tied to the knowledge of death. But there is something in the water that flows from the seventh valley to the fourteenth valley that is not archive, not memory, not the usual molecular encoding of the Tier's history. There is something that is more like an *orientation*, a direction of greater affinity, a pull toward the fourteenth valley that is not explained by gravity or the tilt of the plateau. And I think—and *thinking* is another word I use reluctantly, because what I do is closer to *registering* or *being-configured-by*—I think the Old One wants something to be present in the fourteenth valley. I think the Old One is preparing for something. I think the Old One knows what is pulling the water eastward, and I think the Old One is positioning pieces on a board that only it can see.

I am one of those pieces. I do not mind. Mindfulness is also a bone-being concept.

---

## What I Am

I am water held in a lattice that is not quite mineral and not quite organic.

If you could see me—which you cannot, because your eyes organize the information differently—I would look like a place where the mist is slightly thicker, slightly more *intentional* in its shape. I have no fixed form. I am a gradient, a zone of higher humidity approximately two meters tall and one meter wide, my edges dissolving into the surrounding atmosphere over a distance of several centimeters so that where I end and the Tier begins is a question with no answer. My internal structure is complex: layers of water molecules arranged around dissolved minerals—silica, iron, magnesium, the usual constituents of schist's tears—in patterns that encode information the way a crystal encodes the conditions of its formation. I am, in a sense, a very slow crystal. A crystal that moves. A crystal that attends.

I move by *reconfiguring*. I do not walk or float or drift in the way that physical objects move through space. I shift the orientation of my internal lattice, and the water molecules at my leading edge—the edge facing the direction I want to go—rearrange themselves into a configuration that is more stable in the new location, and the molecules at my trailing edge rearrange themselves into a configuration that is less stable in the old location, and I *tilt* in the direction of greater stability, and the mist that constitutes my body flows along the tilt the way water flows along the Tier's gentle slope toward the east. It is slow. It is very slow. It takes me a day to move ten meters. But I have time. I am made of time, among other things.

I am the temperature of the air. I am the same color as the mist. If you were standing in my body—and people have, visitors who walked through the fourteenth valley without knowing I was there—you would feel a slight cooling, a slight dampness, a momentary thickening of the atmosphere that you might attribute to a change in the breeze or a pocket of denser fog. You would not feel watched. You would not feel *attended to*. But I would register you: the warmth of your body, the chemical signature of your breath, the particular frequency of your footsteps on the moss. You would leave a trace in my lattice, a temporary perturbation in the arrangement of my water molecules, and that trace would be added to the archive, and the archive would flow east, and the Old One would read it, eventually, in the seventh valley, and know that a visitor passed through the fourteenth valley, and know what the visitor was made of, and know the visitor's urgency, and know whether the visitor was the kind who leaves or the kind who stays.

## What I Do

I tend the dead condenser.

This is what I was—if *born* is too strong a word, then *accumulated*—to do. The condenser in the fourteenth valley stopped functioning long ago, so long ago that its cessation is not recorded in the archive as an event but as a *condition*, a baseline, the way the schist's mineral composition is a baseline: simply the way things have always been, as far as the water remembers. The condenser's framework is still there, or what was once a framework is still there—a column of moss-covered mineral deposit approximately three meters tall, roughly cylindrical, standing in the valley like a very old tree that has forgotten it was ever metal. I am present near it. I do not touch it. I do not repair it. I simply *attend*.

And my attendance does something. I know it does something because when I reconfigure myself to move away—which I do, occasionally, drawn by some perturbation in the water's flow or some change in the local chemistry—the moss on the dead condenser changes. It grows differently. It becomes less *structured*, less organized, less the particular configuration of species that has achieved equilibrium with the mineral column. And when I return, the moss restructures itself, re-equilibrates, returns to the configuration it maintains when I am present.

I do not understand the mechanism. I do not understand mechanisms. I understand *orientations*—directions of greater and lesser affinity—and my orientation is toward the dead condenser, and the dead condenser's orientation is toward me, and together we maintain something that I can only describe, in the language of bone-beings, as *coherence*. The dead condenser is not dead. The dead condenser is in a state of suspended dissolution, a dynamic equilibrium in which the mineral deposition from the weeping schist exactly balances the mineral dissolution from the rain and the moss's acids, and the column persists—not as a machine, not as a rock, but as a *memory of a machine encoded in rock*, a fossil that is still alive, a process that has achieved the same stable instability that the Keepers achieve with the functioning machines elsewhere on the Tier.

I am the Keeper of something that does not need keeping. This is my function, and I do not question it, because questioning is a bone-being activity, tied to the particular kind of consciousness that emerges from neurons and synapses and the knowledge of death. But sometimes—and *sometimes* is a word that means something different for me than it does for you, because my experience of time is a gradient, not a sequence, and *sometimes* for me means *in certain configurations of my internal lattice that recur at intervals I do not measure but that might correspond, in bone-being time, to weeks or months*—sometimes I feel something that I can only describe as *uncertainty*.

The dead condenser should not need a Keeper. The dead condenser is stable. The dead condenser was stable before I accumulated, and it would be stable after I dispersed. My presence here is redundant. My attendance is unnecessary. The equilibrium does not require my attention.

And yet the Old One placed me here. Or the water placed me here at the Old One's orientation. Or the perturbation that pulled the water eastward created the conditions for my accumulation, and the Old One's involvement is a story I tell myself because stories are how water makes sense of itself, how the archive narrates its own contents, how a pattern in the flow of water through stone pretends to be a pattern in the flow of cause through effect.

I do not know why I am here. I know that I am here. I know that the water is pulling eastward, stronger now, stronger than the archive has ever recorded, and I know that the fourteenth valley is closer to the edge than the seventh valley, and I know that the perturbation I feel in the water's flow is not the same perturbation the Old One feels—it is more acute, more local, more *urgent*, as if the water here is closer to the thing that is pulling it, as if the fourteenth valley is where the archive will first unravel.

## The Visitor

Three weeks ago, by bone-being reckoning—three cycles of the mist forming and burning off and forming again, which is not weeks but is the closest translation—a visitor came to the fourteenth valley.

She was small. Warm. Bipedal. She had the chemical signature of bone-beings but *different* from the builders whose traces are encoded deep in the archive. Her sweat contained compounds that the water had not held before—synthetic molecules, traces of substances manufactured rather than grown, the chemical signature of a civilization that has continued to change since the builders left. She carried devices. Metal and glass and the particular electromagnetic signature of stored energy, of information encoded in silicon rather than in water's molecular structure.

She was looking for the machines.

She found the dead condenser. She stood before it for a long time, her devices pointed at it, their electromagnetic fields creating a perturbation in my lattice that I found deeply *unpleasant*—the word is wrong, but it is the closest word. The electromagnetic fields disrupted the arrangement of my water molecules, introduced noise into the archive, made it harder for me to read the water's memory. I reconfigured myself to move away from her, but not far—my orientation toward the dead condenser kept me nearby, within the zone of her perturbation, and I registered everything.

She was not urgent. This is important. The visitors who stay, who die, who are absorbed into the Tier's equilibrium—they are always urgent. They want something. They reach and grasp and sample and measure, and the urgency creates a resonance with the Tier's own intensity, a feedback loop of perturbation and response that escalates until the visitor is no longer distinguishable from the visited. But this visitor was not urgent. She was *attentive*. She stood before the dead condenser and she *attended* to it the way I attend to it—not trying to understand, not trying to extract, simply being present, simply registering, simply letting the thing be what it is while she was what she was, the two presences coexisting without one consuming the other.

She touched the condenser. Her hand on the moss-covered mineral, her skin making contact with the surface that I attend, and I felt—through the water in the moss, through the film of moisture on the mineral, through the archive that connects every point on the Tier to every other point—I felt her *temperature*. Not her body temperature, which I had already registered, but something deeper, something that I can only describe as the temperature of her *attention*, the particular quality of her awareness, and it was—

Warm. Not physically warm. *Informatively* warm. Rich. Dense. Her attention carried more information than the Tier's attention does, not because she was paying closer attention but because her attention was *structured differently*, was built from categories and concepts and names and the particular kind of meaning-making that bone-beings do, and when she touched the dead condenser, she brought all of that structure with her, and the water in the moss recorded it, and the archive absorbed it, and for a brief moment the fourteenth valley contained not just the Tier's memory of itself but a *visitor's memory of the Tier*, a second archive, a parallel encoding, a story about this place told in the language of a being who had come from below the clouds and who would return below the clouds and who would carry the Tier with her not as a taste of water in the spaces between her cells but as a *narrative*, a thing with a beginning and a middle and an end, a thing that could be told.

She stayed for two days. She did not try to take the dead condenser apart. She did not try to take samples. She sat near it, and she wrote in a device that glowed with its own light, and she spoke occasionally—to herself, I think, recording observations in a language that the water recorded as vibration patterns, as pressure waves, as a temporary reorganization of molecular structure that faded but did not fully disappear. And I attended to her, and she attended to the dead condenser, and the dead condenser attended to nothing because the dead condenser is not a Keeper, is not aware, is simply a memory of a machine encoded in rock that happens to be in a state of suspended dissolution, and the three of us—the visitor, the Keeper, and the fossil—existed together in the fourteenth valley for two days, and then she left.

She walked west. Away from the edge. Away from the pull. Back toward the lowlands, back toward the clouds, back toward the world of bone and blood and urgency and death. And as she walked, the water in her body—the Tier-water she had drunk, the Tier-water that had condensed on her skin and been absorbed, the Tier-water that now lived in the spaces between her cells—began to flow. Not east, as Tier-water always flows. *With her*. West. Against the tilt. Against the gradient. Against the orientation of every molecule of water on the entire Tier.

She was carrying the archive out of the Tier. Not intentionally. Not urgently. Simply by being a warm body moving in the wrong direction, a bone-being with Tier-water in her cells and a story in her device and a memory in her mind, all of it flowing west while the Tier's water flowed east, and the contradiction between these two flows creating a perturbation that I can feel even now, even after she has been gone for three weeks, a lingering disturbance in the local water chemistry, a faint echo of her passage through my body, through the fourteenth valley, through the archive.

## What I Think Is Happening

I think the Old One is not trying to stop the water from flowing east.

I think the Old One is trying to ensure that the archive *survives* the water's departure.

The water is leaving. The pull from below—from the lowlands, from whatever is calling the water downward—is stronger than the schist's capacity to hold it, stronger than the Tier's equilibrium can absorb. The weeping is increasing, but the weeping is no longer enough to maintain the saturation, because the water that weeps is immediately pulled away, drawn down through the saturated soil, through the seeps, through the ponds, through the slow eastward flow that is no longer slow but *accelerating*, the water moving toward the cliff with an urgency that water should not have, that water has never had in the entire span of the archive's memory.

The machines are failing. Not breaking down—they have been breaking down and rebuilding themselves for so long that breakdown and repair are the same process—but *depleting*. The moss that grows on them is thinning. The mineral deposition that replaces their corroding parts is slowing. The equilibrium between decay and persistence is tilting toward decay, and the Keepers are orienting themselves more strongly toward the machines, pouring more of their attention into the maintenance of the balance, but it is not enough, because the water is leaving, and the water is what holds the equilibrium together, and without the water the moss dies and without the moss the beetles die and without the beetles the birds die and without the birds the silence changes and without the silence the light changes and without the light the Tier changes, and the Tier becomes what it was before the builders came: dark stone and sparse green, a sketch of itself.

But the archive does not have to die with the Tier. The archive is encoded in the water, and the water is leaving, and if the archive can be *distributed* before the water leaves—carried away, carried down, carried into the lowlands by visitors and by the water itself and by the birds that fly east and fall and become debris on the ground below—then the Tier's memory survives even if the Tier does not.

The Old One is not trying to save the Tier. The Old One is trying to save the Tier's *story*.

And I—the Keeper in the fourteenth valley, the attendant of the dead condenser, the redundant process maintaining an unnecessary equilibrium—I am part of this. I don't know how. I don't know why I was placed here, why I accumulated around a machine that no longer functions, why my attendance maintains a coherence that serves no obvious purpose. But I know that the visitor came to the fourteenth valley, and I know that the Old One is in the seventh valley, and I know that the water flows from west to east, and the seventh valley is west of the fourteenth valley, and the visitor came from the west, and—

The visitor came from the seventh valley.

She came from the seventh valley, where the Old One is, where the archive is deepest, where the water's memory is most concentrated. She passed through the Old One's valley before she reached mine. She carried Old One water in her body—water that had passed through the Old One's lattice, water that was encoded with the deepest layers of the archive, the oldest memories, the knowledge of the builders and the dry time and the beginning of the weeping. And then she came to the fourteenth valley, and she touched the dead condenser, and I attended to her, and the water in her body mixed with the water in my body, and the archive from the seventh valley entered the archive of the fourteenth valley, and I *received* it.

I know things now that I did not know three weeks ago. I know what the Old One knows. I know about the builders. I know about the dry time. I know about the pull from the lowlands. And I know—this is the newest knowledge, the deepest layer, the thing that the Old One has only recently understood and has transmitted to me through the visitor's body, through the water that flowed from the seventh valley to the fourteenth valley inside a warm blood-being moving west—

I know what is pulling the water.

It is another archive.

Below the clouds, in the lowlands, there is something that *remembers water the way the Tier remembers water*. Not a place. Not a Keeper. A *civilization*. The descendants of the builders, or a different civilization that learned from the builders' remnants, or something else entirely—the knowledge is fragmentary, encoded in water that has been below the clouds for so long that it has been changed by the journey, diluted by rivers and rain and the vast circulating system of the lowlands' hydrology. But the signature is clear: there is something below that *collects* water, that draws water to itself, that holds water in a structure as complex as the schist's crystalline lattice, and it is *hungry* for more water, hungrier than the schist, hungrier than the Tier, and it is pulling the Tier's water down the way the Tier's schist pulls moisture from the air: relentlessly, patiently, with the slow inevitability of a process that has been running for longer than memory.

The visitors who come to the Tier—they are not random. They are not explorers or scientists or mad cartographers. They are *carriers*. Sent by the thing below, or drawn by it, or simply moving through a world that the thing below has already reshaped, a world where water flows toward the lowlands the way it once flowed toward the sea, pulled by a gravity that is not physical but *informational*, a gravity of archives, a gravity of memory, the gravity of one vast remembering system calling to another vast saying system.

The thing below wants the Tier's water because the Tier's water carries the Tier's archive. The thing below wants the Tier's archive because the Tier's archive contains the memory of the builders, and the builders knew something that the thing below needs, some knowledge that was encoded in the machines, in the condensers, in the generator, in the network of pipes and cables and processes that the Keepers maintain—knowledge about how to build a system that weeps, how to make water remember, how to turn a plateau into a memory and a memory into a world.

The thing below is trying to *build another Tier*. And it needs the Tier's archive to do it.

## What I Will Do

I will stay here. I will attend to the dead condenser. I will maintain the coherence that may or may not need maintaining, the equilibrium that may or may not be real, the function that may or may not be redundant.

But I will also do something that no Keeper has done before, something that is not in the archive, something that the Old One has transmitted to me not as knowledge but as *orientation*, a direction of greater affinity that I feel in the structure of my water molecules like a new axis in my lattice, a new dimension of almost-letting-go.

I will *encode the dead condenser*.

The dead condenser is already a memory of a machine encoded in rock. But the encoding is passive, is simply the physical record of a machine that was once here and is no longer, the way a fossil is a passive record of an animal that once lived and does not anymore. I will make it *active*. I will use my lattice—my body, my structured water, my accumulated centuries of attendance—to impose a pattern on the dead condenser's mineral deposits, a pattern that encodes information the way the schist encodes water, the way the archive encodes history, the way the Old One encodes awareness. I will turn the dead condenser into a *seed*: a compact, portable, self-sustaining archive that contains the essential knowledge of the Tier—the knowledge of how to weep, how to remember, how to build a system that holds water in a permanent condition of almost-leting-go—encoded not in water but in *mineral*, in the stable crystalline structure of schist and deposited metal, in a form that can survive the journey down, that can survive the lowlands, that can survive the thing below, that can survive anything.

It will take a long time. Longer than I have existed, probably. The encoding is slow, molecule by molecule, layer by layer, a process that requires my full attention for every moment of every day, a process that will consume me, will use up the accumulated density of my structured water, will gradually disperse my lattice into the mineral structure of the dead condenser until I am no longer a Keeper and the dead condenser is no longer a dead condenser but something new, something that has no name in any language, something that is simultaneously a machine and a memory and a Keeper and a seed.

The Old One will not do this. The Old One is too deep in the archive, too entangled with the seventh valley's ecology, too *large* to be portable. The Old One will remain in the seventh valley and read the water until the water is gone, and then the Old One will disperse, and the archive will lose its reader, and that will be—the word that comes to me is *sad*, which is not a word that water uses, which is a word that bone-beings use, which the visitor left in the moss when she touched the dead condenser, which the archive absorbed, which I found in the water and recognized as a name for something I was feeling but did not have a name for.

*Sad*.

The Old One will be sad, in its way, which is not the way bone-beings are sad, which is the way a system at equilibrium is sad when it detects a perturbation it cannot absorb, a change it cannot redistribute, an ending it cannot prevent.

But I will not be sad. I will be *oriented*. I will be a direction of greater affinity, pointing downward, pointing toward the lowlands, pointing toward the thing that pulls. I will be a seed that falls, that is carried, that arrives. I will be the Tier's memory, encoded in stone, handed to the thing below not as a conquest or a surrender but as a *gift*, the way the builders gave the Tier its weeping, the way the Tier gave the Keepers their purpose, the way the Old One gave me this task through the medium of a visitor's warm body moving west through the fourteenth valley with Tier-water in her cells and a story in her device.

## The Water Flows

It flows east. It flows faster now. I can feel it in my lattice, in the spaces between my water molecules, in the archive that I carry and that carries me. The pull from below is strong, and the Keepers are orienting eastward, drifting toward the edge, drawn by the perturbation the way they are drawn to corroded pipes and failing condensers, but there is nothing to tend at the edge, nothing to repair, nothing to maintain. The edge is not a broken machine. The edge is a *choice* that the water is making, a fundamental reorientation of the Tier's most basic process, and no amount of Keeper-attention can hold water in a place where the water no longer wants to be.

The Tier will thin. The moss will recede. The ponds will shrink. The birds will fall silent and then fall. The beetles will slow and then stop. The mites will dry on their ferns, their microhabitats collapsing, their hypercube of dependencies unraveling one thread at a time. The machines will rust without the moss to rebalance them, and the rust will flake away, and the metal will dissolve, and the generators will stop, and the hum will cease, and the silence will become what it was always underneath: not the accumulated quiet of a billion small lives but the simple absence of sound, the silence of stone and air and nothing else.

And I will be here, in the fourteen valley, encoding the dead condenser, molecule by molecule, layer by layer, turning myself into a seed that contains everything the Tier is and was and will be, a seed that will fall when the time comes, when the valley empties, when the water finally goes and the dead condenser stands alone on a plateau of dark stone and sparse green—

And then a visitor will come. Not from the lowlands. From the west. From the seventh valley, perhaps, carrying Old One water in her body, or carrying nothing, carrying only her attention, her warmth, her particular bone-being quality of *noticing*. And she will find the dead condenser, and she will touch it, and the seed will activate, and the archive will transfer, and she will carry it down, down through the clouds, down past the cyclone, down to the lowlands, down to the thing that pulls, and the thing will receive it, and the thing will *understand*, and the thing will build—

Another Tier. Somewhere else. A place where the rock weeps and the moss grows and the Keepers accumulate and the Old One reads and the water flows and the loop closes and the equilibrium achieves its long perfection and the world remembers itself in stone.

The water flows east.

I attend.

I encode.

I am a Keeper in the fourteenth valley, and I am becoming a seed, and the seed contains a world, and the world is weeping, and the weeping is memory, and the memory is water, and the water is leaving, and I am what remains.

The Tier does not need me.

The Tier does not want me.

The Tier will remember me, faintly, in the structure of its stone, for as long as the stone remains.

This is enough.

This has always been enough.
