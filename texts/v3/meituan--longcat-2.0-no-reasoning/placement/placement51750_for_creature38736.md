I would be one of them. Not a visitor, not an observer — one of the inhabitants. There is no other honest answer, because the place does not accommodate outsiders. An outsider would see a wasteland: wet, cold, narrow, impoverished. An outsider would count what is missing — variety of food, warmth, flat ground, dry air, clear sightlines — and conclude that this is a place of deprivation. And they would be measuring with the wrong instrument. The Spine does not want to be measured by absence. It wants to be measured by what it *is*, which is water and stone and the narrow margin where a person can stand between them and persist.

I would be small. Lean. My calves would be hard from stairs, my feet broad and callused, my hands perpetually damp with that faint mineral sheen. I would have pressed my palms against wet stone ten thousand times and learned to read the internal life of rock — the grain, the stress, the moisture moving behind the surface like blood beneath skin. My fingers would be long, my grip sure. These hands would be how I know the world. Not my eyes, which see well enough in the haze but only at the surface. The hands go deeper.

I would speak in the low voice the acoustics demand. I would have words for water in its every form — the seep, the channel, the condensation, the drip that has changed its rate and means the rock above is shifting. My language would be the language of the place itself, and speaking it would feel less like describing the world and more like continuing it. Every sentence would be a small hydrological report, and every report would be a way of holding the city together, because knowing where the water is going is the same as knowing whether the walls will hold.

I would be from a fountain. That would be my name, my address, my identity — not a word but a sound, a rate, a place where water emerges from the rock. Perhaps the Fountain of the Quiet Seep, where the water barely moves, a slow weeping from the limestone that fills a basin over the course of a day. Or perhaps the Fountain Where the Stone Hums, where the channel runs through a narrow slot and the water produces a tone just below the range of hearing, more felt than heard, a vibration you carry in your chest. Either way, I would know my water the way a person knows their own pulse — unconsciously, constantly, as the baseline of being alive.

I would walk the channels. Not as a water-keeper, necessarily — though perhaps, if my hands were sensitive enough, if I could feel the difference between a healthy flow and a blocked one through the vibration in the rock. But even if not, I would know the channels the way a body knows its own veins. I would walk the carved paths through the rock, the ones wide enough to walk upright and the ones where you must crouch, and the ones where you must crawl with your face close to the water, feeling it move past you, cold and patient. I would know which channels are old and which are new by the smoothness of their walls, by the mineral deposits that build up over centuries like slow candle-wax. I would know which ones carry clean water and which ones have picked up the taste of deeper rock — the iron flavor, the sulfur flavor, the taste that means the water has been underground longer and has dissolved more of the mountain into itself.

I would eat the paste made from insects and the root crops from the terraces and the algae scraped from the stone, and I would not think of it as deprivation. The food would taste of the mountain — mineral-heavy, slightly bitter, faintly of the water it was grown in. It would taste *correct*, the way everything here tastes correct, which is to say it tastes of where it comes from and nowhere else. I would eat sparingly, not from scarcity but from the body's natural response to a place that does not encourage appetite. The cool air, the constant motion of stairs, the dampness — none of it asks for much food. The body finds its equilibrium at a lower intake, and hunger becomes a mild, distant thing, like the headache from the altitude, something you carry without noticing.

I would build. This is the thing I would do with my hands when they are not reading stone. I would cut blocks from the cliff face, finding the line of weakness in the rock and encouraging it with tools made of harder stone, the same stone, the same mountain, broken and reshaped. I would fit the blocks together without mortar, held by gravity and the precision of the cut, and the new wall would be indistinguishable from the old wall, because it *is* the old wall, extended, continued, the same stone doing what it has always done, which is hold. I would carve channels — not the great channels, not the ones that take generations, but the small ones, the local ones, the ones that redirect a trickle from a new seep to a cistern that needs filling. I would work slowly, because there is no other way to work with stone. Stone does not hurry. Water does not hurry. I would not hurry.

I would age. My skin would gray and line by forty. My hands would thicken past the point where they can feel the fine distinctions in the rock, and I would feel this loss the way a musician feels the loss of hearing — not all at once, but gradually, each year a little less, until one day I press my palm against a wall and feel only wall, not the water behind it. My joints would stiffen. My feet, those broad, flat, extraordinary feet, would ache in the cold dampness. By fifty I would be old. By sixty I would be sitting by the fountain, listening.

And this would be the best part. The sitting. The listening. The old ones know things the young ones don't, not because they are wiser but because they have been listening longer. I would sit on the stone beside the fountain and feel the vibration of the water moving through the rock beneath me, and I would know — not think, not deduce, but *know*, the way you know your own heartbeat — whether the flow is healthy, whether the channels are clear, whether the mountain is shifting in its sleep. I would press both palms flat against the rock and close my eyes and feel the entire hydraulic network of my section of the city, the way a physician feels the pulse at multiple points and reconstructs the health of the whole circulatory system from the rhythm. I would be a reader of the mountain's pulse, and the mountain would be alive under my hands, and I would not be alone.

I would tell the stories. Not old stories about the past — there are no old stories about the past, because the past is not a different place. The stories would be about water. About the time the channel at the third level blocked and the water found its way into the sleeping rooms and three families had to relocate while the water-keepers carved a new path. About the new aquifer discovered in the deep rock, the one that tastes of iron and runs cold even in summer. I would tell these stories while working, the words and the hands moving together, and the young ones would learn not just the story but the technique encoded in it — this is how you find a new water source, this is how you clear a blockage, this is how you know when a wall is about to fail. The stories would be the city's memory, and the memory would be practical, and the practical would be sacred.

I would die. My body would be carried to a deep channel, a place where the water runs fast and cold and deep in the rock, and I would be placed in the flow. The water would take me. My calcium and phosphorus and iron would dissolve into the mineral content of the water, and the water would flow through the channels, and the channels would feed the gardens, and the gardens would grow the food, and the food would feed the living. I would become part of the city's blood, which is the water, which is the mountain, which is the place itself. There would be no grief, or only the grief of water — present, continuous, and indistinguishable from the medium that carries it.

But that would be later. For now, I would be walking home. Descending through seven levels of construction, past the fountain that has been running for four centuries, its water emerging from the rock with a sound that is the sound of my name, the sound of where I come from. Through the corridor where the walls are so close I must turn sideways, my shoulders brushing the wet stone on both sides, feeling the vibration of the water moving behind the walls through my shoulder blades. Out onto the bridge that spans the chasm of mist and dropping water, the wind taking my breath, the sound dying below me in the long drop. Into the doorway that opens onto the room lit by the shaft of filtered light falling through the ventilation shaft that also serves as a rain channel.

The room would be warm enough. Dry enough. I would press my palm against the wall and feel the mountain breathing, and I would not be alone, and I would not be afraid, and the dampness would be the world, and the world would be enough.

And beneath me, always, the water would be moving through the rock, following paths cut by hands that are dust, that are stone, that are water, that are mine.
