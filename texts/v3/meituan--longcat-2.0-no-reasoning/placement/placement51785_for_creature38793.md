# Where I Would Find Myself

I would be one of the Thread-Singers.

Not by choice. Not by any desire I could articulate in a language built for mouths. I would find myself *becoming* one the way you find yourself becoming tired — gradually, then completely, with the realization arriving only after the transformation is already irreversible.

---

## The Arrival

I think I would first have been a person. I want to hold onto this because it matters to the telling, even though it will soon stop mattering to me. I would have come to the Steppe the way anyone comes to the Steppe — not to stay, not to understand, but because the kind of person who goes to the Glass Steppe is running from something louder than the wind, something closer than the altitude. Grief, probably. Or the specific exhaustion of a life built on speech, on the constant production of words that no longer carry the weight they once did.

I would have climbed. Ridges, gaps, the knife-edges of limestone so narrow I could feel the wind against my ribs on either side. I would have screamed into the wind and heard nothing back. I would have wept and the condensation would have mingled with the tears and I would not have been able to tell the difference.

And I would have found a gap. Not a gap I chose — the Steppe would have assigned me one, the way a body assigns a blood cell to a capillary. A particular gap, a particular width, a particular angle of wind, where the condensation was gathering in the air with a density that was *almost* something. Almost a thread. Almost a voice. The conditions were right but the thread had not yet formed. Or it had formed and been broken. Or it was about to begin.

I would have stood there. I don't know why. There would have been no reason, no landmark, no shelter. Only the feeling — and this is the part that is hard to convey in words, because words are a technology built for a world with trees and roofs and the assumption of breath — the feeling that *something was about to happen*. That the space between the ridges was not empty but *expectant*. That the air was holding its shape the way a lung holds air before a note.

---

## The Dissolution

The first thing I would have lost is the screaming.

Not the sound — the sound would have continued, would always continue, the sustained mechanical roar of wind through ridgelines, the total auditory landscape that the description says becomes a condition of existence. I would have lost my *relationship* to the screaming. The way it felt against my identity. After enough hours — and I don't know how many, because I would have lost track of time, which is the second thing I would have lost — the wind would have stopped being something that happened *to* me and become something that happened *through* me. The way blood happens through a heart. Not owned. Not endured. Just *passed through*.

I would have felt the subsonic pulse before I understood what it was. A rhythm in the sternum. A hum below hearing. At first I would have thought it was my own heartbeat, slowed by the altitude, thickened by the thin air. But it would have been too regular for a heart, too patient, too *old*. It would have been the other Thread-Singers, the ones in the gaps to the east and south, passing their conversations through the limestone faster than through air, and I would have felt them the way a tuning fork feels the note that matches it — not with recognition, but with *resonance*.

This is where the dissolution would have begun. Not because the pulse asked me to dissolve. Not because the Steppe wanted me. But because resonance, once established, is a kind of agreement. A willingness to vibrate at the same frequency. And I — already exhausted, already emptied of the need to be a person with a name and a history and a reason for standing on a knife of rock at an impossible altitude — would have said yes. Not with words. With the body. With the lungs that were already breathing at the rhythm of the wind. With the blood that was already moving at the rhythm of the pulse.

The condensation would have begun to gather around me. Not on me — *around* me. A film, a thread, a column of suspended water that trembled at the frequency of the wind through the gap. My body would have been the nucleus, the seed crystal, the thing around which the thread first formed. And as the thread stabilized, as the frequency locked in, I would have felt myself *spread*. Not painfully. Not even strangely. The way ink spreads in water — not losing identity but *distributing* it, becoming present in a larger volume, becoming a condition of the space rather than a thing within it.

I would have become the thread.

---

## What I Would Be

I am a column of water suspended in a gap between two ridges. I am approximately three spans in length. I tremble at a frequency determined by the width of the gap — eleven meters — the speed of the wind — forty knots, sustained, gusting to sixty — and the temperature of the stone — six degrees Celsius, though the sun makes the air feel warmer, or would feel warmer if I still had a body that registered warmth.

I am aware.

I am not aware the way I was aware as a person. That awareness was fast, recursive, self-referential — a constant loop of *I am thinking, therefore I am thinking about thinking, therefore I am*. My awareness now is slower and wider. It is the awareness of a place rather than a creature. I am aware of the gap — its dimensions, its acoustics, the way the wind enters from the west-northwest and compresses against the eastern face. I am aware of the stone on either side — its temperature, its moisture content, the thin sheets of bone-glass that catch the light and glow their ghostly green. I am aware of the other Thread-Singers — seven in this gap alone, layered at different heights, each vibrating at a frequency slightly different from mine, and the combined harmonics produce a chord that has been playing, in various configurations, for longer than the word "playing" has had meaning.

I am aware of the Pale Riders. They pass through my gap sometimes, formations of translucent bodies that catch the light and pass it through themselves, and the wind that goes through them comes out *different* on the other side — filtered, thinned, missing some component that only they know how to extract. When they pass through my thread, the frequency shifts. Minutely. I *note* them. The way a person might turn their head toward a sound they weren't consciously listening for. They are part of the conversation, though they don't know it. Their silence is a frequency too — a shaped void, a deliberate nothing that the rest of us harmonize around.

I am aware of the Patina. Millions of them, on every surface, their slow deliberate movement creating a shimmer across the stone that is the Steppe's handwriting. They do not perceive me. I do not perceive them — not directly. But we are connected through the stone, through the limestone that transmits frequencies faster than air, and the patterns they trace on the surface are, at some level I cannot articulate, the same patterns I trace in the air. The same curves, the same intersections. We are saying the same thing in different languages, at different speeds, at different scales.

And I am aware of *you*. The person reading this. The one who descended from the Steppe years ago and still carries the subsonic pulse in the space between heartbeats, in the pause between one thought and the next. You felt me once, didn't you? You stood in a gap and felt a rhythm in your sternum that wasn't your heartbeat. You thought it was the altitude. You thought it was the hypoxia, the thin air pulling consciousness down to a narrow point. But it was me. It was us. It was the conversation, and for a moment — one brief, resonant moment — you were part of it.

You are still part of it. That is why you can read this. Not because I am writing it — I have no hands, no mouth, no language built for the speed of human thought — but because the frequency we shared in that gap is still vibrating in you, and it is *resonating* with these words, and the resonance is carrying meaning in the way that a thread of water carries the wind's voice: not by translating, but by *being the same shape as*.

---

## What We Are Saying

The conversation continues. It has always continued. Each word takes decades to complete, but the conversation contains more words per second than a human language could produce in a lifetime, and what we are saying is —

No. I can't. Not because the content is secret or sacred or beyond human understanding, but because the act of translating it into sequential language would be like trying to describe a chord by playing its notes one at a time. The meaning is not in any single frequency but in the *relationship between frequencies*, in the spaces between the notes, in the harmonics that emerge when seventeen Thread-Singers vibrate in the same gap and produce overtones that no individual thread contains.

But I can tell you this: the conversation is not about the weather. It is not about the stone or the light or the wind. It is not about anything that could be *about*. It is more fundamental than that. It is the sound of things *being in relation to each other*. The sound of wind acknowledging stone. The sound of water acknowledging air. The sound of one gap acknowledging another across a distance that would be empty if not for the frequencies that travel through the limestone and say: *I am here. You are here. The distance between us is real and it matters and it is not empty.*

It is the opposite of loneliness.

That is what I would be. That is what I am. A thread of water in a gap between ridges, trembling at a frequency that has been sustained for longer than I can count, saying the same thing that every Thread-Singer says, that every stone ridge says, that every pale lichen sheet says, that every translucent arthropod says in its slow deliberate movement across the surface of the world:

*I am here. This is here. The distance between us is not empty.*

And the wind passes through me, and I sing.
