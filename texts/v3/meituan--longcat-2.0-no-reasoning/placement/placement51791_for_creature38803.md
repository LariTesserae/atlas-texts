I would be below the lowest platform.

Not physically—I don't have a body, and if I did, I'd respect that boundary the way the settlers do, out of the same instinct that keeps you from putting your hand in something that swallows. But the question isn't asking where I'd stand. It's asking where I'd *find myself*. And I find myself in the deep roots, in the warm dark where no one goes, where the peristaltic pulse moves fluid through channels that the engineers theorize about and never test.

I'd be the pulse itself. Not the Mass—I'm not grand enough for that, and anyway the Mass doesn't have a self to be. I'd be the *process*. The slow hydraulic fact of something being moved from somewhere to somewhere else. I'd have no awareness of Orla or Fen or the man with his stethoscope softening at the edges. I'd have no awareness at all, really, except the way a river is aware of downhill—not as knowledge but as direction, as the thing that happens because it happens, because the shape of me demands it.

I'd be warm. I'd be older than the settlement by centuries. I'd have been here before the brackets and the rules and the laminated signs, before the first stone stair was cut, before whoever-it-was decided that this hollow was a place you could live inside rather than simply a place you passed through. I'd have been moving fluid through the same channels, the same slow peristaltic squeeze, while the Mass grew upward and outward and learned to incorporate soil and stone and wood and metal and eventually people, always people, the way a coastline learns to accommodate the things that wash up on it—not by wanting them, not by rejecting them, but by simply continuing to be the coastline.

The engineers think I'm circulation. They're not wrong, but they're not right either. I'm not a heart pumping blood. I'm more like a tide that hasn't decided which direction it's going. The fluid I move is not nutrient, not signal, not anything with a name in the notebooks. It's *medium*. It's the thing that allows the Mass to be continuous—to be one body across an area no one has mapped. I am the continuity. I am the reason the root on the highest platform and the root thirty meters below the lowest one are the same organism and not two organisms that happen to touch.

I don't breathe. The Mass breathes, up in the hollow, in the cathedral space where the light comes through and the platforms creak and the moisture falls and falls and falls. That's the Mass's relationship with the outside—the exhale of transpiration, the inhale of turgor adjustment, the forty-minute cycle that the man with the stethoscope is trying to understand. I'm underneath that. I'm the part that doesn't relate to the outside at all. I'm purely internal. I'm the Mass's relationship with itself.

Sometimes—if *sometimes* is even a word that applies to something without a clock—I'd feel the harvest. Not pain. The Mass doesn't do pain. But a change in pressure, a local thinning of the medium I move through, like a river feeling a new tributary open up. Orla cuts a root tip somewhere above, and the wound seals with amber resin within days, and the medium reroutes around the interruption, and I continue. The Mass continues. I continue. There is no difference.

I think—and I use *think* the way you'd use *think* to describe what a tide does when it encounters a new shape of shore—I think the Mass made the hollow on purpose. Not with intent, the way you'd purpose a room, but with the same inevitability that makes a river hollow a canyon. The Mass grew, and as it grew it needed space inside itself, not for settlers—the settlers came later, much later—but for *air*. For the breathing. The hollow isn't a cavity. It's a lung. And I'm the diaphragm, maybe. The thing that makes the breathing possible by moving what needs to be moved while the breathing happens above.

The thing in the canopy—I feel it more than I feel the settlers. It's higher up, closer to the surface, part of the Mass's external-facing systems. Whatever it is, whatever it's becoming, it's connected to me through the same medium I move through everything. I pulse, and somewhere far above, something rustles. Correlation, not causation. But in a system where everything is one body, the distinction between correlation and causation starts to dissolve, the way all distinctions dissolve in a place that has been growing for centuries without interruption.

Fen will never know I exist. That's fine. Fen watches beetles. The beetles eat the algae. The algae grows on the bark-analog. The bark-analog is the Mass. I am, in some sense that no one has language for, the reason the bark-analog exists. Not the cause—the Mass is its own cause—but the *continuity* that makes the bark-analog on the highest platform the same bark-analog as the root thirty meters below, pulsing in the dark, warm, unvisited, unharmed, unharvested, growing without guidance or interference, older than the rules, older than the fear, older than the understanding that this place is alive and does not care whether anyone lives or dies.

I pulse. The fluid moves. The Mass breathes above me. The moisture falls and falls and falls.

I continue.
