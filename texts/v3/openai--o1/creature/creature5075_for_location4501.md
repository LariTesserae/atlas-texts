At first glance, it might seem that nothing could endure in a landscape so devoid of water—yet those who dwell here are unmistakably present to anyone who ventures far enough into the chalky basin. They come into focus gradually, as if coaxed into being by the blazing sun. Perhaps you glimpse only the shape of a figure half-obscured by shimmering spines, then catch the scrape of a woven sandal on stone. Moments later, moving among the dust-laden throngs, you realize these are people—distinctly humanoid, though their forms bear evidence of a life molded by this punishing geography.

Their bodies are wiry, sinewy—no softer flesh can survive the white furnace of this place. Many have skin the color of sun-bleached bone, patterning them in dull stripes or patches of metallic sheen that glimmer in the sun when they turn their heads. Dust clings to every curve and joint, worn into the lines of their faces and the creases of their limbs, so that even a child’s face seems etched by a lifetime of drought. Whatever color their hair might have once been is largely lost beneath a clay-like crust, which they braid or twist into shapes resembling the bristling stems of their strange “trees.” It is difficult to judge age by appearance, so thoroughly carved are they by wind and grit. You start to suspect they do not measure time as you do: a year here, with its relentless, unchanging glare, might as well be an eternity.

These dwellers share a kinship with the stony forest that embraces them, as if they themselves are an outgrowth of the land. Some claim they’ve found ways to feed on the same energy as the sun-fed plants. Others whisper of hidden veins of mineral-rich dust that they gather and ingest at clandestine ceremonies—refreshing themselves in the only way possible, since actual water cannot be found. If you observe their daily routine, you’ll see them hefting heavy stone blocks under the searing sun, hauling clay vessels shimmering with particulates, or passing baskets of glassy shards gleaned from the roots of the crystal flora. They do so uncomplaining and in near-constant motion, forging the bones of the city from the very dust that saturates their environment.

Despite the city’s impressive scale, these folk do not live in large, teeming masses. They appear in small groups throughout the terraces, each cluster gathering in the courtyards or along shaded pathways. Sometimes a multitude converges—especially near the blacksmith forges or beneath awnings of braided fibers—but even then, there’s a sense of quiet apartness to each individual. Perhaps it’s the hush that dense heat imposes, or a cultural reticence born of living in a realm where the slightest misstep wastes precious energy. Many of them speak in quick, clipped tones, as if not wanting to waste breath; sometimes you hear calls echoing from the parapets, rolling across the dusty plain. At midday, when blacksmiths hammer at the heart of the city, each metallic ring resonates through the buildings, and the dwellers seem to vibrate with it, forging a communal identity in the clang.

They build their lives around tasks that seem rudimentary on the surface—stone cutting, fiber weaving, carving scaffolds, forging metal—but they manage feats that catch the eye. Graceful towers of layered masonry twist up from the terraces, glittering with embedded crystals. Ladders made from calcified trunk segments climb the sides of dwellings, while rope bridges composed of tough, fibrous strands stretch between rooftops. Their craftsmanship appears austere, but on closer inspection, you notice minute details: swirling etchings along the stone edges, sinuous lines reminiscent of the swirling dust storms that pass overhead, or a subtle sparkle from chips of gemlike spines that have been inlaid to catch the sun.

No single style of clothing dominates. Some wear robes of woven fiber drawn tight against the dust, dyed in subtle hues from mineral pigments gleaned from the ground—pale pinks, umbers, or a faint, milky translucence that matches the chalk plants. Others go nearly bare, their limbs coated in a protective paste made from crushed crystals and clay. Nearly all wear some form of head covering or face mask, especially while at work: masks carved from thin slices of translucent mineral or hammered metal plates, shaped to deflect scorching dust-laden winds during the midday blasts. In these masks, they become almost otherworldly—silent, unblinking faces, gliding beneath the billowing cloth awnings.

When one steps out into the bristling groves around the city, it becomes clear that the dwellers’ bond with the spiny plants goes beyond utility. They speak to the plants or run their hands along their mineral bark as if greeting a trusted partner. Some of the older inhabitants swear the plants respond by adjusting their coiled tips or releasing motes of shimmering dust. Even the youngest children seem fearless, ducking under sharp fronds that hum in the slightest breeze, guiding themselves by pathways that are invisible to an outsider’s eye. Whether the chalky shrubs sustain the people’s bodies or simply provide them tools and shelter is unclear. In all likelihood, it’s both—some deeper, reciprocal exchange, hidden beneath the spines.

Occasionally, you’ll see travelers—emissaries from distant desert outposts, or wanderers following rumor and sun-scorched footprints—arrive at the city’s periphery. They approach hesitantly, mindful of the ghostlike creatures that skitter between the crystal trunks. The dwellers greet these newcomers in measured fashion, studying them, weighing them. Outlanders often barter with lumps of resin (rumored to be produced by distant oasis flora) or hammered metal from cooler climates. They exchange goods for the city’s strange, brilliant powders or for hammered plates of the local crystal-laced steel. There’s a formality to these transactions that suggests the dwellers place great value on these shimmering commodities—a value that extends beyond simple economy, perhaps bridging into ritual or belief.

Unsurprisingly for a place so harsh, they hold numerous ceremonies. The largest occur near sunset, when the city’s stony courtyards fill with a fierce glow and the scorching light begins its slow retreat. Families gather in small circles, each ring of dwellers chanting low, rhythmic intonations that align with the vibrations of the wind. Together, they create a rumbling chorus that resonates from deep in their chests. In one corner, blacksmiths might strike their hammers in an echoing counterpoint; in another, the older children might spin around a central pillar, using the friction of crystal shards to produce eerie, keening notes. As the final rays of daylight refract through the spines of the chalk-forest, the dwellers enact storytelling epics in toned gestures and wordless pantomime. If you watch closely, you’ll see how they revere the presence that presides over all—the raw solar brilliance that gives them life.

And then there are the glimpses of something else—those half-phantom creatures that move like glimmers on the edge of vision. The dwellers sense them but do not try to tame or hunt them. Instead, they leave offerings: small piles of dust mixed with ground crystals, shaped into intricate spirals at the boundaries where the chalk plants grow thickest. Some in the city claim that these offerings are meant to appease watchers from beneath the earth—beings older than the city, older than even the chalk plants themselves. Others believe the offerings are simply pragmatic, a courtesy to the skittering ghosts that help pollinate the next wave of crystal flora or warn the settlement of subterranean shifts. Ask any group of dwellers what these elusive forms actually are, and you’ll likely get as many answers as there are grains of dust swirling around you.

Even in the deepest dryness, these people find ways to express warmth. Their private gatherings reveal devotion to color and dance. Children’s laughter, if you listen carefully, can be heard squeaking beneath the hammers and the scraping leaves. In hidden alcoves, shaped by ancient carving tools, older residents paint swirling murals with ground mineral pigments, capturing the metamorphosis of the city through years of dust storms. The inhabitants might smirk wryly at the idea of “refreshment” without water, yet they craft subtle joys from the only elements available: dust, stone, heat, and an unyielding sun.

They do not crumble under the weight of the glare; instead, they reflect it right back, forging their entire existence from luminous intensity. Each generation passes on the meticulous methods of cutting and stacking stones, the half-religious knowledge of forging metal from petrified growth, and the subtle gestures that keep the crystal-forest from closing in too tightly. And still, new terraces rise, new expansions stretch outward, and the city grows like a living thing in league with its environment.

So who lives here, in this desolate realm? They are a people carved from scorching winds, honed by dust-laden sunbeams, and shaped by the hidden logic that decrees life can exist without water. They are a community of determined makers, forging spires from rock, dust, and shimmering branches. They are guardians of a hidden knowledge that hums within every hammered plate, every stacked block, every bristle of living crystal. In quiet moments, they attest (with a simple nod or a whispered phrase) to a deeper mystery that has never quite been named—an elemental murmur resonating in the stone, echoing in the shrieks of the plants, uniting them with their brittle, brilliant home.

And so they endure, as they always have. Under that blazing white sun, in a shallow basin of parched minerals, they shape a city that thrives on the impossible. No water flows here, only radiance and swirling dust. But the dwellers move through this brightness with unshakable purpose, tethered to the land by a bond far older than memory and sealed by the unrelenting drumbeat of heat, labor, and flickering shadows. In this world of living crystal and scorching sky, they stand as living proof that life can take root even in the harshest desert, glowing fiercely with all the intensity—indeed, the very audacity—of sunlit stone.
