They suggest no one is here, do they? The emptiness feels absolute at first, marked by crusted reeds and the silence that lingers in every watery corridor. But stay awhile, longer than just a few heartbeats, and things begin to reveal themselves. Perhaps they were always in plain sight; perhaps they exist just beyond our natural senses. In a place lacking day or night, it’s no surprise that life, too, might linger in the penumbra: never in full view, never entirely gone.

Start with the almost-motion you see out of the corner of your eye. A ripple on the still water—yet there is no breeze. A dark shape hunched near a half-collapsed gateway—though you see nothing when you turn to look. At first, you suspect illusions. But illusions seldom leave footprints in the shallow muck. Illusions do not set the pools quivering, as if something heavy has just stepped away. And illusions do not breathe warmth onto the back of your neck in a place that otherwise lacks any pulse. So something must be there.

Some say they are the city’s leftover memories, half-formed silhouettes that emerge from the watery reflections and take shape for a few moments, then collapse again into glistening pools. Others describe them as parasites in the stone, intangible spirits that feed on the faint fungal glow and the slow movement of tides. Still others whisper a more complex truth: that the city itself is alive, and these manifestations are simply its thoughts drifting into physical form. None of these theories feel sufficient once you stand in the corridor alone, the water lapping at your ankles, the hush pressing around you. You sense that whatever walks these halls can be many things at once.

If you grow still enough—or if you wander too deeply—these presences begin to clarify. They linger in deserted corners, where damaged basalt arches form pockets of gloom. They do not speak, or if they do, the sound is lost in the perpetual hush. Their forms are rarely solid for more than the blink of an eye. At times, they appear as silhouettes shaped vaguely like hunched figures—head, shoulders, arms—but shimmering as though they’re fashioned of black water or congealed mist. Watch them from a distance, and you might see a slow turning of a head, the twitch of an arm. Yet, peer too intently, and they flicker out, leaving you with only the glistening stone.

Then there are the watchers who do not appear humanoid at all. Instead, you catch glimpses of undulating coils near the ceiling, drifting through the condensation as though gravity were optional. Later, you find the same shapes standing in murky half-light at the edges of a submerged courtyard, half melted into the watery reflections. At times, they resemble lengthy tendrils of fungus or the slender roots of the reeds. At others, they coil into themselves, forming a configuration that suggests heads or multiple eyes—though like everything else here, the details remain fleeting. If you stare too boldly, the watchers fade, their outlines dissolving as if they were only made of drifting spores.

Some inhabitants are more subtle still. They live in the fungus and mold that lines the basalt walls. Their existence is not discrete forms but rather a collaborative network—the entire colony pulsing in response to changes in heat or moisture, exhaling a faint luminescent haze with each breath. You may notice how the glow intensifies as you pass by, as though the colony reacts to your presence. In the moments before a warm gust of that unpredictable wind, it’s almost as if the fungus subtly brightens, anticipating the swirl. It clings to the ancient masonry, bridging cracks and corners, sustaining itself on the endless damp and the city’s ineffable energy. Later, you might realize the fungus is no mere background detail but an entity that may spread or contract, a living tapestry whose slow shifts signal the city’s own heartbeat.

Elsewhere, there might be voices—though “voice” is too strong a word. Now and again, you sense a thrumming in the floor beneath your feet, a resonance traveling through the old basalt. It could be tectonic movement, as you first assume; but there’s a pattern to it if you listen. The pulses come in slow triplets, then a pause. A hush. Then a single, sharp note. Possibly a signal passed among hidden dwellers that do not walk or crawl but instead exist within the layers of ancient stone. Are they people? Spirits? Something entirely unlike either? You might never see them, but their conversation hums softly through the corridors, muffled, omnipresent.

And then—if you dare press on, deeper than most would—there are the half-living guardians of the labyrinth: tall figures that remain inert for hours, seemingly pieces of basalt sculpture, until your footstep lands on the wrong patch of stone. A drop of water echoes just so, and you sense movement in your peripheral vision. They stand at angles that appear physically impossible, leaning too far to one side or bowing backward at the waist, yet never toppling. Their surfaces are coarse with age, patches of filmy fungus adorning their shoulders and limbs. Their faces—if they can be called faces—are featureless planes, or sometimes just shallow hollows that glisten with watery residue. They mark thresholds: doorways that might lead into deeper realms of the city. Once you cross the threshold, a presence passes behind you, the air shifts, and those watchers are gone. Whether they intend to protect or warn or simply observe remains a mystery, but their quiet vigilance hints at some purposeful intelligence.

There are other inhabitants you might never see directly—but occasionally, they leave evidence behind. Sometimes, the water level is altered, as if a great tail or limb had displaced a significant volume of fluid. Other times, you discover patterns in the thick algae: spiral imprints or elongated footprints that vanish after a single loop around a courtyard. Once in a while, you find shredded layers of that lacquer-like paving material, as though something was testing it or gnawing on it. Follow these signs far enough, and you may catch a glimpse of a sinuous motion disappearing around a corner—a fluid silhouette gliding along a corridor that vanishes off into black water deeper than you’d imagined. The rumor goes that these shapes are old as the city itself, amphibious or perhaps even aquatic, dwelling in labyrinthine tunnels far below the collapsed buildings above, breathing in the darkness just as easily as we breathe air.

All these entities, creatures, and presences are not mutually exclusive. They coexist in a fragile ecosystem built of gloom and humidity, illusions and subtle realities. Some dwell in direct synergy, like the fungus and the watchers; some remain wholly separate, silent guardians who do not care for the others, or possibly do not even notice them. You might see an entire cluster of watchers move past one of those basalt guardian-figures without reaction. Or you might hear sudden, sharp vibrations from deep below, sending the watchers scattering into corners, as if in fear or reverence. Each being lives by rhythms largely indecipherable to an outsider, guided by the timeless heartbeat of this submerged city.

It’s not a society in any sense we understand—no merchants, no children, no leaders. There might have been something like that once, in ages forgotten, but now only these remnants occupy the watery ruins. They do not speak of gods or ancestors, nor display hierarchies in the open. Instead, their existence seems woven into the city’s own cyclical patterns: the slow drip of condensation that calls a group of watchers to gather in the gloom, the abrupt wind that makes the fungus clutch the walls more tightly, that intangible ebb and flow that draws the amphibious silhouettes out of hidden pools.

Look deeper still, and you may suspect there’s a single tapestry of life connecting them all—fungus, watchers, guardians, shifting silhouettes—like threads of one living labyrinth, each expression arising from the city’s own ancient consciousness. The place is so old, so drenched in memory and time, that it might well dream new inhabitants into being. Or perhaps these folk are the ghostly echoes of original citizens, continuing the motions of their daily tasks in forms too blurred for us to recognize fully. You might see a watcher stoop near a collapsed archway, as though rummaging for something, before vanishing in a flicker. Or two silhouettes stand face to face in the gloom, arms half-raised in gestures of either greeting or farewell. Moments later, they dissolve into the swirling wind, leaving behind only the memory of their brief congregation.

When you finally leave—if you can—these denizens remain behind, continuing their quiet circle dances through corridors half drowned in black water. Though to call them “alive” may err on the side of generosity, they are present nonetheless. They move with intention or instinct or something so inhuman we can only guess. They watch, react, drift. Sometimes, they almost interact—reaching out a limb or a shadowy extension as though to note your presence. Even if the city is as close to dead as a place can be, it is not absent of consciousness. These beings are the city’s breath, its echo, its living vestige of memory, haunting the labyrinth of half-submerged streets. They are all that remains of whatever once thrived here. Or, just possibly, they are the city itself, treading water on the edge of existence.

And so, in a place where no day or night divides time, life endures in forms that defy easy explanation. Roots of what once was, blossoms of what might still be, or illusions too real to dismiss—there is no single occupant in these ruins. Instead, there is a silent tapestry of watchers, wardens, and ephemeral forms, all drawn together by the faint pulse of a city that still breathes in darkness.
