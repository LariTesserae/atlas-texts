They do not call themselves anything; names would suggest something too bound by place or time. But here, in this sun-blasted, water-channeled domain, they dwell—unhurried, unchanging, much like their surroundings. To see one of them at first is to doubt if there is anyone here at all. The land seems unpeopled, populated only by vines, birds, frogs, and the like—all artificial or engineered by long-forgotten hands. And yet, in the stillness of midday (if midday even applies in a realm without dawn or dusk), faint shapes drift at the edges of sight: watchers, caretakers, or perhaps simply neighbors to this perpetual hush.

If you try to follow them, you discover that they walk in the shade and angles formed by the massive slabs, choosing corridors of near-blindness where the brightness overhead cannot pierce. At a distance, their outlines blend with the patterns etched into the clay or stone: geometric lines that might be part of some archaic plan. Only when you move closer do you realize that these beings match the environment almost too well. Their skin—assuming “skin” is the right term—looks as though formed of the same hammered materials that compose the ground. The color is a warm, rusted red, shot through with faint, iridescent flecks that catch the glimmer of the water channels. Yet when they step into the light, you can see hints of finely wrought artistry: swirling designs along their arms or shoulders, crisp angles echoing the blocky channels that run underfoot.

They are not numerous, but you sense them everywhere once you recognize the telltale signs: the hush of footsteps that do not quite ring hollow, the slight shift of metal leaves overhead that signals someone passing beneath. Each figure moves seemingly without hurry, as though time were not a factor. You might watch one stoop to examine a fissure in a paving slab, pressing their palm or fingertips into the crack. In that contact, a soft glow might pulse—some energy or intention bridging the gap. Then, with a methodical care, the figure shifts the broken edge minutely, adjusting it, smoothing it so that the seam blends seamlessly with the rest. It’s as if each inhabitant here acts as a caretaker, ensuring that nothing ever changes yet remains perpetually pristine.

In design, they are reminiscent of sculptures—humanoid at a glance, though their proportions can vary in odd ways. Some have elongated arms that allow them to reach the upper ledges where vines coil. Others are short and stocky, built to kneel by the water channels, tracing and retracing the mortar lines that hold the slabs in perfect tessellation. A sense of mild curiosity seems to animate them: occasionally one will lean closer to the artificial vines, pinch a leaf between carefully articulated fingers, and listen, as if the leaf emits a tone or whisper beyond human hearing.

They speak rarely, if at all. In place of speech, they communicate—when they must—through subtle gestures or the echo of a gentle, resonant hum. That hum can ripple through the watery channels and cause tiny vibrations in the hammered clay beneath your feet. It’s like a signal that resonates across the domain, a reminder of a deeper harmony that binds everything here. And though no one utters words, you sense that each caretaker understands the domain intimately, as if following a set of unspoken instructions laid down eons ago. 

In the reflective water basins, you might catch a glimpse of their faces: finely molded features that mix abstract geometry with a hint of the organic. Their eyes—no larger than yours, but oddly angled—seem to look through the realm rather than at it, like one scanning for fluctuations in a system of unknown complexity. At times, you see a caretaker’s gaze fix on some small glitch: a vine that stretches out too far or an insect that flickers like a broken hologram. When that happens, the caretaker steps forward and, with near-reverent precision, adjusts the errant detail. A mechanical frog’s croak might have repeated too soon; the caretaker pauses, tilts its head, and resets something in that creature’s thin metal thorax so that the cycle continues exactly, flawlessly, once more.

At intervals (though “intervals” has little meaning here), they congregate around the largest channels and basins, forming loose circles. In these gatherings, you can almost hear the domain breathe. The vines’ leaves rustle in the still air, as though responding to an unspoken direction. The caretaker figures gaze at one another in a hush that feels like a vigil. Some dip their hands into the water, letting droplets run along their sculpted arms. Others trace small glyphs on one another’s shoulders or necks—perhaps ritual sigils, perhaps ancient reminders of their purpose. Then, without ceremony, they disperse again.

They do not eat or sleep, at least not in any sense we would recognize. Nor do they seem susceptible to the unrelenting heat that presses on the land. If you watch them long enough, you notice that they share the same timeless quality as everything else: no sign of fatigue, no slump of weariness, no flicker of boredom. They move as one might flip through the pages of an unwritten text, each caretaker adopting a role in the upkeep of this living, mechanical tapestry.

Sometimes, you catch them gazing into the distance, toward the horizon where the patterned slabs fade into a shimmer of heat. There is a quiet yearning in those stances, a slight tilt of the head that suggests they may be looking for something—some change, some newness, some evolution that never comes. Yet, if that longing troubles them, they do not show it beyond that momentary pause. They only turn back to their silent tasks, calibrating a crooked spout or repairing a piece of hammered filigree. 

Though each caretaker might have once been made of the same clay and water as these slabs—sculpted in a ritual that predated memory—any distinction between maker and made has long since dissolved. Something animates them from within, a power or essence that defies the dryness of baked clay. You suspect they are as old as this realm, possibly responsible for building it in the first place, shaping corners so square and vines so precise that they almost trick the mind into calling it “natural.”

Were you ever to speak to them (somehow), you might ask how long they have been here, or who taught them their craft. But the question would likely go unanswered. Their eyes, if anything, would reflect your own face back to you, as finely polished as a still pond. Perhaps they do not remember a time before the bright sun that never moves and the water that always drips into the same basins. Perhaps they do not question their perpetual vigilance. Or perhaps they simply know you could never grasp the shape of their history, dwelling in a timeline that loops beyond comprehension.

And so they remain as the realm’s quiet custodians, the ones who ensure each channel flows, each leaf remains unblemished, each mechanical insect continues its undersized whirr and click. In this place where time is pinned in place and illusions can peel back the veneer of reality for a moment before snapping it shut again, the dwellers—these living sculptures—stand vigilant. They are neither wholly organic nor entirely fabricated, neither prisoners of the world nor free from it. They are, in every sense, the realm itself made animate: shaped from hammered earth and watery light, bound to the pulse of an ancient design that carries on, unchanged, bathing in a radiance that never dips below the horizon.
