They are reluctant to appear at first, the dwellers of this hidden realm. The place seems so entirely given over to pipes and rivets, metal scaffolding and dripping condensate, that you could initially believe there is nothing sentient or aware here at all—only the tireless hum of machinery. But if one lingers and listens, and watches the growth of leaves and rivulets of water, life begins to reveal itself in subtle, unexpected forms.

Some of that life is small, nearly secret in its habits. Among the vines tangling around support beams, one might glimpse clusters of pale mites that scuttle away the moment they feel your presence. Fleeting specks, they thrive in the humid recesses, feeding on the faint dustings of organic matter shed by the plants. A sharp eye may catch them swarming the underleaf surfaces at feeding time, then dispersing with silent alarm when a vent releases its hiss of steam. Tiny amphibians—translucent-skinned newts or squat little frogs—establish themselves in shallow puddles, comfortably warm as if living in a giant greenhouse. Their calls are muted, drowned by the churn of turbines, but at quiet intervals—rare though they are—a gentle chirrup echoes behind the metallic drone, a living pulse in an otherwise mechanical heartbeat.

Further overhead, shimmering insects hunt among the vines, minute winged silhouettes that hover in the glow of the artificial lights. At times, they look like flecks of falling dust, until you notice how they dance and dart with obvious intention. Their wings cast tiny rainbows in the harsh fluorescent illumination. They flit about in brief arcs, rarely resting in one spot for long. Perhaps someone introduced them long ago to pollinate the vegetation, or perhaps they’re an accidental stowaway that has adapted to this environment. In either case, they are ephemeral—appearing suddenly, vanishing swiftly, seldom observed for more than a passing moment.

This place has no large animals patrolling its catwalks, nor any sign of a caretaker with a steady hand. But it would be a mistake to think it is unattended. Something else abides here: a presence both invisible and constant. Call it the “Custodian,” if you must give it a name. It is not a single creature but an intricately maintained system built into the very skeleton of the metal labyrinth. Remote sensors rasp and click behind panels that have never been opened in your lifetime. Automated arms, so slender they nearly vanish amid the tangle of conduit, extend from hidden compartments to clear blockages in the water lines. Mechanical eyes—small lenses covered by protective glass—focus in the gloom, scanning the behavior of the plant life, measuring the humidity, silently reading the signals of growth or decay.  

When a leaf grows yellow or a fungal bloom threatens to spread unchecked, the Custodian’s subtle servants emerge. You might catch a glimpse of a spidery, many-jointed arm raising up from beneath a walkway to (gently, almost delicately) pluck away a diseased frond or dispense a mist of chemical that halts a destructive microbe. These caretaker extensions are swift, purposeful, and fleeting—retreating into hidden alcoves as soon as their duties are complete. They never linger openly, as though guided by instructions to remain unseen, giving the impression that the entire place is run by phantoms of metal.  

The plants themselves, of course, dominate the living tapestry. Intricate root systems sprawl through half-buried troughs of nutrient gel, forming dense mats of fibrous tendrils that clutch the metal beams and infiltrate the cracks. Leaves glisten with perpetual moisture, exhaling an oxygen-rich haze into the thick, humid air. Over countless years, families of vines have woven themselves into the architecture, bridging the gap between walkway and support column. Some of the oldest trunks—yes, trunks, for a few have grown thick as saplings—have a woody texture that defies their purely engineered origins. They stand like slow, silent guardians of the catwalk roads, unable to move yet fully alive, patiently abiding here across unknown centuries.

Hidden beneath these leaves and vines, there are also the fungi. Pale tendrils creep across damp surfaces, forming colonies on half-forgotten access panels. Their fruiting bodies—small, ghostly caps in shades of ivory and faint lavender—sprout in overlooked corners. Perhaps introduced to break down decaying matter (on the rare occasion that something truly dies here) or to assist in balancing chemical processes, they have woven themselves into the cycle. Their presence knits together the living and the mechanical, recycling whatever scraps fall from the vegetation or accumulate in water channels.

And in deeper recesses—beyond the wide open chambers, through winding halls that see hardly any visitors—there might be other shapes, other watchers. Some whisper that archaic maintenance droids remain powered by sealed battery banks, roving on silent treads in the darkness. Their sensors flicker red or green in the gloom, scanning forgotten corners for structural weaknesses. Others speculate about human or humanoid figures half-welded into the environment, caretakers from a bygone age who let wires coil around their limbs and kept watch until they, too, became part of the apparatus. Whether these legends have any truth is impossible to confirm, for few dare venture beyond the luminous arcs of the overhead lamps.  

Nonetheless, a sense of intangible awareness-soaked vigilance pervades. Not watchful in a hostile sense—there is no looming monster, no threat. Rather, it is the sense that everything in this sealed ecosystem, from the tiniest mite to the largest fan-blade, is integrated into a single living system. The amphibians nibble on pests clinging to the vines; the vines breathe and release moisture that keeps the fungus alive; the fungus dissolves detritus into nutrients. The well-placed vents purge the stagnant air. The pumps guide the water. The entire arrangement hums in cyclical equilibrium, an intermingling of biology and cold steel.  

If you search long enough for something that calls itself an inhabitant in the usual sense—something with two legs and two arms that might speak to you—you will come up disappointed. Instead, you will find in the amphibians’ tiny black eyes a piquant awareness. In the rasp of hidden machinery, a nonverbal intelligence hums at the edge of comprehension. In the unstoppable rise of vines and the gentle swirl of drifting spores, you will witness the place itself living. It is a communal creature: roots and rivets, frogs and fans, algae and algorithms.

The old dream of a caretaker might still exist, though not in a single face or figure. Here, the caretaker is the architecture of the environment itself, the sum of its mechanical processes and the slow-green stir of its plants. Every function is part of the boundless organism, including the ephemeral inhabitants who flutter or hop or spawn in unseen pools. Perhaps some original designers intended it to be a self-sustaining greenhouse, or a laboratory to test subterranean horticulture. Perhaps they wanted to see how life might persist when sealed off for centuries under metal vaults. Whenever or however they left—if indeed they ever truly left—what remains is this ever-living testament, this delicate balance of plant and pump.

So, who lives here? Marble-eyed frogs in hidden corners, shimmering insects in searching arcs of wings, creeping fungi weaving pale webs across decaying leaves, and, above all, the enormous mechanical intelligence that underlies every breath of air and every drop of steam. An entire living system. One that has found a hush and a constancy in the turmoil of machines, content to keep breathing in the echoing dark.
