They are many and yet rarely seen. Hidden by their own quiet diligence, these plateau dwellers weave among the spires and resinous arches in near silence, as much a part of the architecture as the fleshy crystal growths themselves. From a distance, one might mistake them for figures drifting in and out of the half-domed alcoves—shadows that flicker along pale ridges of the plateau. But draw closer, and you’d see that their limbs seem shaped to integrate seamlessly with their environment. Joints, like segments of smooth chitin, fit into crevices in the resin walls, allowing them to ascend vertically—or even upside down—with effortless grace.

Their gait is languid and measured, as slow as the plateau’s own tectonic shifts. In form, they stand perhaps waist-high to a human, though they rarely hold themselves fully upright. When they do unfurl, one glimpses elongated torsos ringed with overlapping plates of dull-hued armor—amber, dusky green, and the occasional vein of sooty black. Long, slim forelimbs with four-jointed digits can curl inward protectively or expand outward to shape and press new layers of resin. Beneath those plates lies a translucent membrane, a slick and nearly glass-like surface that shimmers in the dusty sunlight for just a moment before seeming to vanish into the gloom of the corridors.

Their silently working mouths are small but efficient. Rows of fine, rasping ridges—something between bone and crystal—allow them to gnaw the resinous material or scrape it into pliable filaments. This “resin-crafting” is central to their existence. Even the youngest among them seem instinctively tied to building: they gather shavings from fresh growths, carrying them in segmented pouches at their sides. Then, slowly and methodically, they contribute to new corridors or repair tiny cracks in older ones. It’s not elaborate sculpting so much as layering, fusing, and smoothing, a process repeated day by day, steady as breath.

From time to time, you can see swirling azure spores wafting among them—this seems to be how they communicate chemical signals. A creature will part its forelimbs, shedding a faint puff of those luminous motes, which are then taken up by an adjacent individual. The recipient reacts with a faint tremor or shift of plates before drifting off through the labyrinth, as if compelled to carry out some slowly revealed directive. No sound passes between them except for that soft rasp of resin being shaped and the clicking of chitinous limbs.

They appear to gather nourishment directly from the environment in ways that, at first, boggle the mind. Perhaps they absorb trace minerals from the porous soil with specialized pads on their lower limbs, or perhaps they feed symbiotically on the chemical exhalations of the spires—those thick plant-forms that tower overhead like an alien forest. When dawn comes, you see them turn their plates to face the rising sun, as if capturing its energy through faint photosynthesis or a hidden chemical reaction. This ritual, repeated each day, maintains the sense of a half-waking, half-dreaming realm of life that draws sustenance directly from light and mineral essence.

Their nests—if “nest” is even the right word—are hollow pockets within the resin cathedrals: snug enclosures, lined with layered sheaths formed from their own secretions, which darken into an insulated shell. These enclosed hollows have no furniture in the human sense; instead, the creatures rest by merging themselves partially with the pliant, semi-living resin, as though letting their own heat be stored and shared by the structure. At times, you might see a faint warm glow radiating from beneath the resin’s surface where one of them sleeps, indicating a gentle exchange of energy that neither wastes nor pollutes—just an ongoing cycle of giving and receiving. 

Socially, they move through the city in near-constant but unhurried flux. When one discovers a new fissure that breaks the plateau’s surface—a sudden crack exposing fresh, chalky rock—wordless gatherings begin. A cluster arrives, each individual adding new resin layers around the fracture’s rim, carefully shoring it up so it won’t collapse into the labyrinth below. Only the scrape of resin on rock and the chitin-click of limbs betrays the presence of these living guardians of the plateau’s structural integrity. They labor not in fervor but in a slow, deliberate rhythm, as unhurried as a tide that must reach its mark.

And though one might assume a place so silent to be solitary, there are surprisingly many of them. In certain broader halls, especially at twilight, you might glimpse a congregation gathered in a large resin amphitheater, archways rising above them like the limbs of some ancient tree. The azure spores swirl in a vortex of communication, dancing on subtle air currents. Plate segments flare to reflect the dusty sunlight that filters through ridged vents in the ceiling. If you stand still and watch, you would sense not anarchy but a synchronous pattern of movement, each individual turning slowly, aligning with the others in a meditative geometry of limbs and plates. It looks ceremonial, though its purpose remains inscrutable—perhaps a silent summoning of future growth, or an exchange of knowledge carried in the chemical signals of the spores. 

Below the surface, in deeper caverns formed by the plateau’s geologic breath, they seem to nurture small aquatic pockets—pools that are not water but rather a thick, syrupy fluid exuded slowly through microscopic channels in the rock. Here, near-subterranean resin vines dip their pale tendrils into the fluid, drawing up essential minerals and—somehow—distributing them throughout the living city. These hollows are among the few places where a traveling observer might smell a thicker, metallic tang in the air and glimpse a faint shimmer of pooled liquid reminiscent of liquid crystal. Small, larval forms of the dwellers float here, encased in flexible resin sheaths. Over many weeks or months, they absorb the fluid’s essence, crystallizing until their chitin segments harden, and then at last they crawl free to join the labyrinth above.

They have no named hierarchy one can discern, but there is an unspoken orchestration guiding them. Perhaps it arises from the plateau itself, as if it breathes instructions through subtle tremors, translated by the spires and the spores into signals the dwellers obey. It might be that they are each but a fragment of the plateau’s larger mind—a collective synergy of geology, biology, and silent desire for growth. If so, their sense of individuality differs vastly from our own. They exist as caretakers, builders, guardians—a civilization grown from the same resinous matter that shapes their home.

Witnessing them is to see creatures at one with their environment in an inimitable harmony of mineral, sun, and slow rhythmic life. They are desert architects without tools or metal, forging dwellings from hardened plant-glass rather than from stone blocks. Their presence is understated but unmistakable, woven so deeply into the plateau’s newborn surfaces that one wonders whether they, or the very land, first conceived the resin city.

And so they live, in the hush of thin air, with no streams or rivers to quicken them, showing that water is not the only wellspring of growth. They move in that stately, deliberate dance through corridors warm to the touch, forging new expansions—an artful collage of geology and biology that continues its patient unfolding, measured not in days or seasons, but in ages of silent becoming.
