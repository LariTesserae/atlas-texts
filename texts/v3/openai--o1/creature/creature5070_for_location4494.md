At first glance, you might assume that those who dwell in this high, cold place would be austere and silent as the wind-sculpted cliffs around them. And in many ways, that first assumption would be correct. They have become subdued and smooth-edged—like the stone—that has stood beneath the stinging barrage of drizzle and sun for countless generations. Yet there also glints within them something of the plateau’s bright vitality, an enduring warmth tucked beneath tough exteriors, like embers carefully banked against the raw chill of their world.

They are tall, lean-limbed people—humanoid in form, but with certain features especially pronounced. Their faces are narrow and elongated; their eyes large and reflective, ringed by thick lashes to wick away mist. They often keep the lower halves of their faces veiled with soft-draped hides or woven scarves, preserving heat and steadily warming their breath. When they speak, the voice is quiet and low, as though pitched to carry only within arm’s reach—loud echoes would be lost or distorted in the swirling air. The subtle hush of their conversation attunes them to the constant background sounds of dripping water and scraping wind.

From birth, they learn to watch for the quick shifts in weather. Their eyes—dark but with an underlying gleam—can dilate widely in the ever-shifting light, enabling them to see the faint outlines within the swirl of mist. Children practice sighting games, competing to spot the first flicker of a creature darting behind a boulder or to detect the subtle shift in shadows that herald a sudden gust of air. Their hands, calloused and deft, handle wet stone with a sure grip, moving heavy slabs into place or redirecting rivulets of water as though they are shaping clay. 

Their garments are layered: the base layer is a tight weave of fibrous strands—perhaps from hardy grasses they collect near the edges of the settlement—pressed and woven together into a coarse cloth. Above this, they drape hides from the larger creatures of the plateau, sewing them with sinew or carefully hammered metal wire. They wear these garments in a style that leaves their arms relatively free; for them, being able to maneuver quickly around slippery ledges is far more critical than complete coverage. Yet warmth is achieved through cunning arrangement of multiple small layers, each capturing heat in pockets of air.

These people are deeply tied to water; it’s their sustaining element, though it’s also their ever-present challenge. They harvest water from the funnel-grooved roofs and from channels they cut in the rocks. Many of their communal gatherings center on the small pools and cisterns dotted about the settlement. Around these pools, one can see them collecting water in hammered metal pans, plying bone sieves through the shallow basins in search of small crustacean-like creatures that live in the mineral-rich water, or simply chanting soft melodies as they watch the day’s light refract in the drifting surface. The water is precious but never scarce—just difficult to manage in its constantly shifting, half-gas, half-ice form.

They are not a populous group; births are infrequent, and every child is welcomed with gravitas that belies the hush of daily life. When an infant arrives, small tokens—slivers of polished stone or gleanings from the damp outskirts of the settlement—are placed in a communal cradle, as if the child is being woven into the very landscape. Over time, those tokens might become part of the child’s personal adornments: tiny carved amulets, stone pendants, or delicate bone ornaments that reflect the luminous droplets of rain.

Despite the chill, the wet, and the thin air, they are not ascetics; they keep fires burning in sheltered nooks, feeding them with the sparse driftwood or weathered scraps of stunted timber that manage to grow in this place. Their cooking is simple, involving hearty stews of the plateau’s small amphibious or fishlike creatures, seasoned with the mineral-heavy salts collected in shallow rock depressions. The smell of their meals—savory and subtly metallic—mingles with the perpetual scent of wet stone in the air. 

And then there are the animals that share the plateau with them. The dwellers do not domesticate these creatures in the typical sense, but they do live in a closely intertwined balance. Some smaller pale-furred shapes nest under the raised floors of huts, nibbling scraps left behind or lapping from collected water. Larger, herd-like beasts pass through regularly—sinuous and quiet, their hides shimmering faintly in the bright-haze light. At times, the dwellers gather at the outskirts of the settlement to watch these herds approach; you might sense a reverence in their stance. They’ll gently guide the creatures toward safer paths or away from a steep drop. Occasionally, an animal is hunted and its hide, horns, or bones repurposed for tools—but such hunts are accompanied by solemn ritual, with small, chanted offerings poured into the nearest cistern as a show of respect.

Scattered on the farther edges of the plateau, you might see a few stone rings or upright pillars. They are not precisely temples nor purely markers: they stand mostly as testaments to how long these people have been here. Each ring of upright stones is a vantage point from which the dwellers once charted the cycles of the blazing sun against the swirl of mist, tracking subtle seasonal changes. Even now, some in their community maintain these observations, gleaning predictions for when the blasts of wind might intensify or when the brittle cold might relent just enough for certain morsels of vegetation to appear.

They have an uncanny sense for those shifts: the subtlest pressure change in the air, the faintest difference in the hiss of wind against rock. And it’s this sensitivity that keeps them safe when, in an instant, the wind can whip roarfully against the settlement, clattering hides and hurling droplets like tiny shards of ice. In such moments, you may see them immediately duck behind a wall or press themselves close to the ground, letting the gust roll over them. Only after the wind settles do they emerge once more, meticulously assessing any damage—re-tying a loosened hide flap, freeing a captured rivulet of water from where it has backed up around a building.

They celebrate the quiet that follows the storms. During these lulls of brilliant clarity, they pause in whatever they are doing to delight silently in the transformation of their home. Children wade into shallower pools and watch the shifting reflections in the water’s surface, sometimes trying to pluck at the ghostly rainbows that flicker across the mist. Elders lift their faces to the sun, letting warmth creep through the layered cloth and hides, as though storing the memory of light for bleaker moments. 

If you should chance upon this settlement, these dwellers might regard you curiously, but not with animosity. They show no great alarm at strangers—there’s less of a fear of intrusion up here than in other lands. To them, the plateau itself acts as the primary gatekeeper: only those who can weather the unrelenting wet and wind will arrive in one piece. Instead, they might motion for you to share in their warmed fires, offering a small earthen bowl of their steeped broth, seasoned with softly glowing mineral salts. When you drink it, you taste the plateau: a mild, metallic tang, aligned with the very stone that rises all around.

In time, you’d see that they value practicality over ceremony, silence over spectacle, and subtlety over bold expression. Yet, woven through every aspect of their life is this sense of watery brilliance, half-formed rainbows, and transformation. They see themselves as part of the plateau’s constant flux, neither dominating nor being dominated, but standing in a nearly mystical equilibrium. And so, in the shining hush of day or in the swirling disarray of sudden squalls, these dwellers persist, guided by an old, weather-worn wisdom that shapes them as surely as the water shapes the stone.
