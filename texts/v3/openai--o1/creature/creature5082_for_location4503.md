They dwell where the plateau’s bones rise into towers and chambers, an unassuming people who move through the stone as though it were a gentle companion. By day, they wander the winding pathways in small groups, wearing garments of finely woven, weather-tough fibers that cling to their shoulders and legs—softly brushed and colored in the mottled tones of wet rock, as if to blend with the walls. Most keep their heads covered with a thin shawl, both to retain warmth in the plateau’s cooling air and to collect the fine beads of mist that roll off the stone. When they greet each other, they do so quietly, nearly in whispers, with a single touch of palms in gentle acknowledgment of shared existence. Unnecessary sound seems a sacrilege here, out of place or unholy in the hush of wet rock. 

They call themselves the Keepers of the Flow—though that name is rarely spoken aloud except when explaining to travelers why the city was built as it is. The heart of their tradition is to guide and preserve water’s journey through the plateau. They have carved channels that direct runoff toward recessed basins, small gardens, and communal fountains; everything is meticulously planned to lose as little water as possible. Children learn early how to shape the flow of rivulets by adjusting slender stones or tightening a segment of root or vine—simple gestures that honor a knowledge held for generations: water here is too precious to waste, yet abundant enough to nurture if handled with respect.

They are not a people of pomp or display. Their skill in masonry and fluid engineering arises from necessity rather than from a desire to impress. Rather than forging metals or intricate mechanisms, they rely on intuition chiseled over centuries of attentive living. Some among them—grey-haired elders with slow, measured steps—are known for the uncanny way they predict the plateau’s “breathing,” forecasting when a new seep of water will break the rock face or when the thin soil will open a nook for a hardy shrub. Others devote themselves to a form of silent horticulture: coaxing the minimal vegetation to flourish, even in cracks amid the masonry, by ensuring every necessary droplet is delivered to the roots. 

Those who tend the rooftop gardens work from sunrise to sunset with a scrupulous sense of care. They climb narrow stone ladders that cling to the city walls, harnessed with braided rope to keep them steady on wet surfaces. Basket by basket, they distribute water from the cisterns that dot each level. In these high patches of greenery grow succulents with plump leaves that store moisture, along with modest herbs that season their simple meals. After a light rain, when the stone still glistens, these gardeners linger on the rooftops, letting the plateau’s breath swirl around them—a moment of communion with the living rock before they descend to rejoin the labyrinth of passages below.

Small families inhabit the quiet warren of chambers that spreads outward and upward in concentric arcs, grouping themselves by craft or by kinship. The potter families, for instance, occupy a band of rooms where the clay deposits are richest; their circular kilns, fired from dried moss and shrub trimmings, do little more than smolder, giving occasional puffs of warmth in the cool air. Other families are weavers, spinning the hardy threads they glean from local plants or from the hairlike strands of certain moss species. Their looms fill the corridors with the soft whirr of thread crossing thread, an undertone to the faint trickle of water overhead.

Participants of the city’s spiritual traditions maintain small shrines carved directly into the bedrock, set discreetly in corners and along dim corridors. Their rituals—if one can even call them that—are hushed affairs, marked more by quiet reflection than by formal ceremony. A series of stone bowls catches dripping water in each shrine, and devotees sometimes watch particular droplets fall one by one, interpreting the shapes of ripples as messages or portents. The ethos of reverence runs deep: water is not merely a resource but a companion spirit, something that moves within the channels of daily life and also transcends it.

Birds of the plateau—long-winged, strong-flying species—visit the city’s edges in search of insects that thrive in the damp corners. Some of the Keepers have befriended these birds over the years; a handful of roosts and ledges have become semi-permanent habitat. When the birds come in to nest or catch fresh water, the locals gently coax them away from crops while offering a share of seeds. It’s a quiet dance of mutual respect, as though each acknowledges the other as rightful inhabitant. From time to time, travelers hear a high, keening call echo through the city’s corridors, followed by a faint flurry of feathers and a ripple of interest: Is a storm approaching? Have new birds arrived to roost? Did one of the watchers sense a shift in weather?

In the evenings, when the rapid streams calm and the plateau exhales the stored moisture of the day, families gather in communal halls—rooms shaped like semi-canyons, open to the sky. Simple meals abound: stewed tubers grown in protected pockets of earth, wild greens from gullies, and perhaps strands of delicate mushrooms harvested from moss beds. Conversation is subdued, occasionally brightening with recollections of times past: a sudden break in the cliff that revealed an underground reservoir of fresh water; a strong wind that uncovered a new ledge for planting; the once-in-a-lifetime flight of a rare bird crossing the plateau. These stories become legends, told not to boast but to appreciate the synergy between the Keepers and the plateau itself.

While their existence is humble, there is a steady pulse of life in these stone chambers—a silent harmony of water’s trickle, quiet footfalls, and slow, purposeful labor. Outsiders might call it austere, but those who live here see it as a sacred partnership, an exchange with the land. With each day spent tending channels or scrubbing cisterns, with every hushed conversation at dawn about the shape of the clouds drifting overhead, the Keepers affirm their place in a grand cycle whose main characters are the plateau’s living rock and the ephemeral courses of water that wind through it.

And so they persevere: a community of watchers, gardeners, and whisperers in the stone. When passing travelers pause to heed the city’s near-silence, they may witness how each life—human, plant, or beast—moves in step with the plateau’s subtle rhythms. In that stillness like a held breath, one realizes the Keepers’ true craft is not masonry or hydrology alone, but a kind of quiet devotion that threads their lives into something timeless. Across centuries, they have let themselves be shaped by the plateau’s slow transformations, belonging to a realm where patience and reverence are the best sorts of technology. Here, on this damp, lifted place of earthen music, they dwell in answer to the hush, keepers not just of water but of a profound and enduring harmony.
