They live in layers—no single group claims the whole place, though once, long ago, someone very particular built it. You can sense the echoes of that older, guiding presence: the neat alignment of stone and clay, the cunning distribution of water channels, and the remnants of wooden scaffolding. But whoever the ancient hands belonged to, they have left (or dwindled to the merest few), and now the occupants are many and varied, each shaped by the damp, echoing halls.

First, you notice the smallest denizens. In every puddle or rivulet of condensation, gnat-size insects twitch in swirling clusters, their tiny legs skittering continuously over the surface of the water. Larger beetles patrol the floors—midnight-blue shells glinting whenever a torch’s flicker strikes them. When threatened by a footstep or the glide of something bigger, they scatter en masse, the noise of their countless clicking limbs a rustle like dried leaves. In the air, moth-like creatures with translucent wings drift clumsily from one dripping alcove to another, feeding on the dripping moisture or the faint traces of fungus that cling to the dome-like ceiling.

Wherever the puddles gather and trickle into crevices, amphibians take hold. Pale salamanders—almost translucent, with watery eyes—slink along the base of walls, flicking their tails to propel themselves through shallow channels as they hunt for beetle and moth grubs. Some of these salamanders are impressively large, half the length of your forearm, with moss-colored ridges along their backs that let them blend seamlessly against any slimy rock. Frogs, too, squat near deeper pools, their throats pulsing in steady, croaking calls that bounce unceasingly off the dome overhead.

Rodents scurry in and out of sight. The smaller ones are lithe and ghostly, their fur so pale that at first glance you’d swear they were illusions conjured from mist. They dart beneath the blocky platforms of stone or vanish behind rows of clay jars, squeaking softly at one another in urgent, high-pitched chatter. By contrast, there are bigger rodents—some the size of cats—whose coarse fur is streaked with dusty browns and greys. These larger beasts move with caution, often traveling in single file as though scouting for danger, leading the smaller members of their brood through narrower passages. Sometimes, you glimpse them perched on high stone ledges, quietly observing everything below with dark, probing eyes.

Lizardlike creatures—and not just the pale salamanders—abound in this maze of carved corridors. A few species cling to the grotto walls and scuttle across the ceiling. Some are slender and swift, with tails twice as long as their bodies, allowing them to pivot and leap in an instant if spooked. Others are stockier, with short horns crowning their heads and ridged spines. These more robust lizards can even gnaw at the creeping moss, scraping it off with specialized teeth before swallowing great clumps. Occasionally, an especially large one—nearly dog-sized—will loom in a corridor, jaw parted slightly to display teeth that look strong enough to snap bone. Their low-slung forms and cunning eyes suggest a territorial streak.

There are also hints of bigger, deeper presences—things that lurk on the edges of the shared spaces. You hear them more often than see them: that booming brood-mother call, resonant enough to vibrate in your own chest. Some claim it belongs to colossal amphibians half-submerged in the seldom-visited lower vaults, or perhaps to squat reptilian matriarchs that brood over clutches of leathery eggs. In the gloom of abandoned side-chambers, shapes move in half-light: silhouettes who might be thick-limbed rodents or something else entirely—there’s a hush and a sense of bulk, as though these alpha creatures prefer the isolation of unlit corners. Their domain is the quieter pockets of the labyrinth, the sub-chambers cut farther into the bowels of the earth, where your torchlight never quite reaches.

And then, the question of any remnant “people”—who shaped this warren to begin with. It’s uncertain whether they persist in some hidden halls. Yet rumors hint at figures stooped under the low archways, glimpsed only in peripheral vision. Their footsteps are soft, but you catch them sometimes at the edges of your hearing—a scrape, a thump, or the faint swish of cloth. Some claim they once were human, or near-human—clever enough to build doors from timber and nails of bronze, to carve stone shelves and shape the flow of water. But the centuries have changed them. Perhaps their eyes have adapted to the dimness, grown wide and luminous. Their speech, if it remains, might be more akin to the chirruping of cave-dwelling birds or the throaty resonance of a salamander’s call. If they exist at all, they rummage quietly through leftover storerooms, collecting what remains useful—battered tools, jars of clay—while living in deeper realms. Their presence is intangible yet palpable: every neat line of masonry, every angled corridor, suggests design too deliberate to have been abandoned completely.

Others insist no people remain at all. They argue the place is fully in the hands of the animal world now, a self-contained ecosystem that inherited architecture it never built. The “doors” are mere obstacles to be gnawed past or hopped over. Shelves become vantage points for predators. Corridors double as hunting lanes and nesting grounds. The wooden scaffolding is just chewable fiber for rodents to tear apart or for insects to hollow out. And certainly, the roiling swirl of life does give the impression of a fortress claimed by smaller kingdoms—dozens of species forging a loud, constant scramble of territory battles and feeding cycles, a city of small beings.

Yet even among the animals, there are hierarchical complexities. The smaller insects keep to the flanks, surviving on detritus. The amphibians lurk by watery spots, confident in their specialized domain. The big predators—and there must be a handful—command wide or strategically important corridors; you can see signs of them in the fresh claw marks raked across clay walls or in the half-gnawed husks of unfortunate prey. Occasionally, a group of rodents scampers across a hall littered with scattered shells from enormous beetles, giving you reason to picture a single massive, armor-plated insect haunting the deeper lairs, or perhaps it’s just the natural cycle of predator and prey made grand by the echoing emptiness.

Among the gloom, where torchlight fails and only the faint glow of bioluminescent fungus remains, the boundaries between one set of dwellers and another blur. Creatures share water sources. Lizards hunt over rods of scaffolding once used by unseen artisans. Salamanders slip under half-rotted doors to hide from bigger beasts. Winged insects breed in the corners of the storerooms, sometimes discovered and devoured by cunning rodents that navigate by scent. Some of these rodents have fur tinged deep orange or mottled with splotches of greenish mold, as though the environment is actively claiming them, layering the labyrinth’s colors onto their coats.

Whether or not there remain the faint echoes of the original builders, the present occupants coexist in a constant state of movement and sound. The architecture endures their scratching claws, their roosting, their hunts, their splashing. And perhaps that is the defining characteristic of those who live here: an unrelenting, modest industry. No single species dominates; collectively they keep every ledge alive with activity. In so many other places, you’d expect damp subterranean passages to molder into decay, but here, life never relents. The humidity, warmth, and labyrinth of cunning channels all sustain a bustling tapestry of creatures. They fill every chamber, every carved alcove, every high nook on the arching ceiling.

Those who take the time to watch—perhaps crouched near a puddle or perched on a half-collapsed shelf—see these inhabitants move like a single layered organism. The drip-dropping of water resonates with the click of insect wings; the gurgle of channels becomes the amphibian’s lullaby; rodents skitter away just as a wide-jawed lizard emerges, snapping at them with precise timing. In the distance, a brood-mother’s bellow resonates again. The place is loud with them, and they, in turn, thrive in that symphony of echoes. So it is that this subterranean warren remains vibrant, long after its original makers faded away: alive both with hidden watchers—if they exist—and with the undeniable, constant hum of scaling claws, flicking tails, beating wings, and drip-drip-drop of cavern water. Here, the dwellers are countless, forging their own codes of survival in a cathedral of stone and clay that belongs to no single species—yet shelters them all.
