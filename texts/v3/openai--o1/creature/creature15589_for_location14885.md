They are creatures in motion, thoroughly alive but with a quality reminiscent of an enormous machine fleet. The largest among them—those serpentine forms—are both majestic and unsettling, each at least the length of a person’s armspan, ridged with countless tiny scutes that reflect the light in subtle, argent flickers. Their heads never quite stay still, drifting in elliptical patterns, scanning the water for something unseen. Their body motions are mesmerizing: slow spirals that resemble tendrils of smoke rising through clear air, graceful arcs that seem to slice through the water with mathematical precision.

And then there are the crustaceans—countless, teeming hordes of them that congregate in glittering swarms. They range in size from minuscule flickers of color, hardly larger than a fingernail, up to segmented beasts almost a foot long, all scuttling in unison. Their shells vary from translucent sapphire to mottled charcoal, blending with the watery light that refracts off the stone columns. When they gather, they snap and clack in an unrelenting chorus, the closeness of their bodies creating an echo chamber that resonates against the terraces of jagged rock. Functionally, they seem to serve every niche at once: cleaning, scavenging, hunting in small packs, forever stirring the water in a coordinated frenzy. Their face-plates bear lines that might suggest eyes, but in truth their primary senses appear to be entirely tactile, each one feeling its neighbors as they surge together like living waves.

Near the newest columns of raw stone, one finds smaller, more introverted inhabitants. There are fugitive lumps of vaguely fish-like creatures—squat, finned globes that cling to cracks in the rock with startling strength. Their mouths open and close in slow rhythms, revealing tiny lamp-like patches inside. With each breath, these patches flare a murky orange, as if miniature coals were set to glow within fleshy lanterns. They often hover so close together that it’s impossible to see the cliff surface behind them. Their bodies seem awkward, but they can dart away with astonishing speed when a roaming serpent glides by. Between them, motes of fungus or microbial growth give off that faint violet glow, providing a spectral lighting in the water’s deeper recesses. Sometimes, the lumps drift through those glowing areas, coloring their undersides with an otherworldly sheen.

Further down in the subterranean labyrinth of columns, hidden in alcoves that might once have been pockets of gas or cooling lava, are even more cryptic forms. These are subtle, more inconspicuous inhabitants: thin, almost threadlike organisms that anchor themselves by tangling into clusters. Their small, shimmering bodies can slip silently between the stone walls, weaving themselves into sinuous, shimmering nets. When disturbed by movement, these nets release pulses of opalescent bubbles that rise to the surface—bubbles that pop with faint pings audible only if you press an ear to the stone. Perhaps these threadlike creatures are some variety of colony, an extended mind cooperating without a visible center, or perhaps each is an individual that has discovered a perfect arrangement to share nutrients and protection. It’s unclear, but together they form one of the lesser-known miracles of this water-swayed realm.

Yet the most striking denizens are those that create the deep hum, the pervasive vibration running through rock and water. Their shapes are large, broad discs with translucent flanges, pulsing in a measured, hypnotic beat. They seem part jellyfish, part manta ray, a living synergy of fluid grace and dense musculature. When they move, the entire terrace resonates, as if someone hit a gong far beneath the surface. Their undersides are ringed with loop-shaped channels that glow with an incandescent life force—dim and flickering like lightning storms within their own tissues. Often you can see them rising, flattening themselves near the surface, bringing tiny schools of caretaker crustaceans along for the ride. The crustaceans feed off flaking tissue or parasitic growth on the discs’ exteriors, and the discs appear to draw some kind of nourishment from the swirling, cleansing presence of these small hitchhikers in return.

All of these living forms—whether serpentine drifters or chattering arthropods—together create the tapestry of an unfinished ecosystem, a place so new that it feels as though it’s still inventing itself. The “factories” here are not built but born, manifested out of eons-old mineral upheaval. The creatures’ bodies sync and align in ways that suggest an innate sense of grand architecture, of corridors and compartments formed by living movement. The labyrinthine columns aren’t shaped by hand, yet they support an almost mechanical dance: each species seems linked to the others through a series of fluid, repeated tasks, as if everything functions with assembly-line precision.

Periods of chaos erupt—an entire wave of serpents might coil rapidly in one direction, sending the crustaceans into a jittery swirl. The finned lumps will break apart, drifting toward separate pillars, while the disc-like giants pulsing in the center intensify the hum for a moment. But then, like pistons returning to a restful position, all eases back into collective calm. The swirling shoals settle, the water hum subsides to a background drone, and life resumes its elegant, rhythmic clockwork.

They live here by responding to those unceasing vibrations, to the faint glow of microbial mats, to the equally unwavering bright sky overhead. Every organism seems invested in the slow, grand cycle of this watery plateau. There’s no evidence of conscious engineering or architectural intention from the creatures—they have simply grown to fit the environment’s fresh angles and edges. Each living being is a cog in the geological machine that rose from dramatic force, but now breathes with quiet inevitability. One can imagine that in another millennium, the stone might wear smooth, new species might emerge or vanish, and yet the overall pulse—the chatter and hum, the drifting and swirling—will continue on, shaping these heights with the unstoppable certainty of biology at work.

In the end, they are a chorus: an unintentional symphony rather than any single species. The serpentine predators, shell-clacking swarms, silent lumps, glimmering fungal mats, threadlike colony weavers, and monstrous purring discs—together they bring the sense of a half-formed realm brimming with possibility. They abide in a watery place that is neither quite a lake nor an ocean, perched at the boundary of the sky. And they affirm, with their robust and never-ending commotion, that life here is both brand new and timeless, born amid violent change yet settling like an age-old system.
