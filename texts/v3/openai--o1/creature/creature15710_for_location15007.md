They have no name, or no single name that could be spoken in a language of air-breathing creatures; yet they inhabit every crack, every metal rib, every cell of this place. They come—not as individuals, but as a single network, a single body continuously unfurling, branching, and reconfiguring itself. The distinction between them and their surroundings is artificial at best; to see them is simply to see the place. They are the labyrinth, and the labyrinth is them.

It might be simplest to call them vines. But “vine” suggests something green and flexible, nourished by water and sunlight. These beings belong to neither domain. Their stems are coarse, lined with inorganic ridges that look like iron filaments. Their coloration, if you can even parse it in the half-light, is a deep blackish-brown, mottled with dark copper and occasional pulses of dull red. There is little softness in them. A veneer of bark-like material coats dense, fiber-optic-like strands that spiral around embedded rods of corroded metal. Part plant and part machine, they grew and still grow: twisting, grinding, devouring.

They have no faces and no outward eyes, yet each root, each cable, seems oddly vigilant. If there is such a thing as sight for them, it is a diffuse awareness radiating through their entire being. They do not merely occupy this gloom; they have woven it, exhaling the dryness from hidden chemical forges. The entire place breathes in slow, invisible gulps—too subtle for human senses, but present in the faint stirring of fibrous dust that drifts in slivers of lamplight.

In the dim glow of uncertain power, these beings exist as shifting silhouettes. At rest, they might appear as dormant tangles, but that stillness is deceptive. Beneath the bark-metal husks, chemical reactions churn with ceaseless motion. Gases vent through specialized pores that might once have been valves in the factory’s piping. Buried deep in their woven anatomy, there could be nodules—organs, perhaps—that regulate temperature, cycling heat through capillary-like tubes made of a flexible alloy. The dryness they create is a byproduct of these internal forges, an exhalation that scalds the air, parches the surfaces.

They are old—older than memory. Traces of them might have emerged in the cracks of the earliest metal plating, taking root in the rust and ash of whatever civilization once built this labyrinth. Over countless eras, they have spread outward, devouring all watery life or any means of creating humidity, until these vines became the sole living architecture. And with that, they grew bolder in design. They latched onto girders, fusing with them, pulling themselves upright until they wove entire columns of steel-trunk hybrids. Where once were catwalks, now curling creepers prick with sharp mechanical bristles, forming rope bridges of living cable.

They have replaced the open corridors with carefully woven bowers of interlocking “leaves.” Each leaf is thick and leathery, yet laced with metal ribbing that channels meager pulses of electricity, gleaned from old power lines. These pulse lines feed the vines’ deeper layers—a slow heartbeat of energy that runs through them all. The vine-flesh never truly rests. It expands, cell by cell, metallic filament by metallic filament, forming new shoots where the environment’s heat is greatest. That heat is part nourishment, part catalyst, pushing their internal alchemy into overdrive.

They do not speak in any sense a traveler could comprehend. Instead of voices, they produce tonal groans—metallic moans caused by shifting tension in rust-eaten beams. Now and then, a bark-shrouded cable slides along a riveted surface with a soft screech, as if announcing its presence to the greater network. Some of these vibrations run so low a human ear might miss them entirely, but they ripple through the vine’s shared body: signals, communications, instructions. A leaf flexes. A root coil relents. A corridor thickens around a newly formed trunk. Like an infinitely slow, concussive dance, these signals pulse through the entire labyrinth, regulating how the vines reorganize themselves.

They feed. But not in the sense of mouth or stomach or even traditional photosynthesis. Instead, they seem to metabolize the trace minerals in the old metal, gleaning energy from decaying industrial chemicals, from faint surges in the labyrinth’s precarious current. Now and then, a vestige of functioning machinery rumbles into motion—some archaic engine or production line that never fully shut down. When that happens, the vines converge, hooking their root-cables into the mechanism’s exposed power couplings, siphoning off the heated residue, devouring the friction for energy. Over time, those machines are enveloped entirely, repurposed. Where the machine ends and the vine begins ceases to matter.

Nor are they uniform. Though all part of the same grand organism, individuality emerges in certain clusters. Some vines favor thick, trunk-like growths: monstrous spines that anchor the weight of entire multi-level platforms. Others appear slender, like fibrous cables winding through smaller beams, bridging the distance between twin columns. These narrower extensions are sensitive to minute changes in heat, flickers in light, or the inaudible hum of electric resonance. Some sets of leaves develop hardened edges, reminiscent of chipped steel plating, forming barriers that funnel the environment’s scorching drafts into specific passages—for reasons unclear. Possibly they’re channeling heat to brood-chambers where new growth stirs in the gloom.

Their reproduction is as alien as everything else about them. In certain hush-filled corners, you can see new buds pushing through cracks in the bark-metal layers: lumps that distend, glimmering with metallic sheen. Some buds might remain small, never opening, while others tear free in slow-motion, revealing tendril whorls—like mechanized flowers that unfurl in total darkness. As these expansions occur, the architecture of the labyrinth gradually shifts—an archway collapses to be replaced by a newly woven tunnel, or a black iron girder is twisted out of shape by a ring of bark-metal growth that squeezes it like a python, until the girder snaps and the vine’s offshoot claims the space.

In the deepest recesses—the scorching hearts of this domain—you might witness great, living “pumps” that were once industrial boilers. These have been colonized so thoroughly that their original shapes are obscured. From vents coated in thick fibers, gusts of searing air belch into the labyrinth’s arterial passages. The vines there are darkest and gnarliest of all, fused so completely with metal that they might as well be mechanical arms. In places, those arms still rotate or shift, as if the original machinery remains half-functional, now enslaved to the vines’ goals. Perhaps these ancient pumps are the labyrinth’s heartbeat.

They have no predators, no competition but themselves. Or perhaps it’s not even competition—merely a continuous rebalancing of space and resources between branching sub-colonies that remain part of the same overarching superorganism. If conflicts arise, they manifest as slow strangulations of certain corridors or expansions over older, decaying stems. Nothing is wasted: dying growth is reabsorbed in a patient cycle of consumption and renewal. Over centuries, that endless churn has replaced nearly every piece of the original structure with these tangled, bark-metal forms.

What sort of consciousness guides them—if any—remains a mystery. They could be more fungus-like, an instinctual hive that simply grows where conditions allow. Or they might harbor a bizarre intellect encoded in the labyrinth’s infinite cables. They never hurry, never chase anything; everything they need is already here, in the dryness, the heat, the humming relics of ancient machinery. There is no need for eyes or for flight. They simply thrive in the only way they know: climbing, coiling, merging, dissolving, and reconstituting themselves, eon after eon.

Neither plants nor machines, neither wholly living nor purely inanimate, they define this domain without contradiction. They have shaped a place beyond water, beyond the scuttling presence of insects or mammals, an immense skeletal jungle of rust and trunk, forging a sealed and scorching ecosystem. In a sense, they are the very soul of this darkness—pulsating, unyielding, perpetually alive in a world of dryness and gloom. And through every root, every cable grown hot with subterranean currents, they persist in a slow, unending metamorphosis, ensuring that whatever once existed here has been absorbed into their single, all-consuming life: the sole inhabitants of an unimaginable, arid cosmos.
