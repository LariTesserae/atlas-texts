At first, you might think there are no inhabitants here at all. There are no scurrying silhouettes darting around corners, no wingbeats echoing in the steaming dark. But that sensation—of emptiness—is only the surface impression. Something lives in these chambers, though it moves with a patience and subtlety that renders it nearly invisible.

If you linger, you start to sense them. A presence woven into the rhythmic drip and thrum. They do not step across the walkways in any manner you would call “walking.” They do not chatter in voices audible to the ear. Instead, they manifest as small, pulsing nodes of plant matter that bulge from the vines like knots on an old tree. The nodes shimmer faintly where they affix themselves to the labyrinth of scaffolding, sending delicate tendrils through the cracks in the metal. Each node appears like a dense seed pod, ridged and glossy, suffused with a fluid that glows a sickly green under the lamps. One might hang silently amid the tangle, or poke up from the wet floor, pulsating in slow intervals, as if breathing through membranes rather than lungs.

You suspect the entire vine system is one colossal organism—and the nodes are its way of being conscious, of perceiving and acting within the labyrinth. They are a distributed intelligence: each node connected to another through twisting roots and barbed creepers. They exchange signals via chemical pulses, carried through the watery channels that spread throughout the city’s sprawl. Where there are mechanical panels or vents, you see slender filaments questing outward, tapping gently, reading pressure changes. Sometimes those filaments pry open a rusted hatch or wrap themselves around a stuck valve. The occupant—if you can call it that—feels more like a many-limbed body than a collection of separate beings.

Long ago, perhaps, this complex was built by and for something else—human hands, or at least something akin to them. But over the ages, the lines between architecture and organism blurred, and now only the vine-network remains. Today, the mechanical apparatus exists to serve its growth, pumping in water and siphoning away waste, regulating temperature and humidity. In return, the living mass protects the metal—enveloping it with layer upon layer of bark, calcified saps, calloused ridges. It is a quiet, age-bound contract, so complete that the city itself has become a singular entity of entwined biology and machinery.

Despite the absence of animal life, there is still a sense of caretaking. The vines themselves appear to “tend” the old machines, winding carefully around critical joints, bridging rusted gaps with living tissue. Sometimes you can hear a wet clicking or a low rubbing noise, as rootlets grind away at surface corrosion and smooth it over with layers of living bark. In those hidden alcoves filled with softly humming turbines, you might spot a cluster of pods swaying in unison, as if orchestrating the timed release of water, or adjusting steam pressure through chemical signals known only within their biological web.  

Traverse deeper into this under-city, and occasionally you’ll spy a node gently dividing into two. A seam opens along the center, and a new, smaller node budges out, glistening in the dim half-light. The older half remains attached, perhaps retaining memory—if such a thing exists for these dwellers—while the neon-green interior of the bud pulses with potential, waiting to find a new niche along a pipe or corridor where it can embed. It may drift, trailing slender vines until it latches onto a damp corner, or quietly attach itself to the underside of a dripping water pipe. Then it will begin the slow process of merging, integrating, whispering its chemical language to every other node in range.

Some of their shapes vaguely hint at faces or pairs of eyes, but it could just be an illusion: patterns in the ridges, symmetrical knots in the bark. You might think you see a half-formed mouth open in the bark to breathe the misty air. You might swear there is a gentle rise and fall, a soft sigh as a burst of steam drifts across it. Yet even if they have no true faces set in flesh, there is an unmistakable sense of awareness, as if every slight stir of the vapor is registered. Nearby vines always adjust their tangles in response, as if listening.

They do not appear to communicate in any way a human might—no speech, no writing—though you will see evidence of a regular cycle of maintenance and design. Old, defunct machinery is occasionally sealed off by thick knots of living trunk so that whatever toxins might leak from it cannot infiltrate the rest of the habitat. Tubes and hoses that remain vital are held in place by root formations that look almost like purposeful bracing, anchored by the plant’s specialized growth. Where a seam in the metal threatens to burst under the subterranean pressure, tendrils weave themselves into the crack, reinforcing it with fibrous resin.

What passes for culture or society unfolds on a time scale much slower than human days or weeks. The vines grow thick around a machine, then spend decades—generations for a short-lived species—quietly molding it into a more stable part of the organic-mechanical matrix. Perhaps centuries ago they spread outward, searching for more territory. But now, just as the walkways feel claustrophobic, so too does the expansion feel arrested. They have all they need: water, nutrients, thick darkness, and a labyrinth of ancient metal to cradle them. In the hush, they simply exist, refining their domain at a nearly glacial pace.

Should you stand quietly, letting your breath match the humid stillness, you might catch the faintest interplay of chemical scents drifting: subtle pheromones that pass between nodes, telling of shifting internal pressures or an imbalance in some far pumping station. A cluster of leaves overhead might tremble, then subside. A hatch below might grind open, letting hot steam hiss out before roots gently press it shut again. Each movement is measured, purposeful, part of a collective effort to keep this environment stable—supporting the vines, the machinery, and the water cycles upon which they all depend.

No figure emerges to greet you—not in any bipedal sense, at least—but you cannot shake the feeling of being watched by the city itself. It’s as though every rivulet, every drip of condensation, every root braiding through metal is an organ of perception. And perhaps it’s that sense of living unity, older and slower than any single creature, that forms the true inhabitant here: a silent, many-bodied presence whose “people” are not individuals but specialized nodes within one vast consciousness.

Where humans might have built temples of steel and overhead lamps, here the vines are both the worshipper and the worshipped, the caretaker and the structure. A single living breath pulses through every corridor and cable, bridging the gap between biology and engineering. If you sit in the half-dark long enough, you might listen to that half-audible rhythm, the synergy of root and rust and water, and realize: this organism—whatever it truly is—does not belong to either realm exclusively. This labyrinth and its occupant are one and the same, and they have been quietly, patiently alive in their subterranean continuity for countless cycles, as unstoppable and unhurried as a deep-rooted forest of metal and vine.
