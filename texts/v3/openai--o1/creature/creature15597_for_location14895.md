They do not show themselves immediately. At first, you might guess there is no one here at all—only the braided trunks and the swaying galleries of leaves, the hush of vines, and the drip of water off moss. But slowly, over time, hints materialize. You’ll catch a silhouette where there should be none, a glimmer of movement in the corner of your eye. The longer you linger in the corridors of living bark, the surer you become that something or someone shapes this place—with intent, though gentle and cautious.

It might begin in the canopy: a faint rustle high above, as if a light-footed presence traverses the uppermost web of vines. Or on a fallen trunk bridging two dripping gullies, you notice delicate footprints—long toes or half-hooves pressed into the wet moss. Perhaps a shimmering sheen of a fine web, not spider-silk but something whorled with intricate patterns, winding around the trunk as if to provide traction. Evidence arises in the dwellings too: the arched doorways shaped from living bark are too neatly curved, as if grown by patient hands. You see piles of dried lichen arranged in a bed-like pad within a root-cavity, or bits of carved seed husks arranged as bowls or cups set upon a natural shelf. Something has been here, quietly weaving the organic with architecture, bridging the gap between wild growth and purposeful design.

As you stand still and watch, you might glimpse them out of the corner of your vision. They are lithe, those dwellers, moving as much through the suspended vines overhead as they do upon the ground. When they appear at all, it’s usually near the edges of your sight: a graceful body, somewhere between animal and person, slipping through the leaf-lattices. Their skin—if it can be called skin—often blends into the vegetation, dappled and moss-toned, or perhaps scaled in places where they are adapted to the ever-present moisture. Some among them could be small and spry, with wide eyes and elongated limbs; others might be taller, with long, trailing filaments that could be hair or living, parasitic vines bound to them in symbiosis. Both forms hint at a deep communion with the plants around them.

They rarely wear anything that is not derived from the greenery. Their garments, where present, are woven from pliant tendrils or layered leaves, secured by knots of fibrous root. Sometimes you might see fungal caps repurposed as hats, or large, translucent seed pods slung across the shoulder like satchels. In the hush of midday twilight, they move among their nest-like abodes, checking the water basins or tending small clusters of pale, creeping fungi that serve as a food source. Their motions are swift, efficient, as if each knows exactly which vine to climb, which root to skirt, how to twist through the natural labyrinth without disturbing a leaf. Even when they must brush aside a branch, they do it gently, never snapping. A reverence for the living city permeates every gesture.

They communicate in subtle ways. You might sense a hum more than a sound—an exchange of calls that feel half-vocal and half-vibratory, traveling through the vines themselves. If you’re patient, you notice how each occupant who passes along a trunk lightly taps it with their fingers or feet, sending faint signals that ripple through the network of roots. These signals might be warnings or greetings, or ways of sharing news about new growth or a shift in the water flow. The dwellers seem to share a near-silent language woven directly into the architecture of the forest.

Their relationship with the plants is a partnership. You see dwellers gently nudging a young vine into a supportive loop so that it will arc and form a rail for a future walkway. Or they brush away fungal spores too thick in certain hollows, preventing overgrowth that might choke out essential moss. Where partial gaps exist in the vine-walls, you might catch someone methodically braiding new shoots to close the breech. This is careful, patient shaping rather than conquering—no saw marks or hammered nails, but an organic guiding of the forest’s own momentum. In a few places closer to the rocky outskirts, you notice that these inhabitants have coaxed the roots to clasp onto the crumbling edges of sharp stone, preventing landslides or bridging natural cracks in the earth. They are stewards, ensuring that life claims every inch in a balanced, stable manner.

Sometimes, hints of their rituals emerge. At certain times, perhaps at the cusp of evening ( when the green glow of dusk throbs faintly in the leaves ), you might hear a low, collective chanting. It’s quiet, carried on the wind and mingled with the drip of water. If you wait and follow the source, you’d find a circle of them gathered near a wide trunk or at the base of a gnarled root. They stand or crouch around an ancient, knotty section where the bark is so thickly layered that it forms a natural shrine. Gently, they place luminous spores or tiny blossoms into hollows in the bark, almost like offerings. The chanting resonates through the trunk and vines, reverberating in the spongy ground underfoot. All the while, droplets continue to fall, punctuating each syllable with a steady patter.

On rare occasions, you might note differences among them—some appear older, bark-like plaques forming across their forearms, or thick roots integrated with their very bodies, as though they are gradually melding deeper with the vegetation. Those elders move more slowly, but you’ll catch them focusing intently on the living structures, encouraging new corridor expansions or teaching younger dwellers how to guide vine growth. Perhaps they have begun to transcend the boundary between person and plant, stepping further into the shared lifeblood of this place. Sometimes, in the mist, it’s hard to tell where an old dweller ends and a great trunk begins.

Small companion creatures also roam among them—a shy, low-to-the-ground fauna that scrambles up bark with wide paws adapted for slick surfaces. These are neither typical mammals nor reptiles but perhaps an in-between: tufted ears and glinting eyes that shift hue with the canopy’s light. They are drawn to the dwellers, trotting at their heels quietly, or resting in hammock-like pockets made of flexible roots. They serve as agile helpers, collecting seeds from high vantage points or carrying water-laden leaves to drinking hollows. You sense a shared trust in their interactions, a gentle synergy that underpins every aspect of survival here.

Whether these dwellers possess tools is hard to say—there are no blades of metal, no machines for cutting. If they do have instruments, they are carved from bone-like roots or from husks and shells too tough to break. Some dwellers may sport a woven sling across one shoulder or a small harness strapped around the waist from braided fibers. If you look carefully, you might see short staves grown from twisted vine, tips hardened with a resin that seeps from certain tree-like organisms. But these are used more for climbing or assisting in coaxing foliage rather than dominating the land. 

Above all, they live so lightly that one might be forgiven for thinking they are transient visitors rather than permanent residents. Yet the nest-like dwellings and graceful corridors woven throughout every cranny tell another story: they are deeply rooted here, in synergy with the living architecture that has grown around and with them. They sense changes in the weather or the rock’s slow shifts underfoot, and they adapt—spreading new shoots, reinforcing a mountainous ledge with fibrous scaffolding, or relocating dwellings from a sagging root to a fresh, sturdy trunk. Flexibility is their greatest strength; they rely on the flux of this environment rather than resisting it.

In the end, what emerges is a delicate civilization of beings who do not stand apart from nature but exist as part of its unfolding tapestry. They have no obvious hierarchy—no grand fortress or centralized power—just a network of watchers, climbers, and caretakers, each attuned to the quiet pulse of the land. Should you linger long enough, you might be gently welcomed into one of their vine-spun homes. Inside, the floor is soft and spongy, with faint luminescence trickling from the walls. A subtle, earthy warmth might surprise you, as stored heat from decaying leaves is shunted through natural channels. Gathering around a small hollow that collects dripping water from overhead, you might watch these inhabitants share the day’s gleanings—local seeds, edible fungi, sap tapped from the trunks—before settling into an unhurried, communal hush.

Here in this thin, cold air, in the perpetual twilight, they live in the synergy of growth and quiet harmony. They are more a whisper than a roar—scarce glimpses of movement among the vines and roots, keepers of a realm that is very much alive and always changing. Strangers might call them sprites or plantfolk or even ghosts of green, but they have their own names, spoken in languages of tapping roots and whispered songs that hum through the wooden corridors. And though they may remain elusive, their handiwork is everywhere: dwellings that bloom from the living bark, vine ramps that swirl to upper canopies, carefully tended mushrooms, and a persistent hush full of shared breath between plant and being. In this damp, misty landscape perched high on fresh-sculpted earth, they are the guardians, the nurturers, the ones who shape and are shaped by the slow, boundless growth that surrounds them.
