They do not arrive fully formed; one does not glimpse them as one would a regiment of identical figures stepping in line. Here, everything—flora, fauna, constructed form—seems to bloom in degrees, and the inhabitants are no exception.

You might see them first in the creatures at the water’s edge: those swift, darting birds that hover like hummingbirds yet skim the water’s surface to pluck insect life from the shallows. They have bright, luminous stripes that catch the angled sunlight, and in certain moments, a faint shimmer travels down these stripes, as though each bird’s heartbeat travels visibly through its feathers. No one knows if these creatures first evolved to survive in thin air near the plateau’s rim or whether they wandered in from lower altitudes, drawn by the currents of energy that glide among the spires and ridges. Either way, they’re intimately at home here, their nests sculpted from a resin-like secretion that hardens into spiral filaments on the rock walls. Even their calls, high and crystalline, mirror the environment’s primal hush.

Then there are the drifters—those pale manta-like shapes that seem to float on gravity’s edge, propelled by slow, undulating fins. They ride the slope of warm air rising from the heated surfaces of stone, gliding between columns of half-formed ridges. When they soar overhead, you may sense more than see them—a shift in the wind’s current, maybe, or a ghostly ripple of sunlight passing above. Their skin is so thin you can make out pulsing lines inside, like the branching of nerves or some translucent vascular system. They are elegantly massive, docile but powerful, roving the sky with neither hurry nor delay. The subtle hum that follows them might be the reverberation of their magnetically attuned tissues interacting with the plateau’s faint magnetic fields. No one has found a single one of them at rest, and legends spun around campfires—even in the advanced city—tell of it being ill-omened to see one land.

On the ground, smaller shapes flit through the sedge and shrubs: hardy, four-legged mammals with weblike membranes behind each limb, presumably to help them leap across fissures or surface streams. By day, these little creatures nibble at the base of the scrub or scour half-submerged stones for something edible. That they survive at all seems a miracle in the almost austere vegetation, yet they appear round-bellied, quietly comfortable, using large, dark eyes to scan for the lizards that lurk among the rock. Occasionally, you might spot a mother with her young—a tiny silhouette clinging to her flank, learning the leaps across watery gaps. At night, these creatures come alive with a gentle glow in the webbing of their membranes, a faint bioluminescence that helps them navigate or perhaps attract mates in the star-washed darkness.

Then, deeper within the gullies and set into niche-like hollows, dwell the more cryptic denizens: lizards with ridged backs that blend flawlessly into raw stone. Their coloration shifts from ashen grey to a dusted charcoal, and you might only notice them once they move in a flicker of hunger toward an unsuspecting prey. They have wide, sharp-edged mouths that make quick work of small rodents or insects. Remarkably, under certain conditions—like the subtle shift of barometric pressure or the approach of thunderstorms—they emit a low, vibrational rattle. Possibly a social call, possibly a warning. Either way, it resonates off the half-carved rock walls in a way that sends gentle tremors through the plateau’s shallow waters.

And then there are the city-dwellers.

Distance lends their forms a certain mystery: silhouettes that move with upright poise amid the shimmering spires, occasionally departing in those sleek arc-shaped craft that hum quietly into the open sky. Closer, you see that some appear almost human—tall, elongated individuals whose features carry lines of luminescence that trace from temple to collarbone, or from wrists to fingertips. Others drift in swirling mantles of delicate fiber, their feet never quite seeming to plant on solid ground. A few might have mechanical accents—arms ending in intricate metallic filigree, or ocular devices embedded where eyes might be. They communicate both with audible speech—soft, measured tones that echo like wind chimes—and with subtle gestures that cast shifting patterns of light from the luminous lines on their skin or attire.

To witness them in their city is to see them balanced in a perfect interplay of technology and biology. Perhaps they are born from this plateau’s living geology, or perhaps they arrived here ages ago, forging a pact with the land to let them shape its potential. Their architecture would suggest the latter: it’s too advanced, too carefully integrated to be accidental. Whatever their origins, they coexist with the environment in ways that make the boundaries between “city” and “wilderness” a gentle gradient rather than a delineation.

In open courtyards, they cultivate living crystals—polished stone grown in fluid chambers that feed on the plateau’s subtle magnetism. These crystals become building facades or are pieced into slender columns that help distribute energy across the city. Sometimes, you see entire families—if families they are—guiding these crystals to form new arcs, using whispered tones and gestures that glow. A faint shimmering field extends from their hands, and like clay being shaped on a cosmic potter’s wheel, the crystal yields to the invisible forces they wield.

They move with an unhurried grace, whether guiding a terrace to bloom from living geology or tending to the swirling flocks of amphibious birds that occasionally nest in the city’s lofty eaves. Communal spaces are designed as open circles where water flows gently along etched channels in the floor, reflecting the star-filled sky at night or the midday sun’s brilliance. In these communal circles, the city-dwellers exchange resources—some intangible, perhaps data or quantum threads of knowledge, and others more physical, like compact, seed-like modules containing nourishment or building enzymes.

Among them, there are those who appear to be the curious wanderers—individuals robed in swirling arrays of fiber and light, who travel out from the city and into the broader plateau. While you walk along the half-solid ridges at dusk, you might see one pausing by a shallow pool, trailing a hand in the water as though reading signals carried by the cold liquid. Another stands on a precipitous ledge, scanning the horizon with a device that whirs softly with each slow pivot. They’re cartographers of an ever-shifting terrain, perhaps, or scientists measuring subtle geologic changes. When they return to the city, their potent data or impressions are woven—as if in tapestry—into the city’s luminous archives. The city, in turn, rearranges itself at times, as though taking note of the land’s new shape and adjusting its own angles, realigning spires to harness fresh energy currents.

Within the settlement’s heart, there are rarer beings who seldom venture to the outer courtyards. They might remain in the elevated spires crowned by needle-like emitters. Their outlines flicker like heat mirages, sometimes dissolving into fractal patterns of color when viewed from a distance. Observers hypothesize these are the city’s sages or engineers, orchestrating the entire web of energy flows, ensuring that the synergy between plateau and construction remains stable. If you stand near the base of their towers and close your eyes, you might perceive a faint chorus of pulses—signals traveling in layered frequencies through the rock, the air, and beyond, weaving the city’s power into the environment’s living tapestry.

Even in the wilderness far from the city, signs of half-intentional sculpting appear. Water spirals into newly dug channels or flows across a lattice of carefully positioned stones that appear to have been pried from the plateau’s crust. Groves of sedge-like plants grow in meticulously curved patterns around certain rock formations, as if directed by some guiding hand. And, indeed, the animals too seem touched by whatever presence the city dwellers have. A large beast bounding across the plateau might have intricate, glowing lines braided into its horns or spines, a mark of some benign genetic attunement. The plateau’s cycles of birth, growth, and re-formation are subtly guided, yet never fully controlled.

In this swirl of life—amphibious birds, floating drifters, lizards, mammals, and advanced city dwellers—there’s no single hierarchy. The city dwellers do not impose dominion, nor are the animals merely feral creatures ignored. Rather, all these inhabitants, from the smallest lizard scuttling along the water’s edge to the flickering figure orchestrating energy flows in a spire, exist in layered symbiosis with the land. They are the stewards, the shaped and the shapers, participants in a dance aiming to keep the plateau vibrant and evolving.

And so, as you stand on the rocky outcrop at dusk, feeling the wind’s sharp touch and observing lights awakening in the city’s crystalline structures, you might catch a glimpse—a moment of recognition—that none of these beings, no matter how subtle or grand, stands outside the land’s continuous unfolding. They have settled into the quiet spaces between shifts and fractures, forging a gentle rapport with the forces of creation. The quiet hush beneath the wind, the glow within the city’s geometry, even the softness of the animals calling in the distance—it all belongs to a fluid tapestry of life brushing up against the edges of time.

They live as part of this plateau that is still determining its final shape. Their existence is not a rigid statement of identity but an ongoing process, an ever-shifting note in the plateau’s rising symphony. Perhaps that is their shared essence: each creature, each city dweller, even the biology of the half-formed rocks themselves, is a gesture of becoming—quietly, unmistakably shaping and being shaped by the land’s vast dream of what may be.
