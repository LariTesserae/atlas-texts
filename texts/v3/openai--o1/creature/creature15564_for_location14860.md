They do not rush—these dwellers of the black-rock city. Perhaps hurry was burned out of them, baked away under the constant heavy warmth. They move with a deliberateness that suggests a measured nature: every step carefully taken along the slick basalt ridges or metal walkways, each breath focused on parsing the hot and moisture-thick air.

If one were to wander through an unsealed doorway or peer through a window left half-cracked, one might catch a glimpse of them. They are tall but slightly hunched, with torsos draped in stiff, rust-colored fabric that seems woven from strands of metal filament. Their faces—if these can be properly called faces—are partially concealed by apparatuses: filtered masks with curved, visor-like panels that protect their eyes from the swirling grit and chemical haze. The masks feed into wide tubes that loop behind their shoulders, plugging into small canisters strapped to their backs. These canisters vent, with soft whistles, a pungent gas that mingles with the ambient steam.

Yet not all of them dress this way. Some have embraced the environment in such a way that their very skin—pale in some, dark in others—has become layered with a semi-translucent exoskeleton. This organic plating merges with garments of flexible metal mesh. It is rumored they cultivated symbiosis with the mineral-fungal protrusions that cling to the city’s outer walls. In places where the “fungal crystals” have attached to their bodies, one might see faint veins of luminescence creeping beneath the surface of that exoskeleton, pulsing slowly like a second heartbeat. Among this group, the boundary separating biology from geology is blurred; their armor is grown as much as it is forged, and they exude the quiet confidence of a life sharpened and polished by the plateau’s harshness.

What unites them is a shared inclination for secrecy, or at least privacy. Doors remain shut more often than not, drawn closed by thick, grime-streaked shutters that mute the hiss and clank from outside. Within these sealed interiors, passageways run like hidden tunnels, linking small, austere living spaces to workshops or storage vaults. Dim lights flicker across lengths of copper or steel cable inside the corridors. You might hear the perpetual drip of condensation into carefully placed catch basins, each drop echoing faintly against metal walls. It is in these interior pockets that their communities flourish—entire labyrinths of humming machinery, living spaces built around ancient steam turbines or near scalding water conduits.

The people themselves—call them survivors, descendants, engineers, or simply dwellers—have developed a culture shaped by caretaking the old machines. Many are adept at repairing tangles of pipe and gear so that the city can keep breathing beneath the ashen sky. Despite the mechanical hush outdoors, inside there is a continuous background of activity: the dull roar of pressurized steam, the rhythmic grinding of old pistons, the swoosh of water through labyrinthine channels. Their spoken language often becomes indistinct in these clamorous interiors, so they incorporate gestures—tapping out codes on metal rails, letting condensed droplets fall into resonant bowls that create echoes to guide each other or convey meaning in subtle ways.

On occasion, down certain dim hallways, the dwellers keep vaults of shimmering crystal-fungus—precious lumps that grow from carefully tended substrate beds of porous stone and chemical residue. The dwellers whisper that these formations are more than decorative or even pragmatic resources: they speak of them as living archives. Some say the crystals store memory traces or primal knowledge gleaned from the city’s earliest eras. Perhaps centuries of exposure to the swirling chemical-laden haze shaped the fungus-mineral into something that can recall. Whether that is true or mere superstition remains unclear. Nonetheless, it is only with permission that any stranger can witness such a trove.

Then there are those who rarely emerge from the subterranean or sub-level workshops—call them the machinists or the keepers. These dwellers spend most of their lives maintaining the older, deeper engines that keep the settlement stable. Some are rumored to have replaced their limbs with mechanical ones, letting metal braces and gear-driven joints support them within the city’s tight underbelly. By relinquishing some portion of their humanity (if human they ever were), they can endure the relentless heat that radiates from superheated pipes. The machinists’ eyes have a glassy sheen, possibly from long hours among the furnace-like glow and caustic fumes. The stories say they speak little, preferring the language of calibrations and the hum of cooling fans to words. Yet others claim the machinists sing in low, harmonic dronings, audible now and then when echoing up some vent shaft in the quiet of “night.”

Besides these masked citizens and half-augmented engineers, there is more stirring in the gloom. Furtive shapes skitter across the city’s girders—large, insect-like creatures that rely on the piping systems as highways, using suction-cupped feet to cling to vertical surfaces. Chitin plates overlap across their segmented bodies, sometimes reflecting faint glimmers of basalt black or crystal sheen. They are scavengers, gleaning nutrients from the trace minerals that condense on the city’s surfaces, or occasionally chipping away at fungal protrusions. At times, one might see them cluster around a hot-water drip, their mandibles parted to lap at the steaming trickle as it seeps from rusted joints in the metal walls. While they seem skittish, they have been known to grow bold when larger gatherings of their kind are at stake; more than one traveler has reported swarms massing over a broken pipeline, drawn by some chemical or mineral deposit. It is said the dwellers do not fear these swarms but watch them closely, mindful that competition for resources can quickly flare into conflict.

Then, in the higher reaches—where scaffolding extends precariously over yawning canyons—drift other, more elusive silhouettes. Some dwellers call them “the Phantoms.” They might be a cousin-stock to the main populace, or perhaps a different evolution entirely. Their forms appear humanoid in outline yet translucent or shimmering around the edges, as though perpetually half-blending with the drifting steam. Tall and willowy, they move by means of delicate, pivoting limbs that let them traverse narrow ledges and precarious walkways in near silence. What function they serve in the city is unclear; some stories maintain they are watchers or sentinels, attuned to subtle shifts in temperature and pressure. Others speak of them as echoes of those who once lived here, intangible remnants that refuse to abandon these basalt towers. When glimpsed momentarily in the corner of one’s vision, their faint glow might recall the pulse of the crystal-fungal arcs that sprout from the walls below.

At the heart of the settlement, somewhere beneath the tall industrial towers, rumor places a congregation of elders—call them governors, or simply the oldest of the old. No one is certain how many remain, nor precisely what they are. Some say these elders are purely mechanical by now—souls, if souls they have, surviving in a labyrinth of rotating memory cylinders and fluidic computation webs. Others claim the elders are in fact the living foundation of the city, not machines but labyrinthine fungal masses that have colonized entire vaults beneath the ground, and all else—people, machines, daily routines—merely exist to help them thrive. If these rumors are true, the dwellers themselves may serve as custodians, caretakers who feed and maintain the grand, ancient presence that anchors the city’s equilibrium.

Despite the myriad forms of life here—metal-clad engineers, half-living machinists, insectoid scavengers, drifting phantoms, and rumored fungal-minds—the city moves to a single slow pulse. The heat, the condensation, the hiss of steam, and the subdued clang of metal: these are the tempo. Each inhabitant, whether biological or mechanical or both, is integrated into that broad, perpetual cycle. Even the city’s structures—those towering black frames and layers of toughened glass—seem to give the impression of a slumbering organism. Pipes, ducts, and scaffolding might simply be extruded veins and sinews, with the dwellers flowing through them like blood cells, each playing its role in sustaining and preserving.

And so the settlement continues. Mostly hidden. Partially awake. They dwell in the hush, neither welcoming nor shunning outsiders—more like watchers who accept visitors only if they can stand the heat and the mystery. Within the metal halls, families (whether born or built) gather around flickering luminescent crystals for warmth and faint light. In distant wings, machinists ply their craft, ensuring that the old turbines and pumping stations never falter. High above, translucent silhouettes pace the scaffolds, scanning the horizon’s fog-shrouded edges as if waiting for something—some shift, or perhaps some return. Meanwhile, the ancient city endures, populated by those who have found a way to live in this environment that is equally flesh, metal, fungus, and stone. All of them, collectively, are its spirit. They do not name themselves loudly, nor do they proclaim dominion. They simply persist, shaped by and shaping the slow rhythms of heat and haze, in a place that looks empty but could not be more alive.
