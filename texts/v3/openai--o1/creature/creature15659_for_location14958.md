No matter which way you look at it, or what angle you approach from, you will not find the typical “creatures” you’d expect in such a vast environment; you won’t discover anything with flesh or fur, no beating hearts, no insects crawling in the dust. Yet life dwells here—strange, uncanny life, life that violates tidy definitions of “living” or “aware.”

At first, it seems that the only inhabitants are those crystal tubes—the “plants,” if one can even call them that. But these glassy, translucent organisms fill more than just a botanical niche. They grow in small, widely spaced clusters on the rock spires, half-formed plateaus, and precarious ledges. With each breath-like wave of their fractal filaments, they feed off the atmosphere’s mineral tang or some raw cosmic radiation that seeps through the cracks in reality. And though they do not move in the typical sense of walking or slithering, they are by no means passive. Their flexible stalks rotate—almost imperceptibly slow, yet always oriented toward subtle shifts in that constant thunderous roar, as though tuning themselves to an ongoing cosmic frequency. They, in many ways, embody the spirit of this realm—existing at the boundary between solidity and dream.

Watch them long enough, and you see odd signs of coordination. One cluster will glow brighter in watery pulses, and miles away, across a canyon whose floor might not even exist, another cluster flickers an almost identical response. These shared patterns loop and echo, as if each plant is part of a neural net scattered across a half-real topography. It is impossible to know whether they are communicating, or orchestrating, or simply resonating in unison like singing bowls. Their filaments, in constant gentle motion, brush the stony air, gathering what nourishment they can. Sometimes, it’s as if they reach for each other across impossible distance. Other times, they retract inward and intensify their internal light, hinting at some slow, secret process of self-transformation.

Yet if you wander deeper, you sense an additional presence—an almost intangible inhabitant of this place. It does not present itself in normal physical form. Sometimes it feels like pressure in your chest; sometimes it’s a shimmer that hangs at the periphery of vision. You might think of it as an echo of everything that ever was here, or perhaps an embryonic intelligence forming from the planet’s raw, shifting matter. Some have described it as “the half-born mind of the world” itself.

• It gathers in the hollows between mountain ridges, swirling like a faint, pearlescent vapor. 
• You may catch it drifting behind a broken stone column or seeping out of the cracks that vein the ground. 
• Sometimes it manifests as a low resonance in the great thunder, a specific chord that thrums through your very bones. 

When you focus your attention on it, this presence recoils or disperses, only to coalesce again out of your line of sight. It never forms a solid shape for more than an instant—always dissolving into an elusive haze that glints in the lavender half-light. Some explorers have insisted that this presence carries memory: not just memory of the long-vanished inhabitants but of the land’s future processes, as though it exists outside straightforward time. It is a gentle but unnerving intelligence—perhaps not benevolent, but certainly not malevolent. It’s more like a watchful ocean current that suffuses the entire domain. No single vantage can confirm its form. It flickers beyond standard perception, exactly the way the mountains do when they quake and the spires flicker in that roar.

And speak of those vanished people—trace them, perhaps, in the ancient carvings or scattered tools. You may not see them walking among the columns; yet their imprint lingers. Once, there were hands here clever enough to shape stone into bowls, to mold metal into crude hammers. A rough circle of scorched earth hints at gatherings around a fire. You can envision a small community, high on those precarious ledges, living in sync with this half-born terrain—possibly wincing each time the land roared or the spires warped. Maybe they prayed to the roaring presence in an attempt to calm it, or to harness it. Their structures have since crumbled and shifted—like the rest of the environment, they weren’t anchored in stable reality. If they left behind anything beyond the stone circles and chipped columns, it has vanished beyond detection or merged with the swirling haze that seems to contain all that remains of conscious thought.

So what remains as “life” in this realm? The crystals—skeletal forests clinging to half-broken ridges. The presence—an ambient, amorphous awareness that ripples in the very air. And perhaps still more: ephemeral shapes that flicker at the corners of your eye. One moment, you’re sure you saw silhouettes of bipedal figures shimmering near the next spire, their arms outstretched in supplication; in the next, the thunder shifts pitch and they are gone. Are they some ghostly remainders? Or new forms forming? It’s impossible to be certain if they are illusions, echoes, or embryonic beings in the process of “hatching” from that roaring crucible.

Late watchers, if they stay for days, speak of “proto-life” that emerges from the cracks in the ground—tiny, drifting motes of luminescent dust that swirl upward in the quake-laden breezes. Often these motes fuse with the filament tips of the crystal plants, flaring into momentary brightness that pulses along the stalks before fading back into the gloom. It’s as though the land’s raw energies, the plants’ crystal biology, and this intangible presence are all part of a single, pulsing organism—a living tapestry that doesn’t need the usual rules of biology. Instead, it draws upon cosmic resonance, mineral taste, slow tectonic motion, and that forever-thundering sound.

So, in truth, this is not a place inhabited by discrete species in neat categories. Rather, existence here is a continuum—from the embryonic planet-skeleton and its roaring formation, to the ephemeral mind that seeps through every crack, to the tall, translucent pillars of the crystalline plants. They all represent aspects of life at a fundamental, incipient stage—some partial, some evolving. Nothing hunts or grazes; there’s no predator-prey cycle. Instead, everything “feeds” on the slow birth of the world itself. Even those faint spectral glimpses of the vanished people feel like part of a single living matrix—perhaps memory-echo "organs" of this realm, waiting to re-meld into reality’s fresh expansions.

Who lives here, then? 
• The crystal growths, shimmering like a silent chorus, perpetually “tuning” the realm’s frequencies. 
• The intangible presence, a planetary or cosmic consciousness in embryonic form, drifting on sub-audible pulses. 
• The spectral remnants or proto-beings that flicker at the fringe of perception, not quite ghost, not quite corporeal, maybe building into new life. 
• And behind all these, the memory-traces of an ancient (or future) civilization, whose artifacts remain half-buried, reappearing whenever the land’s tectonic churning exhumes them.

They all intertwine into one intangible ecosystem—an ever-unfolding web of contradictory existence, thriving in a place that should not be, suspended between creation and collapse. If you stand still long enough, you can sense the entire realm stirring slowly around you: the crystals broadcasting subtle signals; the swirling haze manifesting almost-words in the echoes; the vast mountainous spires inching higher or shifting shape. It is a place of half-formed life—where the people, plants, and planet itself all hover in embryonic states, forging an uncanny, resonant community in the endless, thundering hush.
