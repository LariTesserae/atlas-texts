They tend to move in small groups of two or three, never straying far from the large pillars or the walls of newly cut stone. Their bodies are nearly colorless—translucent in places—echoing the bright, undiluted glow around them. You might miss them at first in the shimmering interplay of light and water, for they slip near the surfaces like reflections all but swallowed by their surroundings.  

They have broad, flat hands with thick webbing between the fingers, which taper into short claws of horn or chitin—tools grown from their own flesh. These claws tap, tap, tap against newly cut edges, feeling the surfaces or testing a joint as though verifying that everything has been joined correctly. When they move, it is with short, methodical strokes of their limbs. There is purpose in each gesture, as if each creature is guided by a singular desire: to assess, to shape, to continue building.

Their skin ranges from a dull pearl to a pale turquoise—like something illuminated by dawn light, just a whisper of color. On their narrow faces, you see wide, dark eyes set well apart, taking in the watery corridors. These eyes seem calm, almost meditative, as though constantly gauging the submerged space. You notice some individuals sporting subtle ridges or patterns upon their forearms and shoulders—whorls or knots that might indicate family lines or craft guilds. Perhaps they come from different clans, each group tasked with specific duties in raising these walls.

They rarely make a sound. Now and then, a low, humming call resonates from deep within one’s chest, echoing upside the stone and wood. At times, two or three of them gather around a pillar, pressing their bodies against the new masonry. Their humming unifies, carrying a gentle vibration in the water that resonates with the structure. You get a sense that they’re testing the stone’s solidity, or perhaps breathing life into it in some ritual only they understand.

When they work, they do so with simple, direct tools—mallets of wood and metal, chisels carved from bone or hammered out of rough iron. Each blow is powerful if unsophisticated; they rely on precision in their repeated motions, rather than advanced machinery or refined technology. Among them, the older artisans (perhaps it is only the few with slightly larger ridges of bone on their brows) direct the younger ones. These elders supervise the placement of each new block, nodding or humming in approval if the fit is good, hissing softly if it’s misaligned. Occasionally, they must wedge their claws into the slightest cracks, maneuvering the stone or beam just so, coaxing it into alignment.  

Much of their time is spent standing quietly, pressing a palm against the stone or against the wooden braces, listening to the flow of water. They appear to sense in the currents what the walls need, adjusting a bracket or adding a pin. If anything demands a shift, they communicate it quickly—though seldom with words. A tilt of the head, a subtle series of whistles, a flick of those webbed fingers… and the group sets about repositioning an entire beam or resealing a seam with hammered metal plates.  

Though the architecture is imposing, these beings are not large or threatening. Their bodies are lean, streamlined for underwater motion, yet they’re no bigger than an average human. There’s a patient, almost reverent air about them, as though they consider the building of this waterbound hall a sacred or vital act. Occasionally, one of them will pause before an unfinished corner of the structure, gazing upon it with wide eyes, silently envisioning the next steps. Then, with smooth thrusts of their legs, they glide away for more tools or to consult an elder.  

They seem drawn to the floating motes of light, those swirling luminescent orbs embedded in the walls. You’ll see one carefully approach a fixture, running webbed fingertips over it as though polishing or adjusting its angle. When satisfied, they nod and swim off, leaving the orb shining all the brighter. Others hover near the hinged panels that let water stream in or out, monitoring the pace of its flow. From time to time, they open or close these portals with great care, controlling the currents with nearly ritualistic solemnity.  

Between tasks, they slip into narrow passages behind the columns—hidden pockets that might serve as living quarters or storage chambers. Inside these recesses, you catch fleeting glimpses of woven bedding and piles of chisels or half-shaped wooden beams. In one, you see a cluster of unfinished metal rings and brackets—faintly tarnished, waiting to be polished or fitted onto a brace. The inhabitants move in and out, retrieving items, returning them, occasionally pausing to exchange a muted series of clicks that you guess may be an expression of camaraderie or thanks.

For sustenance, they appear to subsist on some form of engineered nourishment: you glimpse them cracking open small ceramic canisters containing a paste of pale algae or plankton, grown in hidden tanks. They gather around these canisters in quiet circles, siphoning the contents through narrow slits around their mouths. Their method of sharing food is communal—no one body claims more than it needs, and they leave enough for others who might pass through.  

At intervals, they slow their hustle and gather before one of the great pillars, their luminous eyes half-lidded in a moment of collective stillness. You can almost sense an unspoken ritual in these gatherings, as though the hall itself calls them to pause. Together, they listen to the groan of the wooden braces and the thunder of water surging through channels, letting the structure’s voice wash over them. In these moments, the hall feels animated: the stone and timber answer the resonance in their calls, as though the entire place is breathing, hearts beating in common rhythm.  

No single leader seems to dominate this society. Instead, authority seems fluid—flowing from elder to elder depending on the nature of the work. Indeed, the entire population shares a sense of unity with the watery environment. They are cooperatively forging a home from raw elemental forces, matching the flood of sound and current with an equally tenacious craftsmanship. Their every motion, from the subtle inspection of a corner to the methodical chiseling of a block, expresses this devotion to forging a new world beneath the radiant, watery sky.

They do not speak words as we know them, yet their presence hums with thoughtful intention. You feel it in the swirl of the currents, in the torque of their simple tools, and in the way they hold their wrists and claws against fresh stone. It’s as though these people exist primarily in the dialogue between stone, water, and will—three forces harmonizing to create a space that belongs to them as much as they belong to it.

And so they live in the hush of this place, accompanied by the roar of water pressing against stone, wreathed in the glow of newly placed orbs. They are architects and guardians both, shaping and maintaining these halls in a gentle cycle of labor, inspection, and reflection. The meager strands of plant life offer them no distraction; the watery realm, the raw materials, and the living pulse of the structure itself are their true ecosystem. They dwell here in the primal now of creation, forging with simple tools and solemn care a fortress or sanctuary that will stand—still unweathered, still vibrant, for as long as their steady vigilance can sustain it.
