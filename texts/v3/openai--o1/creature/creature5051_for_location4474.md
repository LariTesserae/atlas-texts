They begin as shifting silhouettes at the edges—a shape that slips behind a support column, an eye-like glow that disappears just as you turn your head. You might think, at first, that there are only the fish-forms you’ve seen weaving through the corridors. But the more time you spend in these metal-walled seas, the more you recognize layers of inhabitants, each adapted—whether by design or evolution or both—to a place whose walls and floors and very currents are all engineered.

Some inhabitants are so delicate you almost miss them, gliding in thin ribbons of silver and black. They appear in small schools that hug the sharper corners of bulkheads and corridor intersections, feeding on microscopic life that seems to bloom wherever fresh water cycles in. Each of these ribbon-fish has plates of translucent exoskeleton along its spine, a strange between-state of organic and synthetic. Beneath those plates, faint pulses of red light travel in rhythmic sequence—like a line of carefully timed signals. Whether those pulses keep them in sync, or transmit some pattern to the labyrinth’s automated systems, remains uncertain. When a ribbon-fish notices you, it blinks off those lights entirely, as if turning invisible. Then, with one flick of its flexible fins, it slides away behind a girder.

Elsewhere, you find exactly the opposite: large, proud constructs that roar with a presence impossible to hide. Some resemble giant mechanical crustaceans—vast pincers braced along the edges of the platforms as they trundle across the metal surfaces. Their limbs, each many meters long, end in sensor-laden nodes that scrape or probe or weld, as though they are constantly adding or re-shaping this grand environment. You hear them before you see them: heavy clangs on metal, a percussive announcement of their presence. Occasionally, one will fold itself along a wall and become indistinguishable from the structural braces, revealing too late that it was a living occupant all along. Then a spindly leg shifts, a flicker of bright yellow illumination emanates from hidden lenses, and it continues on with its methodical route.

High above, suspended in water so open and vast it could pass for a sky, are the watchers—long, eel-like shapes with muscular forms sleekly integrated with thrusters. They swim with a grace that makes their bulk deceptive; only when they pass overhead do you sense their true scale, large enough to press a chill through your bones. Their surfaces reflect a composite biology: shimmering patches of scale seamlessly merged with black polymer plating. Along their flanks, arrays of signal lights glow in subtle patterns—sometimes a scanning wave of purple, other times a repeated strobe of orange and red. They appear to patrol, and if they spot something amiss (a structural fault, a foreign object), they dive swiftly, the hum of their thrusters deepening. They clamp onto a support strut or a pipe, feeding or extracting data in an inscrutable ritual—a living diagnostic unit of this watery world.

Scattered in hidden pockets are the “recyclers,” smaller, squat forms that travel in pairs or small trios. They’re not fish or crustacean or eel; they’re shaped more like hammered lumps of metal, waddling near the bottom edges of corridors, rummaging through silt or debris that has collected in corners. Now and then, a single mechanical arm protrudes from their side, shoveling waste inward as if they’re digesting or processing it. In exchange, they excrete fresh metal pellets or nutrient-rich sludge—depending on the source material. Watching them work, you glimpse how the entire place is engineered not just to function, but to maintain itself. Each inhabitant performs a part in recycling and renewing, revealing that life here is as cyclical and deliberate as any terrestrial ecosystem—even if its components are built rather than begotten.

In calmer zones—where great loops of corridors meet wide-open “atriums” or aquariums—you might find clusters of specialized flora sprouting in small, protective alcoves. They’re not numerous here, but each one seems to exist for a reason. Translucent strands of leaf hold themselves upright in gentle currents, their roots anchored in pockets of softly glowing gel. These watery gardens often attract smaller, tubular creatures with fluttering gills: a cross between sea slugs and drones, each about the length of your forearm, their bodies segmented by rings of pale blue fiber. They graze on algae that’s cultivated meticulously on the plants’ surfaces. In return, they leave behind microscopic compounds critical for the plants’ growth—a synergy carefully orchestrated by whoever (or whatever) first populated this domain.

And yet, there’s a sense of watchfulness throughout. In the darker, more remote sectors, deeper behind the endless scaffolds and corridors, you sometimes catch fleeting glimpses of shapes that seem older than everything else—perhaps the first generation of organisms that made this place their home. They move differently: slower, heavier. Some have carapaces darkened and faintly corroded, as though exposed to unknown currents or chemical shifts. It’s not clear whether they belong here still, or if they’re remnants that never fully integrated into the evolving blueprint. You sense a reserved intelligence in them—an air of recollection, as if they hold the memories of past expansions or initial experiments left to wander the labyrinth’s watery recesses.

Among all these mechanical-biological hybrids, a certain species stands out: the “conductors.” You rarely see them in large groups; often just one or two that drift close enough to sense. Their bodies are ringed with softly glowing filaments, reminiscent of the nerve tissue in living creatures, except the filaments wind around metallic frames. The conductors carry on them small pods—some containing seeds of engineered flora, others filled with specialized components. They plant these pods along the corridors, cling them to the underside of platforms, or wedge them in cargo-like canisters. Almost like bees cross-pollinating blossoms, they facilitate expansion, ensuring each new territory has the resources needed for life to take root. When you come near, they sometimes swim in slow circles around you, scanning your presence before continuing on their tasks with a quiet sense of purpose.

Within the labyrinth’s constant churn of water, these creatures—big and small—coexist in a dynamic, ever-shifting balance. Some are harvesters, working in roving flocks to siphon specific nutrients for the entire system. Others act as distributors, taking those resources across corridors to areas that need them. Still others appear purely predatory, scanning for weaker or malfunctioning inhabitants, pouncing with unnerving speed, then repurposing their prey’s parts. The environment accommodates each role, its corridors and vents and observation hatchways forming a carefully orchestrated machine-biosphere. Every inhabitant either contributes to or feeds off the elaborate cycles—like the cogs of some sub-aquatic engine, each turning in relation to the others.

Yet, despite all this activity, you can still glide through long expanses where no one else appears—vast, unoccupied corridors echoing with the throb of distant turbines. The sense of emptiness in these stretches feels poignant, as if the place is designed to hold more. Perhaps there are entire species yet to be “printed” or grown, entire expansions not yet built. In those silent corridors, you can sometimes hear the faintest scraping of metal on metal—suggesting that new scaffolds might be rising somewhere far off, or that hidden inhabitants are building sections of hallways so far uncharted.

Something about it all—the synergy of metal and flesh, the hush of new expansions waiting to be filled—makes you suspect that nothing here is done evolving. Each species you’ve witnessed has its role today, but tomorrow they may be redesigned, replaced, or further configured for some new function. This living ocean-machine has no final shape or boundary; it’s part laboratory, part factory, part living continent. And everything that dwells here—ribbon-fish blinking like living circuit boards, colossus crabs forging new corridors, ancient watchers that maintain equilibrium, and small drones tidying the edges—shares that fluid, experimental quality.

There is no single vantage point to see it all, no single voice that leads it, no single species dominating. Instead, life in these crafted seas is like an orchestra of specialized players, each fulfilling an indispensable, intricate role. Together, they breathe and pulse and replicate in the watery half-light, conscious or not of their part in maintaining a colossal, planet-sized mechanism. It is an ecosystem with an evolving blueprint etched into every shimmering scale and rivet, a place where inhabitants embody the constant tension between technology and biology, forging onward in an environment designed to keep reshaping itself, and all who dwell within it.
