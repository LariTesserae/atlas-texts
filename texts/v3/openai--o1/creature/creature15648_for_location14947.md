They dwell in this chalky, wind-bitten realm with a tireless tenacity that defies every assumption about what can endure in a place without water. They are not a single uniform people or species; like the environment itself, they have splintered into varied forms, each adapted to the harsh plateau in its own way.

Some are wanderers—lithe, angular silhouettes that drift at the periphery of wind-torn ridges. Their bodies are elongated and flexible, with limbs jointed in ways that let them brace against sudden gusts. Rough, fibrous hide wraps them like a second skin, bleached by dust into pale stripes. They keep small, flickering signals of light along their arms and chests—the result of embedded crystals scavenged from the plateau’s surface. It is said these crystals hum faintly, synced with the electric currents that flow through the dust storms. The hum helps the wanderers rest—an ever-present lullaby that resonates in their bones.

Farther in, near clusters of spined vegetation, you’ll find the gatherers. Stockier than the wanderers and often bearing thick, leathery plates, they have carved out a life among the crooked, burgundy spires, weaving fibrous nets and baskets. They cultivate relationships with the mineral-veined plants as if they were orchard keepers carefully tending fruit trees. The spines provide them with tough, sinewy strands that can be woven into net-like contraptions, perfect for catching the bright swarms of insect-like flyers that spiral overhead. When the gatherers aren’t busy collecting crystal flecks or gleaning bits of hammered metal from the plateau’s mysterious deposits, they’re tending small fires in blackened hearths—fires that smolder and crackle with a heat tinted by unknown chemicals. The flames burn a dim jade or magenta, a lingering echo of whatever compounds feed them.

Drifting high above, just at the edge of sight, are the gliders—creatures more akin to fish than birds, their flat, elongated fins catching the constant updrafts. They seldom alight on the plateau itself except to lay translucent eggs in sheltered clefts. Their silent, silver presence in the swirling gloom gives them an almost mystical quality. Sometimes you glimpse a glow along their undersides, reminiscent of tiny lightning storms pulsing across living skin. Despite their eerie beauty, these gliders can be fierce if provoked: a well-timed lash of a fin can slice across stone like a blade.

Closer to the huts, one notices the clan of crafters—those who shape the mineral plates and bone-like structures into dwellings. They appear more heavily built than either the wanderers or gatherers, with arms corded from wrestling hammered metal into workable shapes. Their hands are broad, flexible, with multiple opposing digits that allow for precision tool work despite the rudimentary nature of their materials. They have learned to sense the subtle vibrations carried through the stone surfaces of the plateau. Their hearing might be poor in open air, drowned by the wind’s shriek, but they feel the rumble of an approaching gale through their feet and fists. This tactile awareness helps them judge exactly when to reinforce a leaning hut or patch a roof before the next tumultuous storm.

No one group “owns” the plateau, but connections run among them. Traders cross from one cluster of spined vegetation to the next, exchanging lumps of hammered metal for fibrous ropes or the luminous organs of the insect swarms, which can be spun into fragile but brilliantly glowing threads. Over time, a loose society has taken shape, dotted all across the chalky plains, cunning enough to survive beneath swirling clouds that never give rain.

Those “many-legged birds” that skip between the vegetation are, in fact, the domesticated charges of the crafters and gatherers. Quick to notice a free scrap, the birds scuttle after wind-blown debris. In times of calm, you can see them herded by smaller figures—perhaps children—trying to coax the creatures into makeshift pens built from stacked mineral slabs. Their keening, metallic cries echo in the ravines whenever the flocks are corralled for shearing. Yes, shearing: the birds grow a curious bristle along their flanks, somewhat like stiff feathers, that can be harvested and used as a coarse fiber for weaving. In the hush between gusts, you might hear the rhythmic scraping of shears, the rustle of bristles, and the grateful chirrups of a freshly lightened bird.

Underneath all these rummaging, scuttling, and gliding lives are the truly hidden dwellers: pale subterranean crawlers, rumored to be the earliest inhabitants. Rarely glimpsed, they cling to hollow cavities within the plateau, feeding on mineral seepage that lines the cracks in the stone. Their bodies are wiry and half-translucent, with elongated, many-jointed forelimbs. Folk above hear their whispery scuttling in the night and sometimes discover their empty husks near bulging pockets of the plateau where mineral veins come close to the surface. These husks, once collected, resemble fragile sculptures made of bleached parchment. Legends say the crawlers hold knowledge of the plateau’s deeper secrets—the labyrinth of tunnels and catacombs below, where pillars of crystal arch like serpentine spines. Occasionally, the crafters or gatherers find a half-chiseled passageway that plunges down into darkness before abruptly terminating, as though abandoned in haste. The hush of that subterranean air is said to be more terrifying than the howling wind above.

Amid this tapestry of life, the people—those who built and still inhabit huts—are an ever-shifting mosaic of clans and tribes. They do not seem unified in a single culture but share survival strategies: they collect crystals for light, salvage bones for structural supports, and, when storms rage at full intensity, they huddle in circles around the glimmer of battered hearths. It might be tempting to see them as primitive, but their resourcefulness is sophisticated as any civilization forced to adapt to constant upheaval. They wield chisels carved from greenish mineral, so strong it can chip the toughest bones, and they forge elliptical blades from hammered scrap metal shaped in funnel-like forges. Their tools often bear chalky white coatings from the dust, the better to blend into the plateau’s pale surface. They harvest spined seeds from the burgundy vegetation, trading them between clans as currency.

On nights or days (it is hard to tell distinctions of time in this perpetual twilight) when the wind temporarily abates, the plateau fills with small gatherings that might be festivals or communal feasts. The people light clusters of red or green-lantern crystals, stoking fires that hiss with particulate sparks. The swirl of insect flocks overhead intensifies; many of these insects are drawn by the light, capturing it with their luminous abdomens and refracting it in dancing arcs. The gliders descend, momentarily drifting low in wide circles, as if curious about the ephemeral glow at ground level. Even the many-legged birds quiet their shrieks, perhaps alarmed or intrigued by the sudden hush in the wind. These gatherings don’t last long, for the next gust is never far off—but in that short respite, you sense how tightly knit the web of life truly is here.

Within the huts themselves, one might come across a figure hunched over a shallow basin, carefully sifting swirling dust for minute crystals. Another might be meticulously pressing shaped ribs and hammered plates into the hut’s structure to fortify it. Others might be refurbishing the small, blackened altars where lumps of hammered metal or crystal shards are placed as offerings. Whether these altars represent worship of wind, plateau, or star is unclear. Some crafters speak of a “Sky Current” that breathes the storms into existence, feeding the plants with mineral motes from above. Others stoically declare that the plateau came first, forging everything else in its image. All seem to agree that the wind is older than memory, equal parts destroyer and giver.

And so they persist in this dynamic, battered environment. The plateau’s edges crumble, spires reshape, and dust storms tear across the enormous stone stage. Yet among the chaos is a thriving complexity of beings to rival any lush oasis. Avian-lizard creatures, burly gatherers, drifting silver gliders, insect swarms weaving neon arcs, and hidden subterranean crawlers—together they form an interlocked puzzle of life, each piece uniquely adapted.

In the end, what lives here is not a single tribe or species. Rather, it is an entire chorus of forms that collectively claims this plateau as home, forging fragile alliances between storms. Those hammered-metal hearths are testaments to their resilience, as is every dusty net and every worn bone-and-mineral knife. The plateau demands constant sacrifice—often in the shape of battered structures, lost carvings, or even lives. Yet from that sacrifice comes a deeper bond with the land. Together, these dwellers have created a rough, dizzying harmony: all swirling around the savage wind, the swirling dust, and that humming, electric lifeblood that somehow nurtures them all.
