They call themselves many names, these people on the green plateau, but outsiders—if any were to chance upon this damp world—might simply call them the Mossweavers, for the way they coax leaves, roots, and fungal threads into the shapes of dwellings and paths. In the heavy mist, they appear at first as ripples of movement across slick bark and spongy ground, their figures nearly camouflaged by layers of living foliage clothing. Every limb, every tool, and every garment seems braided with the greenery around them, as though vines themselves chose to unfurl and wrap these people in living protection.

Closer up, one would see that their bodies are lithe, legs and arms long for climbing between the trunk-bridges and natural ledges formed by colossal roots. Their fingers and toes splay wide, skilled at finding footholds on wet bark. The coloring of their skin ranges in mottled hues of deep brown and olive green, helping them blend into the forest around them. A faint sheen on their bare skin suggests oils they render from seeds and amphibious creatures, offering them a degree of waterproofing. Even their hair—often woven with bits of lichen or braided with mossy strands—lends them an almost plant-like appearance. 

These people move through their settlement with confidence born of generations spent in a place where vines can slip from underfoot and rainwater can appear in a sudden cascade from the canopy. Their steps are sure along narrow walkways lashed to trunk after trunk; each footfall is practiced, almost reverent, so as not to jar the delicate vines that hold everything in place. Beyond the walkways, they handle the daily tasks of survival with quiet skill: harvesting succulent leaves and edible mushrooms from the forest’s living walls, snatching insects from the air with nets hung high in the branches, or carefully shaping new huts by guiding the growth of pliable saplings into rounded frames.

They speak with voices that carry over the incessant dripping and churning of water. Sometimes their calls are sharp, like birds’ cries, echoing against the bark and disappearing into the layers of damp leaf canopy. At other times, they communicate through low murmurs and patterned clucks—an evolving language drawn from the natural chorus surrounding them. When the wind gusts and droplets fall in a sudden shower, you can hear them burst into laughter, bright and quick, catching at leaves overhead or shielding their baskets of gathered fruit.

Among them are elders—those who have known many cycles of seasons on the plateau—who pass on an oral tradition of how the settlement was first coaxed from the living forest. They tell of how thick vines were twined to form the earliest huts, each corner interwoven with silent gratitude, so that the forest itself offers a roof and walls. These elders still cultivate mosses in small, carefully tended patches, weaving them into comfortable bedding or insulating mats. They speak of the plateau’s natural cathedrals—where roots and trunk arches create especially large, hidden chambers—and how certain sacred gatherings take place there in evenings filled with rolling fog. The flicker of resin torches illuminates the streaming water runs, while drums—fashioned from hollowed logs and stretched animal hide—beat a pulse that reverberates through the wet air.

Within those tree-rooms, the Mossweavers celebrate life’s rhythms. They might chant in a way that mimics the percussive drip of water around them or dance with steps that mirror the scuttling and soaring of the creatures they share this plateau with. Occasionally, the hush-then-roar lullaby of distant waterfalls punctuates their ceremonies, and the people attune themselves to the pounding harmony by swaying in unison. They see in the flow of water a great continuity—raindrop merges with stream, stream merges with root, root merges with trunk, trunk merges with leaf, and leaf in turn welcomes the next raindrop. 

Children learn to climb before they learn to run. Their earliest games involve swinging between vine-bridges, discovering how to balance on slick bark and leap across narrow water channels. Wide-eyed and fearless, they grin through the haze of forest mist, ever agile, ever quick to adapt. Their laughter mingles with the chirps and warbles of avian companions that live among the canopy huts. Some families even maintain a tradition of training certain bright-plumed birds to carry small woven pouches—a rudimentary courier system for seeds or small trinkets across the settlement’s higher branches. 

Animals beyond the settlement also play a significant role. The plateau teems with myriad, half-glimpsed shapes: slender-bodied lizards with fans of luminous frills, amphibians whose bodies blend so expertly with moss-laden rocks that only their blinking eyes give them away, great heavy-browed herbivores that pass in measured strides and make the root-bridges quake beneath their weight. The people have found ways to coexist with these creatures without driving them off. Sometimes you will see a young Mossweaver perched on a thick root high above, quietly whispering or chanting a low tone that seems to soothe a towering beast trudging below, guiding it gently around the huts.

Food sources come from the forest in myriad forms. Honey-like saps drip from wounded vines into carefully placed gourds. Leafy rosettes sprout in crevices where decaying logs meet the living trunk, ripe with succulent hearts. Large, flat fungi serve as plates or containers, drying out to a durable leather-like texture. In many huts, you will see bunches of these fungi hung up to cure, while families gather around, braiding and weaving new nets by communal torchlight. 

And always, there is the water: trickling down trunk-gutters, channeling into hollowed knots of wood or large woven cisterns. Staggered spouts direct water from one level of the settlement to another, forming a dynamic flow that the Mossweavers have turned into a communal utility. With carved bowls of living bark, they scoop out water for drinking, cooking, or bathing. Children delight in chasing little rivulets that form miniature waterfalls between roots, turning the flow with small stones or bits of leaf to create swirling eddies.

At the plateau’s edge, where the slippery rock descends into chasms cloaked in mist, a handful of watchtowers—really just platforms lashed to the sturdiest trunks—sit perched. From here, watchers peer through the shifting fog for any changes or approaching storms. When rain finally belts down or the cliffside runoff turns into roaring cataracts, the watchers sound hollow horns made from large beast bones, alerting everyone to secure the walkways and anchor the cisterns. Yet rarely is there any panic; these people know the plateau as intimately as one might know the lines on their own palm. Turbulence, whether from wind or water, is met with calm caution, for the Mossweavers trust their vines, their roots, and each other.

Occasionally, they gather in small groups to travel to lower elevations in search of new seeds or flinty stones for toolmaking. Their descent down the slick, algae-coated cliff faces is an art in itself—ropes of woven bark looped around stalwart trunks, steps carefully tested on half-hidden footholds. When they return, they bring stories of how the plateau appears from below: an improbable mass of green perched on a vantage that feels both remote and central to the swirling life around it. Their tales feed the settlement’s collective memory, weaving an expanding tapestry of knowledge that is never formally written but is recited, sung, and threaded into the rhythms of the day.

In the end, the Mossweavers—or whatever name any tribe prefers to use—live as if they are part of the plateau’s own living skin. They leave no strict lines between themselves and the forest’s architecture, no wedge between the comfort of home and the realm of beasts and birds. Every humid breath is taken in tandem with the rustle of leaves overhead; every footstep resonates into the network of roots underfoot. Their society—grown, not built—reflects the plateau’s vitality, always shifting and renewing but never rushing to tear down and remake. Everything about them, from the shape of their huts to their songs at dusk, speaks of a love and respect for the unstoppable surge of life that saturates the high, wet forest halls.
