They are not immediately obvious. You can wander down corridor after corridor, accompanied only by the stuttering hush of distant hydraulics, the drip of water, and the dull, metallic thunder of this world of machinery, without glimpse of anything that might be called an inhabitant. The first impression is that no one at all dwells here. But as you move deeper, you begin to sense that the place is not vacant or deserted; something here—all of this machinery, or something behind it—has found its way into life.

In the rattling gloom, it is easy for the mind to half-conjure shapes flitting beyond the corner of vision. Once in a while, you’ll hear a scraping sound that isn’t only the grind of a gear—an awkward stutter in the shadows, a faint pattern that suggests movement from something not anchored to the floor. Or you’ll see a brief spark of light that isn’t just a faulty lamp short-circuiting, but an angular reflection of metallic limbs shifting behind a twisted pipe. Long seconds pass between hints, and you wait, body thrumming with the engine-noise, until the subtle sign recedes. You move on, but you’re uneasy, imagining peculiar life flickering in and out of gloom.

Sometimes you pass clusters of half-finished parts—slabs of hammered metal or half-welded shells that suggest bodies in the making. They are vaguely anthropomorphic, though the “heads” might be elongated cylinders or ornate orbs. Here, too, you wonder: were they built and then abandoned, or are they in a stage of metamorphosis, waiting for the next piece of assembly to continue? Rust mottles these shapes, but they still gleam in patches, as though some mechanical caretaker polishes them occasionally, checking for flaws. The caretaker itself remains unseen, but the evidence is there: the parts lying in neat lines, bolts arranged in tidy rows beside them. It does not feel like the random clutter of scrap. Something is methodically making—repairing, perhaps designing—new forms for this place.

Then there are the watchers. This is only a name you give them, for you never see them directly. They inhabit the stern recesses behind the biggest machines: the vault-like turbines and the vast hydraulic pumps whose pounding resonates in your bones. You get glimpses of eyes—no, not eyes, but glimmering rings of pale light that recede if approached. In the shrieking storms of steam, you imagine tall silhouettes with branching spines. Or you smell something like hot metal and acrid oil, only to discover footprints in the greasy grime. Sometimes, in the half-light, you catch a reflection in puddles of condensation on the floor: a hunched shape that scurries away on multiple limbs, or else a rigid figure gliding majestically across a catwalk overhead, trailing swirling vapor.

For a long while, you tell yourself it’s a trick of the echoes, or illusions generated by the overhead lamps that flicker unpredictably. Yet the evidence builds up, each fleeting sign confirming that these watchers are not illusions. If they have faces, it’s impossible to say what expression lies there. They seem a part of the mechanism, intimately integrated into the architecture. They are no more or less strange than the gargantuan gears themselves. Possibly, they’ve lived here forever, an essential piece of the vast loop of machinery: guardians or maintainers, ensuring the unceasing processes remain on track.

But are they truly the only inhabitants—or even the primary ones? Another presence is felt: smaller, more insect-like things that scuttle in the wet corners, near the broken floodlights and the shallow troughs where water continuously trickles. You catch them in your peripheral vision: faint, glossy shapes that dart across the gloom. Once in a while, you’ll hear a sharp metallic clink just behind you, find a single drip of oily residue, then catch a glimpse of something retreating behind a battered steel partition. They are skittish, quick, and absolutely silent aside from the smallest taps when metal touches metal. They could be mechanical pests—some strange evolution of machine lice or rust beetles. Or perhaps they’re prototypes set loose to test certain corridors. Now they survive in the wild pockets that the watchers don’t frequent, living off the seepage of fluids and the hidden rivulets of lubrication. If they behave as living creatures, stealth is their means; if threatened, they vanish into the labyrinth of piping where no larger entity can follow.

Then there are the rumors (for one can have rumors even in places nearly empty): that down in the deeper recesses, the area that rumbles so profoundly you can feel the floor ripple, there dwell still other forms. Some are said to be tall as towers, shaped like machines yet with a grotesque tangibility that suggests living flesh interwoven with metal. It’s impossible to confirm because so few come back from those depths with coherent stories. The accounts that do return are riddled with half-finished sentences, as if describing them is too much. “It was…like an engine but breathing…eyes that were hoses or tubes…a mass of cables slithering…” And then the speaker falls silent, haunted by the recollection. But the gist is always the same: in the lowest cathedrals of machinery, something enormous and alive lumbers onward, fueling or commanding the plant as a beating mechanical heart might drive blood through the system.

Yet for all these sightings—angular watchers, scuttling insects, cyclopean behemoths—there remains a more pervasive occupant of this domain: the place itself. Each corridor, each pipe, each floor panel seems to pulse with slow, methodical intention. No single consciousness is easily singled out. Perhaps everything here is part of some collective mind, the watchers serving as nerve endings or sensory nodes, the smaller scuttlers as cleansing cells, and the subterranean behemoths as great organs. If so, the faint whisper of fungus and skeletal vegetation is a foreign infiltration, or maybe a quiet symbiosis: the machine-world’s acceptance of organic life that can nibble away at corrosion and feed on the damp. It all coalesces into an ecosystem so vast and so alien that calling any one creature “the inhabitant” seems too narrow.

You also can’t quite dismiss the possibility of humans, though you never see them. A half-human presence may be here, living with skin blackened by oil and hair that has turned dull from endless humidity, huddled in near-darkness. Perhaps they are the ones who gather lumps of half-processed ore from the conveyors, repurposing them for their own needs, forging tools from the endless supply of metal scraps. Maybe they are the descendants of those who built this labyrinth, existing in out-of-the-way chambers behind locked bulkhead doors. They might avoid the watchers; or perhaps they have some subterranean covenant with them. Now and then, you might catch a reflection in the water that almost looks too humanoid—though when you go to investigate, the corridor is vacant. If they are here, they live as ghosts. They know this place’s hidden corners and have learned to harness enough of its resources to survive in anonymity.

And so, the question of who or what truly lives here has no single answer. At times, you may feel as though the machinery itself is the animating force—the place’s mighty pumps and shifting belts the real “organisms,” while any other creature is merely a passenger or parasite. In other moments, you swear you sense a collective intelligence behind each clang and hiss, as though the entire edifice were more than the sum of its corroded parts, orchestrating exact motions with a grand, inscrutable aim.

The watchers, too, grow more concrete as you allow your eyes and ears to linger in the swirling gloom. Perhaps they wear tattered robes of metal plates. Maybe they glide on rails that extend organically from their elongated limbs, or stand perched on stilt-like contraptions. Their silhouettes are mutable in the flicker of broken lights: some tall and gaunt, others squat, multi-limbed, or wheel-mounted. Their “faces” might be arrays of glinting lenses or layered metal masks that shift to reveal interior workings. If you dare get close enough (assuming they let you), you might hear the whirr and hum of small servos near the joints. And in that moment, you’d realize they’re exquisitely maintained, as though time’s ravages have been held at bay. They are the keepers, or the caretakers, or simply the scions of an ancient design.  

And yet, an alternate possibility tugs at your mind: that all these shapes—humanoid or insectile—are illusions of half-decayed artificial intelligence systems that once roamed freely, manifested by malfunctioning projectors flickering in the wet gloom. Perhaps the watchers exist only as flickers on the edge of reality, now passing spectral glimpses through the layering of centuries. But if so, how do footprints appear? Whence the carefully arranged piles of newly welded forms? The mind balks at the contradictions.

In the end, any attempt to name or definitively catalog these inhabitants feels incomplete, overshadowed by the living tapestry of steel and water. The real occupant is the labyrinth itself, a juggernaut of mechanical processes and unstoppable cycles—its minions, whether born of metal or the product of your own deep-seated, primal fear, are but tendrils of the larger being. They appear and disappear, ephemeral and ephemeral no longer, shifting with each seismic groan. They are pieces of the puzzle: watchers that roam the high catwalks, skittering swarms rummaging the run-off, breeding illusions that cling to the half-light. Some may be survivors descended from ancient architects, still forging shapes or tinkering with pipes far in the recesses below. Others could be fresh emergences of the place’s own evolving syntax—a new living language of metal.

Nothing stands still here. As the gears engage and the entire structure shudders, you sense that some timeless momentum pushes onward, forging new forms and discarding old ones. In the sudden hush after a machinery surge, you might see a figure turn its head, as though aware you are watching. Or you might hear an echo of footsteps in a chamber you know to be empty. The plant itself breathes, and all that exists within it is drawn, in some way, into the breathing. Over and over, forging, recycling, repeating—a testament to the unstoppable, ongoing life of the labyrinth.

So: who or what truly lives here? A line of watchers with metal spines and luminescent eyes lurk around the next corridor. A swarm of mechanical insects hunts for drips of lubricant beneath the pumping stations. A reclusive cluster of half-human survivors keeps to secret warrens. Towering colossi rattle the deepest halls, bridging the boundary between the animate and inanimate. And, ultimately, the entire structure, from the riveted steel supports to the perpetual conveyor belts, from the pounding pistons to the drifting mists, is itself alive—animated as a single body, incomplete without its scattered cells of hidden creatures.  

In this domain, life wears many faces—or no face at all. It is a tapestry of metal, water, inertia, and unguessable purpose. Every hiss, every clang, every shift in the gloom hints at something stirring, just barely out of direct sight. Perhaps it has always been this way. Perhaps it will always be, as the labyrinth’s spindly vegetation and slow exhalations of steam continue to shape new life, forging inhabitants out of rust and imagination long after any original blueprint has been lost.
