They are difficult to name, these residents of the high plateau of water, because the line between mechanism and organism—between biology and industry—has blurred until it’s nearly meaningless. Still, one can glimpse distinct populations, each curiously adapted to the constant, bone-rattling roar and the unrelenting cold.

•

First, there are the swift and sinuous shapes that patrol the upper layers of the water. If you stand quietly, you will see a faint outline breach the surface for what might be a breath or a taste of air. They have translucent bodies like filaments of pale gold, though sometimes in a slant of that diffuse light you catch a shifting band of lavender or pale green along their flanks. They move too quickly to be studied in detail, but they leave behind clues of their nature: in the wake of each slice through the water, tiny ripples carry a humming resonance that lingers for a second or two, just long enough to confirm they are more than illusions.

In the moments they break the surface, you might see a delicate convolution of gill-like frills on the sides of their elongated throats, or faint ribbons of cartilage that wrap around their bodies like a partial exoskeleton. Their eyes, if they have eyes, are subsumed beneath layers of nearly transparent skin; instead, they appear to sense the environment with a combination of electric pulses and pressure differentials in the super-dense air. They rely on subtle shifts in the industrial hum to find their bearings, forever aware of the clang and thud of metal spires as it oscillates through water and air alike. They are made for motion: slender, fluid, ephemeral.

Yet they’re not frantic creatures. In the hush between waves of metallic pounding, you see them drift in swirling arcs, nibbling at something invisible just below the surface. Perhaps they filter nutrients from the mineral-laden currents that swirl perpetually in this watery roof-world; perhaps they consume the faint plumes of steam that erupt from broken pipes. However they feed, they do so lightly, as if each slender shape requires only traces of nourishment. Their proliferation, then, arises not from the bounty of any obvious source but from their sheer numbers and efficiency at gleaning these hidden streams of energy.

•

Then, deeper below, loom the behemoths. They are slow-moving colossi, neither wholly machine nor wholly flesh. A single glimpse of one can feel like witnessing tectonic plates shifting under the sea. Their massive backs or shells—sometimes segmented, sometimes ridged—jut out from the water in crusted arcs. Embedded across these surfaces are dull sheets of metal plating, riveted seamlessly into living textures. Down their spines, lights pulse in mesmerizing sequences of blue, white, and amber, reminiscent of hazard lights on an abandoned factory floor. With every pulse, the water around them shudders, as if in sympathy with their signals.

Many theories might be spun about their purpose. In some corners, it almost looks as though these creatures were grown or built to shepherd the mechanical spires, polishing them with the abrasive scouring of their metal-laced flanks. Or perhaps they were once used as living cargo-haulers, back when someone or something needed to transport materials across the watery plane. Their wide, impervious undercarriages push aside the frigid water with languid sweeps of enormous fins banded in steel and keratin. Each stroke resonates as a subsonic rumble in the water, echoing the throb of the distant engines.

They never seem hurried. Their cycles appear as methodical as any clockwork. You can watch one for hours, see it drift near a spire, graze its side with a metallic-sheathed tail, and then angle away with Herculean grace, leaving behind a swirl of glowing bubbles. These ephemeral bubbles sometimes carry a faint phosphorescence—teal or purple—drifting to the surface in a slow, wavering ascent, only to collapse when they meet the cold air above. In these moments, the place briefly feels alive with color, a reminder that for all its monochrome severity, the plain of water can spark with brilliant display.

•

Along the surfaces of the basalt crags and the dripping, riveted columns, there exist smaller creatures that cling to metal scaffolding and mineral outcrops alike. They look at first glance like clusters of nodules or lumps of slag, easy to miss amid the frost-encrusted cables and pipes. But if you stay long enough, you eventually notice them shift position, creeping along beams with a slow, deliberate crawl. Their outer shells have a matte, slate-gray appearance, making them nearly indistinguishable from the stone. Yet beneath a crack in that shell, you might catch glimpses of a riotous, shifting array of color banding—like a glowing circuit board or bioluminescent coral.

Some believe these dwellers serve a cleansing function—perhaps living on the byproducts of the endlessly churning machines. Their bodies appear studded with specialized pores that inhale the briny air and the mineral-laden drips of condensation. The pores extrude tendrils of living tissue that, in turn, reel in possible nutrients from the swirling vapors. Meanwhile, an interior set of metallic plates, faintly warm to the touch, suggests that they convert these industrial runoffs into the energy they need to persist.

Occasionally, a basalt outcrop might groan—a resonance of the engines, or a shift in the stone’s internal stress—and the entire cluster of these shell-like creatures pulses with some shared impulse. Their shells open just a fraction, revealing pale, luminous filaments within, before they clamp shut again. It happens in the space of a breath, if it happens at all. Local mythology—even if “mythology” is too grand a term for rumor in such a place—holds that these are the original caretakers left behind when the makers vanished, quietly performing tasks of maintenance or calibration.

•

Closer observation along some half-submerged walkways reveals yet another class of inhabitants: bounding, near-quadrupedal figures that scuttle across bridging struts and into the shadows of battered corridors. They move with an uncanny fluidity, as though every movement is part dance, part mechanical pivot. Their bodies are sheathed in overlapping plates reminiscent of hinged machinery; each step sounds like the click and tick of clock gears. If one sees you, it might pause, tilting its angular head in what could be curiosity or calculation, lens-like eyes reflecting the cold, gray light. Then it slips away, vanishing into vents or behind warped plating.

They seem to favor not the open water but the labyrinth of metal supports beneath the platforms. One might find them hunched quietly before defunct control panels, slender limbs gently probing rusted levers or half-buried wiring. It’s as if some fragment of caretaker’s programming urges them to check and re-check the failing hardware, though they lack the necessary dexterity—or intelligence—to effect repairs. Still, it’s possible they glean small morsels of energy or raw materials from the slight warmth that these ancient consoles exude. Perhaps they exist in symbiosis with the humming spires, living scavengers that rely on the stray currents and residual pulses that the ancient machinery continues to emit.

•

Beneath the surface, in the vast midwaters that remain mysterious even in relative shallows, smaller clouds of ephemeral planktonic shapes drift in faintly luminous swarms. Unlike anything known on earth, these drifting constellations of life function more like a living network of filaments—delicate, translucent cells extending threadlike chains through the water. When a pulse of the machinery rumbles through, you see them respond in waves of color, passing signals among one another like a slow electrical storm. They feed on microparticles of minerals and perhaps the decaying detritus that sloughs off the giants’ shells. In turn, they become a food source for the swift pale predators near the surface, creating a self-contained loop of energy consumption.

Some explorers posit that these floaters are the real bedrock of all life here. Unable to anchor to any soil—since no land, no vegetation exists—they anchor, instead, to each other, weaving ephemeral scaffolds of tissue in the water column. At times, bits of these scaffolds detach and drift away, forming a hazy bloom in the distance. It’s possible the entire plateau is ringed with these drifting, luminous webs, each patch an independent colony delivering nutrients via chemical reactions that rely on the undersea streams of mineral-laden water.

•

Now and then, signs appear that not all inhabitants are purely biological. An oblong shape drifts by, surfaces for a moment with a hiss of compressed gases, then submerges again—leaving you uncertain whether you observed an automaton or an organism. It bears no eyes and no visible mouth, only a sleek hull covered in barnacle-like attachments. Some watchers claim these “hull-born” entities have seals or flanges matching the design of the spires’ piping, as if they were manufactured in the same foundry that produced the rusted walkways above. Others tell stories of seeing them open a hidden compartment on their underside, from which smaller, scuttling shapes emerge, built of the same black metal. No one has found any conclusive evidence for these claims, but enough fleeting sightings suggest a plausible mechanical lineage. They might be the final iteration of a self-replicating system, drifting indefinitely, scanning or sampling the environment, with no caretaker left to guide them.

•

All these residents exist in a curious dance with the industrial thunder that dominates their world. The physical environment never changes—no tides to rise and fall, no hours of sunlight to shift their rhythms—yet each species bears a cyclical pattern deeply ingrained. Under the unvarying metallic roar and the constant colorless glow, the creatures coordinate with each other and with the machinery in an endlessly repeating choreography.

Sometimes, if you watch long enough, you sense that these cycles are intertwined. The swift surface dwellers leap in sync with the subsonic exhalations of the ancient turbines. The large, half-metal leviathans shift their routes precisely when the rhythmic pounding hits certain crescendos. The shell-creatures on the basalt outcrops crack open their carapaces in a mass display only after a wave of deep vibrations rings out from those subterranean pipes. And the scuttling quadrupeds on the spires evoke the image of unknowing priests, tending an altar whose functional purpose eludes them, yet whose rituals they cannot abandon.

•

In the end, the “who” or “what” that lives here defies neat categorization. Some contain more flesh than metal; others, more metal than flesh. They share, however, an origin that neither you nor they can fully remember—an ancient impetus that brought the metal spires into being at this cosmic altitude of water. And they all serve, in some unspoken capacity, the maintenance of a world built on unwavering routine.

So the plateau remains: cold, timeless, hammered ceaselessly by the drone of half-forgotten engines. The inhabitants live in cyclical adaptation around that drone, weaving a precarious food web from mineral-laced flows and the relic exhalations of centuries-old pipes. No caretaker is visible; perhaps there never was. And yet life endures here—strange, self-contained, half-glimpsed, bound by an industrial heartbeat no one can shut off, continuing on, century after century, in a place where nothing else changes.
