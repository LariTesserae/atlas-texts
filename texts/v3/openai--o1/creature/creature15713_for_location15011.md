They do live here, though you might not see them all at once. At first, you notice only movement at the edges of sight—a shifting silhouette above a pillar, or a faint ripple among the vines. Yet there are inhabitants in every corner: the small ones, the hidden ones, the nearly invisible presences, and perhaps something larger that you can only guess at in the hush.

Some of them are easy to spot: the skittering insects with elongated limbs, more joints than they strictly need. You observe them climbing the stone columns with a rhythmic, mechanical precision, each segment of their legs clicking into place. When threatened or startled, they seem to hiccup in reality—glitching from one spot to another a finger’s breadth away, as if they occupy two positions briefly before settling. In clusters, these insects form living mosaics along the warm, pale floors, creeping and rearranging themselves in symmetrical patterns. If you stay quiet, you can see them clack their mandibles in delicate conversation—a series of ticks and taps that echoes in stony corners.

Among them scuttle lizards with scales like fractal mirrors. When they move, the patterns seem to break apart, as if you are looking through a kaleidoscope that reassembles once they halt. Their eyes flicker with a faint luminescence, reflecting more of the hidden light in the ceiling than should be possible. Individually, these lizards appear skittish, but in groups they cooperate, turning their heads in synchrony, pausing at the same fleeting moment. Every so often, you catch them leaning against the vines, almost as if they feed from the rustling leaves or share some secret warmth with the greenery.

There are also small, rodent-like creatures you might call mice or voles—but with ghostly thin fur that reveals luminous threads beneath their skin. Their eyes gleam too brightly in the gloom, and you sometimes see them vanish behind an altar, only to reappear in an entirely different crevice moments later. They seem timid but also watchful, quivering in place until they perceive danger, at which point they melt into the architecture, blending into corners and reemerging somewhere else. They gather scraps of plant matter—bits of succulent leaves that have dropped or been pruned by the vines themselves—and you get the sense that neither the leaves nor the rodents truly mind this. Their existence appears symbiotic. Whenever one of the rodents chews on a piece of leaf, you can hear a faint hum, as if the plant quietly resonates in pleasure or acknowledgment.

Then there is the avian life: birds with long beaks designed to probe the joint lines in the stone, pecking at lichen or minute droplets of resin-like secretions that seep from the finer cracks. Their feathers shimmer with a dull, ceramic quality—feathers you might think made of some flexible tile rather than keratin. In flight, they flicker as the overhead faceted plates glow more intensely where the birds pass, as though the crystals in the ceiling respond to their motion. You might see them nest atop pillars, weaving small alcoves from pliable vine tendrils that the greenery zealously provides. There is a welcome hush in their calls; they rarely shriek. Instead, their voices take the form of subdued clicks or an almost wooden knock, echoing as the sound drifts down the empty corridors.

But beneath and behind all these smaller creatures is a presence that suggests the original builders still remain—perhaps not in human form, perhaps not in any form that makes immediate sense. If you look closely at the archways and corners where the angles bend strangely, you may spy graceful figures that seem carved from the same pale stone—until one of them turns its head, or a limb twitches, or an eye opens where there was no eye before. They are part of the architecture: caretaker-entities who wear the temple’s shape as a vast cloak. Sometimes you see them half-merged with walls, arms crossing seamlessly from vine-wreathed stone into living tendon. At other times, a large shape might step out from a pillar, as if peeling away from it, then melt back in an instant later.

When you see them more directly—if you dare to fix your gaze too long—they look tall and spindly, with elongated limbs and broad, flat torsos. Their skin (if you can call it that) is a stone-like membrane laced with hairline cracks, through which a hint of greenish or amber glow can be glimpsed. You never see them speak; you never hear them make more than a faint grinding sound, like a chisel against rock in the far distance. And yet you sense they communicate among themselves, perhaps through glances or subtle vibrations. Occasionally, you hear a deeper click ring out in the silence. It might travel down the corridors as if echoed by the architecture, a summons or a question. You never see them build or carve—yet the illusions of shifting pillars and corners might be their handiwork, done with a softness so deft that you only notice the changes after they’ve occurred.

The vines and these stone beings seem in perfect accord. One moment the vine will shift, offering up a succulent bud; the next, the stone figure might incline its head, gazing over it with something approaching reverence. Then the vine quietly retracts, content, perhaps fed by invisible energies of the architecture. It is as though the entire place is a single elaborate organism, with the caretaker-entities as specialized cells—rarely glimpsed but vital to the matrix of stone and leaf.

Other denizens resemble living shards of architecture spawned by the building itself. You might see an angular creature shaped like a partial arch, scuttling across the floor, or glimpse the flicker of an oddly contoured shape gliding along a wall, imprinting a faint pattern behind it as it moves. These beings could be illusions, cast by flickering contradictions in the geometry—or they could be real living sculptures, their surfaces shimmering in the steady, impossible light. Every so often, one of these ephemeral things might pass through a corridor, leaving behind a faint hum that resonates in your chest, as though it momentarily alters the frequency of the entire realm.

In the deep recesses—places you haven't yet ventured—rumor echoes of larger presences: labyrinthine creatures reminiscent of coiled serpents or living columns that move imperceptibly across centuries. You might suspect that the soft heartbeat you sometimes feel through the floors belongs to one of these leviathans, an ancient caretaker older and vaster than the rest. If so, it rouses only faintly, exhaling a long, silent breath of warmth that keeps the vines alive in this waterless domain. Or perhaps the pulse belongs to the structure itself, the grand parent of all these creatures, an architectural consciousness that welcomes and shelters them.

Yet for all their strangeness, the life-forms here do not seem malevolent. Instead, they appear to be devout keepers of a fragile harmony—participants in an otherworldly ecosystem that has gathered around the fresh-chiseled stone. Their existence weaves in and out of the architecture, integral but never loud, each creature calm in its place, from the flickering insects and fractal-scaled lizards to the half-stone watchers who glide through walls. It is a quiet synergy: each shape, each motion, each hushed sound echoing an unspoken vow to keep the dryness and light stable, warding off the ordinary decay that the outside world might bring.

And all the while, that persistent, rhythmic pulse resonates in the backdrop, a subterranean heartbeat or a slow drum. It might be the shared breath of the vines and stone. It might be the joint will of the caretakers—every living being fused into one silent congregation. The entire place breathes in unhurried measure, unchanging yet always just on the brink of shifting. Reality ripples softly at the edges, as though it stands ready for a new flourish of life or structure, for new creatures to be carved from stone, or for existing ones to evolve that extra joint or fractal pattern. And in that hush, you sense that whoever or whatever lives here is part of a living tapestry: a place made on purpose, quietly shaped by careful, patient intelligences, where architecture and life are no longer separate but two halves of one birthing creation.
