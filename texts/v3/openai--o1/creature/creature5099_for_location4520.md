They are not a single species, nor a single people, and it is difficult even to say they are a “they.” The creatures dwelling in this aqueous cathedral—this half-formed realm of churning water and freshly minted stone—seem as unsteady and protean as the environment itself. One moment, you think you see a sinewy biped laboring over a rampart of wet clay; the next, there is only swirling water and a lingering distortion of shape.

Beneath the constantly crashing currents, near the pale flutters of vegetation, you might glimpse the most “solid” inhabitants: lithe amphibian-humanoids that cling to the newly formed rock spines. Their skin sometimes appears metallic or pearlescent in the shifting light, as though each scale or patch of flesh is uncertain how to reflect the flickering brightness filtering through overhead water. They have wide, shining eyes that can rapidly change focus, tracking the blasts of liquid overhead. These beings never stay exposed for long; with each new deluge, they slip into pockets of half-submerged tunnels or scramble behind a half-built stone archway, doing their best to dodge nature’s building and unbuilding of the terrain.

When you catch them at rest (if that state can be called rest), these amphibious forms gather in small numbers on the leeward sides of razor-sharp ridges. Their limbs are wiry, their frames built for clinging as the world around them is battered anew. You can see slender webbing at their fingers and behind their ears—rapidly retracting and extending based on the density of water or air in the immediate vicinity. Perhaps these are gills that transition to rudimentary lungs depending on the swirl of fluid or the sudden rush of air. Their motions have a fluid grace that mirrors the currents above, but their glances are constantly darting, tense, all too aware that a stray surge might wrench them loose at any second.

Though they are the largest creatures you might see, they appear in such brief, fitful moments that you can’t easily determine a hierarchy or social order. You might guess they share food (likely those tremulous plants or captured insects), trade bits of pulp and stone for makeshift shelters, or scurry to warn each other of an oncoming torrent. Their homes—if they can be called that—are the half-ruined huts of driftwood pulp, mud mortar, and stone scraps. Some look newly built, but others appear older or partially digested by the swirling environment, suggesting that generations might have tried to eke out a living here, never quite finishing the job before the world reconfigured itself again.

Not all inhabitants walk or crawl. At times, you might see swirling, serpentine shapes gliding right through the thicker bands of water overhead. Slick creatures with elongated bodies, flexible fins, and multiple sets of translucent membranes that flare and contract, letting them slip through the chaotic channels. They might be “fish,” if fish could breathe in half-water, half-air, and navigate gravity that’s turned inside out. Their colors are fleeting coruscations of pink, indigo, or deep green, quick to vanish into the murmuring gloom. When they fade from view, one wonders whether they dissolved or simply slid behind a swirl of fluid, invisible but always close.

Then there are the tinier inhabitants—the ephemeral insects that cling to the half-born leaves. Some look more crystalline than chitinous, their wings as thin and reflective as shards of ice. You catch them only in the corner of your eye, a glimmer on a pale, fingerlike frond whipping back and forth in the current. They flit into near-vacuums that form unpredictably—suddenly occupying air pockets to do whatever it is they must (sipping nectar from the translucent plant stems?), only to be swept away or pressed beneath the water once again. At times, these insectlike beings swarm in small clusters, as if seeking each other for safety or for the fleeting exchange of nutrients. Their glimmering wings and elongated legs make them seem halfway to becoming the next swirl of watery light.

Spidery forms, too, haunt the edges of your vision: multi-limbed creatures that emerge from cracks in fresh stone, quickly weaving sticky, ribbonlike filaments that float and shimmer in the watery flux. These webs are ephemeral—just as they begin to stretch from one spire to another, the next surging current tears them apart, scattering them into swirling ribbons. Yet the creatures persist in weaving. Each destroyed thread is replaced with another in ceaseless adaptation, as though the act of weaving is more important than the final structure, in the same way the entire realm is always reinventing itself.

And there are the ephemeral echoes of older patrons, presences that might be more memory than flesh. In corners where fresh outcrops scrape the watery canopy, you may sense the echo of a voice or see the fleeting silhouette of something tall. These shapes are vaguely humanoid, but they vanish as soon as you try to perceive definite features—displaced ghosts of ancient builders or explorers, perhaps. Sometimes they linger just long enough that you might see a hand pressed against a newly formed wall of stone, as though testing its solidity, before the whole scene sloughs away like wet sand.

In the chaotic spaces between these distinct forms drift other, less concrete entities—cloudy conglomerations of matter, lumps of what might be living mineral or half-formed jelly, swirling in the watery currents with no evident sense of direction. Occasionally, these lumps collide and fuse or crumble and dissolve. They may be nascent organisms struggling to find stable shape, or simply lumps of the world itself, flirting with becoming alive. In a place where fluid reality is in a constant state of birth and reach, the line between living creature and raw environment blurs.

If there is a uniting factor for these inhabitants, it is their shared necessity: agility, readiness for disruption, and an instinct that this realm’s synergy with life is precarious at best. Each inhabitant—long-limbed amphibian, shimmering fish-serpent, ephemeral insect, or vague watery phantom—knows without knowing that anything too rooted, too weighted, will eventually be torn away. So they remain light, flexible, watchful.

One can imagine them forging an unspoken culture: a culture of perpetual hazard, frenzied creativity, and acceptance that no structure—physical or social—will outlast the next monumental surge. Perhaps there are moments of communal rest, softly illuminated in one of the temporary air pockets, with a hush settling as the watery realm passes them by. In that hush, maybe they share subtle gestures or near-silent calls, letting the resonance of vibrating water carry meaning. Then, the next wave arrives, reconfiguring everything, scattering the dwellers again into their hiding places or sweeping them into the open.

It’s fitting that in a space so luminous, so violently reworked, the beings that reside here look as though they’re partially made of that same bright turbulence. Even the fauna that appear most opaque—like the amphibian-humanoids—carry a translucent quality at their extremities, dissolving into the chaos like living illusions. And still, they persist, their presence adding to the place’s singular reality, breathing life into the watery churn.

If you linger here long enough, you might glimpse a time-lapse sense of continuity: of small communities forming at the base of a stone ridge, carving out a flimsy shelter, forging a moment’s respite—and then the entire ledge is lost beneath a new torrent. Yet the same dwellers might reappear hours or days later on a new outcrop, again building, again persisting. Like the realm, they, too, are caught in eternal creation and destruction. They are part of the roar, part of that unstoppable birth of raw existence. If there is any single truth to them, it is that they are neither finished nor undone, but always in the process of becoming—just like the world around them.
