Some inhabitants here never let themselves be seen outright—they dwell in the twitch of a leaf, in the swirl of mist, part of the hum that pervades this mossy hollow. Others move with more deliberate steps or clank of metal. As you wander the basin’s soft, uneven floor, you feel their presence like a heartbeat just out of sync with yours.

At first, you might suspect the basin is deserted aside from a few bright-plumed birds darting between colossal ferns and the odd salamander wriggling in a carpet of wet moss. But hints emerge if you stay. Perhaps you notice footprints—an astonishing variety of them—pressed into the soft soil where the slope flattens into a mossy ledge. Some imprints look humanoid, though smaller than a typical adult’s foot, with splayed toes that might suggest a lifetime of barefoot climbing. Others could be elongated stubs, as though from a large, weight-bearing animal with padded ankles that press into the loam. And then there are track marks that resemble nothing you’ve ever encountered, as if some multi-limbed machine tread gingerly across the forest floor.

Follow the footprints, and you may come upon a tangle of vines parted in a rough arch. Beyond it, you might see evidence of an individual or a small cluster of caretakers. The first occupant often glimpsed—but seldom approached—is a figure stooped under tattered oilcloth, hooded in fronds of dripping moss. A caretaker or hermit, forever tinkering with the half-ruined power station. One moment, they’re hefting a toolbox up a precarious stack of crumbling stone blocks; the next, they’re adjusting an ancient valve that hisses with fresh steam. You might spot the glint of goggles, reflecting sudden rays of sunlight through the canopy—an inscrutable stare peering back at you before turning away. They rarely speak, if they speak at all, and the waterfalls’ roar swallows any language they might utter. Yet the caretaker’s domain stretches over every panel of rusted steel and coil of wire. There’s a studied gentleness in how they lay a hand on the twisted metal, coaxing the station’s heart to keep pumping, as though it’s a living creature in need of calming.

Not far from this tinkerer’s realm dwell small clans of mechanical drones that scuttle through the undergrowth. They aren’t standard machines; they look cobbled together from salvage. Glimpsed from afar, they might be mistaken for large insects with segmented bodies of riveted metal and flickering eyes that glow faintly under the gloom. They fuss at the pylons where moss creeps up the metal skeleton, brushing away intruding vines or reattaching loose cables. Occasionally, you’ll hear the mechanical hiss of hydraulics as they fold their legs and settle, waiting for instructions only they understand. Their chitinous shells drip with condensation, so they seem to sweat alongside the leaves. Perhaps they serve the caretaker—perhaps they’re guided by older, half-forgotten directives coded into them before the station’s partial collapse.

Nature’s creatures, meanwhile, tread as softly as ghosts—because in many ways, they are. They’re fleeting glimpses: a vine-snake so well-mottled by algae and lichen you won’t notice it until it flickers across your peripheral vision. A few amphibians thrive where the rivulets trickle in perpetual gloom; glossy-skinned salamanders with vibrant, electric-blue stripes that blur as they slip over the wet boulders. Rare birds nest in the thick canopy, their plumage shot through with neon streaks of turquoise and saffron—colors that appear almost artificial in the dim underlight. Yet these animals feel like visitors, deterred from truly flourishing by the pervasive mechanical resonance. They come to feed on the insects that swarm near certain waterfalls or to lay eggs in mossy hollows, then vanish into the swirling cloud cover.

It’s rumored, by those who dare to wander deeper, that other dwellers inhabit the uncharted hollows and narrower ravines behind the waterfalls. Now and then, you hear stories of slender shadows glimpsed at the edges of cliffs, singled out by a sudden shaft of sunlight. These shapes reputedly climb the raw stone faces with preternatural ease, arms long and muscled, hands as sure as mountain goats’ hooves. Their eyes glint red in the gloom, or so the rumors say. They never linger when approached. Some wonder if they are feral remnants of an older settlement—people who adapted to these narrow corridors and mechanical hazards, losing themselves to the hush of water and the faint clang of metal until they became as much myth as memory. Others suggest they might be the original engineers, living off the station’s meager resources, half-wild but still mindful of their duties to keep the power station from failing entirely. Then, of course, there’s the notion that they’re simply illusions cast by the steam and dripping vines… but you swear you’ve seen footprints vanish into a slick, vertical wall of stone.

And in corners too damp or secluded for human or large animal comfort, clusters of strange fungi sprout in glimmers of phosphorescent color. No one would call fungi “inhabitants” in the sense of having desires or agency—yet here, their constant, patient expansion marks them as significant characters in the basin’s tapestry. Some shimmer in watery teal, dotting the power station’s corroded undercarriage; others cling to the base of massive trunks, quietly dissolving dead plant matter and whatever machine debris rolls their way. They follow no blueprint but their own fungal sprawl, bridging nature and metal in slow, unstoppable growth.

Late at night—if there even is a true “night” in this microcosm, with its perpetual cloud cover—you might experience something else. The power station’s lights flicker in a half-forgotten rhythm, cables crackle softly, and in the corners of your vision, you catch momentary outlines. Figures or creatures too fluid and fleeting to be pinned down. Some say ghosts of mechanics lost to time, or spirits of the mountain drawn to flickering neon. Others claim these are no more than illusions cast by the interplay of fog and an overtaxed imagination. Regardless, you can feel the basin itself taking in a breath, as if the entire ring of cliffs exhales a warm, humid sigh of cautious acceptance.

So the inhabitants here are neither a single tribe nor a single species—they are a layered community of caretaker machines, an enigmatic tinkerer (or perhaps a small circle of them), half-wild watchers on the cliffs, odd amphibians and birds that flit in to claim brief territory, luminous fungi that weave through root and metal, and the intangible presence of the place itself. All exist in a web of tension, as if each day is a truce: the hum of the power station offset by the unstoppable green hush of vines; the drip of water staving off rust so the metal might endure a little longer; the aloof visits of bright creatures staving off silence.

And always, all keep an uneasy harmony, abiding under the sharp jaws of rock that frame this cathedral of water and steam. If you stay long enough, you too might feel woven into the tapestry—another living thread in the unhurried story playing out beneath those newly hewn peaks. Here, in the hush between waterfalls and reverberating iron, in the shifting labyrinth of fungus and vines, life continues, quietly and persistently, forging alliances that only become clear long after you’ve noticed the footprints in the moss.
