They do not resemble people, not exactly. You might catch a half-formed glimpse of them in the watery haze, a distortion of the brightness that resolves momentarily into something shaped like a body—but never fully. Mostly, they move in slow drifts along the jagged edges of stone as if the rock provided them a current to glide upon. They leave no footprints nor any trace of passage, apart from a hush that falls in their wake—a settling of echoes. In the moments right before they appear, the constant tumble and clatter of shifting stones seems to pause, as though reality itself draws a held breath.

Some call them an ancient echo of those who once built the high stone huts. Others say they are the long-forgotten architects of the mountains themselves, still weaving new spires into existence by means beyond mortal comprehension. But if you watch carefully from a safe vantage, you will see these presences do not truly climb or walk. Rather, they flicker in and out of the luminous cracks in the air. One instant, you glimpse a slender silhouette perched on a narrow ledge; the next, it is gone, reappearing perhaps yards away on a different outcropping, or vanishing entirely. It is impossible to guess where one will materialize, for they seem to follow currents of logic that slip beyond typical geometry.

Most of the time, these inhabitants manifest only as faint shimmers, like sunlight bent across a waterfall’s mist. Their outlines hint at elongated limbs, cloaks that might be cascades of pale color, or smooth, mineral-like plating—no single shape persists for longer than a heartbeat. Now and then, you think you perceive a half-glimpse of a face: patient, impassive, neither kindly nor cruel, fixed in an expression that knits astonishment with resignation. You can’t be sure if these features truly exist or if your mind is struggling to fill in an empty shape. For these beings are more suggestion than substance, as though the glare and the stone and the watery veils conspired to create living illusions.

They appear unconcerned by the precariousness of the world around them. Where rock walls lean at impossible angles and fall away in sudden shear drops, they do not hesitate or flinch. Sometimes you’ll witness a sudden shift in the stone—a ledge crumbling, a spire collapsing—and out of that tumbling chaos, one of these figures emerges unscathed, seamlessly stepping from a new crack in reality. Do they cause these collapses or simply anticipate them, letting gravity and time reshape the mountains so they can slip in between? You cannot say. It all seems part of a continuous cycle of creation and dissolution.

In the old huts, you might find cryptic markings—delicate etchings in stone that look like repeated versions of the same swirling symbol, or interlocking spirals. Touch them, and behind your eyelids you might sense a strobe of bright images: silhouettes gathered around the same symbol, each figure blurring at the edges. Perhaps the huts were once tangibly occupied by simpler folk—people who chipped stone with primitive tools and built walls to keep out shrieking winds. Yet the huts remain inhabited still, though the new tenants have no need for roofs or protective barriers. Every so often, you catch the faint hum of something shifting inside a hut’s gutted doorway, as if a presence tests the old thresholds, trying to remember the shape of a life once lived in shelter.

Though they leave behind no footprints, they do leave ripples in the air. In narrow canyons, you may follow these ripples like trails. Step into one, and the hairs on your arms rise; you taste metal at the back of your tongue, sense the slow, clenching twist of space where rock meets sky. The brightness grows uncomfortably intense as your eyes strain to accommodate a new angle of being. Perhaps it is how they communicate—through silent rearrangements of distance and perspective. For in those moments, the geometry of the cliffs seems to breathe, spires bending closer, canyons retreating farther, as though the mountains were never as static as they appear. The illusions—these inhabitants—live in that fluid boundary between what is solid and what simply seems so.

When the waterfalls crash, stirring thunder in the canyon floors, you sometimes see them congregate at vantage points, half-lost in swirling mist. The brilliance refracts against their forms and casts incomplete rainbows across the stone. Each arc of color warps around them, and you can almost see how their shapes twist inside the spray, echoing the water’s trajectory. They stand or hover there, as if listening intently to the relentless pounding of water on rock. In those moments, they gain a more tangible clarity, akin to silhouettes carved from rainbow light. Then the waterfall recedes to a mere drizzle, and they dissolve back into half-presences.

No language emerges from them—no voices, no calls, nothing but the low resonant ringing that sometimes answers the sound of scouring stone. You might wonder if that tone is their communal heartbeat or some vestige of a memory older than the peaks themselves. Indeed, when a spire emits its profound, bell-like note, these ethereal forms brighten for a moment, as if invigorated. Perhaps the bell tone holds them here, binds them to the ridges they helped shape or once inhabited. Maybe they are the caretakers of that resonance, preserving it across eons in this luminous wedge of existence.

They remain watchers, keepers of a domain balanced on the knife-edge between worlds. They do not feed on flesh or water or even sunlight in the way living creatures do. Instead, they consume the transitions of matter, thriving on the interplay of creation and decay, stone worn by water, wind rearranging canyons. Whenever a crack widens or a boulder shifts, they drink in that moment of change like breath. Their presence suggests that the land’s perpetual realignment is not random but orchestrated—like a slow and ancient dance in which the entire range participates.

Among their few intelligible gestures is the arc of a limb—as if beckoning, or warning you away. They do not attack, not in the physical sense, but you can feel them tug at the edges of your perception. Lingering too long in this place risks becoming like them: half-here, half-elsewhere, caught in a flickering threshold where your shape unravels into distortion. Some travelers swear they’ve seen lost wanderers who stayed too many days among the radiant peaks, their bodies turning translucent, their eyes gleaming with the subdued light of an unspeakable clarity. Whether that transformation is death or metamorphosis remains unclear.

So these beings—some might call them ghosts of the architects, or guardians of the luminous stone—dwell in a realm that straddles solidity and dream. Their huts are mere remnants of a time when creatures with hands and feet and breath in their lungs eked out a living in high, wind-ravaged cliffs. Now, only echoes remain: the huts themselves, the intricate markings, and these drifting presences that quietly maintain the ceaseless shift of rock and haze. Watching them, you sense that they do not cling to life as you know it, nor do they hold any illusions of eternity. They simply exist, woven into the slow exhalation of this domain, guiding the mountains between collapse and renewal as eons stretch on.

That is who inhabits this land of glaring light and watery stone: not animals, nor people, nor any living thing you can name. Singular and solemn, they float where spires groan and waterfalls churn, resting in the tension between worlds. And perhaps they, too, are part of the brightness itself—an emanation from the sky that bleaches shapes into stark relief, a piece of the fracturing horizon made animate. If you listen with more than your ears, you can feel them hum in your bones. In a single seismic breath, they might rearrange the very mountainsides, or slip away in the next half-beat of silence, leaving nothing behind but the stony hush. Yet they are always here, in quiet vigil, sustaining the precarious splendor of peaks that should have fallen long ago.
