They live in the margins of dimness, that border between what the soft glimmer reveals and what remains swallowed by shadow. At first, you only ever glimpse them as outlines — smooth-limbed figures that slide behind a dripping archway or vanish into the cracks between wall and stone slab. Their eyes, if you manage to see them in the half-light, are moonlike: pearlescent, oddly unfocused, yet capable of catching the faintest glow. In that faint glimmer, you recognize something vaguely amphibian in their long limbs, in the flatness of their faces. But they are not exactly frogs or salamanders. Their skin has a certain opalescent sheen that suggests a mineral or fungal influence, as if they have quite literally grown out of this wet, stony domain.

At times, you catch the whisper-soft beat of their steps — flippered, spine-crested feet pressing delicately on stone — as they traverse the labyrinth of stacked dwellings. They almost seem weightless when they move, leaving no prints in the thin film of water that coats the corridors. And the watchers are quiet, uncannily so, yet you sense a heightened alertness in them. When one scurries off, a second and third may still be observing from a different vantage, gazing at you in silence. You come to suspect there are many more than you see. Perhaps dozens, or hundreds, living behind those rough stone walls, among the watery hollows, above in suspended lofts. Whenever you try to turn and catch a clearer look, they melt into the gloom with a liquid grace.

In the more open courtyard spaces, you get slight hints of how they spend their time. You might see one stooping at a shallow pool, dipping a spindly limb into the water as though testing its temperature or gleaning some nutrient from the mineral-laden fluid. You catch sight of them brushing their palms gently across the pale fungal growths that sprout along the stone. Those ghostly fans and spines sometimes quiver as the creatures interact with them, and you wonder if the watchers harvest a kind of sustenance from the fungus, or if the fungus itself is an extension of their bodies, or if they are simply communing in some cryptic way. Their relationship to the environment appears as natural and effortless as breathing.

Now and then, you see one or two watchers positioned near a vertical shaft or perched on ledges overlooking deeper chasms. Their posture is statuesque, almost meditative — calling to mind guardians or sentinels. They do not chatter or call out in any way you can discern, but the subtle tilting of their heads, the flick of a translucent membrane at the side of their throats, suggests an unspoken language. You suspect they communicate in vibrations, perhaps even in echoes too low for your ears to fully perceive. Moments before the resounding hammering and scraping intensifies from those distant recesses, you notice them tense, as though anticipating each thunderous wave.

That perpetual pounding — which reverberates through the entire city of stone — seems intimately tied to their existence. It rattles the walls, sending droplets scattering from stalactites and spidery mossless ridges overhead. Whenever those metallic crashes reach a crescendo, the watchers gather in small clusters, each cluster forming a compact ring. The watchers clasp one another’s forearms or rest elongated digits on each other’s shoulders in a delicate, ritual-like manner. The posture suggests they are absorbing the vibrations through their limbs, collectively attuning themselves to the primal rhythm. Perhaps it’s a form of worship, or a way to measure the earth’s shifting energies, or even a biological necessity — as though the underground roaring replaces sunlight for them, fueling their growth or sense of time.

Little about their forms suggests the typical hallmarks of civilization: no clothes, no forged tools, no fire. Their bodies themselves appear to be their tools, with flexible joints capable of strange contortions, able to scale wet surfaces effortlessly. Where a human might slip or fumble, these beings ascend, gliding along pinched ledges or dipping beneath archways no taller than a small child. If you pause long enough, you might see them carefully shaping wet clay with slender fingers, pressing it between stones to seal a gap or to mend a fault in the labyrinth walls. You might even catch them smoothing the edges of a block, subtly chiseling with a lateral spur on their forearm that’s as sharp as a flint blade. It’s not loud or obtrusive; it’s patient and methodical, an ongoing maintenance that’s both softer and more persistent than any human masonry.

There is a timelessness to their society. They do not live in neat huts with lined-up furniture. Instead, the watchers occupy the entire sprawl of architecture as though it were one continuous organism. Sometimes, peering through a low arch, you find an alcove where a small cluster is resting, piled together in a tangle of limbs, as if mutual warmth or shared moisture is essential. You see them breathing in slow synchrony, each exhalation faintly visible as a wisp in the cooler air. To a casual glance, they might be lumps of slick stone, merging with the environment. Looking away for a moment, then back, you realize they’ve vanished, leaving the alcove as deserted as everything else here seems on first inspection.

Yet while they appear reclusive, the watchers are not entirely solitary. In rare moments, you hear a low, humming resonance. It emanates from somewhere deeper in the warren of dwellings, a tone that rises and falls like an underwater chorus. You can’t place the mechanism by which they create these sounds — no mouth movements or singing is visible — but you suspect it’s a collective resonance they produce by vibrating specialized membranes. In that echoic gloom, the watchers’ hum mingles with the city’s dripping water and the thunderous knocks of unseen forging or crashing. Together, these form an eerie lullaby of stone, water, and voice.

The watchers’ primary sustenance may lie in the interplay of mineral-rich condensation and those pale fungal fans. Possibly some slow, hidden process breaks down the rock itself, converting ancient elements into consumable matter. Their lean frames hint at a life of sparse nourishment, but they don’t seem to suffer for it. Instead, they appear lithe, agile, and well-adapted to the damp chill. Their skins glisten with a faint translucence that reveals tracings of branchlike veins beneath the surface — veins that carry not just blood but something luminous, an unearthly bioluminescence that glows faintly when no other light intrudes. It is this glow, you come to realize, that you sometimes mistake for the “flecks of light” on stone. They are the living lanterns of this underworld, though they rarely shine in more than the feeblest glimmers — just enough to sense each other, just enough to reflect softly on the wet stone.

A few hints suggest a long lineage of watchers — ancient ancestors who first raised these stacked dwellings. The way the watchers walk certain curved corridors, how they pause at what might be a time-worn inscription in the stone, or the gentle reverence they show for architectural details: all these point to a deep cultural memory that predates any visitors. Sometimes, a watcher’s fingertips hover over cryptic carvings as if reading their texture, gleaning meaning from grooves that you cannot interpret. You can only guess at what stories or histories might be inscribed there—perhaps sagas of subterranean floods, genealogies of watchers, or the cause of the unceasing pounding that scores their every day.

That incessant hammering is a key puzzle. Up close, you might sense jets of steam or warm gusts escaping from hidden fissures, suggesting geothermal activity. Or perhaps some unseen cascade is at work, a waterfall crashing in the far depths behind thick rock walls. The watchers treat the pounding with neither alarm nor curiosity; it simply is, as constant as a heartbeat. And all the while, their existence unfolds in symmetrical tandem with that chorus. They dwell in their stone labyrinth, quietly expanding it or reinforcing what was built in centuries past, as though they and the city were one living mechanism.

In the end, it seems the watchers embody the hush beneath the city’s noise: the better you listen, the more deeply you sense a unity between them and the place. The hammered echoes and watery drips define the city’s pulse, while the watchers are its silent breath. Together, they remain in a near-eternal stasis — a loop without a next phase, with no bright sunlit future nor any cataclysmic fall. Their world is cyclical. The watchers glide around corners and slip into alcoves. They reemerge to cradle mushrooms or stir a trickle of water, then drift into darkness again. The city murmurs with each drop of condensation, while the watchers’ faint luminescent glints come and go, as fleeting as will-o’-the-wisps.

They are the keepers and children of this unchanging realm. Neither wholly animal nor fully elemental, these lithe beings breathe the damp air in unison with a landscape that has shaped them as much as they have shaped it. Here, in the pocket of hollow earth, they will remain — guardians of a subterranean equilibrium, unseen yet ever-present, merging with the mineral hush and the steady thunder of time below the world’s crust.
