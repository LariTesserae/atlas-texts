At first, it seems no one and nothing resides here except the plants. The water is cold, pressing on every side; the ruins stand empty. Indeed, the place looks long abandoned by any society that once shaped these rough stones. Yet the hush is strangely alive—there’s a subtle sense that something stirs within this expanse of currents and swaying flora, as if the watery gloom itself harbors unseen tenants.

If you stay still among the drifting fronds, letting yourself be carried in the surge, you might begin to sense movement just beyond the silhouette of a ruined arch. Not fish or crustacean—there are none—but faint transitions of light and shadow. Often these shifts could be the turbulent water refracting the glow from silver-leafed plants; as soon as you look, they have vanished. Still, the sense of quiet vigilance persists. It’s almost as if something merges so perfectly with the water’s motion that you can only see it by the ripples it leaves behind.

Sometimes, in a fleeting pulse of warmth that rolls through the currents, you might feel a presence gliding nearer. It stirs the water in a pattern different from the rhythmic swells—like a careful navigation. If you reach out, your hand meets only the icy swirl, though you could swear there was a shape displacing the water a moment before. For a second, you might glimpse the faintest glow along sinuous outlines—shimmering contours that refract the ghostly light from those phosphorescent spores. Then it’s gone, lost among the tangled stems and ribbons of kelp. You might doubt your own senses, but over time, these fleeting encounters accumulate. Something is there.

Imagine a being that learned to become one with this drowned world, so fully intertwined with the water’s flow and temperature that it leaves no trace but soft eddies of motion. You might think of them as echoes of the ancient builders whose structures lie half-buried in silt. Or perhaps they are a separate race entirely—entities native to the water, never constrained by airy realms. They are not solid. They flicker at the edges of vision like living currents, intangible and fluid. Instead of flesh and bone, they are formed of the same swirling essence that shapes the cyclical tides. Whether they ever had a civilization or dwell in the ruins left behind by others is a puzzle. Yet you sense an intelligence in the way they circle near ruined huts, slipping through broken doorways, as though retracing paths etched in memory.

Occasionally, you hear a faint clinking or tapping—almost like echoing hammer strokes from an age long past. It could be a swirl of debris colliding against the stone blocks, or a resonance created by currents running through broken pillars. But there’s another possibility: maybe those are the tools of these unseen dwellers, still working in darkness, piecing together lost corners of a world changed by the unstoppable flood. One moment the sound is there—sharp, purposeful—and the next it is swallowed by the roar of surging water.

Then there are the plants: luminous leaves and ropey stems. Although they cannot speak, they might serve as the dwellers’ guardians or larders, or perhaps even their living messengers. You can imagine the dwellers tending them, coaxing out new fronds to brighten the blackness. Deep within the arches of vegetation, you might catch glimpses of softly quivering clusters—like breathing habitats, lit from inside by pulsing spores. Sometimes, these pockets of growth ripple in a very deliberate way, as though responding to signals neither visible nor audible—a hidden language of water pressure and shifting heat.

As you venture deeper and the watery gloom grows thick with tension, your conviction about these dwellers strengthens. It’s in the sudden hush between roaring currents, when an unseen shape drifts a little too close. It’s in the swirl of sediment that billows from a crevice, revealing faint lines of movement converging on one spot. At times, the water around you feels neither purely liquid nor purely air, but rather a membrane that something can slip through, as if passing between realms. The dwellers exist at that threshold—captivating and disconcerting.

They leave behind almost no direct trace of themselves. The lumps of metal hammered into stone, the half-collapsed huts, even the spiral carvings might have been from a different people entirely. Still, these fluid residents display an inexplicable caretaker’s presence. The carved stones—once tools for an older race—now serve as silent meeting points or boundaries in the dwellers’ territory. The hammered metal rings might be vestigial anchor points for them to cling to amid violent currents, or the remains of a simpler era they have since outgrown. 

There’s no single name for them. They reveal themselves slowly, on their own terms, forming from ripples of water that darken for just an instant before vanishing into the backdrop. Perhaps they feed on the faint warmth that pulses occasionally through these channels. Or they absorb energy from the phosphorescent spores. Or they require no nourishment at all, sustained by the sheer motion of currents. They might be a new evolutionary path, or living ghosts of the old ones—no one has learned enough to say. 

What is certain is how crucial they are to the pulse of this realm. In a place so seemingly void of animal life, they are the watchers and keepers of balance. Their presence orchestrates the silent interplay of roaring water, drifting rock fragments, and luminous flora. They guide the swirling tides that swirl in from hidden depths, ensuring that each broken column and each shimmering leaf continues its dance of creation and erosion. Through their quiet vigilance, the watery world endures, always shifting yet somehow stable.

And so, if you linger long and attune yourself to every subtle shift in the water’s currents, you may begin to feel the hush of their regard—a slow, steady awareness that never wavers. It is neither unwelcoming nor hostile, but ancient, massive, and patient. There, in that lofty submerged terrain, the dwellers exist as living currents made real, shaping and shaped by the watery universe they inhabit, guardians of a drowned world that still breathes in quiet, inexorable rhythms.
