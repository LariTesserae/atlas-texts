They do not appear all at once.

At first, you catch only hints that anyone—or anything—lives here at all. In the early morning, when the plateau is still half-shrouded by mist, brief flickers cross your periphery: a willowy shape slipping around a wet vine, a pair of luminous eyes glinting in some half-constructed tower, a tremor among the ferns. It can be difficult to tell if it was just the machine-lights dancing on droplets or an actual living presence. Sometimes you hear a clank in pipes overhead, like shifting footsteps that never quite arrive. Other times, you think you glimpse a blur of color or reflectivity: a shimmer of plumage, or a metallic glint from some synthetic limb.

Over time, you begin to piece together how these inhabitants fit into the environment. First come the smallest: insects with elongated, translucent wings and carapaces like droplets of liquid glass. They gather among the succulent-like growths to feed on sweet, sticky excretions beading at the base of each fleshy leaf. At daybreak, these insects flit into the open to bask in reflections from the metal scaffolds. The greenhouse-like effect of the plateau’s humidity envelops them, and they vibrate softly—resonating with the hum of turbines. You notice them especially when the drizzle stops, because they catch the newly brilliant sun on their wings, scattering prismatic arcs across the moss.

From the corners of your vision come creatures the size of a clenched fist, perched on the edges where vegetation and twisted conduit meet. Their hides shine with a slick luminescence, as if the moss has transferred its emerald sheen into their skin. Some have narrow, birdlike forms with tufted antennae shaped into curling horns. Others are reminiscent of rodents but elongated and graceful, bodies adapted to clamber up spindly pipelines or scuttle along the undersides of steam-wreathed catwalks. Their movements suggest an innate caution; they merge into background shadows when the heavy machinery kicks back into its rolling thunder.

In the deeper clusters of vegetation—where vines tangle into thick knots and water pools in hollows beneath the half-formed rock—there are ephemeral dwellings. You wouldn’t notice them unless you linger: arched tunnels woven from roots and bracket fungi, each cluster forming something between a nest and a labyrinth. This is where the plateau’s more communal species gather. They are lanky creatures with elongated limbs and wide, reflective eyes that blink sideways. Their deft fingers, tipped with tough, flexible nails, peel back moss to reach the underlying stone. Sometimes they are observed drawing patterns in the mud—almost like sketches or glyphs—only to wash them away moments later as if testing how the water might react.

Occasionally one of these beings emerges among the scaffolds and catwalks, stepping carefully to avoid rusted edges or protruding cables. They seem unafraid of the metal constructs, even comfortable perched atop swaying walkways as they watch the turbines spin. Some stand quietly for hours in places where steam bursts through valves, letting the warm vapor swirl around them. Others rummage among toolboxes left behind by unknown builders or drag bits of spare piping back into the undergrowth. You can sense a faint reciprocity between them and the forgotten machinery: the creatures remain respectful of the contraptions’ functions, avoiding active gearboxes or whirring belts, as if fully aware that these mechanisms keep the plateau’s watery environment in delicate balance.

Then there are the watchers who appear near the half-built towers—creatures that bear a faintly metallic aspect themselves. Their feathers or fur, if that’s what it is, glints with an alloy sheen, and their eyes reflect an inhuman intelligence. They squawk or chatter in short bursts, like compressed steam escaping a valve. In the fleeting glimpses you get, they seem to be observing everything: the growth of moss, the drifting scud of clouds, the slow erosion and expansion of the plateau. Some watchers come to rest on the older turbines, letting their talons click against the metal in a gentle staccato, as if they’re measuring the stalwart spin. Others disappear into small access hatches in the scaffolding. These hatches, you realize, lead into interior chambers carved out of the stone below, where the mechanics hum even deeper than the ear can easily track.

You might think that people—human or otherwise—would inhabit such a boldly industrial site, but direct evidence of them is rare. Sometimes you spot footprints in soft pockets of loam, too large or too deliberate for the agile root-dwellers. Tattered fabric occasionally drapes from scaffolding. Tools lie half-buried in moss, rusted yet still recognizable. There is a sense that a more methodical presence once called this place home, a group that attempted to harness the plateau’s raw, dripping resources. But if they remain, they’ve become elusive or unobtrusive. Perhaps they dwell in the largest towers where the steam is thickest, carrying out adjustments to the massive engines that churn unseen in the depths. You suspect they come out only under certain conditions—maybe when the seasons tilt just right, or when a valve must be replaced for the plateau’s equilibrium to hold.

Still, in the hush between the cyclical clamor of pumping water and grinding gears, you sense that the plateau teems with watchers of every scale—some small and bright, others tall and quiet, a handful mechanical, and some so entwined with the vines and stone you can’t label them one thing or another. Their roles intertwine like vines on rock: pollinators, scavengers, caretakers, perhaps guardians. Though they differ in physiology, all navigate this precarious realm with a measured pace, aware that each droplet, each length of steel cable, each tuft of moss has its necessity.

It is a place as much about transitions as it is about solitude. Creatures emerge or disappear at the edges of seeing. Machinery roars and quiets in an endless wave. The plateau is neither purely industrial nor purely wild, but in that union lies a life that is subtly profound. A broad winged creature might coast overhead—caught briefly in sunlight—combining the mechanical silhouette of a glider with the unmistakable curvature of living bone. Its cry, if it voices any, is quickly drowned out by the turbines’ churn.

The dwellers of the plateau persist in the slow dance of formation and decay. They cling to the new stone as it rises, test out the half-assembled scaffolds, and slip among the webs of vines bridging them. Unhurried and methodical, they wear an alert patience that mirrors the plateau’s own will to grow, to shift, to turn rain into rivulets and steam into motion. Whether born of moss, metal, or some hybrid of the two, they belong to a realm still deciding its fate, each occupant another subtle current in the grand circulatory system of water, vapor, life, and industry that defines this high and mist-laden world.
