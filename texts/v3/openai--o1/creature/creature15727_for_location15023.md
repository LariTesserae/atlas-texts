They call themselves many names—or at least, we might guess so, if one could parse the cautious tapestries of noise they emit when they gather. More often they are quiet, turning their soft eyes to the ground or beyond the horizon as if listening for cues at the edges of perception. In the hush of this plateau, every rustle of scaled foot or flick of a leathery wing can be a signal. You can glean certain constants by watching them: they move slowly in the open heat but spring into swift, silent motion whenever shadow and moisture converge. They congregate near the half-inactive machinery that still exhales occasional vibrations, as though seeking vestigial energies or the protection of steel canopies.

Set apart from the smaller animals that scurry and descend, these bipedal beings—spindly-limbed and stooped, their skin patterned with faint, shimmering stripes—have built low-slung dwellings in the lee of the older industrial edifices. You might mistake them for a long-deserted workforce, but they are no mere squatters. Indeed, they appear to share some direct lineage with the settlement’s original engineers—or perhaps they arrived at some point afterward, drawn by the trickle of water from the vents and the faint pulses of the old storied machinery.

There is an air of ritual in the way they move through corridors of shuddering catwalks. They place their hands—long-fingered, each digit ridged for grip—against the rusted columns and press softly; it may be temperature they read, or the hum of fluid lines hidden within. They never gather in large groups. Instead, small clusters traverse the plateau at separate intervals, as if each band follows its own cycle determined by the angle of sun or the level of condensation on the stone.

Their faces are shielded by sweeping bone-like ridges that arch to either side of their elongated skulls, shading their large, pale eyes. Their pupils grow wide in the midday glare, absorbing the punishing light, but at dusk, their eyes shrink to mere pinpricks that reflect in the gloom, glowing faintly with the same phosphorescent hue you glimpse in the other creatures of this land. Like the fungus that glistens on damp stones, their skin has a subtle, opalescent sheen—just enough to suggest that the environment has shaped them, or that they have shaped themselves, to mirror the watery crescents clinging to the rocks.

When they speak—if that soft, aspirated whisper is truly speech—they do so near the glimmering vents. They seem to coordinate a daily vigil around these round portals in the rock. One can see them laying broad, layered strips cut from the translucid fungi near the runic markings. Perhaps they are offerings or nutritional rations. Perhaps it’s a means of calibrating the vent openings, ensuring the subterranean channels flow as they must. In the blasts of steam, these bipeds stand resolute, unflinching, with hand and cheek pressed close to plumes of scalding vapors. Their skin flushes dark but doesn’t blister—some adaptation, some long-ingrained tolerance built upon centuries or millennia of living so close to the plateau’s inner heat.

They hold a certain synergy with the smaller quadrupeds that skitter across the water-slick ground. Those squat creatures—scaled in a pattern reminiscent of a shield—scavenge scraps of dropped fungus or lap at the trickles that form whenever the bipeds open or redirect a hidden sluice gate. Observing them, you’ll see a curious collaboration: the bipeds stir a lurking microbial soup from beneath the settlement’s surfaces, leading to brief, intentional spills of mineral-rich water. Immediately, the quadrupeds swarm in, feeding on the detritus and gleaning the crusted minerals that might otherwise choke the flow. In turn, these small beasts’ presence seems welcomed—like living scrubbing-brushes, they keep the channels tidy, allowing the deeper water to continue its slow seepage and not stagnate. Once they’ve had their fill, they retreat into unseen burrows or slip back under metal beams that clang underfoot in the thinning light.

By contrast, the winged scavengers—a more restless lot—linger high on the catwalk railings, folded wings shining with moisture, waiting for a moment of opportunity. Their flexible jaws snap up the most delicate, filmy fungal growths that appear overnight in hidden recesses. Yet they also delight in siphoning off the thin runoff that cascades from pipe outflows. Knobbly heads that dart in and out of shadow, flickering tongues slurping from rivulets: it’s a ceaseless chase, punctuated by tiny squeaks and rasping chirrups. In the gloaming hours, their phosphorescent patterns shimmer around stunted, curved horns or crest-like flaps on their heads, helping them find each other in the web of pipes overhead. It’s not uncommon to see them perched in clusters beneath walkways, dozing fitfully in the heat and humidity, glowing faintly like a cluster of aquatic insects swirling in a tidal pool back on more temperate planets.

The large predators, though, are rarely seen up close. A fleeting shape—a ridgeline of spines or a hint of elongated jaws—breaks the horizon now and then, usually near one of the battered old loading ramps or the low-slung huts where the bipeds store metal drums. Some say these brutes were drawn here long ago by a thirst that only the plateau could quench. Their eyes shine a dull gold, set deep in cup-like sockets that taper to bony crests—structures that may help cool the blood running through them. They wait in absolute silence, akin to living statues, until one among the smaller fauna dares a sip at a communal runoff pool. Then they strike with coiled swiftness, jaws snapping shut with a crack that echoes across the drenched stone. The bipeds do not hunt these predators. Instead, they watch them from a distance, as if resigned to their role in nature’s precarious balancing act.

Indeed, these lean, stoop-shouldered bipeds seem to favor caretaker tasks over anything else. When storms roll in—rare, teasing, ephemeral—they hurry to brace makeshift barriers against certain gullies, channeling floodwaters into shallow cisterns or turning them away from what remains of active workshops. At the hum of distant thunder, you can see them scurry along overhead walkways with large, perforated sheets of corrugated metal—part canopy, part funnel—to gather precious droplets that slip easily away on the polished steel. It is a choreography of necessity, ensuring that when the next blistering day dawns, they will have at least a minimal store of power or water or perhaps some intangible resource gleaned from the convective hiss of moisture-laden air.

Curiously, the old industrial core—vast cylindrical vats, silent conveyor belts, and lines of overhead cranes—still hums with the faint suggestion of capacity. Something stirs there, perhaps in deeper chambers, where complex ductworks gurgle and bubble as if potent chemicals or liquefied minerals still run. This might be the realm where the bipeds gather at intervals too infrequent to track, sending out a heavy, syncopated drumming that resonates through the columns of steel—like a signal or a summons. Yet if they mine anything from these storehouses, their methods remain hidden. Now and then, a sizzling glow behind a half-closed gate hints that arc-lights or energy beams are being tested. The bipeds pass calmly by, their focus unwavering, as though the old machines are as much a part of life here as the shifting clouds overhead.

And always, that luminous fungus: flexible sheets, pallid, half-translucent and softly gleaming. Over time, you notice deposits of this fungal growth in strategic locations—places where metal struts meet rock or where water channels conjoin. The bipeds sometimes scrape away a film-thin layer, rolling it carefully into tubes or patching it onto corroded machine housings. The fungus fuses with the steel when appropriately treated, forming watertight seams. In the stifling humidity, the fusion sets quickly and glimmers like a newly minted coin. Walk those catwalks long enough, and you see these patches everywhere—like a tapestry of subtle repairs, weaving the settlement’s skeleton into something half-living, half-metal, bridging eras of function.

In moments of rest, you might witness the bipeds simply standing in the sun’s glare, heads bowed, arms slack. Steam rises from their shoulders as droplets of condensation escape in tiny plumes. It could be a form of meditation or a moment of communion with the plateau. Their luminous stripes flicker in small pulses—perhaps an unconscious reaction, or a conversation too subtle for an outsider to decode. Meanwhile, the tight-lipped hush remains. No raucous calls, no celebratory bursts. This place fosters a quiet harmony, dependent on the unpredictable moisture exhaled by ancient vents and on the cyclical hush of machines once designed for an unknown purpose.

All around them swirl the lesser beasts: the scaled quadrupeds, skittering in lines; the winged gleaners, blinking their luminescent signals; the lurking predators with glimmering eyes. Each breath of steam from the subterranean labyrinth fosters new waves of fungus and new opportunities for life to flourish. And at the center of it all, guiding or simply enduring, these tall, watchers-of-machinery remain—hands pressed to rusted surfaces, eyes half-closed as they shepherd this land of shimmering haze and scalding brilliance.

In the end, one is left with a sense of anticipation—not dread, nor easy comfort, but an almost reverential pause. The plateau’s guardians, or tenants, or heirs—whatever they truly are—are not idle. Their movements betray a purpose braided into every rivet of the settlement and every seep of mineral-laden water. They look upon the scorched horizon, mindful of each wing-beat, every shift in the fungus, each drip of condensation carving new gullies through the rock. They wait for the next cycle, be it a slow renaissance of industry or a final submission to the creeping, glowing film that might one day envelope it all. Here, in the hush beneath the blazing sun, life continues on its own terms, alien yet unapologetically alive, forging equilibrium in the shimmer and steam.
