You might first think nothing lives here at all—only storm and stone and water trapped in a ceaseless upheaval. But if you’re patient, small hints begin to surface. Nearly imperceptible at first, movements flicker in the swirling haze, colors or outlines that dance at the corner of your vision. Often, just as you turn your head to focus, they’re gone. Look directly, and you’re staring at empty, roiling air. Yet every so often, in the fractured intervals when the plateau trembles or the steam recedes, these beings reveal themselves in subtle, half-formed ways.

They are creatures of shifting boundaries, just like the plateau. Some seem amphibious—elongated torsos and limbs striped with watery luminescence, as though formed from the same eddies that spin upright at the plateau’s edge. But their eyes are not the dark, glassy orbs you’d expect in an aquatic creature. Instead, they glow or pulse irregularly, like small embers submerged in haze. Others have translucent bodies studded with spines or filaments that ripple independently, each filament trailing off in fractal patterns before vanishing into the mist. It’s unclear where flesh ends and vapor begins.

Most of these creatures—and “creature” might be too solid a term—appear part elemental, part living entity. Their forms distort the same way distance distorts here: they might be small, flitting shapes in one moment, then loom as towering silhouettes in the next, spines bristling like the ridges of a reef. Sometimes they scuttle halfway across the plateau, leaving only the faintest bright track in their wake, a line of shimmering droplets that fades just as quickly as it’s made.

Near those half-finished altars, you occasionally glimpse beings that move with more deliberate purpose. Where the smaller, scuttling shapes dart with caution, these stand in tall, almost regal silence. Their presence seems tied to the stone slabs—perhaps they’re wardens, or survivors of an older rite. They possess elongated limbs and wear swirling patterns that mirror the fractal designs on the altars. Their histrionic silhouettes suggest ceremonial grace, as if each motion is part of an ancient religious dance performed in tandem with the booming, endless roar. When they shift their posture, it’s with an unsettling fluidity, as though they can flow into new shapes instead of needing to bend.

In the shifting gloom, you catch hints that these entities might communicate with each other—or perhaps even with the very shrines they roam among. At times, a slab will glimmer with fractal light, and a figure will place one spindly hand upon it, generating a shared pulse that resonates through the plateau’s stone. You feel the vibration in your own bones, a silent chord layered beneath the ear-splitting crashes of water and wind. Then, just as quickly, the connection breaks, and they vanish into the haze, leaving the altar’s patterns rippling with afterimages.

Beyond these robed-like watchers, there are things flitting in columns of vertical water—serpentine shapes, their segmented flanks lined with glowing membranes that shimmer in unison, like synchronized pulses of fluorescence. They slip between water and vapor, occasionally piercing through the boundary to snatch something from the plateau surface—perhaps droplets of nutrient-laden water, perhaps bits of the spindly reeds that cling to the rocks. Mostly you see only a length of pearlescent tail whipping out from the upright waves. Sometimes you hear a wet whistle or trill that seems to reverberate inside the watery columns.

And then there are the ephemeral hordes—small clusters of fractal-limbed forms that resemble insectoid creatures, each limb jointed in improbable angles. They move scorpion-like across the stone but dissolve into hovering droplets the instant the thunder grows too intense. It’s as though they can only hold themselves together in the moments of calm between the plateau’s trembling fits. If you stare long enough, you can watch them fade from matter into mist, leaving a faint impression of their geometry behind, like the afterglow of lightning on your retinas.

One must also speak of the watchers who hide in plain sight—those bulging eyes glimpsed between slabs of rock. Some have a luminous, fishlike quality, each pupil ringed with swirling halos of color. Others seem more reminiscent of cautious amphibians or sea mammals, glimpsed briefly as they peer out from subterranean fissures near the plateau’s base. When the plateau shifts unexpectedly—heaving upward or tilting in some minor quake—they slither or scrabble away like phantoms, slipping through cracks that you could have sworn weren’t there a moment before. They might be ancient dwellers or freshly spawned illusions, and the difference doesn’t feel terribly distinct in this place.

Occasionally, you’ll sense that you are being watched not by any single pair of eyes, but by the place as a whole. Flickers of lightning cast shadows on the half-sunken shrines, and those shadows sometimes move on their own. Their shapes don’t match the angles of anything present, as though they’re shadows of entities that exist outside the visible realm. They stretch across the slick rock, expand, contract, and then vanish when the next wave of mist rolls through. You wonder if they are some older, more primal inhabitants—ghosts or guardians that predate even the stone altars.

No one here seems fixed to a single identity or category. Amid the fog of shifting times and landscapes, life itself wears fluid boundaries. Some may be entirely aquatic, living in the swirling oceanic columns, venturing onto the rock only when the water stands upright enough to meet them. Others might have begun as land-based but have adapted, their skin turned translucent under the perpetual assault of steam and chaotic energies. The lines between species, even between living beings and the environment, blur.

If they have any social structure, it’s as transient as everything else—likely dictated by the roar of the plateau’s clash with water, by the frequency of lightning arcs, by the sudden migrations of swirling tides. Maybe these are solitary wanderers, each forging its own path through the ephemeral realm. Perhaps in the calmer intervals—whenever those might come—they gather to shape or tend the stone slabs, leaving cryptic fractal inscriptions glowing with leftover energies. Or, when the plateau descends below the waves, they reorganize in some deeper region, continuing their existence in a more stable domain that you will never see.

Some who dwell here seem half aware—like living illusions that flicker in time with the roars and pulses. It’s possible that these shapes and watchers are not fully physical but manifestations of the plateau’s birth-throes. They could be splinters of the land’s own consciousness, testing out forms in the newly emergent world. When the ground shudders, these ephemeral creatures might simply be the plateau reaching for a shape to call its own, a fleeting experiment in being alive.

At the core of it all is a sense that each inhabitant, from the serpentine flickers to the tall, robed guardians, belongs to the same swirling mystery—part wave, part stone, part echo of some ancient force. They flow where the environment is hospitable—sliding between folds of vapor, lurking behind newly splintered rocks, or traversing watery corridors that form, vanish, and reform in the blink of an eye. Their survival seems dependent on attuning themselves to the plateau’s restless energy, feeding off the resonance that pervades the air.

Finally, whatever does live here does so in a perpetual dance with instability. Every tremor could reshape the ground, every shift in the watery columns could snuff them out or transform them. Nothing is certain—neither the shape of the land nor the shape of those who inhabit it. Time and motion themselves are fluid, blending birth and destruction in every thunderous roar. For these living presences—call them creatures, call them ghosts, call them living storms—it is all the same. They exist in the cusp between ocean and sky, in a place that has not decided if it wants to remain. And in that liminal chaos, they find their moment to be.
