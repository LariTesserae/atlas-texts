They keep themselves out of sight. Sometimes you notice them only by the faintest shift in the water or a silhouette in the corner of a porthole-like window. Then, if you pause and look from the right angle, you see them retreating deeper into shadowed corridors or behind half-rusted machinery.

Much of the time, the locals could be mistaken for the place itself: half-buried in the metal architecture or blended against the sediment-streaked walls. When you glimpse them up close, you might see that they share many of the city’s traits — segments of plating that mirror the pitted iron catwalks, mottled skins or shells that mimic the fractal patterns of corrosion. It’s as if everything beneath the surface here must adapt to this environment of whirring pistons, scorched piping, and briny currents. No organism can stay purely organic for long. The watery darkness and mechanical wilderness demand modifications, or camouflage, or both. 

But there is no single type of inhabitant. It’s more of a continuum. On one end, you might find formidably mechanical beings — squat drones with thick carapaces, built to weld and rivet, repairing leaks or reinforcing structural failures. They drift with a purposeful slowness, thrusters or fins humming out small bubbles as they navigate from corridor to corridor. You might see metal arms hinged at ball joints, serrated “hands” for cutting away rusted detritus, lamp-like heads that swivel to cast bright cones of light through the dusty gloom. These units are so ubiquitous you forget to think of them as living, yet the more you watch, the more you sense an intelligence behind the swiveling cameras or sensors. They float in groups of two or three, exchanging coded signals you can barely perceive — silent bursts of fizzing digital chatter that swirl outward into the watery murk.

Huddled in recessed alcoves, you might then see what look like hybrids of flesh and machinery, shaped vaguely human or crustacean or fishlike. They have limbs or appendages that seem to be flesh, or leathery hide, or bioluminescent mucous… but fitted at certain joints are mechanical couplings or flexible metal bracings. Perhaps these are the city’s technicians, who have over centuries adapted bodies that thrive in thick, near-boiling water under punishing pressure. Their eyes, if eyes they still have, are large and milky with a ghostly sheen; some blink with a metallic pivot. Where they dwell, you often see small shrines of scrounged gear parts, or curated fragments of steel they treat with reverence — as though worshipping the city’s mechanical heart. The boundary between their biology and the city’s metalwork has blurred so far that it’s hard to see where creature ends and environment begins.

Then, too, there are the specters. Some claim they’re illusions cast by fluid distortion in the half-light, but you’ll swear you saw an eel-like silhouette drift by. Or something that looked like a coiled serpent of cable and twisted biomass wriggling through a fractured pipe. Or a scuttling shape with far too many jointed legs, each tipped in a clamp that allowed it to cling to slick surfaces. Whether these creatures are survivors of a more natural oceanic lineage or the results of centuries of bio-industrial leaks and experiments gone astray is unknowable. Their presence is fleeting at best: you see them sliding between structural beams, navigating narrow ventilation ducts, or vanishing down blackened mineshafts that lead to the city’s deeper compartments.

It’s not just the big forms you notice. Between every gear, in every seam of corroded metal, you find entire micro-worlds of smaller life: spore-like growths and oily flecks that might be living nano-organisms or spore clusters thriving in watery gloom. Small mechanical parasites scuttle across pipe joints, looking for exposed wire or an unsealed seam to feed upon. A rust-fueled mold spreads in patches, maintained or cultivated by…something. Every surface bristles with faint signs of hungry, opportunistic micro-mechanisms that feed on heat or certain metallic compounds. In the same way reefs support countless hidden creatures, so does every inch of this industrial labyrinth host a cosmos of specialized inhabitants.

Where the city’s main chambers open wide, you sometimes detect — behind hazy glass or half-lowered bulkheads — the outline of larger figures. They might stand upright, wearing tattered garments sealed at the joints or heavily plated suits to endure superheated water. Possibly they’re the overseers, quietly managing the unstoppable turbines. They pace in methodical circuits or hunch over indecipherable consoles riddled with glowing gauges. You can’t tell if they notice you or if they’re too embedded in centuries-old protocols. Some watchers claim these presumed dwellers are humans who long ago adapted to a watery fortress of their own making; others suspect they’re as much machine as any drone, with only a handful of living cells nestled in mechanical shells. Their faces, rarely glimpsed, appear stiff with salt deposits or congealed lubricants. If you see them move, there’s a faint grinding noise, as though each joint in their bodies must be coaxed into motion.

Perhaps stranger still are the watchers in the vents — the presences that track your movements from the labyrinth of overhead conduits. You only sense them in the corner of your vision: watchers pressed to grated screens, observing. Their sightlines follow you, but as soon as you turn, they slide backward into darkness. Briefly, you might see the glimmer of reflective eyes, a flash of bristling hardware, or a serpentine tail snaking out. There’s tension in their regard, not quite hostile, not quite curious, but always measuring. Occasionally, a battered speaker or control console crackles with a reedy voice that might be a leftover recording or might be them, speaking gently through the city’s skeletal intercom system. Then, the static ceases, and you hear only the pervasive roar again.

All these inhabitants, from the humblest gear-cleaner drones to the shadowy watchers, exist in a tenuous balance. They are intimately tied to the sink-and-surge pulse of water, the city’s vibrations, the ceaseless rotation of turbines. Many of them perform maintenance because to fail is to let the sea crush them all. The columns must be reinforced, corroded pipes replaced, steam ducts kept from boiling over. Everyone (or everything) has a role: patrolling, sealing cracks, venting pressure, or scavenging leftover mineral deposits that could impede the flow of cooling waters. It’s not a kindly or benevolent system; more like a necessity. A choreography of survival. Firefights of steam and pressure must be resolved quickly or else the entire place collapses under nature’s unstoppable weight.

There’s also an unspoken hierarchy. The largest or oldest mechanical constructs — towering “sentinels” in certain vault-like areas — give off the impression of being asleep or in some standby mode. Yet you see cables snaking to them, bringing power, or carrying signals. At times, you hear a sub-bass groan from these arcane machines. Then smaller drones swarm around them, reattaching wiring or scraping off layers of scale. These old sentinels could be the original guardians of this undersea forge, so powerful that they need only stir occasionally to remind everything else of their dominance. The watery gloom trembles when they do.

And there are those who wander alone: whether outcasts or explorers or something else. They roam the passages, seeming neither fully drone nor flesh, always scanning or searching for something rumored to lie deeper in the city’s labyrinth. Faint stories whisper about a hidden core, a colossal mind or engine, that keeps the entire system running. Local legends say it beat like a living heart once upon a time; others claim it’s just a nuclear furnace or geothermal vent fueling the city’s power. Nobody can confirm one way or the other. But the wanderers keep searching, sure there’s a massive, locked chamber of swirling valves and molten light that still hums with primeval energy. When they pass you in a half-lit corridor, you might hear them murmur about “the Engine” or “the Heart,” their voices a watery rasp, but they’ll never stop long enough to share what they truly know.

So the city is not silent after all, and not empty. Here and there, layered among the engines’ roar, is the quiet commerce of life. These inhabitants move with slow, deliberate purpose, often fading into the swirl of machinery and gloom as if merging with the environment itself. There is no farmland, no schools, no bright public squares. Instead, everything is workshop, corridor, or control station, and every living thing tries to persist in this churning environment. 

To onlookers, it may seem an existence of endless maintenance and caution — a precarious dance to keep the water out, the heat under control, the rust from eating everything away. But those who belong here do so deeply, with no illusions of a simpler life. They have become as rhythmic and ceaseless as the turbines themselves. This is their city, these are its rhythms, and they have bound themselves to the place like calcified barnacles to iron. Each inhabitant — from the whiskered eel-bot weaving through ducts to the stoic hybrids patrolling catwalks — is an integral component in a living, breathing mechanism of metal, water, and unstoppable momentum.

And if you let yourself float in the thrumming half-dark, eventually you, too, feel the city’s pulse passing through you: the sense that the entire undersea labyrinth breathes and that all who dwell within its walls are merely its many organs. From the smallest gear-scraper to the unseen watchers in the vents, they all move in concert, each one a piece of the city’s strange new fauna. A population of living machines and machine-like lives, forging meaning in the warm, dim, unending noise.
