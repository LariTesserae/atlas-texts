They make their homes in the slick crevices and metal-wreathed corridors as a matter of course, hardly ever questioning why this place should be so damp or so labyrinthine. To an outsider, the population clusters at first appear like a hundred disparate tribes layered upon one another—some with metal-tipped limbs or shells like living armor, others with stooped backs or elongated, jointed fingers. Still others show no exotic differences at a glance, save for a faint sheen across their skin, a subtle elongation of their pupils, or a minute humming in their breath when they speak. 

But beneath these surface details, they share a common rhythm: a quiet, instinctive devotion to the living city, as though each pounding engine inside the scaffolds is matched by a heartbeat in their chests. They come in every size and shape imaginable, and they dwell in sub-communities shaped by function or by the local environment, all abiding together in the necessary labor of this place.

If you wander near the open channels of water, you may see the small amphibian folk known collectively—by those who speak the humanish dialect—as the Drowzers. They scuttle on four limbs (though one seldom notices the front pair, which are often clasped together, carrying scavenged fungus or scraps). Drowzers have bulging, dark eyes and velveteen skin mottled with damp lichen. They cleanse the channels of debris, clearing blockages so water flows, in turn securing their livelihood from the gleanings that wash down. They dwell in tight-knit family groups, crouched in the shallow runnels and low, dripping arches, their croaking speech echoing at odd hours.

Meanwhile, overshadowing the Drowzers are the exoskeletal harvester-guilds, nicknamed “Crusts” for their overlapping chitin plates. Each clan is rumored to descend from ancient insectile lines that burrowed into the basalt eons back. The Crusts have never been known to wear conventional clothing—not that they need it. Their carapaces fluctuate in color from ashen black to tawny rust, streaked with scars from ongoing squabbles over territory and resource rights. Some can climb sheer walls, hooking their spined limbs into the city’s metal lattice to reach the best vantage points for gleaning fungal harvests. They use precise mandibles to pare away fungal blooms from the undersides of pipes, collecting them in woven pouches. At dusk, you might see them retreating into hive-like enclaves—side-chambers that lead deeper into the plateau’s stone—their luminous compound eyes flickering in the twilight.

Higher still—and more seldom witnessed up close—are the majestic winged beasts that spiral among the catwalks and towers. They have broad leathery spans, gracefully bridging territory after territory as they glide on hidden air currents. Call them drakes, call them gliders, call them scythe-wings: many names sit upon them, since each clan of city-dwellers sees them differently. Some claim they are near-sapient, capable of remembering individuals who have fed or harmed them. Others think they are simply oversized scavengers, with no sense of loyalty or comprehension. By day, they can be glimpsed perched high on basalt outcroppings or metal stanchions, their tufted silhouettes still as statues, the ridges of their spines glinting with condensed mist. Come sunrise, they uncoil with resonant shrieks, launching into slow, patient loops, always watching for an easy meal or a strategic perch.

As for the half-human workers, they show the widest variety in appearance and daily routine. Their ancestors, it is said, were once purely human—traders who came for minerals or pilgrims who sought refuge on the plateau. Now, countless generations on, these city-dwellers have undergone slow, subtle transformations: webbed fingers for climbing slick basalt, or a second, nictitating membrane in the eyes against constant condensation. A few sport branching horns or ridged shoulders that look suspiciously akin to the exoskeletons worn by the Crusts—the byproduct, no doubt, of some deep genetic reciprocity or the city’s unceasing influence. Stoic and methodical in their tasks, these folk tend to the massive rivets that keep steel pipes anchored, service the rust-slicked mechanical hearts that power the foundries, and watch over the half-submerged hatcheries for the more fragile species. They speak in hushed, rolling tones, their language peppered with mechanical jargon that an outsider might find as alien as any insectile chitter.

Out on the peripheries of the terraces, among the half-collapsed towers and questionable scaffolds, live specialists who repair ancient designs. Some are watchers, perched day after day along precarious platforms, their keen vision scanning the swirling fog for signs of structural failure in the basalt. Others are tinkers who carry small mechanical tools at all hours, perpetually adjusting the city’s clockwork rhythms—tapping on valves, tightening bolts, and sealing freshly discovered leaks. Often, those watchers and tinkers work with small domesticated creatures: waddling, short-skulled beasts—call them “scuts”—that serve as living pack carriers for everything from metal struts to gleaned engine parts. Their stubby tails flick in excitement whenever they sense a new spill or a fresh sputtering in the pipes.

Far below ground, in shadowy caverns made by water’s timeworn passage, dwell entire hidden communities. Among them are the Refractors: luminous amphibious beings that convert certain algae and fungi into a bioluminescent glow to light their watery homes. The Refractors emerge after nightfall to trade with the upper city—bundles of phosphorescent spores for scraps of metal or mechanical parts. For them, the city overhead is a place of clamor they can scarcely imagine, so they maintain a polite distance, taking advantage of the city’s labyrinthine channels to remain discreet. If you traverse subterranean tunnels at dusk, you might spy a wavering green or violet light ahead: a Refractor returning home from a secret exchange.

Throughout this place, every species feeds off each other’s task in subtle synergy: the scraps from manufacturing feed the fungus of the lower levels; the fungus feeds the exoskeletal harvesters; their gleanings feed the pipe-maintainers and watchers, who in turn maintain the city’s great machines, ensuring the plateau remains a functioning environment for all. It is a puzzle of interdependent roles fitting, misfitting, and refitting over the generations. 

Competing alliances form, too—a friction that never quite escalates into open warfare but simmers in daily negotiations. Perhaps a winged drake feasts on the scuts needed by pipe-workers, or a troop of Crusts expands a fungal grove that the Drowzers rely on for breeding. These tensions keep each group razor-focused on preserving their niche in the city’s grand, churning clockwork. Over time, the cycle remains stable, and the city—part basalt, part roar of steam—continues in a slow, breathing loop.

Despite their differences, all denizens share a certain hush in their souls—an acceptance that the city endures, that it has seen centuries pass like drifting clouds. From the oldest watchers to the youngest broodlings, they learn from each new flood of rivulets, each break in the pipes, each thickening patch of fungus. Here, existence is relational: no one, no thing, can truly survive in isolation. The scattered calls of winged fliers remind them all that survival is communal, that the steaming valves pumping in the foundries are meaningless if the rivulets clog, or if the fungal blooms fail, or if the watchers miss the creeping cracks in the basalt.

And so they go about it—carving small pockets of home in the basalt’s terraces, building out metal huts near the towers’ bases, installing rickety footbridges over watery gaps, urging each generation to adapt to living with water trickling endlessly underfoot. The city, for all its rust and soot, is their cradle. The place shapes their bones, their customs, their entire sense of purpose. A faint, echoing hum always underscores their lives, that layered resonance of metal straining against watery stone, sealing them in an ancient, living loop. 

Thus they thrive in quiet exertion and well-worn cyclical tasks: patching a broken coupler on a steam conduit, guiding newly hatched amphibian-larvae to safe pools, ensuring the spindly exoskeleton-wearers do not devour crucial lichen to the point of collapse. They are mechanical custodians and biological caretakers both—a tapestry of shapes and forms, each bound in patient unity, warmed by the basalt’s hidden heat and enveloped by the constant whispers of condensation. Here, in what might appear at first to be a bleak and colorless domain, the true vibrancy resides within the living tangle of creatures—a chorus of unnumbered voices, each still forging a life in this timeless, dripping city.
