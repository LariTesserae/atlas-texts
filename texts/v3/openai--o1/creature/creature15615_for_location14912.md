They live here in the hush. At first glance, one would say it’s only the plants—a variety of ferns and mosses and stringy lichens clinging to the rock. And in simple terms, that is true: no creature readily shows itself, not even the small scuttle of an insect’s legs across the damp stone. The place presents itself as a plant realm through and through, sealed off from the typical bustle of animal life. Yet as you linger, allowing the silence to loosen your mind, impressions begin to collect—impressions that the life here is more than merely green things rooted in wetness.

Look carefully at the ferns, tall but gentle, each frond subtly different in its pale veins. The droplets that cling to them tremble with a faint pulse, not merely from your presence but from something deeper, some low, rhythmic motion in the very water itself. Mosses spread in unhurried waves across the stone, forming irregular patches like small continents. Their colonies glow electric green, as if each filament of moss is storing a quiet radiance in its cells, gleaning it from the subdued sunlight and the ever-present moisture. And from the edges of these mossy islands, lichen strands hang with watchful stillness. They sway, but barely, as if aware of any shift in the heavy, humid air.

Seen from a certain angle, this arrangement—a group of ferns here, a patch of moss there, a fringe of lichen overhead—almost forms patterns. The shapes appear, for a moment, like silhouettes, as though the greenery itself has arranged into a silent pantheon. One might fancy that these plants are standing sentinel, each occupying its own patch of rock with patient intention. In a place empty of overt animal life, the plants take on the role of watchers. There is no chatter or rustle beyond their breathing in the damp air and releasing it again. But that hush begins to speak volumes about how alive they truly are. If you stand very still, your breath hushed, you almost sense the faint synergy between them: water shared through rock crevices, root-tips meeting under the stone, delicate signals passing in the slow pulse of growth.

Indeed, if you kneel beside the trickles that course down the walls, you’ll see how the mosses appear to gather at precisely the spots where water collects most reliably, as though they had a collaborative pact with the drips. Each tuft expands in a gentle, sub-aquatic gesture, soaking up the droplets. And then that same moisture slides on, continuing to feed the next cluster of green. These plants live slowly yet purposefully; they thrive in the overshadowed, water-ruled hush. Century by century, the colonization creeps outward, reaching new stones once a layer of smooth patina has formed.

But is that all that lives here? Perhaps. It seems so—no pawprints, no half-chewed leaves, no webs strung between branches. And yet, in the softness of the air, in the rhythmic dripping that resonates almost like a heartbeat, there's a suggestion of something else. You notice shallow grooves etched into a boulder near the center of the bowl. Viewed straight on, they could be random abrasions. But from an oblique angle, they resolve into a pattern, an odd geometry that might imply design. Here and there, the moss has begun to fill those channels, reclaiming them into organic shapes. You can almost see vestiges of purposeful marks—scrapes that might once have been letters, or repeated notches that might once have been some system of counting.

So if there is another presence in this place, it lingers in the realm of memory or spirit. One could imagine a long-ago inhabitant—something older than recorded time—working quietly with the stones, shaping them in small ways. Perhaps it was an early person, or a creature that humankind never saw, leaving behind tracks that are neither footprints nor claw marks but careful, deliberate lines in the rock. The place does not reveal whether they remain, watching unseen, or if they vanished completely. The vegetation has taken up the duty of keeping watch.

Sometimes, in the corner of your vision, you might sense a ripple at the rim of one of the shallow pools. Was that an insect’s wing disturbing the water’s surface? A trick of the light? A bubble from an unseen fissure in the rock below? You look, and see nothing new. Even the puddles here seem to keep secrets. But the feeling lingers: not a feeling of emptiness, but instead of presence—an unshowy, deeply patient presence that resonates like an echo in your chest.

As you roam farther, you discover pockets of thick, spongy moss that carry a subtle, glowing brightness. Kneel down, run your fingers across it, and feel the water welling up between the luminous threads. It’s as if the moss itself is collecting quiet stories from the stone: eons of rainfall, the memory of ancient tools tapping softly in the dawn of a lost civilization, and the slow garment of lichen that drapes over everything in a gentle hush. The lichen, too, seems to share a hidden dialogue with the place, each tendril measuring time in centuries. Observe their color: a pale, ghostly green, stippled with specks of silver—a shade that belongs to hidden corners and seldom-trodden grottos.

Look upward; there at the top of the encircling walls, close to where the sky peeks in, are faint traces of old waterlines where heavier rains once poured. Even those lines bear a sort of signature, a broader swath of color in the rock, or a slight wave in the mineral residue. You’d swear it looks like the remains of a handprint or a shape pressed against the stone, though the rational part of your mind insists it must be mere geology. Still, the notion persists that something—someone—left a record in these wet-etched surfaces, and that the plants and water carry its legacy to this day.

So who lives here now? No single creature steps forward to claim dominion, no chorus of frogs or call of birds. The dwellers are a tapestry of green and gray, of slick stone and slow drips, of intangible memories that weave themselves into every new thread of moss. They are the guardians of an unspoken pact, the keepers of water and rock, the silent things that do not need to speak or roam. To the casual eye, it is an empty place—just plants in a high, damp hollow. But as you linger, it becomes plain that they inhabit this refuge with a quiet, collective awareness. They sustain an ancient continuity, bridging present and past, connecting the living hush with the ghostly traces of prior presences.

Whatever shaped those faint tool marks, if it was truly an intelligence with form and hands, is gone or asleep. Maybe they will never return. Maybe they are here but invisible, dissolved into the water, into the pores of the rock. In the end, the place belongs to the plants, and the water that nurtures them is their silent ally. Together they compose a measured, deliberate existence, timeless in its pace. And if there is more—if there’s a secret occupant who is neither plant nor animal—the land keeps that secret well. Nothing stirs but the leaves, the gentle dripping, and the memory embedded in stone. They live here as a single chorus of stillness, ferns and moss and lichens, bound to the careful drip of water, abiding in the hush that envelops the entire highland hollow.
