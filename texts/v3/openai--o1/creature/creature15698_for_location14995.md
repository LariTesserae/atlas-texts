They dwell in layers, as if each ledge or crevice in the stone invites a new expression of life. At first glance, you might notice only the boldest inhabitants: the goats. Knobby-kneed and surefooted, they move like dancers across the sheer faces of the cliff, springing from one outcrop to another with that particular, almost arrogant bounce that marks they’ve done this a thousand times before. Their horns arc backward in elegant sweeps, their eyes never quite looking where their hooves will land because they seem to know by instinct. There’s a constant hum of bleating and hoof-scrape somewhere above you—built into the background like a steady heartbeat for the gorge.

Alongside them, the birds. The bigger raptors claim the high currents of air, circling in effortless glides. They perch on narrow spires, scanning for movement below. Sometimes you see the quick stoop of a hawk or falcon, a sudden arrow of motion slicing down between walls. Then there are smaller, feistier flocks: tiny scavengers with scratchy calls, social and loud, darting in and out of the cracks as if they have a hundred anxious errands in a day. Doves, tinted in dull grays and browns for camouflage, flutter in gentle spirals near the waterfalls, their coos echoing off the stone as though some hidden choir sings within the cliffs. The air rarely stays still enough for quiet; there is always a flap of wings or a startled whistle from one bird warning another away.

Below, in the damp undergrowth, a different world bustles. The short grasses and thick ferns tremble as small creatures bound through, tilting the stems in quick arcs. Whether they’re hares or something akin, with muscled back legs to leap across debris fields, or smaller murine shapes that scurry around the rock edges, they connect the green floor to the water threads. You’ll hear a rustle, then a splash, then silence—until another patch of undergrowth quivers. Amphibians lurk in wetter pockets: slick-skinned frogs the size of a child’s palm gather at pool edges, croaking in a rolling, sonorous chorus come dusk. Between the rock trickles, salamanders slip out to feed after a rain, their patterns vivid in the gloom, like secret living jewels resting on a moss bed before disappearing again.

Within these watery hollows, insects arrive by the thousands—though rarely do they form great clouds. They hover in softly lit patches, drifting across the waterfalls in glimmering clusters. Moths with paper-thin wings appear at twilight, going about their nocturnal exchange with pollinator flowers that cling to the cliff corners. Bees—short, hardy species adapted to cooler temps—buzz from crevice to crevice, collecting from miniature blooms that hide in the damp cracks. They’re quiet, purposeful, rarely traveling far before returning to their hidden hives made of claylike comb or nestled in unwatched cavities behind the stone.

Higher up, the wind-blown ledges hide feline silhouettes. Sometimes you see them at dawn or after a sudden storm, stepping with padded caution along the rim of an overhang, eyes reflecting a ghostly light if the sun or moon catches them just right. They are apex hunters: lean-bodied, tuft-eared, and agile enough to vanish with a single lunge across a narrow gap. They peer down into the amphitheater, scanning for goats that lose their footing or unsuspecting rodents venturing too far from cover. Their presence is more felt than seen—caught in a fleeting track or a wary hush that falls over the ground-level creatures.

And overshadowing it all is a kind of ephemeral host: that memory of people who once called this ring of peaks a home. It’s as though a faint human residue still clings to the air in the shape of old posts and half-collapsed huts. But no one steps out of these dwellings. Instead, moss creeps up the stacked stones, and spiders spin webs where doorways used to be. In some huts, you can still see a neat ring of stones forming a fire pit, or you spot a half-buried pot at a threshold. The vines claim everything: the rafters in the huts, the rope-bridge anchors on the cliff, the lean-to that’s half-slumped beneath an overhang. If anything living occupies what humans built, it’s likely small animals sheltering at night, or birds roosting where a roof once covered a family.

Some travelers swear the whispers of those old inhabitants remain—echoing in the babble of waterfalls, or in the wind that spirals through cracks. Those faint echoes mingle with the living chorus of goats and frogs and raptors so seamlessly that you can’t separate them. There might even be a rumor of people still alive here—a clan that’s gone deeper into the hidden channels beyond any path. But if they do exist, they move in near silence, leaving only rumors of footsteps across watery moss.

Beyond these larger shapes, even smaller realms appear upon close inspection. Tiny color-shifting lizards nestle in miniature caves behind dripping ferns, emerging to blink in a stray sunbeam before darting back into shadow. Nocturnal insects slip into watery rivulets by night, while slender fish navigate the shallow streams that twist through the center of the ring. On the underside of certain surfaces—where the rock is smooth from centuries of wind—clusters of pastel-shelled snails cling, feeding on algae. They seem to glow under the mist-laden sunlight, as if wearing porcelain armor.

All of these forms—hoofed, winged, furred, scaled—thread themselves into an overlapping tapestry. Their calls and movements reverberate off the stone. They navigate each other’s boundaries with tension and fluidity—predators stalking, prey darting to safety, scavengers tidying the aftermath. And at night, as that silver moonlight hits the waterfalls, something intangible flows through the amphitheater. It feels as if the very shapes of life become more pronounced: the goats standing watch on a ledge, the catlike hunter peering with glowing eyes from a high vantage, the frogs swelling their choruses in the pools. It’s a place where eyes shine back at you if you turn your lantern too quickly, where the hush can break into a clamor of wings, hooves, water, and wind in a single moment.

So who truly lives here? In one sense, everything. The stone itself holds the memory of vanishings and emergence. The dripping water fosters new growth every time the clouds burst open. The birds, the goats, the amphibians, the insects, and those lingering shadows of human craft form a shared domain that’s constantly remade. This circle of peaks is a congregation: plants anchor the cliffs, animals pulse through every crosscurrent of breeze, and the old huts stand watch in silence. Together, they inhabit the crossing point between rocky confines and clouded sky—a living testament that even in the harshest vertical prison, life finds niches upon niches to huddle and sing.
