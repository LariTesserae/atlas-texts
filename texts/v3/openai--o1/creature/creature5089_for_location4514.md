They move slowly. In the cool hush of the submerged plateau, their bodies slip easily through the water, pale limbs trailing faint ripples. At first glance, it’s not obvious if these beings are more human or fish: each one has a distinctly upright frame, yet their skin bears faint shimmers of scale along the forearms and calves. Many possess wide, translucent fins that fan out behind them like regal, drifting cloaks, letting them maneuver deftly through corridors of living kelp. When they speak, it’s in a softened series of vowels and clicked sounds, pitched to travel through water much like the chitter of certain sea mammals.

And yet, there is no single shape that defines them. Some who inhabit the city look more amphibious, with webbed digits and large, fluid eyes set slightly to the sides of their faces. Others have hair that floats about them in sinuous tendrils, sometimes adorned with small shells clipped to braids. A few have frills sweeping back from their cheekbones, flaring open whenever they inhale from the watery currents. It seems their forms have slowly shifted over centuries, molded by the same drifts and tides that shaped the plant-forests around them. Perhaps they came once from dry land—maybe distant ancestors breathed air under bright skies—but that origin story is as hazy as mountaintops here. Now, their lungs have adapted so well that they can pass hours—or maybe entire days—without surfacing.

They are quiet, and not. Within their gatherings, conversation runs gentle and low: subtle pulses of resonant humming pass between them, aided by shifts of their fins and swirling movements of hands. But in the wider city, they’re anything but silent. Their horns—large conch-shell instruments carved and etched with swirling patterns—bellow notes across open squares of drifting vegetation. Whelk trumpets punctuate the watery hush with brief, urgent calls that echo through algae-choked halls. And they use smaller shell-pipes, too, each shrilling out quick sequences of notes when messengers travel from one end of the district to another. Everywhere, the toll and cry of these instruments dominate the city’s audible tapestry, layered over the squeaks and clicks of sea mammals who have found kindred voices among them.

In their day-to-day lives, these people embrace the same unhurried rhythms that shape the architecture. They’re gatherers of the forest’s gifts, swimming through swaying curtains of broad green leaves, plucking edible seed pods from the thick crown of algae above, or coaxing tiny, luminous polyp-lamps from deeper shadows to bring them up to the main concourses. Some raise small gardens: inside living courtyards formed by braided kelp walls, they cradle clusters of gently glowing sea-fruits or cultivate spore-laden anemones used for dyes and pigments. A few craft tools—simple, yes, but elegant in their own right. They fashion nets of fine cord from fibrous stems, carve coral shards with sharpened edges, or create decorative jewelry from shell fragments carefully inlaid with bright fish scales.

Children dart about with bursts of unstoppable curiosity. They take advantage of the quiet pockets between drifting leaves to play, chasing schools of color-shifting fish or squirting each other with jets of water. Their laughter (an odd, lilting sound, half giggle and half bubble-spray) rings through corridors woven from thicker branches, bouncing joyfully off stone outcroppings that appear ancient as the plateau itself. Wherever the children appear, small eel-like creatures show an odd fascination, winding around ankles or wrists in a form of friendly greeting, as if drawn to their energetic presence.

Craftspeople among them, the ones who shape kelp into dwellings, do so with reverence. Instead of clear-cutting or uprooting, they gently guide growth. They knot fronds together in spiral patterns, secure loops of vine overhead to form canopies, and pack smooth stones into the hollow of old roots to create natural seating or counters. Over time, the architecture is coaxed into shape: grown as much as built. Each “room” feels soft around the edges, with a living membrane that filters water in and out, letting in faint sunlight from above while keeping the interior gently calm.

From a distance, one might think the branches and fronds form labyrinthine cathedrals, but the dwellings are, in fact, intimately small. The inhabitants enjoy gathering in communal “rooms” at the center of each cluster, where they share meals of kelp-based stews or shellfish broths. They lay out woven mats on smooth sections of stone floor, traded from deep ravines, then pass around hand-carved cups brimming with sweet, fermented sea-nectar. Snatches of conversation blend with the chatter of bright crustaceans scuttling along the walls. Visitors are welcomed by a soft swirl of color: the swirling, gently flashing patterns of their garments or body markings that convey friendship and guidance.

Animals here exist on equal footing—a broad collaboration binding all creatures. Long-bodied sea mammals with flexible forelimbs ferry messages or help haul larger items. Crab-like guardians the height of a child trundle lazily along the city periphery, clearing away debris. Even the schools of fish that thread the watery streets serve a role, picking parasites from the footpaths of the living architecture while performing a kind of continuous, glittering dance overhead. It’s all part of a patient system where each moving piece—plant, person, and animal—knows its pattern in the swirl of daily life. Over eons, they’ve learned to trust one another: the forest fosters them, and they in turn care for the forest, pruning away excess growth, protecting vulnerable young shoots, ensuring the cyclical flow is never broken.

In the calmer outskirts of the settlement, some inhabitants live in near solitude: healers and contemplatives who cultivate rare blooms that only open at twilight, or record each generation’s history by carving slow, fluid script onto large stone tablets. One can find these outlying enclaves by following lines of softly glowing kelp-lamps that weave like a trail of faint constellations into deeper channels. The people who dwell there sometimes sport more elaborate frills around their neck or arms—adaptations to the cooler, darker stretches—and they develop subtle patterns of luminescence along their cheeks. They are the philosophers of this watery realm, listening to the deeper hum of currents that pass along the ridges and valleys, reading subtle shifts in water temperature like poets reading the wind.

Still, for all these differences among them, the inhabitants share one unifying sense of calm. No matter their role—whether a kelp-shaper or a caretaker of luminous sponges, a musician or a child at play—they exist in unhurried harmony. In the heart of the city, their gatherings can be lively, brimming with the chorus of horns and social chatter, yet an undercurrent of serenity weaves it all together. The water sways them, the tides guide them, and they trust in the ongoing dance of life around them. They don’t look upwards to the faint shimmer of sunlight with any longing for a lost shoreline. Instead, they occupy this drowned plateau as if they have always been here, melding the calm timelessness of deep water with the adaptive spirit of people who once knew the world above.

Nothing stands out so much as their collective acceptance of change: for even the strangest phenomenon—a new species drifting in from distant depths, or the discovery of a bioluminescent algae bloom that enfolds an entire block—eventually becomes part of their tapestry. The city’s residents allow everything to integrate slowly, methodically, letting growth and time have the final say. It’s a culture that sculpts itself as the underwater forest sculpts the city: organically, in gentle, breathing pulses.

If one drifts to the far edges, where the columns of kelp recede, you can glimpse the next horizon of ridges or ravines trailing off into cooler gloom. But always just at the boundary of hearing lingers the steady hum of the city’s heartbeat—horn calls, rustling fronds, living walls. A quiet, persistent promise that in these fluid halls, beneath the sunken summits, life isn’t mere survival. It’s a slow, deliberate dance of interwoven species who, in their own patient way, have made a timeless home in the hush of cool water and the measured drift of aquatic green.
