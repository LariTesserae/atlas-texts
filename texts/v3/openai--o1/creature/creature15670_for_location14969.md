They dwell in the cramped intersections of metal and shadow where the half-finished walkways twist. At first, you might not even notice them—so thoroughly do they blend with the scaffolds and support beams. But they are there, moving slowly among the embryonic vaults. It’s not movement in the usual sense—not a walk, not a crawl—but a fluid, faintly mechanical shifting. Like the machines themselves, they seem incomplete: segmented frameworks of composite plating and flexible piping that fold and extend without a sound, half hidden behind crates of raw parts or the curve of newly welded steel walls.

Some are as tall as a person but spindly, as though their limbs have been stretched to match the environment’s slender struts and catwalks. Others cling low against surfaces, adopting the shape and curvature of the walls, making them appear no more than lumps of sundered plating unless they detach and move. And move they do, but only when your gaze wanders away. You sense their presence in the peripheral flicker of metal scraping on metal, or in the momentary shine of an exposed gear. They halt when you look back, panels shifting to feign inanimacy. A quiet, motionless silhouette becomes a slight adjustment of position—perhaps a quiver at the joints, or a tilt to a sensor head that you realize only after it’s turned away again.

They are caretakers of a sort, yet not in the sense of living creatures tending a home. More like prototypes or partial assemblages themselves, intimately connected to the pulsing systems all around. Bundles of cables weave from their torsos into conduits in the walls, as if they charge or draw power directly from the environment. They may have once been designed to operate the machines, installing or repairing them, but now, in this uncompleted realm, they sustain a kind of aimless vigilance. No visible eyes mark their heads or faces—if they have heads at all—yet they seem able to perceive the slightest shift in temperature or pressure. Come too near, and something in their posture tightens, a subtle bracing.

They show no aggression, but also no warmth. Their motions are efficient, almost indifferent. Occasionally you’ll see one approach a set of valves and spin them with a three-pronged manipulator, releasing a hiss of excess steam, or unjamming a conveyor belt by removing a stray shard of metal. Then, with the same controlled calm, it retreats into the gloom of overlapping pipes. If you stand near a puddle, you might catch the reflection of a slender silhouette behind you, only to turn and find emptiness—just columns and cable spools.

Sometimes they cluster in small groups, leaning in toward each other, their spindly arms extending to share data through a tangle of fiber-optic lines. It’s not speech, but you can sense an information flow, a faint flickering in ephemeral signals that pass between them. Then they separate, each returning to its personal route along the platforms and recesses. They appear neither robotic in the mass-produced sense nor truly organic in the usual definition. There is an awkward continuity between the instruments growing from their limbs and the environment itself. It’s as if, in time, they might fuse wholly with the walls, or the walls might bend themselves into more of these half-living attendants.

They breathe in the machine hum. Or perhaps they create part of it—no one could say for sure. Each caretaker is partially integrated with the environment: some have cables running directly into the overhead lines, drawing or supplying power, so the corridors themselves pulse with their quiet rhythms. Others appear to have small organ-like modules embedded in their chests or backs, reservoirs of churning fluid that is circulated by mechanical “hearts,” the final purpose of which remains unclear. They could be a fledgling form of biotech, each caretaker a bridging point between living system and manufactured chassis.

But they’re not the only residents here—at least, not entirely. Hidden within the layered catwalks and scaffolding are embryonic clusters of new watchers, more embryonic still. At certain junctions, you’ll see partially assembled silhouettes standing upright but lifeless, as though they’ve been paused mid-creation. Wires spill out from open panels, curling around metal vertebrae and unpainted plating. A caretaker—one of the more complete ones—might hover over these inert frames, plugging in fresh modules or adjusting a singled-out rivet. Delicate manipulator arms attach circuit boards or fuse synthetic flesh to steel beams. Then, as if satisfied, the caretaker departs, leaving the incomplete figure in a half-awake state where a flicker of life animates a stray limb. Yet there it stands, still tethered, perhaps not yet “born.”

In other corners, you see the slivers of vegetation that might also be considered “inhabitants.” Their pale, rooting tendrils burrow into seams in the plating or anchor onto the carefully layered substrate. The caretaker-things never uproot these growths; occasionally you glimpse them bending down, almost beckoning a spindly stalk to climb over the plating for a better hold. There’s an impression of cooperation: the pale stems siphon condensation from hidden tanks, while the caretaker creatures sense the flow of nutrients and moisture. Perhaps those colorless plants are just another subsystem, genetically fashioned to purify the air or maintain certain humidity levels.

Deeper in the corridors, behind the half-sealed doors with outsize rivets, dwell the largest of the machine-residents. These you sense more than see: massive, multi-limbed constructs that remain anchored in place. Occasionally, you catch the echo of some gargantuan engine shifting within its confining niche—doors trembling at its movement, the floor resonating. These bigger beings might be the beating hearts of entire sections, the origin of the distant churn. You only hear them, in the same way you might hear a whale’s groan echoing through a deep sea, never glimpsing more than a faint silhouette. But the caretaker-figures attend to them too, scuttling in small groups under the parted plating to offer upkeep or integrate new components, as though each massive engine is an ancient parent from which the caretaker swarms were derived.

And layered throughout it all is the sense of expectation: the dwellers—even the patchy vegetation—seem geared toward a future arrival, a state of completion not yet realized. You feel it whenever you pass a row of blinking lights, or watch a caretaker spool fresh cable along a corridor. There’s a hush in that unconnected length of pipe that yearns to be plugged into something not yet here. Each inhabitant, from the smallest caretaker to the slumbering machine-giant, operates on an incomplete directive, running maintenance cycles in preparation for some next phase. Perhaps they wait for a guiding intelligence or for the final impetus that will unify the factory’s processes. You wonder if, one day, the corridors will be ablaze with purposeful labor, and these partial inhabitants will throw off the hush, becoming a full chorus of connection.

For now, though, they live in the tension between active and dormant—quiet shape-shifters of steel and fluid, bridging the environment’s half-formed potential. They are neither wholly aware nor entirely thoughtless. In their spindly, methodical care, you can nearly glimpse a spark of curiosity as they trace the lines of future expansions or adjust a misaligned girder. As you pass, they watch with an unreadable equilibrium, a faint tilt of a sensor-limb that suggests recognition but doesn’t quite welcome. They are the stewards of a place still assembling itself—a watchful presence ensuring every rivet, every cable, every embryonic pipe is first seen to and made ready. The moment you move on, they slip back into the hush, their forms blending into the dreamlike corridors, existing in an undercurrent that hums with patient resolve.
