They do not have faces, or if they do, no one has seen them. Masked by their own shapes, they grow crooked and tight in the cramped hollows, pressing themselves into the fissures in the stone. From a distance, they resemble mere flora—at least, that is what one’s eyes insist upon in the dim, half-light. Yet, as you step closer, you notice subtle shifts in the way they curve or tilt toward you, as if perceiving your presence, turning to regard you with unknowable senses. It happens so gradually, so gently, that at first you dismiss it as an illusion. But then one of them—long-limbed and covered in glistening nodules—makes a visible, calculated bend toward your hand, drawn by the heat of your body or by the quavering echo of your footstep. You realize they are not just plants.

You might call them a single species, though each specimen displays a cunning individuality in its precise posture and arrangement. Some hold candelabra-like appendages aloft, encrusted with glimmering barnacle growths. Others are squat, radial lumps that extrude spike-tufts. Still others twist upward in wiry braids, forming looping archways or leaning precariously from outcropping to outcropping, bridging dangerous gaps. Many have translucent membranes stretched taut over internal cavities, pulsing in time with the constant crashing heartbeat that suffuses the entire realm. Here, “root” and “stem” cease to be distinct: the boundaries separating one trunk from another blur in a labyrinth of interwoven ties, a hive of living columns.

Yet these denizens are not mere vegetation. It is not water that nourishes them but something else, an invisible currency that seems to flow from the realm’s metallic pounding. Their succulent, waxy flesh resonates at just the right pitch, as though siphoning off enough of the machine’s ancient thunder to keep themselves alive. Glimpsed in the gloom, you can see faint pulses of light that flare within their bulbous sacs, traveling up coil-like stems or sinking down again into the ground. It may be some electromagnetic infusion, or an energy that has no name in the outside world. Whatever it is, it replaces rain and sun and soil, sustaining them in ways that no ordinary lifeform could manage.

In time, if you observe closely, you may see evidence of roles—communal tasks or specialized behaviors that suggest these dwellers are not entirely passive. There are tall, luminescent clusters whose nodules glow a faint green-blue, brightening every time the hammering clang echoes. These luminous colonies seem like watchtowers, scanning the horizon for shifts in the sonic tide. You see flatter expansions creeping along the lower ridges, their surfaces so glossy they nearly reflect the rock’s miniature skyscrapers behind them. Snakelike filaments branch out from these expansions, insinuating themselves under rusted metal plates and riveted panels, coaxing out old bits of mineral or flaking alloy. At intervals, the filaments slip back inside the mother-forms, retreating with their gleaned nutrients or raw materials.

Elsewhere, some individuals function as living scaffolds, bridging precarious tears in the cliff face. Above these bridging forms, others gather in ropy clumps, a canopy of braided stems as if to create a stable platform. It is unclear whether they do this for themselves or for something else. Perhaps, in a distant age, they were grown—or engineered—to serve a species that walked upon them, harnessing their tough, pliable bodies as living walkways. Now, no such masters remain to direct or exploit them, and the dwellers continue their bridging, forging new overpasses in blind repetition of an ancient function.

Some appear drawn to the half-submerged structures. The heavily corroded huts and half-buried machine parts are swarmed by creepers that plug themselves into any opening—ventilation slits, twisted piping, open rivet holes. When the unending clang reverberates, these creepers twitch. Delicate sensor-like tips flutter in the mechanical wind, some reacting a heartbeat late, as though tasting the metal thunder before funneling it deeper into the structure. At moments—standing still and watching them—you’d swear you hear tiny chimes or squeaks accompanying the booming percussion, as if these dwellers are answering back with their own small chorus.

Though there are no obvious eyes, limbs, or mouths, you sense a quiet, deliberate mindfulness emanating from every tuft. Perhaps it’s an intelligence distributed throughout the entire environment, with each frond or shoot acting as one node in a grand, subterranean network. You see no illusions of personality or emotion, at least not in any human sense, but you sense a patience, a total acceptance of the cyclical, never-changing conditions. The hammering never ends. The enveloping darkness never lifts. In that suspended moment, these lifeforms thrive.

They show no fear of the rock’s sharp edges or the abrupt chasms—they’ve adapted to cling and coil, to nest themselves into the jagged terrain as though they were always part of it. Even the ancient catwalks appear to have become natural perches for some species of thick, trunk-like creeper. Metal squeaks softly under their weight as they occupy corners of the battered walkway, swaying as they devour rust and harness whatever lingering energies remain embedded in the steel’s molecular structure. Their surfaces glisten with an unidentifiable residue, perhaps a byproduct of their obscure metabolism—a shining chemical polish that never quite drips, never quite hardens, always half-liquid in the half-light.

In the deeper fissures, you spot motile forms. They are small, spore-like clusters that shuffle in slow bursts. A closer look reveals that the “shuffling” is, in fact, the subtle lengthening and contracting of tuberous feelers that pass from crack to crack, bridging micro-gaps in the mineral floor. They feed on flecks of debris or trace residues from the pounding vibrations, the latter channeling through the mineral flakes like a deep, resonant hum. Whenever they reach a cluster of bigger dwellers, they pause, trembling, as if exchanging pulses—offering up their gleaned morsels and receiving something intangible in return.

So it is in every corner: a bizarre synergy that functions in place of predator and prey, pollinator and bloom. Each occupant appears to rely on the others, though it happens in ways less linear than typical food chains. The entire realm, from the highest spined pinnacle down to the smallest hidden grotto under the warped catwalk, seems fused into a single living engine, one that hums with the same unstoppable cadence as the ever-present hammer. They absorb tremors, share chemical pulses, trade electromagnetic surges. Even inactivity—cool, quiescent waiting—forms part of their repertoire, as some shapes enter a dormant state, husk-like structures poised for the next wave of vibrations.

And though you see no flashes of eyes or quicksilver darting of animals, there is a presence that cannot be ignored. It is in the graceful arc of a root bridging a gap. It is in the silent communal breath of so many leaves—though leafless in the conventional sense, wholly alien in function—nonetheless striving, year upon unchanging year, in earnest persistence. You sense it in the faint scraping lull that hints at movement mid-pulse, in those ephemeral chimes from the pipe-filled huts, in the rhythmic twinkle of smaller nodules along the ridges.

They are the dwellers of this sealed domain. Perhaps they have forgotten what it means to be anything else—or perhaps they never knew. They do not mourn the absence of water or direct sunlight. They do not wonder about outer worlds or different climates. Their universe is entirely realized within the cramped, spiny confines and in the colossal, resonant clang that is both cradle and heartbeat. They feed on a force the rest of nature would hardly recognize, spun from rust and leftover industry, from the whispered memory of cataclysmic tectonics and manmade contraptions. The place is alive because they are alive—and they exist so symbiotically with their environment that they might as well be the environment itself.

In that unchanging twilight, these dwellers remain, year after year, century upon century. They are neither malevolent nor benign; they simply are—and their presence saturates every crag, every battered scrap of metal, every unsettled breath you take. Lost in that realm of pulsing, living machinery, they fuse into a single tapestry of greenish shining limbs and bristling shoots, every inch entwined in the hush of timeless equilibrium. And so they persist, guardians of a cyclical thunder that once came from an unknown machine, or from the planet’s own heart, or from some overlap of the two—no one truly knows. Only the dweller-forest remains, silent except for the all-pervading clang, existing in the locked moment that forever loops, waiting for nothing in particular, content in its own delicate, unearthly hum.
