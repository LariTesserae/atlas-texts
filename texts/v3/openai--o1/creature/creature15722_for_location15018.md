They live here in layers—this place might be devoid of birds and beasts and buzzing insects, but it is far from empty. The denizens are the vegetation itself, but also something more: an animating force fusing rock and vine. If you stand still, long enough and quietly enough, you can sense them. Not in the shapes of scurrying mammals or flitting birds, but in the upside-down world of roots that grip newly upthrust stone and the dawn of hardy spores that swirl on the wind.

At the lowest stratum live the ferns. Their spiral fronds are such a deep green that they almost consume the light rather than reflect it, curling and unfurling in fractal patterns. In each tight coil you could imagine a thousand instructions for how to inhabit newly made stone. They root in the shallow grit that sifts into cracks, splaying outward in defiant resilience. Every day, they search for ephemeral beams of gloom-dimmed sunlight, and every night they fold themselves as though bracing for the next battering gust. Yet they thrive on that chaos, drinking in the swirling mists that catch under their leaves.

Above them, the vines—the next layer in this colony—scale the towering spires with a fierce determination, thick and ropelike, their barky skins covered in small ridges that function like grappling hooks. They cling to rock faces that still hold the memory of tectonic violence. These vines are explorers, cartographers of the fresh scars in the earth. Their tendrils probe the stone as though reading scripts etched at creation’s moment. When the wind tears at them, they bend but do not break, anchored by knots that wedge deep into fissures. Over time, they form a living net that drapes over sheer walls, bridging spire to spire with braided arcs of living rope that glisten with condensation.

Dotting the vines in unexpected pockets are carnivorous pitcher plants—though you see no insects here, these plants seem determined to find a meal any way they can. Their long tubular mouths remain open, brimming with dew and the tang of acidic secretions. Sometimes the wind shears off bits of leaves or seeds, and those tumble into the pitchers, decaying in the fluid to provide some essential nourishment. Even in a world with no visible animal life, the pitcher plants abide, patient and sure that someday, something might stray too close.

In the shadows and corners, you’ll find the mosses and lichens clinging to the angled edges of broken slabs. These form a subtle, spongy tapestry of color: lung-colored greens and dusty blues, half hidden by the gloom, half illuminated by stray shards of cloud-filtered light. They grow in slow flourishing flurries, each patch a self-contained metropolis of microscopic worlds. When you lean in, you sense how thoroughly alive they are—miniature jungles in their own right. And an earthy smell rises off them, mineral-laced and damp, as though the stone itself were exhaling.

Then there’s the hidden level, the smallest dwellers: the spores, the pollen, and the drifting seeds that ride the wind’s chaotic gusts. They swirl unseen in the constant collision of air currents, colliding with the monolithic spires before snagging in a crack or an angled ledge. Some seeds are equipped with feathery filaments not unlike gossamer sails—though here, no gentle breeze coaxes them forward. Instead, the storms hurl them violently across the gorge until they land by chance in some sheltered nook. Those that survive grow into new tangles of creepers; the cycle continues with every tempest.

But perhaps you sense something else, ubiquitous yet intangible. It reverberates in the hiss of steam through seeping vents in the rock and resonates in the deep groan of precariously balanced slabs. Maybe it’s the spirit of the young geology itself—tremoring just beneath the surface, pressing outward with tectonic breath. This presence has no face or limbs, but it moves in the vibrations of stone scraping stone, in the rhythmic hum of wind chiseling new ridges hour by hour. It saturates the damp air. If there is an awareness arching across the high spires, it might lie in the synergy—the unstoppable union—between living plant matter and half-cooled magma shifts.

You might call them “the engineers” of this newly minted cathedral of rock: an alliance of roots and rhizomes, of sporing life that whirls outward in each gust. There is a monstrous intricacy in the row of ferns that vault upward like scaffolding, in the vines that rope together broken debris like living nails, binding each fragment of the shattered earth. Collectively, they hold the land in place, performing the evolutionary labor of stabilizing the rough, violent edges. Even the wind, as harsh as it is, helps them by carrying seeds where no mammal or bird can.

All of these dwellers essentially form one vast organism—one slow, unstoppable tide of growth, amorphous and unstoppable. Sometimes a new cleft will split open in a rock face, and from that newborn gap, a spurt of pale shoots will poke out within days, turning green as they meet moisture and the ghost-light overhead. Or a slab will crack and tumble, revealing a sparkly seam of copper ore, instantly glistening with condensation; soon enough, the creeping lichens move in, nibbling at the dark minerals in their microscopic way.

So what lives here? Not a single species but an untamed coalition: a mass of leaves, vines, spores, pitchers, mosses—and the land itself. They exist in concert, propelled by storm, by the steam that scalds in short pulses, by the raw impetus of the world’s internal churn. They are all participants in an immense process of growth. Even the wind seems woven into their existence, ready at any moment to rip seeds free and fling them across the hollow. It is a living system in flux—an “industrial symphony,” yes, but not for metal and fuel; rather for rock and root and spore and survival. Each panorama of greenery is an entire community, an extension of a singular striving for life. And perhaps that striving is what truly dwells here: a robust, nameless living momentum that does not wait for mammals or insects—it simply is, and it is enough.
