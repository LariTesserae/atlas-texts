They don’t fill the corridors in constant motion. They aren’t huddled together in rowdy gatherings or bustling factories. In fact, you’d be forgiven for believing there is nothing here—at least nothing with a pulse or breath. Yet the place is neither abandoned nor vacant. Something dwells in these cramped and newly-cut halls.

At first, you wonder if you’ve caught a glimpse out of the corner of your eye: a faint flicker of movement near a sealed conduit, or a tight shape hunched in a recess where the light barely reaches. When you look directly, there’s no one there—only tools lying dormant, shadows thrown by the glow from overhead tubes. There’s a sensation that the watchers in this place know where their vantage points are best: behind pipes, under metallic walkways, or beyond the half-finished scaffolding. If you see them at all, it’s only as outlines or silhouettes, faint and fleeting. They never make a sound. Sometimes you can sense them, the feeling of being observed in a silence that is not quite empty.

You notice signs they leave behind. Discarded lengths of wire, newly-welded plates still warm to the touch, footprints that are not muddied with dust or water but rather scuffed into the raw rock. Occasionally, a greasy handprint marks a wall where someone has leaned for balance or paused in a moment of labor. Or you might find a patch of chipped stone where a piece of machinery was moved just seconds before you turned the corner. They must slip away when they sense another’s presence—quiet, adept, purposeful.

In the deepest segments of the hive-like corridors, behind those heavy iron doors, dwell the main coterie—if you can call it that. By the time you hear the hiss of the door’s mechanism, they’ve already retreated to the subterranean gloom. They occupy sealed rooms lit by the cold, stable glow of fluorescent panels, far from the partially built tunnels at the periphery. It’s there, in those more complete sections, that they oversee the engines and calibrate the massive machines whose rhythmic thump echoes throughout the settlement.

They seem not to require typical comforts of living: you do not see personal quarters or communal halls, no kitchens or sitting areas. Perhaps they eat in a manner that avoids standard fare, or perhaps they subsist on stored rations, precisely measured and efficient. There are no obvious containers of water or large storage for the usual resources, yet something is fueling them. You see evidence of tiny, carefully managed greenhouses hidden behind thick glass partitions, possibly cultivating some exotic strain of algae or fungus—short-lived growths the color of ash or pale cream. Small enough to be overshadowed by the endless machinery, these pockets of living matter might be harvested for nourishment or chemical processes.

Their clothing—when you get a rare glance—appears utilitarian to an extreme: dull, heat-resistant fabric wrapped in narrow plates of metal or composite to shield against scalding surfaces. It covers every inch of them, leaving only the vaguest suggestion of a human or humanoid shape underneath. Hoods or helmets conceal faces, tinted with a reflective gloss. Filters or ventilation tubes protrude from masks, hissing softly as they draw in the stifling air and pump out a low trickle of respiration. The shape of the suits tends to thin out at wrists and ankles, suggesting an almost gaunt physiology beneath. Some suits have mechanical limbs or exoskeletal segments integrated into them, augmenting what might otherwise be frail frames, enabling them to haul massive components through the labyrinth with relative ease.

And yet, despite their mechanical trappings, there is a discernible carefulness—an almost reverent manner in which they handle the wiring, measure the coolant, and check the pressure of the steam valves. They do not simply race through a shifting maze of corridors; they pace it, calibrating and engineering as though each corridor, each column, each conduit is part of an organism they must keep alive. When a fuse crackles or a pipe begins to hiss with too much force, they appear from nowhere to repair it, to coax the machinery back into a rhythmic hum.

Though they seem dedicated to maintaining this vast subterranean construct, it doesn’t feel like a place built for their comfort—or indeed for comfort of any kind. It’s as if they, too, are serving a purpose far larger than themselves. Perhaps they were sent by some distant authority, or perhaps they’re self-directed, forging a place that will someday be truly inhabited. You can sense a worshipful quality in how they refine and expand: more corridors, deeper chambers, sealed compartments that rumble with devices you can barely imagine.

At times, you hear them communicating—a sequence of muted clicks and hand signals, or short bursts of static from radio earpieces. Articulate words are scarce. They prefer an economy of movement and gesture to convey their meanings. Thick gloves splay or clench in repeated patterns, and masked heads nod in acknowledgment. It is the language of function over form. They seem comfortable in the fervent heat, damp fog rising from scorched metal, the swirl of steam. Perhaps they have adapted, or perhaps these suits shield them so thoroughly that the temperature doesn’t matter anymore.

A few hint at tasks beyond pure maintenance. One might see a slender figure hunched over a control console, inputting lines of code that recalibrate atmospheric vents. Another might be operating a mechanical arm that carefully lowers a half-finished metal beam into place. Rarely, you might catch sight of a small group sliding open a sealed crate, pulling out fresh plating or a roll of cables for future expansions. Their single unwavering aim is to keep the place growing, pulsing with fresh technology as if they’re building a body from the inside out.

And so they linger among the hiss of pistons and the glow of half-powered lights, minding the steady churn of engines, coaxing raw stone into symmetrical machines. Distributing water in fleeting streams to keep moisture at a precise balance. Checking the heat distribution so that nothing overheats, nothing stalls the unceasing construction. They slip between the living and the machine world, bridging the gap with quietly efficient steps.

What little personal choice they exhibit can sometimes be seen in those tiny courtyards—a rare slate bench, or a vent angled just so to direct a brief breeze into a resting station. Small touches that might suggest a living mind behind the mask: a perfectly upright stool next to a trickling channel of water, or a piece of paneling polished enough to reflect one’s own image in the gloom. Some measure of aesthetic, perhaps, or maybe a faint flicker of comfort.

In the end, you cannot be certain if these inhabitants are wholly human, if they’re augmented, or if they’re something else entirely—descendants of a once-human workforce merged seamlessly with the machinery they maintain. But they are unquestionably part of this place. Without their ceaseless labor, the labyrinth might grind down to lifeless metal and rock. With each footstep that resonates in the hush, they uphold a fragile equilibrium, ensuring every spark, every drip of condensation, every jolt of energy finds its place in the grand subterranean design.

So they live: masked, methodical, half-hidden shadows in a city that builds itself from within. They fill the claustrophobic void with patient industry, maintaining a realm that seems to breathe through mechanical vents and steam valves instead of lungs. While you may rarely see them up close, their presence permeates every surface, every humming corridor. They are the subtle, persistent animus of this underground machinery: quiet, precise, entirely devoted—and as intertwined with the city as roots with soil.
