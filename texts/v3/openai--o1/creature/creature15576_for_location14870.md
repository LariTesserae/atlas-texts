At first, you might assume that no one lives here at all. With no footprints in sight and no crannies for birds or beasts to nest, the citadel appears empty, an unmanned stage set for water and stone alone. And yet, the more time you spend wandering those seamless passages, the more certain you become that something does dwell here—a presence that is neither plant nor animal but tangible all the same.

You pause in a corridor and sense it: a faint vibration underfoot. It’s as though the mineral floor carries a pulse so low and slow you almost miss it. A soft resonance forms in the hollows of your chest, as if, by positioning yourself just right among the tight angles, you catch the echo of a living breath. There is a suggestion of watchers, perhaps not made of flesh. Instead, they could be shaped from the same faintly glimmering mineral that lines every wall. You wonder if they blend so perfectly with the architecture that you have walked past them countless times. Maybe these watchers are statues that, under certain conditions, shift from a rigid stance and move deeper into the structure, vanishing behind angled planes. You look for them in your periphery, suspecting that, if you stop focusing so hard, you might catch a silent pivot or a slight rearrangement of form.

Other times, you suspect the “inhabitants” are purely elemental—some aspect of the water that threads these terraces. You feel that the water, guided by grooves and gutters, carries secrets in its flow. You catch glimpses of shapes in the reflective surfaces—distorted faces in the rivulets that gather, in fleeting patterns, on the floors. It almost seems that the watery reflections have consciousness, swirling in basins, rising and falling, leaving behind no trace except a subtle glisten. Perhaps these shapes have no need for the usual markers of life; they do not worry about nourishment or heat. They exist in perpetual motion, gliding along arcs of channel-work. Whenever you try to follow them, you lose track of where one rivulet ends and another begins. The watery outlines merge into a deeper network, then vanish.

On occasion, you find little hollows carved into the stone, some no larger than your hand, nestled at various heights. Perhaps these once held offerings—tiny cups or tokens left behind by whoever originally built this place. Or maybe they exist as vantage points for smaller denizens—creatures that cling to the wet surfaces, translucent as the water itself, scuttling out of sight the moment they sense your arrival. Even if you can’t see them, you can’t shake the feeling that your presence has been registered, that some set of invisible eyes track your movements from behind the perfect geometry of the walls.

Another notion enters your mind: perhaps the “people” who shaped this city never left. Maybe they have simply become so attuned to their handiwork that they fade seamlessly into the planes of stone. Think of them not as ghosts but as living embodiments of craftsmanship—folk who, over countless generations, smoothed the corners, chiseled out channels, and arranged each glimmering slab in quiet devotion. They might stand in stillness for decades at a time, letting the water run over their feet. In an age measured by eons, they have learned that breath and motion can be luxuries. Their existence is measured not by quick heartbeat or daily routine, but by the slow wearing down of edges and the methodical drip of water. If indeed they remain, they have all the patience in the world, preferring soft, timeless adaptation to any showy display.

Others might say the place is inhabited by an intelligence that neither moves nor stands but holds each line of architecture in its collective awareness. A single mind, or a hive mind, that saturates the entire citadel: it drifts in the echoes of the channels, in the minute hum that trembles through the floors. It uses no language you can decipher; its speech might be in the precise angles of each corridor and the carefully measured flow of moisture through each conduit. Why it does this—whether as a quiet worship of geometry or as a caretaker ensuring the structures endure the centuries—remains unclear. You suspect it is as old as the stones themselves, that it “thinks” in geological time, guiding the small rearrangements that keep everything strong and unbroken.

Or, perhaps, nothing here is truly separate from the citadel. Consider the possibility that the existence you sense is the citadel itself—alive in its own right, shaped by a nameless race that has long since departed. Imagine that the slow seepage of water, the hush of wind in the upper corridors, the bright mirrors of mineral surfaces are all part of its memory, living out an endlessly repeating cycle. In that view, the caretaker—this entity of stone and trickling liquid—continues to rebuild and refresh itself. It allows the sun to bleach its surfaces, the water to wear them down, and then, gradually, the place rearranges or heals. You never truly catch it in the act, but your intuition says the lines aren’t always where you saw them last; corners shift, channels move. The citadel is its own craftsman, imperceptibly rearranging its shape in response to changing conditions.

From another perspective, you can also imagine a lineage of reclusive artisans still living in the labyrinth’s hidden alcoves. They might not be human; they might be something more stoic. Imagine slender beings with arms shaped by endless carving, their hands calloused and strong, their eyes pale and reflective from generations spent amid dazzling mineral glare. These artisans hardly speak, communicating instead through subtle knocks of chisels against stone. Maybe each new generation apprentices under the last, continuing the labor begun centuries ago. Their presence would be so subdued, so perfectly in tune with the hush of this place, that they slip from corridor to corridor with barely a whisper. Were you to catch sight of them, you might only glimpse a faint silhouette set against a bright slab, or the quick motion of a chisel raised and lowered. Then, in a breath, gone behind a ramp or pillar.

And so, as you walk the winding passages, you realize the life here is not what you’d call conventional. It is a realm of merged possibilities: invisible watchers made of stone, watery shapes caught in reflections, slow-shifting architecture that hums with a collective awareness. Or perhaps a hidden lineage of master stoneworkers, continuing their endless craft in the city’s depths. In truth, all these speculations might be true in their own way—or none of them. This place sustains a hush that allows your mind to wander through layers of speculation, each as valid as the last. Yet one shared element remains consistent: some form of careful, deliberate presence here, guiding or maintaining this watery fortress. It’s too precise, too systematically shaped to be an abandoned relic.

Whichever vision you favor, you sense they all converge on a single purpose: the preservation of an ideal—of geometry, or water’s unceasing flow, or the patient shaping of mineral surfaces. This is a citadel that resonates with continuity. You feel it in the hush of corridors, in the bright reflection of the relentless sun on the pale stone, in the trickling channels meticulously routed from level to level. Life here is not a matter of pulses or petals or the scurry of small creatures; it is architecture itself—the profound demonstration of an ancient will that persists long after any mortal builder might have been laid to rest.

In the end, you leave with the strong, lingering impression that “who” or “what” lives here is both everywhere and nowhere specific. It is the echo in the shifting corridors, the drips that keep perfect time, the sculpted angles that never truly repeat yet follow the same abiding design. Inside this monumental labyrinth, life transcends the usual boundaries, becoming something at once architectural, elemental, and quietly, unshakably alive in its own slow, steady way.
