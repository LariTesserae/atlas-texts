They are many and varied, not just in species, but in the way they move, how they exist in this realm of half-light and torn metal. Most of them, you never see all at once. Instead, you catch glimpses: a swirl of color between drifting kelp, a faint silhouette skulking behind a corroded column, the subtle flicker of movement above your head where the beams of an abandoned rig form a skeletal ceiling. So you piece them together in your mind: all these underwater inhabitants, each dwelling in its niche among the battered stone and decaying machinery.

Some have shaped themselves around the plateau’s contours over countless generations. They perforate the rock with hidden burrows—vernacular architects chiseling silted ledges into labyrinthine nests. Fish as small and transparent as glass shards remain invisible until illuminated by odd shards of drifting sunlight. When a gentle ripple catches them, their bodies flash with iridescent prisms, like a dream flickering at the corner of your eye. You sense a living tapestry, made not of threads but of glimmering scales and twitching antennae.

It begins with the plants: towering kelp whose stems are thick as a person’s forearm, whose surfaces feel rubbery and tough. They grip the edges of the plateau’s cliffs with claw-like root structures—an anchor of knotted tendrils. Here, minuscule snails creep in slow, meandering spirals. Their rosy shells, no bigger than a fingernail, leave gleaming trails of biofilm. Within this canopy, whole communities form an interwoven city: spindly sea spiders, impossibly delicate, creep across the kelp’s broad leaves. Spider-shrimp, their shells ridged and spiky, cluster near the plant’s base, pick through drifting particulate, then scuttle for cover whenever a larger creature passes by.

Farther along, thick curtains of algae coat anything that doesn’t move. Rusted scaffolding transforms into a living scaffold for swirling, leaf-like colonies. Colonies within colonies—tiny corals that have seized upon rust-blistered surfaces and built pastel towers of calcium. These corals are usually no bigger than a clenched fist, but each one teems with polyps, their slender tentacles fluttering gently in the mild currents. Lean in close, and you’ll see amphipods (little crustaceans reminiscent of miniature shrimps) patrolling the coral’s exterior. They pluck out parasites and nibble on drifting food, each fulfilling a role in this vast, unknowable chain of life.

Schools of fish flow around these structures like living rivers, silver threads in the half-light. There are the small, darting ones—blue and green chromatic scales that flash as they approach the algae, feed, then vanish in an instant. There are also heavier-bodied fish: slow, ponderous types that seem to guard certain vantage points, feeding on barnacles and crusts that accumulate on broken metal edges. Some, with broad, plate-like mouths, rasp at the surfaces, cleaning them of algae. Others, large and spined like mythical creatures, dig at the silt with thick, downturned jaws, kicking up particulate clouds that glimmer as they swirl.

Beneath the plateau’s overhangs, in realms where the sunlight never quite reaches, dwell the quiet watchers. Conger eels—slender and muscular—coil in recesses that seem impossible to enter, sliding out only to feed or to glimpse passing potential prey. Their milky eyes can register vibrations more than light, making them intensely sensitive to the mechanical churning that echoes from old turbines and half-wrecked fans. Occasionally, from within these dark hollows, one sees a pulse of bioluminescence—glow-in-the-dark plankton disturbed by the eel’s movement, or tiny shrimp that scintillate when brushed.

High above, near the plateau’s summits, the kelp forests grow so densely they form corridors of swaying green, blocking out any reference to a distant horizon. Among these flexible corridors, fish gather by the hundreds, swirling in mesmerizing patterns, their collective body forming a single shimmering shape. Sometimes they might cluster to feed on drifting nutrients in the water column; other times they part like a shimmering curtain to reveal a large predator gliding behind them. Perhaps it’s a sleek, curious serpent with glossy, eel-like fins, or a hulking, blunt-nosed creature that has adapted to the plateau’s steep walls and swirling currents. You glimpse these hunters only briefly—shadows flitting through the undergrowth, accompanied by a resonant call that vibrates through your bones.

And then, among the scoured remains of an industrial past, new communities have taken root—both animal and mechanical. In the largest, half-collapsed structures, old pipes still pulse with the last sputters of power: hidden vents expelling warm, mineral-laden water. These outflows create miniature hotspots of life—a swirling mass of luminous plankton, filter-feeding tube worms, and pale, slender crabs adapted to the near-constant gush of mineral-rich flow. In these spots, entire micro-kingdoms arise, feeding on the chemical bounty in the water. Some of the more advanced metal corridors still release the occasional hiss or clang, startling shoals of fish into fractal flight. Along the bases of broken walls, spiky sea urchins cling to rust-riddled surfaces, their bodies encrusted with flecks of metal they’ve collected to augment themselves. Over many generations, these urchins have incorporated the plateau’s debris into their spines, so they look almost like walking sculptures.

Rounding a corner of twisted scaffolding, you might see a cluster of peculiar, crustacean-like creatures that congregate around flickers of bioluminescent fungus. The fungus has found purchase in the moist, shadowy recesses where bits of rotted wood (perhaps broken crates or old shipping pallets) remain. These crustaceans wave fans of feathery appendages, combing the water for suspended plankton and gleaning nutrients from the fungus in the process. They click at one another under the water—a language of taps and rattles that echoes softly in the claustrophobic gloom.

Farther out, beyond the metallic ruins, the plateau continues, wild and largely unaffected by anything humans once built. In those deeper recesses, colossal anemones sway in slow, methodical pulses, their stinging arms reaching to snatch unwary fish. Vibrant sponges cling to every rocky protrusion, and in their many tubes and pores, neon gobies and other tiny species live by the hundreds. A few ancient sea turtles sometimes wander here. Old as the world, they swim with unhurried grandeur, grazing on the algae that carpets the upper surfaces. In their wake, remoras cling in a pale cluster, hitching a ride, all part of the endless, cyclical interplay of this watery domain.

It’s not just the animals—there’s a deeper presence in the half-sunken factories. Inside the skeleton of a large metal hull, you might spot flickering lights that are not purely mechanical. The brightness shifts color in a steady pulse. Peer closer, and you discover ghost-like creatures: jellyfish with luminescent stripes throbbing along their bells. They seem to favor the hidden corners where old lighting systems once illuminated loading bays or transit rails. An echo of the station’s original glow, now replaced by drifting globes of living fluorescence. Sometimes, you hear a hum or a faint stirring of gears continuing their ancient routines: robotic arms that twitch once every few hours, guided by malfunctioning circuits. Around these spasms of obsolete machinery, small schools of fish wait for scraps of dislodged barnacles or startled crustaceans—an opportunistic harvest gleaned from the machine’s unpredictability.

And then there are those who do not make themselves so obvious: large, prehistoric-looking crustaceans, with thick, chitinous shells and segmented tails, scuttling across the scoured floors. They leave trails in the sediment, vanishing into undersea gullies where the lights from above can’t reach. In the depths of these gullies, you sense forms so immense that their presence reverberates like thunder. If you catch a glimpse—a pair of luminous eyes, or an oddly shaped silhouette cutting through the swells—you can’t be sure whether you’ve seen a relic from a primeval age, or something thoroughly modern and unbelievably huge. They are the wardens of this deep plateau, neither wholly ruling nor wholly hidden, but in slow, deliberate motion that shapes every smaller life around them.

Between pulses of surging current, in that fleeting hush, all the creatures of this watery world hang suspended. Tails and fins and tendrils, quiet in mid-sway. Everywhere, micro-communities cling to drifting scraps of debris; planktonic swarms swirl in the dim columns of light, funneling upward. And through it all, those slow rotations of mechanical fans, humming behind thick hull walls like an ancient heartbeat. Here, life—whether it’s fish or algae, jelly or eel—has learned to rely on both the time-worn stability of the rock and the occasional, still-living rhythms of the remaining underwater engines. Neither truly natural nor fully artificial, it’s an ecosystem that merges the old currents of the Earth with the remnants of an industry once reliant on those same tides.

Yes, the storms come, and the waters churn, and the entire city of life is whipped into upheaval. But no matter how fierce the onslaught, these creatures endure. Some are battered and torn away, others retreat into holes or behind the groaning wreckage. When it’s over, they emerge again, in full array. Each occupant seems bound by a shared understanding that the plateau remains—for now—a relatively consistent anchor in a world of relentless change. The scars on the rock, the slow-dying machines, and the flourishing green canopies testify to their resilience. In this domain of persistent motion and cyclical disturbance, every living fragment is simultaneously tested and nurtured by the same unstoppable tide.

So, who lives here? An entire, teeming multitude, from translucent fish so delicate they’re nearly invisible to hidden giants that echo with ancient memory in their deep roars. There are bright reef denizens that dazzle in shoals, and solitary watchers in crevices, cunning hunters and improbable scavengers. Each, in its own quiet or riotous way, has found a home amid the plateau’s industrial wreckage and kelp forests, forging a continuum of life that stretches from dawn-lit shallows to the uncharted gloom. They are the inheritors of the ruined metal skeletons and the timeless stone, inhabitants of a kingdom forever in flux, yet somehow enduring, just as the tides that shaped it all eons ago continue to reshuffle the silt, shift the reefs, and breathe life through every corner of this submerged world.
