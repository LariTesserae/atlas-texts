At a distance, you might imagine the plateau is deserted—an expanse incapable of harboring even the most resilient beings. Yet as you wander, you start to sense presences. They do not rustle or call out with any voices. Instead, they seem woven into the equilibrium itself, clinging to the edges of the pervading roar like faint echoes. Their exact shape or number isn’t obvious, and at first, you might mistake them for some trick of the haze. A drifting wisp might condense into something that watches from behind an outcropping, only to disperse again into threads of mist. Though impossible to pin down at first, you begin to suspect that forms do indeed live here—and have done so for ages beyond measure.

If you pause at the brink of one of those half-collapsed stone walls, you may notice the slightest suggestion of movement tangential to your field of vision. A shrouded silhouette could be perched on the rubble, limbs elongated and barely distinct from the stone itself. No fur or obvious hide stands out—just a surface that seems mottled with the same gray-brown hue as the ancient rock, as though each being gathers the color and texture of its surroundings and weaves them into its own body. If you blink, the figure may vanish, swallowed by the roar and the ceaseless wind. Yet an imprint remains, a sensation of having been observed by something intensely watchful.

They dwell in the thresholds of this place—where the ragged stone meets the stale pockets of moisture or where the cracks in the plateau slip into deeper hollows. Some individuals favor the shallow pools that appear near the cliffs; others tuck themselves into the clefts where tiny patches of lichen cling. They move as though partly immune to the biting cold and the inert time, navigating corridors of stasis with uncanny ease. Though the wind is punishing to any unshielded form, these creatures navigate stiff gusts as if slipping through a seam. Their steps do not register as footprints; they leave behind merely a faint rearrangement of dust and ice crystals—a sign you might only notice if you have studied the ground long enough to know what is natural and what is not.

Occasionally, you may see what looks like a cloak of translucent membranes draped over a hunched back. Then you’ll glimpse another shape that moves quadrupedally, though not quite like any known animal—its limbs bend at angles slightly too sharp, retracting and extending in slow, ritualized patterns. Once in a rare while, you might even catch the glint of eyes, reflections floating in the haze, but they do not flash with typical warmth. Instead, they hold a dull luminescence—the faint impression of a distant star, half lost in a sky that refuses to let time press forward.

Their manner of existence suggests that sound has no role in their communication. The roar overhead obliterates voices, so these beings appear to communicate through the subtlest shifts in stance or posture, maybe even changes in how the shadows fall on their shoulders or how the pale lichen-green momentarily stains their stone-like skin. You sense that entire conversations flow in the intervals of mist that ripple across them, brightening or dulling in waves that an untrained human eye could scarcely interpret. The environment, locked in a perpetual hush of roaring wind, seems to facilitate these silent signals. It’s as if each wisp of condensation or swirl of dust is part of a massive, wordless language written across the plateau.

Sometimes, when the horizon warps and tears of shimmering air expose slivers of something beyond comprehension, these secretive dwellers react. You might catch sight of them kneeling, or perhaps folding themselves into a compact shape—almost a bow. It’s unclear if they fear these illusions or revere them. But in that moment, they become more visible, outlines more distinct against the bleaching gloom, as though they’re caught between two realities and briefly forced into clarity. In the next heartbeat, the shimmer fades and the creatures melt back into the swirl of moisture and rock. They might leave behind the echo of a form in your vision, an afterimage of a crouching figure that resonates like a half-forgotten dream.

On rare occasions, these beings gather near the broken remains of the stone huts. They cluster close, pressing themselves into corners where the walls still stand waist-high, their bodies half-lost in the rubble. Some appear to be carefully examining the fragments of bone sometimes found there. They lift the pieces with elongated fingers and hold them against the dim light, as though searching for memory in the marrow. A hush deeper than the roar swallows the moment; you sense the weight of something ancient, a ritual that’s repeated many times before. Perhaps these bones belonged to their ancestors, or to intruders from different worlds, or to an even stranger lineage—a clue about where the plateau leads beyond the boundaries of normal rules.

It is unclear if they eat in any way you can recognize. The lichen is too scarce to support many creatures. The scattered droplets of water seem too meager to sustain life outright. Yet each inhabitant appears as a concentrated knot of presence, as if it draws sustenance from the suspended energy of this place—feeding, perhaps, on the timelessness itself. The plateau’s stasis could be the source of their vitality; trapped in a land where time barely moves, they have adapted to endure in a kind of parallel drift, as though their life cycle itself has become unbound from the usual flow of hours and years.

Observing one individual for long is nearly impossible: the illusions shift, the roar swallows clarity, and you find your orientation wobbling. Just when you think you’ve pinned down a shape—perhaps a thin torso glimpsed through the haze—it slips out of view, replaced by rolling fog and refracted light. You might only realize later that you have indeed seen multiple creatures, their forms slightly different each time. Some are taller, spindlier, with limbs that taper to delicate points; others are squat, with broad shoulders and draped membranes that might be a form of insulation or camouflage. Yet all share the same quiet synergy with the environment, as if they calibrate themselves to the plateau’s unchanging equilibrium moment by moment.

A final enigma settles on your mind when you consider how they came to be here at all—were they always part of this place, coalesced out of the same primordial rock and frost? Or did some great migration deposit them on the plateau long ago, leaving them as vestiges of a time when these heights were different, when the air was more forgiving, or the roar less overwhelming? Perhaps they intended to depart but never found a way through the fracturing boundaries. Or maybe they’ve chosen the stasis willingly, preferring a suspended existence where memory never fades and the present never races away.

They leave behind no artifacts or markings beyond those half-ruined huts—if those huts are indeed theirs. It could be that their tools and relics, if ever they had any, were eroded to nothing in the eons before time froze. Or maybe they build with intangible materials, rewriting the haze and illusions into ephemeral structures that collapse when looked at too closely. In that sense, these creatures exist as living mysteries, guardians or refugees from forces not entirely bound to linear reality.

Should you find yourself on this plateau for more than a fleeting hour, you might forge a kind of mutual, silent acknowledgment with them. In the motionless hush, broken only by the roaring wind and the unseen rumble in the firmament, you may catch a small tilt of a head or a minor shift in posture that feels like curiosity or even acceptance. But such recognition is fleeting. The moment you attempt to move closer, the illusions tighten their grip, and the watchers sink back into the gloom, blending seamlessly into the stone and time-locked air.

And so these denizens remain—inseparable from the plateau’s unyielding stasis, shaped by the same invisible forces that have ground the stone into its final form and frozen every shred of time in place. They are bound here as observers or guardians, or perhaps as prisoners of equilibrium. You walk away not entirely certain whether you have truly seen living creatures or merely the plateau reflecting fragments of your own awareness against its mist and unending roar. Yet the memory of their presence lingers like a secret knowledge: someone or something endures here, in this cradle of wind and stone, outside of time’s usual dominion, steadfast through weeks or centuries or eternity—as permanent and unchanging as the roar in the heavens.
