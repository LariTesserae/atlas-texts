They are human in basic contours—tall or short, rangy or stocky—yet when you catch them in profile, there’s an elegance about their silhouettes, as though time spent amid these airy elevations refined their forms. The people move lightly, without seeming hurried. Their limbs possess a certain fluidity, as if they’ve learned through generations how to distribute weight on precarious stone ledges. Seeing them at work in the city, you notice their poise: a child balancing a woven basket of ferns with nothing but a single hand on the rim, or a laborer carrying a newly chiseled block of stone while sidestepping an unexpected curling root.  

The inhabitants dress in garments spun from coarse but well-made fibers—cloudy grays, muted ochres, soft olive greens that echo the subdued palette of rock and slope. The cloth is woven so sturdily that if you brush it with your hand, it almost feels like the ridged bark of an ancient tree. Occasionally, families with more means—or who have honed their weaving craft across many generations—demonstrate subtle pattern-work in their clothes: faint geometric lines in black or russet, or simple repeating motifs reminiscent of the ridges and terraces that shape their world.

Their hair tends toward darker shades, often cropped short so as not to get in the way of physical labor, though some wear it wound into thick braids or miniature topknots, practical styles that keep the fine mist from clinging perpetually to their scalps. Their skin color runs a broad range, but always with a faint dusting of earth tones, as though the environment itself has infused them. High cheekbones or broad foreheads sometimes give them a stately look, yet their eyes—deep-set and calm—suggest an acceptance of this place’s hush.  

You see that they live simply but methodically. The daily rhythm here follows a patient cycle: a morning gathering at the cisterns to collect water, a midday meal shared outdoors, an evening lull when the sky’s light shifts so subtly it’s barely perceptible. One group might head to quarry stone, painstakingly cutting blocks to maintain their homesteads or slowly replace worn sections of the walls. Another group might venture onto the plateau’s fringes in search of wildgrowth—fungi in the damp places, bright berries in the vine-laced gullies, or fresh leaves from the ferns that burst forth in shady pockets.

In the nights, they gather within wide courtyards under that perpetual twilight. Small braziers, fueled with bundles of resinous wood, cast flickering amber glows. Yet the darkness never fully settles—there’s an odd subdued quality to the sky, as if it’s determined to remain faintly lit. So each courtyard conversation occurs in a half-lit hush, voices carrying quietly along the curves of stone. Storytelling is more an exchange of thoughtful recollections than a lively feast of songs; still, there is warmth in these gatherings. Elders speak of how the city was formed—perhaps guided by the shapes of the ridges themselves, perhaps drawn by the endless trickle of water seeping into the basin. Their grandchildren listen with a patient curiosity that never quite blossoms into outright wonder, as though they too feel they have all the time in the world.

From a distance, the population can feel reticent, almost secretive. Yet as you linger, you begin to notice they have no reason to rush toward new acquaintances. In a place where each hour is akin to the last, and where tomorrow will scarcely differ from today, they proceed at a measured pace in forging bonds. Hospitality surfaces in gestures rather than words: a shared cup of the mild, leafy infusions they brew in heavy clay vessels; a quiet demonstration of a weaving technique, or a guiding hand placed lightly on a traveler’s elbow to show the safest path across a slick rock ledge.  

Their language, too, is something of a mystery. Its arc and cadence flow gently, shaped by soft consonants and drawn-out vowels, reminiscent of a breeze that never quite becomes wind. You might spot them conversing near the water channels, exchanging a deliberate series of syllables and subtle gestures—perhaps the slight tilt of a head, the faint shift of posture. Strangely, although they speak in quiet tones, the words carry well in the natural acoustics of those stone corridors. They produce no loud exclamation, no abrupt shouting. Even children’s laughter has a more mellow ring.

As for their beliefs, they behave as though the land itself is perpetually mid-breath: they thank the rock for its stability, the clouds for safe shade, and the water for its unceasing generosity. You might find small earthen shrines nestled in corners where cracks in the stone walls allow a trickle of water to gather in a shallow dish. Around the city are scattered little tokens—woven twigs, shaped stones, and tiny offerings—that speak to an understated reverence for the land. And yet, to call it worship would be too strong; it’s more a cordial, ongoing partnership.  

A few individuals claim a deeper insight into the city’s hidden rhythms. These wise-ones, or watchers, seem to command no formal hierarchy, but people quietly respect their words. They read faint cracks in boulders or note delicate changes in the echo of water flow—like watchers expecting some slow-shifting omen. Occasionally, you see them pressing their ear to the rock, listening for a tone or resonance that the rest barely perceive.  

And then there are children—running through narrow lanes, playing some game of hide-and-seek along half-hidden terraces. Strangely, you never see them grow from year to year; they remain the same size, you think, though the youngest ones must age at least to a point, else how did they learn to speak so fluently? The older inhabitants rarely speak of it, but they exude a steady acceptance, a faint sense that the flow of time here doesn’t require commentary. Perhaps that’s why the elders laugh so softly whenever a visitor tries to question how old someone might be.

Animals, too, are part of their households—not inside, necessarily, but always near. The catlike creatures, lean and tuft-eared, seem comfortable winding around a family’s ankles at one moment and vanishing into the vines the next. The goats that scale rocky outcrops appear more agile than any mountain breed one might find elsewhere, as though centuries of existence at these high altitudes have honed their capacity to cling to sharp edges with near-spiderlike dexterity. And the pigeon-like birds, sometimes kept in lofts atop stone towers, provide eggs and occasional meat, though the city-dwellers never seem to overharvest, taking only enough to meet daily needs.

Despite all this, you notice an unsettled feeling that occasionally glints behind their calm eyes. It’s hinted at when an elder peers at the sky a moment too long, or when a laborer lingers beside an unfinished stone block. It’s as if on rare occasions, they remember they are caught in a stillness the rest of the world doesn’t share. Some hush in their gaze suggests a longing to feel time’s forward push—yet they do not speak of it openly, nor do they abandon the city. Maybe the sense of stability has become as essential to them as breath.  

Legend holds (in hushed whispers) that a handful tried to leave, climbing down the plateaus to the valley below, only to return within a season, changed. Their footsteps slower, their eyes holding a deeper hush. They never speak openly of why they came back, nor what happened in the lowlands. Some suggest that beyond these ridges, time moves freely, causing a disorienting rush for those who’ve known only this perpetual noontide hush. Others spin rumors that the city itself summons them home, that its walls and walkways thrums with a subtle gravity, tugging them back.

In day-to-day life, though, routine prevails. You see them patiently carving new spoons from scraps of timber, patching cracks in the thatch, re-lashing rooftops to weather the next drifting fog. Children chalk shapes onto the walls—strange geometric patterns, half-lost within a week when the dampness washes them away, only for new shapes to appear the following day. A woman stirs a large pot of soup made from leafy greens and goat’s milk, stirring slowly for what feels like hours. She invites passersby to share a bowl, as if mealtime itself is unbound by hurry.

Even if you linger here for days, you sense the inhabitants’ calm acceptance of your presence. They do not pry or pester. They offer simple gestures of friendship, then leave you to wander. Their tolerance is spacious; they have no reason to crowd or conflict. Where else is one supposed to go in this realm that never seems to end or begin? In this suspended existence, curiosity happens at its own pace, and each new face is merely another silent ripple in a well of unbroken water.

When you finally depart, if you choose to do so, they watch with neither sadness nor relief—only that gentle caution. And in their glances, you might read a quiet, haunted question: Why would anyone want to leave a place so still, so serene, even if it is touched by strangeness? Or perhaps, amid their mild farewell, they wonder whether leaving is truly possible.  

And so they remain day after day, year after year (if years indeed pass here at all), cutting stone, spinning cloth, tending to their modest herds, whispering to water shrines, building family bonds across eons that never quite mark themselves. Each person precisely, perpetually suspended in that cyclical hush, joined in an unspoken accord with the high ridges and the drifting clouds. They are people of the plateau—enduring, patient, neither fully dreaming nor fully awake—and though their city hums with quiet industry, one can’t help but sense a spell cast over them: a timelessness that they wear like a second skin, a weightless chain that both nurtures and ensnares.
