They do not have a single name for themselves—no need—but others might call them the Plateau Folk. They are a small community, and at first glance, they could pass for any group of mountain-dwelling people: lean-limbed from climbing steep slopes, arms strong from hauling baskets of rock and timber. Their hair often hangs in wet ropes around their faces, damp from the unending mist, but they hardly seem to notice. Their clothes are a patchwork of woven fibers and crudely tanned hides, carefully stitched together to hold up against the ceaseless spray and the gusting winds.

Those who visit might be surprised by their calm. There is no sense of hurry in them. If one catches them in daytime, they move with a deliberate and untroubled pace, going about chores that seem timeless—mending ropes, polishing copper chisels, gathering the thick mosses to set into the walls for insulation. At sunrise, several might convene to watch the light crack through the swirling clouds, gazing outward across the endless ridges below. At dusk, they softly hum or chant in small circles, their voices echoing in the plateau’s open spaces, mingling with the roar of water. Yet they never appear hurried; they do not scold children or shuffle others along. Time flows languidly here, shaped by the day’s changes in weather rather than mechanical schedules.

Though their dwellings may seem primitive—built from large, hand-fitted stones and roofed with tightly thatched reeds—there is a tidy elegance to them. The stone walls are etched with looping, spiraling designs as though to map the movement of wind eddies or swirling water. These etched lines sometimes converge in a motif reminiscent of the waterfall’s plunge. One might press a palm against these designs and feel a faint vibration from deep within the rock, as if the lines have captured the mountain’s hum. It is unclear whether the villagers inscribe these patterns purely for art or if they believe in some deeper resonance. When asked, they merely smile and tap the stone softly, as if to say, “Listen. Feel.”

Every household has a central hearth, though none burn large fires. The air here is always cool and damp, but smoke is unwelcome—it clings stubbornly, swirling with the fog. Instead, they burn small lumps of peat or twisty vines that smolder gently, giving just enough heat to ward off the mountain’s chill. One can see a faint glow emanating through thin doorways at night, the flickering orange light joining the hush of swirling water and the low moan of shifting mountain stone.

They raise no livestock. Instead, they harvest from the plateau’s ephemeral bounty: plump succulents that store water in their leaves, mushrooms that sprout in the moss-laden crevices, and hardy vines that produce bright, bitter berries. From these, they prepare warm broths that have a surprisingly hearty flavor, or ferment them into tangy drinks. Sometimes, the more daring among them trek down the mountain’s slopes to trade with lower settlements—exchanging bundles of dried mosses and small hammered-copper implements for salt, thin-spun cloth, or sweet honey. Yet these journeys are infrequent, as though the Plateau Folk only grudgingly acknowledge the world below.

Apart from the handful of humans, there appear to be others. When the fog grows thick, silhouettes move where no human figure should be—gliding along precarious ledges or scaling wet rock faces with unsettling ease. Shadows vanish into a swirl of vapor as soon as one tries to look more closely. An observer might assume these are the same goatlike creatures glimpsed now and then—but a second glance reveals something else: more fluid in motion, sometimes bipedal, sometimes not. Some among the villagers speak of them in hushed tones, calling them “those who never fell,” or “the watchers,” though whether these watchers are spirits, beasts, or something in between is uncertain. Occasionally, the watchers emit low calls carried on the wind, a sound that resonates as a pent-up chord—a rush of longing or warning or both.

The children of the plateau do not fear these watchers; instead, they once in a while leave small tokens—twine-wrapped shells, painted stones, or carefully braided bits of mountain grass—at the edges of half-formed chasms or near the swirling patterns etched into walls. In return, on rare occasions, a child might find a single bright stone resting on a doorstep at sunrise, or a curious swirl of water left behind in a shallow dish that glimmers strangely, as if lit from within. The villagers are discreet about such matters, but they smile knowingly and whisper that the mountain has many inhabitants, not all of which can be seen plainly.

Indeed, the plateau itself seems to have birthed more than just human dwellers. If one sits quietly among the spongy moss at twilight, they might glimpse small, translucent amphibian-like shapes that cling to wet stone. Their eyes, reflecting faint silver in the dimness, blink slowly as if trying to puzzle out the presence of an intruder. Hardly larger than a person’s palm, these creatures nudge aside the moss in search of hidden insects. When startled, they slip into pools of water so quietly that one questions whether they were ever there.

All the living things that endure these slopes—including the villagers—share a certain harmony with the relentless flux around them. They know the plateau is not static. Land crumbles away one day, and new ridges form the next. Truth be told, the people themselves seem to be waiting for something, as though they arrived too soon in a place not fully set. Perhaps they wait for the mountain to finish choosing its shape, or for more of their kin to find the half-formed paths. Their gatherings in the courtyard are subdued, and their fires at night are muted—because the real spectacle belongs to the waterfalls, the rolling mists, and the trembling bedrock that occasionally hums with the voice of deep earth.

They cultivate terraces in the few flatter expanses, stooping over the wet ground to coax out edible tubers. They harvest them gently, almost ceremonially, returning leftover vines and cuttings to the soil. Some ancient tradition, passed among them by the elders, dictates that nothing must be taken too quickly, and anything borrowed from the plateau should be returned in some measure. Their old stories, told late at night, revolve around the interplay of mountain and water—how, in primeval times, the mountain rose from a drowned world, and water was the memory of that ocean. In their myths, every droplet that flows off the cliff is a piece of the plateau’s spirit, carried away into distant realms and eventually returning as swollen clouds, drifting back to rejoin the cycle.

Many have a gentle reverence for the resonance that seems ever-present. Often, the villagers take a moment to pause in their daily tasks—perhaps leaning a copper adze against a rock, or stilling a spinning rope—to tilt their heads and listen. A hush falls. The waterfall’s roar is there, the wind’s cry weaving through it, and underneath, that low, barely audible tone as though the stone itself has a pulse. Now and again, those notes seem to swell in unison, sending a small thrill beneath the skin. The villagers might softly hum, matching pitches until all the sound melds together in a brief, haunting chord. The hum then dissipates, and life goes on as quietly as before.

Yet for all their stone houses and swirling carvings, the Plateau Folk remain elusive. Their number is small enough that it can be easy to believe the settlement is only half-inhabited, that some of the buildings stand empty. In truth, some families dwell behind the waterfall itself, in hidden grottoes softened by greenish light and constant spray. Others nestle in secret nooks carved from raw stone or perched on ridges above precarious drops. Every so often, the entire settlement might gather beneath the open sky for a ritual or festival—when the mists are less thick and the clouds part like drapes. The sounds of their chanting magnify in the broad courtyard, and for a moment, the emptiness of the place is filled by their voices. Then, in a flash, a bank of windblown fog wraps the plateau, and the villagers recede into their huts and hideaways again.

They are human—and yet they are something more, or something apart. Their faces show a sort of timeless acceptance, as if they have lived a dozen lives with the mountain already. Their voices are quiet but resonate distinctly against the stone. Their eyes carry the reflection of water and swirling clouds, holding a subdued glow at candlelit gatherings. In the early morning, the older ones step forth to greet the day, arms raised to the drifting vapor, lips forming silent greetings to the watchers on the cliffs or to the watchers within the stone.

In the end, who lives here? Humans, yes—folk who have mastered a fragile existence in a place still deciding whether or not it wishes to support life. Watchful shapes among the rocks, half-seen and never fully explained. Tiny amphibian creatures that flit in the pools. And the mountain itself—an entity of shifting stone and resonant hum—deserves to be counted among the living presences. The plateau is not merely backdrop; it is intricately woven into their daily survival and their beliefs, a slow and steady heartbeat that matches their own.

So they remain, perched on that damp threshold between water and sky, forging a patient bond with a land still in flux. They polish their etched stones, gather their crops of glistening vines, and raise soft chants to whatever power binds the mist, the mountain, and their humble dwellings. One day, perhaps the mountain will finish its grand shaping, and the watchers may step forward to stand among them openly. Until then, the Plateau Folk live in reverent partnership with this primal place—quiet stewards of a world on the cusp of becoming.
