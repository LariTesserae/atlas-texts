They are neither wholly organic nor mechanical, but occupy a frontier in between, born of the unearthly meeting of mineral depths and the ever-present hum. At first glance—if one can see anything in the dim—they seem like restless shapes flickering through steam and cold precipitation, glittering in the glow of crystal outcrops or luminous vapors. Yet the longer one looks, the more distinctly they emerge, until the rocky flats seem alive with countless denizens, each uniquely adapted to the rhythmic pulses beneath the surface.

Life swirls thick around the fissures. Near the narrower cracks—where the water seeps the slowest—the smaller, more delicate beings flourish. One might see clusters of tiny, centipede-like creatures whose segments are translucent enough to show faint pulses of shifting coloration. Each pulse ripples from head to tail, or sometimes from tail to head, in time with the inaudible tremors that pass through the bedrock. By day (if day can truly be said to dawn here), they cluster in damp shadows, nibbling the edges of pale fungal ridges. Their mouthparts look curiously like crystals that can scrape, dissolving the fungus’s outer surface into a nutrient slush that they suck in. Closer inspection reveals how they anchor themselves with chitinous hooks—these hooks have a faint metallic sheen, clicking on the stone as they scramble about. When threatened, they lock onto a crack’s edge and vanish into the depths, as though pulled by unseen currents.

Occasionally, from below, larger creatures ascend through the cracks—a swirl of living fluid. At first they appear to be shapeless masses, each one about the size of a human head or torso, eddying in water that drips from the rock. But as they rise and rest on the surface, they sculpt themselves into rough-limbed forms. It’s as if they coalesce only once they meet the open air, their surfaces taking on the same mineral patina as the wet stone around them. Travelers who observe these entities for any length of time realize with a shiver that each has an almost clockwork regularity to its motions—the limbs extend and contract in repeating cycles, distributing collected sediment across their fluid bodies. These sediment layers crystallize rapidly, forming plates or spines that shift position to capture the next wave of mineral-laden moisture. When the arcs of steam from the milky pools flicker overhead, these creatures absorb the glow and refract it, temporarily shining like living lanterns in the gloom.

Looming close to the water pools, the largest denizens claim the surface as their territory. Hunched forms, chest-high and broad, with plating that looks as though it’s made from plates of basalt or blue-gray granite. Their forelimbs end in wide, shovel-like appendages ideally suited for sloshing water and scraping up silt. Their heads (if one can call them heads) fuse into thick ridges of rock-like exoskeleton, and they emit low, grinding bellows that resonate in harmony with the subterranean drone. Observers might guess that these bellows help them navigate the land’s hidden depths of frequency—like whales in a mineral ocean, attuned to far more than the physical eye can see. Periodically, they stop whatever they’re doing, tilt their mass in place, and seem to “listen,” perhaps tuning in to a wave or pulse that traveled from the core. Then, with mechanical certainty, they plod on, assigning themselves some obscure, never-ending task.

In the shallow channels where water runs more swiftly and forms rivulets, one finds serpentine creatures—silvery, elongated bodies that slip between half-submerged stones. Their movement is stealthy and continuous, each creature gliding with the sound of soft metal dragging over glass. In fleeting glimpses one might notice a swirl of opalescent color within their scales, an organic sheen suggesting a chemistry that defies easy categorization. Bend too close and these serpents hiss—an airy exhalation that resonates in water and rock simultaneously. The hiss, oddly, carries an undertone of chiming, as if a miniature bell had been struck deep underwater. Once again, it is that same rhythmic signature: the environment breathes, and these serpentine hunters move in sync, siphoning subtle energies from the mineral-laden flows and, on rarer occasions, snatching smaller crustacean creatures in a fluid whip of motion.

Above, in the chill-laden gloom, the winged forms are more ephemeral still. Some flash across the flattened horizon like living sparks, their wide membranes reflecting any available luminescence. Their wingbeats create percussive bursts that echo off cracks, and those echoes can loop back upon themselves—an auditory kaleidoscope that adds to the mechanical chorus. Others, with “feathers” that resemble soot-coated metal filaments, ride the thermals of rising gas bubbles. Their heads have beak-like protrusions that open to produce quick, metallic shrieks. Often, the sound rings back in strange harmonics—hearing them is like listening to an alien wind chime. Sometimes the birdlike creatures engage in short, screeching duets, weaving echoes of each other’s calls. Observers might see them cluster around pockets of shimmering vapor, apparently feeding on the flares of energy produced when those vapors collide with the freezing air. 

Near the fungal or crystalline outcrops that glow faintly, there exists a different kind of life-form, which scarcely moves at all. They look like living columns or spires, draped with layered, semi-translucent membranes. At first, they could be mistaken for stationary mineral deposits. But watch one for a while, and you’ll see the slow, creeping shift of its outer membrane as it absorbs the dim glow from the fungi or crystal growth. Occasionally they tilt or bend, sending ripples through the entire structure, as though they absorb the constant hum from below and convert it into some slow, methodical motion. Tiny offspring—smaller versions of themselves—sometimes bud from the main column, dropping onto the ground and crawling away like snail-paced lumps of gel, seeking new cracks where they, too, might root themselves in the damp stone.

Far below, in the recesses of those humming fractures, a stranger category of inhabitant waits in partial dormancy. They’re seldom glimpsed except when seismic or energetic surges momentarily crack open deeper channels. Then one might see an enormous, labyrinthine network of filaments or cables stretching out beneath the rock surface, glowing with dull luminescence. These cable-like growths pulse gently in time with the environmental drone, bridging the gap between pure geology and alien biology. Some suspect these might be the true “brains” of this realm: linked to everything on the surface, quietly shaping and reshaping life in a silent conversation with the pulsing rock. Rather than controlling from a singular seat of power, this subterranean web exists as life’s framework, a wide neural net that senses impulses across the domain and orchestrates the slow dance above.

That is the overarching nature of life here: every creature, from the smallest translucent scuttler to the massive, lumbering plate-beasts and the drifting, shrieking “birds,” partakes in the place’s grand, unending performance. This is not a food chain in any typical sense. It’s more like a cyclical system of frequencies, each form playing a unique chord of existence and exchanging energies with the rest. The underlying drone feeds them, and in turn, their movements become the audible face of that engine’s heartbeat. There is no hierarchy as we’d perceive it. Instead, there is an unfathomable synergy of fungal crystals, living fluid, plated colossi, serpentine swimmers, aerial shriekers, and deep-rooted networks—an orchestra, each member attuned to the next through pulses and hums.

From a distance, one might imagine these beings as pieces in a vast industrial process, laboring without guidance on tasks unknown. Yet up close, it becomes evident that they are simply the expressions of the environment’s inexorable power—the product of primeval energies that have folded stone and water together into an ongoing genesis. They survive by the same mysterious rule that shapes the cracks in the rock, that draws moisture from the gloom, and that churns out luminescent gasses. No one entity here dominates or subdues the others; rather, they coexist organically, emanations of something older than time.

So they persist, these dwellers of the machine-like land, built of resonant pulses and primordial chemistry. In every rivulet, every milky pool, and every swirl of vapor from the cold crust, one sees them pulsing or gliding or continuing in their silent, purposeful cycles. Each part of the landscape teems with them. And as the environment’s colossal hum rises and falls, these life-forms adapt and evolve in harmony. Stars may glimmer faintly overhead, but down here, beneath the roiling clouds and the endless fissures, existence follows the droning heartbeat of stone and water, worlds away from any sunlit domain. They are a living testament to the power of an ancient cycle—a life-cycle without apparent beginning or end, forever humming through cold stone, slick surfaces, and half-seen shapes that gather at the edges of vision. Their essence is the hum itself, incarnate and unbroken, echoing through the fissures of a place that shapes its own inhabitants out of time’s immeasurable remnants.
