At first, you see only impressions of them: fleeting movements in the densest tangles, slight shifts in the layered moss, a trembling in the trunk-thick vines. They move in the corners of your sight, stirring the air just enough to send a shudder through the humidity. In the hush of this place, they are a presence—some intelligence stirring among the fronds and creeping stems, impossible to spot unless you catch exactly the right angle of light.

They are usually silent. If you hear anything, it is a series of low clicking sounds, so quiet and infrequent that you could almost mistake them for the fracturing of ice-laced stems. But the hum you sense underfoot seems to intensify whenever they are near. They step lightly among the shallow rivulets, stepping stones of root and mulch that lead deeper into the gloom. Sometimes the vines themselves part for them, leaves curling aside to make a narrow corridor that closes up just as quickly after they pass. Then only the faint shimmer of moisture remains as evidence that something moved through.

You might glimpse a shape: tall, slender, draped in watery layers of fungal filaments, or stooped low, enmeshed in a coat of living moss. Some have tendril-like protrusions where arms might be; others have broad, spined growths sprouting from shoulders or crown-like hollows in their bark-surfaced heads. They seem an amalgam of plant and something else—a quiet, animate force that has no name in any human tongue. Their skin, if it can be called skin, is half bark, half chitin, half something pliable and luminous. In places, it looks as though there’s a dull metal sheen threaded into their flesh, an echo of that half-heard mechanism that pulses in the soil.

They live in and among the network of vines and epiphytes as though it were both cradle and fortress. It’s impossible to know if they were born here, or if they’re slowly grown—sprouted from pods that emerge from the breeding ground of decaying wood and hidden contraptions. Tiny gears and filamentous strings coil around them like living jewelry; some have hollow rods rising from their shoulders, gently chiming in the breeze. There’s a surreal grace to their movements, like they float rather than walk, carried forward by the plateau’s subtle vibrations.

Their purpose is unknown. Perhaps they tend the vegetation, fostering the swift expansions of vines one moment, pruning them the next. You might see them delicately peel away rotted growth from a trunk, only to feed it to a hollow near the roots. In return, new vines slither out from those hollows—fresh, glistening green that clings to stone and fungus in an instantaneous, unstoppable way. There’s a cyclical ritual to it all, a rhythm that synchronizes with the faint rumble beneath the ground.

When the plateau’s weather shifts and the drizzle cools to a ghostly frost, you may witness them gather in the gloom. Perhaps it’s for warmth, or perhaps they align themselves as part of an ongoing cycle of tasks. They cluster around the silvery shafts embedded in the forest floor—unthinking contraptions that whirr softly. Some figures extend slender, root-like limbs to connect with the machines, and a faint luminescence travels along cords of bark and filament. In these moments, you can almost see the plateau’s invisible heart beating in tandem with them, a synergy of mechanical hum and living resonance.

At times, they appear to vanish entirely: no movement in the undergrowth, no slight bending of the arching vines. You may walk for hours without seeing even a trace, until a spidery figure peeks from the gap between trunk and moss, gazing with an almost curious tilt of its strange head. Then, in a blink, it’s gone—absorbed by the green again. It’s unclear whether each individual has distinct consciousness or if they are part of a hive mind. The hush of the forest offers no clues; the creatures neither speak in any recognizable way nor leave any decipherable footprints.

Their eyes, if they have them, are glimpsed only as subtle glows: faint pinpricks of teal or gold in a tangle of leaves. Some say they do not see with eyes; they listen to the mechanical thrum below, or sense chemical signals traveling on moist breezes. Indeed, the entire plateau brims with silent messages: spores drifting, vines snapping to attention, mosses breathing in unison. This choir of living signals might be enough to guide them as they merge seamlessly into the tapestry of forest, stone, and machine.

When bursts of accelerated growth break out—those sudden storms of new vines and billows of spores—they are in the middle of it all. It appears they coax the plants to move faster, or at least they ride the wave of energy. Their limbs glow with ephemeral patterns, and the vegetation around them twitches and swells in response. Slender creepers lock together like interlaced fingers, forming a lattice of green that rears up, aided by the creatures’ quiet direction. If they do speak, it is through these transformations in the forest rather than with any audible words.

Nor does their existence end at mere caretaking. There’s a watchful aspect to them, as though they guard the plateau’s secrets—the submerged mechanisms or ancient energies buried here. Sometimes, in the distance, you’ll notice them poised by stony outcroppings, silent sentinels scanning the swirling clouds. Their presence feels like an extension of the plateau’s will: an embodiment of everything that’s hidden, mechanical, and powerfully alive.

They are not malevolent, but they are also not gentle by any human standard. One might see them wrest older growth aside to make room for new expansions, severing ancient vines with a swift motion that sends sap jetting like pale blood. They do this with a sacred neutrality, lacking remorse or satisfaction. It is just the cycle of growth, death, and renewal that perpetuates this cold, damp domain.

You sense that if something else—something foreign, perhaps even human—trespassed in a disruptive way, these beings would respond like the vines themselves: quickly, decisively, and without emotion. For them, everything is simply part of the plateau’s silent industry, the unstoppable progress of living machinery.

By night (or what passes for night under those shifting clouds), they glow faintly at rest, silhouettes blinking amid the gloom. You might hear a deeper, slow pulse beneath your own heartbeat, as though the plateau and all its inhabitants share one slumbering breath. The hush at these times can be so profound that the dripping rivulets seem deafening, each droplet a miniature waterfall echoing into emptiness. If you stay still long enough, you’ll see them shift in barely perceptible gestures, branches swaying over the mechanical contraptions in a lullaby of gear-clicking and vine-creaking.

Whether they are spirits of the place, biomechanical stewards, or simply the plateau’s plant-life taken to a strange new evolution, you cannot tell. They defy easy definition. They live in the cold drizzles, thrive on the spongy carpets of decaying growth, and utilize the pulse beneath the earth as a kind of hidden heartbeat. Whenever you catch them in that half-light, you know one truth: this plateau has shaped them, and in turn, they shape the plateau, in a grand cycle beyond the scope of human hands or reason.

And so, in the enveloping fog and swirling spore clouds, these watchers remain—the ephemeral architects and guardians of a living labyrinth. Machine-like yet organic, spawned by the hum of a subterranean power no one has ever fully explored. Root and gear, bark and metal, vine and wire, they form a single entity: the plateau’s silent, animating voice, always present, always guiding, always building and renewing. Here, no single shape can define them, for they are the life of the plateau itself—tireless, cold, patient, and utterly unwilling to yield.
