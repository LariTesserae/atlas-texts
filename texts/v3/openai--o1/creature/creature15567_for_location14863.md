They are not numerous—whatever remains here is scattered in pockets, like seeds in the cracks of a forgotten ruin. It would be wrong to speak of them all in one breath. Each resident has carved out a tiny foothold in this oppressive underbelly, living in uneasy partnership with the machinery that surrounds them. Some can’t be seen but only guessed at by the faint disturbances they cause: a shift in the condensation on the walls, a trembling cable that vibrates more than the slow mechanical hum could account for. Others reveal themselves if you linger long enough, though they do so tentatively, as if every motion could be their last.

Deep in the labyrinth, near warm junctions of thick piping, you might catch sight of a pale lizard-like thing, its skin glistening like damp chalk. It’s entirely hairless, built sleek to slip through the narrowest conduits, with wide, reflective eyes that shine back at any sudden glow from a flickering filament. When it breathes, its slender ribcage expands in quick, shallow pulses, accustomed to the stale, low-oxygen air. These creatures—let’s call them corridor drakes—nest behind panels torn loose decades ago, building small hollows from desiccated fungus fibers and bits of rusted metal they scavenge. Their diet is uncertain, but they’re known to snap up insects in the gloom, or occasionally nibble on the green fuzz clinging near steam vents. They vanish in an instant if you approach, leaving a ghostly ripple of motion behind them.

Elsewhere, in dripping hollows where thin fungal webs cling to rivets, dwell the rasp-footed harvesters: silent, spider-like creatures with elongated forelimbs. At a glance, they look mechanical themselves—each joint braced, each segment fused by some fractal geometry. The harvesters scuttle in slow arcs across the walls, collecting the pale filaments of fungus that grow in the dank corners. They pack these strands into a translucent abdomen that glows faintly with bio-luminescence, turning them into a dim lamp in the gloom. You might glimpse a cluster of faint lights high on a walkway, weaving in and out among the pipes. Whether they’re storing the fungus for food or for forging filaments into egg sacs is unclear; seldom does anyone get close enough to learn.

Certain corridors, narrower and half-flooded by leakage, host colonies of snail-like forms clinging to the wet metal. They’re slow, almost imperceptible in motion, with shells that mimic corroded rivets so perfectly that they’re sometimes left undisturbed by passing machine arms or the occasional corridor drake. These shells are tough but not quite metal, sheltering a slick, amphibious body adapted to both dryness and sudden pools of scalding water. The snails feed on traces of mineral deposits and whatever dripping condensation they can manage. When something larger draws near, they release a quiet hiss, reminiscent of steam, retreating very slowly into their shells.

And then there are the watchers, if you believe in them at all—larger silhouettes that roam the networks of scaffolds and catwalks in the biggest chambers. Occasionally you’ll hear metal stepping against metal, a hollow clang that ceases as soon as you hold your breath to listen more carefully. Some say these watchers are the last vestiges of whatever workforce once maintained the place—bipedal constructs or half-living drones that never received a shutdown command. Others whisper that they’re mere illusions of the heat shimmer, spied in flickering perimeter vision. But sometimes, if you stand very still in an upper chamber where the catwalk circles overhead and the lights struggle to ignite, you’ll catch the reflection of two orbs, like dull lanterns, peering down. And then they’re gone with a wrenching squeal of metal, as though the scaffolding itself lifted and departed.

In deserted corners far from the main arteries of pipes and walkway traffic, you might find the caretaker flora—a persistent swirl of pallid vines, so thin they resemble wires, threaded through rusted grates. At first glance, they seem to be inert cables left behind by the factory’s original designers, but if you touch one, it yields with a softness that metal would never have. In those chambers, time seems even more distorted, the air nearly unmoving. Over countless years, the vines have threaded deeper, bridging cracks between panels, so that if you watch carefully, you may see the entire length give a subtle twitch, as though it inhales the hot breath of the room. Some whisper that these plants have developed a quiet intelligence, responding to changes in temperature or the presence of new bodies. Proof of this is scarce, but the vines have made the place their own, forging a creeping tapestry that you sense is very much alive.

Small machines do remain—and though they aren’t exactly “alive” in the conventional sense, they fit seamlessly among the other inhabitants. They’re automated servitors or drones, built to maintain the labyrinth’s key functions. Their plating is scored with the scratches of decades, and their dotted lights blink like an insect’s eyes. Some stride on spindly legs to check the integrity of ancient control panels, while others roll along the floors, sucking up debris from the cracks. They almost never speak—if they did, it would be in ancient codes or the hush of wireless signals. Occasionally an echo of mechanical chirping reverberates off the walls, as though one machine calls to another from far away. Then, just as quickly, it fades. These caretaker drones appear single-minded, but who programmed them or why they remain active is lost in the gloom.

Finally, there is a presence that transcends these scattered living things, a slow, methodical force that might be the sum of all the machinery and dormant systems still cycling here. It resides behind walls, in the thrumming of the pipes, in the rhythm of the ventilation. Perhaps it isn’t a consciousness, exactly—more like the echo of design or a pattern given shape by centuries of hissing valves and rotating fans. Yet in the hush, you can sometimes feel it attend to you, as if the entire structure is aware of your footstep. The watchers on their catwalks and the corridor drakes in their hidden lairs serve as faint living ornaments to this old power, reminding you that, despite the decay, something still holds the labyrinth together.

So this is who remains. The place itself is alive with intangible presence, watched over by constructs that patrol out of habit or fading directive. Below, scurrying beasts and spindly harvesters claim narrow corridors, while hidden lizards and snail-like entities cling to metal surfaces gleaning moisture. Thread-like vines weave the architecture into living tapestry, and somewhere, behind layers of pipe, a caretaker hum pulses on. Together, they form an uneasy tapestry of life in the hot dark—a place where survival shapes itself to the labyrinth’s demands, where the old routines have outlived their masters, and every breath stirs a question: who maintains whom, and for what purpose?

Such questions, of course, have no ready answers. This is simply the way of things here, in the silence that thrums with distant machinery. A slow, shared life persists underground, thriving in neon gloom and rusting steel, clinging to the faintest possibility that tomorrow, the heat will still pulse, the pipes will still groan, and every living thing—mechanical or otherwise—will continue its watchful existence in the corridors’ lingering glow.
