They move through the murk with a kind of gentle purpose, blending so deeply into the damp hush that at first you barely notice them. But if you’re patient—if you let your eyes adjust to the labyrinth of living vines and soft gloom—you start to see outlines of figures among the twisted root architecture. It is difficult to say whether they are human, strictly speaking. The shapes shift: sometimes tall and sinuous, sometimes hunched and bestial, always draped in living fibers. Vines coil around their limbs, or weave into what could be clothing stitched from leaves. There is a sense that these beings grew out of the place itself, part-mechanical, part-organic—endless hybrids of city and forest.

They move in small groups, rarely alone. Often, they climb effortlessly along the living floors, as though these meshworks of roots were as firm as stone. In the gloom, you see faint glimmers where filaments wind around their torsos or throats—thin cables that pulse with dim electricity, suggesting they rely on a current that flows just beneath the bark-like surfaces. Sometimes, you catch a glimpse of mechanical additions: a metal brace integrated into a spindly arm; a chest plate of hammered steel, pitted with the same lacy pattern that vines carve into rock. Their eyes, if you can see them through the dark, gleam like low-burning lamps. When they breathe, there’s a soft hiss that mingles with the hush of vents and dripping water.

Their gestures are calm, patient. They seem to run silent checks on the half-hidden machinery: turning a valve on a corroded pipe or adjusting a cluster of living vines that has draped itself too snugly around a spinning cog. You get the sense they maintain this place in some quiet synergy. They don’t tear the vegetation away; they guide it, coax it, ensure it doesn’t choke off a conveyor belt or a panel of flickering lights. And in turn, the vegetation keeps them cocooned—offers them cover, nourishment, a circulatory system of watery sap that might be used in ways you can’t entirely fathom. The synergy is half cautious, half reverent.

They do have dwellings—or perhaps “dens” would be a better word. In the corners of the grand halls, in those in-between pockets where vines form hollow hemispheres, they’ve cultivated half-rooms. You notice irregular silhouettes: layered floors of knotted wood and metal where they gather. Sometimes you see interior walls that appear grown from broad leaves, soft as felt, forming spaces where these beings rest. Their silhouettes flicker against the gloom from within those dwellings, momentarily illuminated by the dull glow of a battery or the sudden crackle of a living filament that coils around a pillar.

And there are smaller ones, too—adolescent or childlike shapes that nearly vanish into the tapestry of leaves. They scamper through the tangle of vines overhead, sometimes riding along on thick, rope-like lianas that the older ones, with a series of subtle whistles, gently loosen or tighten. It’s possible these vines double as transport—makeshift elevators grown deliberately into a network of vertical highways. The children seem to know precisely which vine to tug or twist to get where they want to go, bounding from platform to platform with fluid grace.

You hear them communicate, though it rarely sounds like speech. Clicks and low hums bounce off the living corridors, accompanied by the whispered rustle of leaves. Occasionally there’s a metallic clink, like a small chime. They could be sending signals through the metal catwalks, perhaps tapping out codes with their feet. When intruders are near—fox-like creatures or bizarre insects that might eat through cables—these inhabitants utter a light hiss or a series of small percussive clicks. Then, just as silently, they disappear into the woodwork, their luminous eyes extinguishing as if on a dimmer switch.

Their purpose among the humming generators and half-lit platforms is layered: one moment you’ll see them testing the tension of vines that cradle a heavy steel vat, the next they’re examining a cluster of spore-like bulbs that have sprouted from an exposed pipe. Some carefully chip away vine overgrowth from a control panel, while others cup the dripping condensation from leaf funnels to sip or store in clay vessels. They might be forging new pathworks at the frontiers of the city-forest, massaging the fresh, fibrous bark into shapes that will eventually become scaffolds or walkways. They function almost like living architects, engineering spaces as the place continuously remodels itself.

Some among them seem more mechanical than organic—rare individuals with entire limbs replaced by intricate steel frames. You hear the whir of gears beneath flexible plating. Perhaps these were once part of the settlement’s original crew, kept alive by the biotech that took over. Others appear almost plantlike themselves: eyes shaped like seedpods, hair or fur replaced by trailing creepers that hang around their shoulders. Their skin merges with patterns of bark, as though it’s uncertain whether they were human at all—or if they sprang from the soil with an innate understanding of mechanical constructs.

You notice their ornaments, too—small trophies or tokens that dangle from belts of twisted vine. These could be shards of circuit boards, fragments of luminous crystals grown in the darkest recesses, or tiny carved charms made of root or bone. When the flicker of ephemeral light catches these tokens, they glow with subtle phosphorescence, as if storing the recycled energy of the place. Sometimes, in the hush, you hear them softly tinkling together, a quiet lullaby that resonates through the columns of organic fiber.

And what of their social patterns? When they gather in those “cathedral” spaces ringed by giant petals, you see them stand in near-stillness, heads inclined, as if listening for something profound at the threshold of hearing. Perhaps they commune with the place itself—the city-forest that flickers in and out of reality, shifting vines moment by moment. It’s as if they are attuned to that cosmic “glitch” you’ve witnessed: they sense when the floor might suddenly re-form or when the overhead canopy might warp aside. When the architecture flickers, you see them remain poised, unstartled, as though acknowledging a breathing organism’s unpredictable heartbeat.

Other lifeforms—those swift, mammalian shadows or the floating birdlike silhouettes—seem less “in control.” They scramble or flutter away when the glitch occurs. But these bipeds, or sometimes quadrupeds, calmly hold their ground. Perhaps the weird physics is part of their daily existence, weaving in and out of shape as fluidly as the city’s living floor.

They don’t show hostility unless threatened. If you watch from a respectful distance, they might allow your presence in the gloom. One of them—taller, with a broad collar of living leaf-lattice around its shoulders—might turn toward you. In their gaze, there’s curiosity without alarm. They’ll watch you for a moment, their vine-laden limbs nearly camouflaged against the architecture. Then, almost matter-of-factly, they’ll continue their careful, reverent tinkering: adjusting the next line of cables, checking the pulse of a battered steam valve, encouraging some fresh bark to grow into a shaped support.

Whatever they are—descendants of a lost industrial crew, or a new species birthed by the union of rusted machines and relentless vegetation—they thrive in the half-light. They do not conquer this space so much as guide it, living at the intersection of metal pylon and creeping root. Patient, primal, quietly humming with energy, they move in time with the slow pulse of the machinery and the green hush of nature. They are the keepers of a place still deciding what it wants to be, at one with the living forest’s dream of corridors and cathedrals, forging a civilization half-real, half-flicker—the inhabitants of a world constantly rewriting itself.
