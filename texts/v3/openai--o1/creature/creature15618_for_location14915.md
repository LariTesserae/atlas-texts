They are difficult to see at first—difficult even to acknowledge, in a space so full of other details. You might catch a flicker of movement behind a tangle of vines or sense the weight of wary eyes from the upper galleries. Only after you settle in, letting the damp heat seep into your clothes and breathing with the city’s measureless, sighing pulse, will you notice the ones who call this place home.

At first, you notice them as pale smudges in the distance, nearly blending in with steam-wreathed scaffolding or the slick surfaces of basalt columns. In the dim light, their outlines converge with the labyrinth of pipes, so you see only odd contours—a curved shoulder half hidden by a steel beam, a delicate hand resting on a valve’s wheel. When they move, they do so with unhurried caution, each step placed to avoid trembling the catwalk or sending echoes across the metal deck. It quickly becomes apparent that secrecy is not their goal; it’s simply their nature to act with minimal disturbance in a habitat where every sound reverberates. Their eyes, larger than a surface-dweller’s, reflect faint glints of light, revealing in an instant they’re adapted to perpetual twilight.

They vary in shape and size. Some are squat and broad-shouldered, their arms crisscrossed with sinewy muscle from hauling heavy materials: they might be the keepers of the machinery, those who maintain the pipes and pumps that feed the city’s slow heartbeat. Others are lanky and slight, almost brittle-looking in the gloom, slipping between columns of rock to check on condensation lines or fiddling with the valves that control steam flow. Their skin tones fall somewhere between alabaster and a faint veined gray. A few have short shock-like hair, while others keep the scalp shaved entirely, a practical necessity in the damp tunnels.

You see small clusters of them occasionally in the open concourses—families, or perhaps work teams—exchanging tools and murmuring in low voices that blend with the churn of distant machinery. On scaffolding landings, they trade clipped instructions; down near the floors where volcanic warmth pulses, they set up instrument stands or convey large metal containers of water to storage tanks. Their language is quiet, a series of soft clicks and vowels that you sense more than hear. Even when they speak an audible phrase, it floats and dissipates into the haze.

Alongside their industrial tasks, they are gardeners of sorts. The vines and broad-leafed plants—so omnipresent in this domain—receive delicate attention: these inhabitants occasionally kneel before a cluster of twisting roots, fingers exploring the soil to confirm moisture levels, or gently trim away leaves that have browned in the heat. Curious tools, half-engineered and half-organic, appear in their pouches: blades grown from gleaming bone, or trowels forged from old, battered scrap. The subterranean dwellers seem to consider the flora neither friend nor foe, but part of the same grand organism that the city itself inhabits.

Many structures built here—tall cylinders with thick windows and banks of blinking lights—are more than mere habitations; they are laboratories and workshops. It appears these folk are perpetually testing the water’s purity, calibrating the steam vents, and fiddling with the electrical grid that snakes overhead. One might see, on occasion, an open hatch in a building’s side, revealing interior rooms jammed with measuring devices, arcane instrumentation, and what look like both mechanical and biological experiments. The vines wind around these buildings too, and it’s unclear whether the dwellers have harnessed the foliage itself as part of their machinery. Some vines sprout luminous pods that sway overhead, lighting the labs in pulses of bio-luminescent green.

Others among them keep to the highest ledges, near the ceiling where the rock arches like a newly formed cavern. From there, you might see watchers—robed in layered strips of tanned hide or perhaps woven plant fiber. They carry rods with luminescent crystals affixed at one end. In the half-dark, it’s easy to confuse them for part of the jagged rock, but they move with slow grace, stepping carefully around condensation drips and ensuring no water collects in precarious pockets overhead. Whether they perform some ritual or simply guard against cave-ins is unclear. Their posture remains vigilant, heads turned slightly downward, scanning the city of pipes and vines as though reading the subtle signals of steam and illumination.

Occasionally, at intervals akin to the city’s slow respiration, communal gatherings occur—though “gathering” is too strong a word for what might merely be cyclical maintenance. You notice them converging at the base of a massive, central pipe—one that churns with geothermal breath. From hidden compartments, they retrieve irregular crystals, each swirling with captured energy. One by one, they fit these crystals into slots on the pipe’s exterior, aligning them with glyphic symbols etched into metal plating. Steam issues forth in measured bursts, and the watchers hum a guttural sequence of notes that reverberates eerily among the scaffold levels above. Some crystals glow, others remain dull, and the dwellers exchange them again, sliding them back into pouches or carrying them into the deeper recesses of the city. Then they disperse, returning to their quiet, individual routines.

While direct conversation with them is rare, you’ll sense an underlying empathy. If they come upon a traveler who’s clearly lost in the maze of corridors and catwalk-ladders, they gently offer direction—maybe by tapping a metal walkway or shining a dim, handheld lamp. Though cautious in nature, they are not hostile or aloof; instead, they behave like caretakers of an environment whose deeper intricacies they alone grasp.

For sustenance, they cultivate fungi in dim warrens lit by bio-luminescent pods. Long troughs of spongy growths anchor near the warmest vents, exhaling a musty scent that mingles with the city’s tang of metal and humid air. Bent figures stoop over these troughs, dressed in makeshift aprons. They gather up the fungi carefully, placing them into wide woven baskets that, once laden, are carried upstairs into communal kitchens. Now and then, a spiced aroma drifts through the corridors—the smell of these subterranean crops roasting, perhaps, or boiled into a thick, nourishing broth.

Other creatures walk or flutter here, too—small, winged enigma-birds that flicker past lamps, sporting translucent feathers that catch and scatter the faint light. They may have arrived long ago, drawn down natural vents that open to the surface. Many sport tufted, feeler-like antennae, used to navigate the dark with subtle pulses of echolocation. The underground people pay them no mind beyond the occasional affectionate glance, as though they’re simply another essential thread in this interwoven tapestry.

Even rarer, you glimpse something bigger, scuttling across a ledge: an insectoid shape with chitinous plates that glisten in the steamy air. The dwellers do not show alarm; they share the space with these creatures, stepping aside if a many-legged form chooses a walkway or pipe to traverse. Each occupant, whether winged or many-limbed, seems to abide by an unspoken code of coexistence.

Their city is thus a slow-churning synergy of metal and magma-heat, of leaf and loom, of hush and thought. You suspect this pace of life extends far beyond daily tasks: these denizens likely measure time in the shifting angles of vines, the growth of new fungal beds, the accumulation of each minuscule mineral deposit on the rock walls. Their technology, while industrial in appearance, resonates with a harmonic approach that sees living and mechanical processes as parts of a single system. Long ago, perhaps, they discovered a better equilibrium in these caverns than surface societies ever quite managed under the open sky.

When you do finally behold one of them fully, face to face, you’re struck by the gentleness in their features. Large, reflective eyes that blink slowly, as if adapting the conversation to the underlying tempo of the city. A faint sheen of condensation on their cheeks, an echo of the living humidity that saturates the chamber. A sense that, if you lingered here, you would slip into that same measured rhythm: step, breath, drip, hum. This is life underground—a place where time no longer rushes but settles into the living rock, where metal grows like a skeleton for the vines, where its denizens tend each system of pump and leaf with equal dedication.

They do not proclaim themselves with banners or call themselves by any single name. They exist, quietly, in concert with the ancient basalt columns and the newness of jagged ceilings—another layer in the strata of this vast subterranean realm. Watching them, you sense this city is not truly built for spectacle or glory; it is shaped for balance. For them, existence here is perfectly ordinary. The hush that pervades every pathway is not an omen of emptiness but of abiding presence—these dwellers are simply part of the deep hush, breathing with it and keeping its slow pulse alive. And if you remain long enough, your heartbeat, too, may align itself with that soft, unhurried thrum, becoming one more chord in the still and vital symphony beneath the earth.
