They dwell in layers—creatures upon creatures, woven into a dense tapestry of clacking limbs and dark, leathery shapes. Much of the life here remains at least partly hidden, existing in the interstices of rock strata or huddled under the minimal shelter of a basalt overhang. At a first glimpse, the inhabitants appear uniform: scuttling exoskeletal beasts, all barbed legs and jointed thoraxes. But further watching reveals subtle differences in color, texture, and movement—a dozen species, maybe more, each adapted to a nuanced niche in this blasted place.

Closer inspection shows that many of these exoskeletal forms carry a dull glow in the seams of their segmented plating. It’s not a bright glow—just a deep, rust-hued luminescence that seeps between the cracks of their armor whenever their bodies flex. It might be the residual heat they’ve absorbed from the mineral-rich stone, or perhaps an alien form of chemical phosphorescence. Some scurry in the open, scraping at the ridges to pry loose motes of nutrient-laced deposits. Others cling to vertical planes as if gravity hardly matters, poised upside down with their legs splayed wide, occasionally swiveling their mandibles to taste the air.

Beneath them, stirring more slowly, are the squat, leathery-fleshed creatures—their hides pocked with small vents or folds that can close like gills in the dust-laden environment. These lumps move as if waking from a centuries-long slumber. Their limbs, if they have any, remain half-tucked beneath their soft mass. Instead of walking, they heave themselves forward in short, slow pulses, a motion reminiscent of monstrous slugs. When threatened, they can swell up, revealing banded ridges of chitin hidden under the pliable outer skin. Some of their bodies appear marked by scars or patterned ridges—evidence of past conflicts with the exoskeletal swarms or of the daily rigors of scorching dryness. Often, they remain dormant until some instinct—for food, for mating, or for defense—coaxes them into sluggish action.

Then there are the creatures that rarely show themselves, known only by fleeting glimpses of strange appendages or the whisper of movement in the tunnels. Small, many-eyed heads emerge in the cracks, bristling with hairlike sensors that taste the swirling air. Others come in needle-like shapes compressed into long columns, rippling like centipedes as they vanish down subterranean corridors. Even deeper within the hollows, one might find creatures layered in fibrous cocoons, as though spun from the very mats of filament clinging to the stone. Their exoskeletons appear fused with those fibrous excretions, so that they seem part creature, part environment. Occasionally, they detach themselves from the tunnel walls and move en masse, a living patch of the supposed “mineral fungus.”

Despite these variations, nearly all of them share one overriding trait: an acute sense that replaces the absent daylight. They navigate by vibrations, chemical traces, and minute shifts in temperature. To see them in their element is to watch them pick up on signals invisible to human senses. A subtle rise in heat from deep within the cracks might send a wave of exoskeletal foragers scuttling to glean fresh mineral deposits. A sudden hush in the constant scraping might warn the leathery lumps to seal themselves tight, as if bracing for a predator’s approach. In the swirling gloom, these creatures seem to communicate in a hush of clicks and gentle tapping on stone, attuned to each other in ways we can only guess at.

Many of the exoskeletal creatures form moderate groupings—the low arches of fused stone are riddled with hives of them. They build out from old basalt remnants, adding secretions that harden into surprisingly delicate, honeycomb-like structures. From a distance, these hodgepodge “buildings” look worn and natural, mere lumps in the stony ridges. But up close, one can see rows of alveolar cells, each hosting eggs or storing half-crystallized minerals gleaned from the scorching environment. Young hatch in pockets of this honeycombed architecture, feeding on a gelatinous residue that their elders may exude. And so an entire generation rises to scratch its way across the rock spires.

Meanwhile, the leathery lumps appear less communal—most keep to themselves or form small, sluggish congregations. When two lumps encounter one another, they circle in a languid dance, sizing each other up, exchanging chemical signals through their vent-like openings. Such meetings can end in mating, a clumsy pressing together of corpulent forms, or in slow-motion violence—one swelling to overshadow the other, contorting its hidden bands of chitin to bludgeon any rival. One may see the aftermath in the form of irregular, flattened patches where two lumps have collided, leaving behind thick smears of the loser’s remains.

Within the mysterious tunnels, smaller creatures thread the space between these more prominent inhabitants. Sling-like amphibious shapes with whip-thin tails, though “amphibious” might be an ill-suited word since there is no water to be found. They appear to dwell in the hollow pockets, descending headfirst into deep channels. Their slick bodies glisten as if coated in some resinous fluid protective against the heat; occasionally they dart out to catch unwary prey. Where they store the moisture that keeps them so glossy is anyone’s guess; possibly they distill a precious liquid from minerals, or from the body fluids of whatever they consume.

Farther along, tucked in the gloom behind collapsed arches, you’ll find a different breed: long-limbed, nearly transparent creatures that hover in the dark. Their jointed legs can fold so compactly against their chests that they seem like floating rods of cartilage. Only when they feel threatened or set upon by the denser, chitinous swarm do they unfold those limbs in a sudden burst of motion, scuttling away at mind-boggling speed. People who’ve glimpsed them speak of skull-like faces, which might be only illusions cast by the interplay of faint, red-tinged glows and the creatures’ translucent bodies.

All these organisms rely on relationships we can barely perceive. One might harvest mineral deposits that feed a neighboring species, which in turn becomes prey to a third. Some produce excretions that others scavenge; some gather in thick mats to conserve and channel heat, while others nest within those mats for shelter. The place is so saturated with life that each shard of rock or cluster of basalt ruins can host multiple interlocking food webs, all churning quietly in the heavy dark.

No single species dominates; even the largest forms might be beset by sudden swarms of smaller scavengers eager to strip them if they become weak. Yet there is a sense of equilibrium. Those battered, half-ruined arches could be the remnants of attempts—either by the lumps, the exoskeletal hive-builders, or older inhabitants altogether—to create controlled zones for certain species, or to pilot some form of architecture that might corral resources. If so, it seems that no group gained lasting mastery; instead, the forms that remain have each found an unspoken accord with adversity.

Perhaps the only real constant is the heat and dryness that saturate every life cycle. Fluids are precious currency, and many species show careful, near-obsessive conservation. Even respiration seems minimized—short, rapid draws of the hot air, exhalations that pass through specialized filters or vents. Each day (or what passes for a “day” without a sun) sees the faint stirring of the entire collective—broad pulses in activity, triggered by quivers in the all-consuming darkness overhead. They converge on newly exposed mineral veins, or converge to devour a stricken behemoth. Some silent signal tells them it is time.

Over unknown millennia, these inhabitants have honed their survival to a needle’s point: they do not waste, they do not rest idly, and they have no illusions of a softer world. Their forms reflect the stone itself—jagged in places, worn smooth in others, tempered by searing air that never relents. Adorned with layers of chitin, leathery folds, or translucent frames, they carry their realm with them, each one a sliver of this bizarre domain’s essence. 

In the end, it is their collective presence that defines this high, rocky enclave more than its parched wind or basalt walls. They are the movements among the cracks, the erratic glow that bruises the darkness, the hush of shell on stone. They throng every crevice, fueling their bodies on mineral residue and each other, forging a cycle that unfolds in a hush so profound that the occasional clack or rumble feels like an earthquake. In a world where all typical notions of habitat evaporate, these inhabitants defy expectation—tough, clandestine, quietly thrumming with a life-force older than memory. They endure in the shadowed furnace, each shape uniquely fitted to survive in the unlikeliest of strongholds, woven together in a web of need, adaptation, and ceaseless, heated dusk.
