They call themselves many names, though they have yet to establish which one will endure. Their language is still in flux, splintering and merging as different groups arrive from various aboveground lands and mingle in these depths. Physically, they resemble humans closely—though there’s a slight angularity to their features, as if shaped in parallel to the newly hewn walls surrounding them. Some have high cheekbones and prominent brows that might be part genetic, part the result of a modest but constant shortage of direct sunlight. A certain pallor colors their skin, even for those who arrived only a few weeks earlier. Should one linger long enough, it’s said, a permanent greyish tint sets in.

They’re a mix of pioneers, survivors, and explorers—people who have come here for many reasons. A handful arrived first, discovering habitable pockets of air and water. Then others followed, drawn by rumors of space to build anew without the wars or confinements of the surface world. You can see that diversity in their makeshift clothing: some wear pieced-together leather vests or wraps, others patchwork cloth from old tarps or salvage. A surprising few have garments woven from strange fungal fibers, a new innovation of the underground craftspeople.

They’ve begun to shape cultural practices around their environment. For instance, many have developed a habit of wearing headwraps or earthen caps to protect themselves from dripping water and falling pebbles. Some even incorporate bits of polished crystal or dull metal into these coverings for decoration or to catch stray light beams. A sense of pride arises in those who can salvage or trade for a particularly glossy mineral, turning it into ornaments on belts, necklaces, or staff-tips. Such trinkets are both practical—dimly reflecting lantern light in cavern gloom—and symbolic: each piece testifies to the hours spent mapping uncharted tunnels or extracting raw stone by hand.

Children scamper freely through the corridors, climbing up the improvised tiers and platforms with remarkable ease. Their eyes are bright and curious, quickly adjusting to the filtering gloom. The youngest ones have a distinct confidence moving about—from an early age, they’re taught to navigate the slopes and gullies, to recognize the more delicate rock formations or hidden channels of water. Sometimes they’re the ones who spot new cracks in the walls, peering inside to guess if the space beyond is stable or hiding valuable resources.

Within each cluster of dwellings, however, it’s the elders who guide the settlement’s direction. Yet “elders” might be someone thirty, perhaps forty, for there are no truly old folks here—this colony hasn’t existed long enough for ancient matriarchs or patriarchs to emerge. Still, some bring wisdom from surface tribal groups or prior underground expeditions. They sit around modest fire pits—charcoal smoldering in clay braziers or shallow rock hollows—discussing how best to strengthen walls against potential collapses, how to store water so it doesn’t turn stagnant, when to wait for new caravans of resources from the surface. Their council meetings produce an informal but functional leadership dynamic. Everyone in the settlement seems comfortable with the arrangement, bonding over the shared adventure of carving out a permanent home from these newly opened caverns.

One of the most intriguing sights are the “drift-seekers.” These individuals, whether drawn by curiosity or out of a personal calling, dedicate their days to exploring the labyrinth of corridors as it twists deeper into the earth. They dress in layered wraps and carry simple but robust gear: torches bound in thick cloth, chisels, rope, and a water gourd. Their eyes bear a reflective gleam, as if they spend more time in darkness than the rest. Drift-seekers return periodically with tales of tunnels that lead to underground pools stocked with pale fish, of narrow passages too tight to squeeze through, or of sudden wide chambers embedded with radiant crystal clusters that shimmer in torchlight. Occasionally, they bring back new animals—delicate amphibians or insects the color of raw quartz—gifting the settlement a sense of the wider life that lurks in those uncharted depths.

Meanwhile, back in the main caverns, other specialties emerge. Some are “mason-makers,” meticulously chipping away at the hardest rocks to shape rudimentary blocks and arches. These men and women have thick forearms and wear protective gloves of stitched leather. Under the flicker of torches, you see them leaning in, carefully testing the rock’s grain, listening for the telltale ring that guides the next strike of their chisels. Nearby, “fire-tenders” oversee the limited charcoal fires, a role that merges both practicality and prestige—controlling the only source of high heat for forging. They watch over lumps of ore with intense focus, coaxing the molten metal into new shapes. Children stand back, wide-eyed, marveling at the transformation of dull stone into bright metal under the glow. 

A distinct group known as “spore-weavers” attends to the subtle vegetation. They cultivate the pale mosses and lichens, carefully scraping them from shaded crevices and transferring them onto wooden boards or damp sections of rock. Under their watch, the fungal blooms—stringy and spidery—thrive enough to be swung in nets or woven into cloth-like sheets for insulation or patchwork. One sees them wandering the corridors, small clay jars of spore-laden water at their side, anointing new areas with carefully measured droplets. If asked why they perform these repeated, patient tasks, they’ll say it’s to ensure the settlement’s ecosystem remains stable. Some spores help purify seepage and filter out minerals from the water channels; others provide a measure of nourishment if prepared in the right way. This is a quiet but critical practice, ensuring their underground habitat offers more than just rock.

In pockets of the settlement, you’ll find artisans layering small lumps of damp clay into vessels. They may embellish their pots with faint tracings—speckling them with crushed mineral dust or scrawling rudimentary motifs. Some patterns hint at the earliest attempts to chronicle this community’s story: outlines of pickaxes or silhouettes of the jagged rock formations—symbols that may one day evolve into a coherent script. Each pot or jar represents a crucial container for water, grain, or for the rare store of salt. Some are used to transport fine shards of crystal or lumps of ore from one part of the settlement to another. Even in their infancy, these crafts show the slow shaping of a distinct material culture taking hold.

Not all who dwell here are people of constant, purposeful labor. A subset—artists, mystics, or simply contemplatives—doggedly search out spots of solitude away from the main hubbub. These individuals gravitate toward niches in the rock where water cascades in thin trickles or bright crystals cluster. There, you might catch low chanting, humming that resonates with the subtle dripping of water. Rumors circulate that some have begun to interpret the shape of cracks in the rock or the pattern of mineral veins as omens. Whether this leads to an emergent religion or some ephemeral set of beliefs remains uncertain, but for now, it infuses daily life with a hint of wonder. After all, in a realm where crystals literally grow from the walls and blind salamanders lurk in watery pools, a sense of magic or reverence seems almost inevitable.

Nor is the settlement limited to a single human-like species. Within this sprawling domain, there are whispers of others. In the farthest reaches—where the light from vents barely reaches and the air turns colder—some speak of shorter, broad-shouldered folk with uncanny night vision. They appear under the cover of the cave’s natural gloom, trading dense mushrooms and pungent herbal extracts for rope or pottery. No one is entirely certain whether these squat dwellers have lived here longer or migrated from depths unknown. Sometimes they’re glimpsed only in the corner of one’s eye—a flicker of movement in a side passage—leading some to call them “the Stone-Kin.” Their presence remains an enigma, fueling whispered theories that these caverns might be part of a much larger network, with entire lineages adapted to subterranean life.

But despite the variety of faces, trades, and beliefs, perhaps the clearest unifying thread is a collective sense of forging a future in an unformed world. These settlers operate on a gentle but persistent optimism. Everything down here may be raw and unsettled—rock still trembles sometimes, water sources shift unpredictably, and the fates of deeper tunnels remain a mystery—but the people press on, day by day, building and learning. In quiet corners, carved runes or chalk-drawn patterns begin to appear on fresh walls, the first attempts at telling this new society’s story. 

Standing in one of the great caverns, you can observe them going about their routines: pulling wooden sledges laden with rock, bartering baskets for lumps of metal ore, huddling around the warmth of a small fire. Amid the hush, you sense a strong undercurrent of life—these folk are not merely surviving but actively imprinting themselves on the stone. Their homesteads rise in half-finished tiers, marked with incomplete scaffolding, hammered pegs, and the occasional bit of decorative crystal. Along the walls, spindly fungus begins to glow softly in the darkness, a faint bioluminescence some have learned to cultivate.

It is an environment and a people in mid-transformation. Their lives are inseparable from the caverns themselves; just as the rock opens up in new corridors and fresh mineral seams, the settlement expands, bridging one chamber to the next with rope bridges and newly built walkways. Over time, they will shape this place—and be shaped by it—until the hush of dripping water and the chisel’s gentle scrape become the everyday harmony of a civilization that continues to invent itself with each new dawn, even if that “dawn” is merely sunlight filtered through jagged vents above. Here, beneath the earth, they live on the cusp of formation, both settlers and place revealing themselves little by little, forging a shared existence out of stone, water, and an irrepressible will to transform the unknown into home.
