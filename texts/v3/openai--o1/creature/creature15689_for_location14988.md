They call themselves, in their own murmured dialect, the People of the Threaded Roots. Their forms echo the textures of the place they inhabit: some tall and vine-sinuous, others broad and bark-skinned, still others adorned with the glimmering shells of the local arthropods. At first glance, one might take them for a single species—especially in the thick half-light—but in truth they form a loose confederation of related shapes, more like many branches from the same ancient trunk. Their eyes, whether large and reflective or small and quick-narrowed, adjust to gloom easily, absorbing every mote of light given off by bioluminescent blossoms or flickering overhead lamps. One sees muscle lines in their limbs as they maneuver among drooping vines and steel scaffolds alike. They move with the easy grace of a people who have lived in dappled shadows for generations.  

You might mistake them initially for silhouettes, half-lost behind enormous spore-laden pods; but approach closer, and you’d find the People individually distinct. Some have elongated fingers tipped with flexible, root-like filaments that they use to thread vines into place or gently guide creeping stalks across a beam. Others go clad in industrial patchwork: aprons or tunics fashioned from layered leaves and heavily worn cloth, reinforced with hammered metal plates or riveted scrap. Here and there you might spot more elaborate gear—a harness of riveted segments, an exoskeleton-like contraption echoing designs borrowed from local insects, complete with whirring mechanical joints that let the wearer lift heavy pipes or hold giant valves steady.  

Their clothing, for all its patchwork nature, betrays an old and careful craftsmanship. Every kicked-up hem, every collared opening, is embroidered with the patterns of branching roots or repeating leaf shapes. Tools dangle from belts made of braided creepers. Light orbs—small lumps of crystalline fungus wired to tiny steam-powered capsules—hang about their waists or from staff tops, providing focused beams of pale luminescence in especially dim corridors.  

They appear to breathe deeply of the damp, heat-laden air in a way that suggests symbiosis with the environment. Indeed, the People of the Threaded Roots speak of a “shared respiration” in half-whispered tunes that slip between the echoes of machinery. Their kind see themselves as caretakers—not merely of the plants, but also of the machines. They claim that mind and root have grown together for centuries, each supporting the other. When a pipeline hisses with steam, they stroke its surface as though calming a living creature. When a new creeper emerges from a dripping groove, they coax it along polished rails for structural reinforcement.  

Wander into the “rooms” formed by thick clumps of towering fronds or the winding halls of ancient stone buttressed by coiled vines, and you find them assembling around worn wooden tables (also grown, not built). In these communal spaces, elders and children alike share warm, plankton-like stews or feast on bounty gleaned from this subterranean ecosystem: mushrooms with vibrant caps, succulent vines that exude a sweet syrup, or the flesh of large tunnel fish drawn from hot springs below. The People accommodate an array of dietary needs; some prefer the high-protein insects that swarm in the cathedrals of roots, while others cultivate and roast seeds from bulbous fruiting vines.  

Their voices blend seamlessly into the broader cacophony of pounding gears and whistling valves. Their spoken conversations slip into melodic hums—part lullaby, part mechanical chant—that resonate with the shifting of metal plates or the subtle, groaning stretch of mighty roots. Some keep the rhythms not with drums but with small gear-work contraptions that click and rachet in time. The People’s language pulses with the clang and hiss of industry, forging a music of synergy with the place itself.  

Indeed, while the flora and the old machines initially appear to battle for dominance, the People navigate easily between them, treating props of steel and tangles of green as a single entity. They have no strict lines dividing greenhouse from foundry: the forging of new metal parts might happen next to a cluster of glowing blossoms, which, in turn, are carefully pruned to prevent them from tangling in the newly crafted gear assembly. When a machine part breaks, these dwellers scavenge the city’s labyrinthine corridors, rummaging for salvageable steel or coaxing living roots into shapes that can brace a broken beam. Their technology is neither purely organic nor purely mechanical, but a living blend that harnesses the city’s heartbeat of heat and humidity.  

Children race nimble-limbed across steamy catwalks. They carry compact seed dispensers strapped at their waists; at intervals, they climb to precarious vantage points and sow the seeds of new vines anywhere there’s a promising patch of moisture. Meanwhile, older artisans tinker in half-lit workshops, forging an endless supply of valves and piping that feed new growth with nutrient-rich broths siphoned from deeper wells. In every nook, you see evidence of continuous repair, growth, and reconfiguration: a metropolis shaped and sustained by the People’s daily labors.  

They have a complicated relationship with the many creatures scuttling or lumbering around them. Small lizard-like animals often find ways to share living spaces, holed up in rafters or weaving in and out among spilled grain. The People seem keenly attuned to these innocuous neighbors, offering them small scraps of food or capturing them carefully if they become stuck in the machinery. Larger beasts—quilled, furred, scaled—occasionally stalk the corridors, their presence formidable. While an unknowing visitor might fear for the dwellers, it’s clear the People know these creatures, too. Often a subtle handshake of mutual respect emerges: a hush descends, people glance to one another in silent warning, and the big animal passes with a watchful grace. In the deeper recesses where the largest predators dwell, the People speak in solemn tones of ancient compacts and cautionary tales.  

These inhabitants speak of themselves as keepers “walking the mesh” or “tending the spine.” For them, the sprawl of vines, roots, pipes, and machinery is a single living entity, an ecosystem stretching across centuries. While they sometimes trade with the surface—a rumored but seldom-seen world—most can count on two hands the times they have traveled above. Sunlight is more a half-legend, recalled in old stories of scorching brilliance, overshadowed by the gentle green radiance that rules their daily existence.  

If the People harbor any illusions about the fragility of their home, they hide it behind resolute faces. Their traditions emphasize careful stewardship: never let the vines choke a vital gear; never let unregulated steam scald a new root system. From infancy, each person learns to feel the pulse of the city—knowing when to open or seal a valve, how to shape a new creeper to shore up a tired wall. Their celebrations—ritual gatherings of song and gear-synchrony—reaffirm the tenuous balancing act between blossoming canopy and pumping piston.  

So they endure as a patient, industrious populace, molded by a place where iron and sap and stone have long intertwined. They move with resilience between the labyrinthine columns of living bark and rattling metal, guided by a deep-coded knowledge that if they failed to maintain the city’s hidden tapestries, it might all collapse into watery ruin. Yet you sense no urgency in them as they bustle about their tasks; only a steady, purposeful care. The People of the Threaded Roots live here as if they were grown from the same ancient seed—part plant, part craftsperson, part caretaker of humming machines and shimmering thrives. And in this perpetual half-light, amid clang and drip, they thrive, weaving themselves deeper into the lush and steamy heart of a place that is at once fantastical greenhouse and spontaneous foundry, forging life anew with every whispered breath of heat.
