They do not move in the usual sense of locomotion; the entire place makes a single motion for them. They are each a living filament in the greater organism of this city, a population of spindly, lignified stems and bark-wrapped branches that rises and falls in slow, communal surges of breath. They are plants, yes, but that name feels too small for what they have become. They are the City itself.

Once, they were seedlings or shoots, perhaps sprouts coaxed from the ground by the hidden hands of an ancient people. Over eons, they received guidance—patient twisting and braiding into arches, corridors, amphitheaters. But now, with all teachers and caretakers gone, the plants have settled into a perpetual half-life: no leaves, no fruits, no seeds that drift on the wind. They cling to each other in the close dark, bridging gaps to form doorways and repeating shapes that recall spiral staircases or distant towers. But it’s hard to call them individuals. They are distinct threads in a larger tapestry, each trunk an organ of some slow, quiet creature spanning every corridor. You could almost say they are one mind wearing many shapes.

In the hush of eternal night, they have found a way to sustain themselves without sunlight or flowing water—an impossibility to outside logic, yet here it persists. If they have a lifeblood, it must be thinner than air, a faint chemical shimmer passed through fibrous contact. They share resources across woven lattices: perhaps an ancient nutrient was stored in their woody cells, rationed out over millennia, or they draw on subtle energies that drift at the threshold of existence. They do not need to hunt or grow. Instead, they pulse gently, continuing a kind of respiration older than any known realm, exhaling in unison a sonorous groan that ripples through all the corridors at once.

Near the surface of the older trunks, the bark sometimes splits into antique fissures, releasing miniature puffs of spore-like dust. These spores drift in eddies of their communal breath, glowing in a spectrum invisible to mortal eyes—maybe it’s a pulse of heat or a swirl of chemical luminescence. They land again on the wood, eventually taking root in the cracks between ridges of bark. They may develop into slender filaments themselves, but so infinitesimally slow that you could watch a single trunk for your entire lifetime and see scarcely a change. Not quite dead, but so close to a motionless existence that time itself forgets to mark them.

Within this city, each trunk is cousin to another, each vine interconnects with a hallway or walkway overhead. It is a clan of branches that has become architecture—homes, walls, and inhabitants merged into a single identity. Perhaps they share a collective memory of the long-lost people who shaped them, a subtle echo stored in their lignin: the shape of a hand, the clipped edge of a tool, the faint warmth of chanting voices. Now, that memory resonates only as faint undertones in each inhalation, swirling among the pillars like half-forgotten songs.

Although they make no sound other than the rhythmic thrumming of their group breath, it isn’t pure silence. If you were to close your eyes and concentrate—that is, if you could see nothing anyway—you might sense a frequency just below hearing, a subsonic interchange that passes from trunk to trunk, root to vine. The city’s inhabitants may be “talking” in an unhurried, barely perceptible conversation across centuries. It is nothing like quick chatter; more like the passing of ancient secrets from one generation to the next, repeated with geological patience.

The dryness that pervades this city does not kill them—it preserves them. Imagine them as petrified guardians, still faintly alive, their wooden tissues calcified into unyielding shells. They no longer require the flow of sap or the flex of green growth. Whatever exchange of life happens, it has more in common with a statue’s subtle weathering than a typical sprout’s fresh outburst. Over tens of thousands of years, some columns may topple; their falling rhythms become part of the architecture’s slow dance. A broken trunk might roll quietly into a new corner, bridging old passages or closing off a corridor. Others awaken to that gap in coverage, knit themselves across in the slow language of bark and fiber, sealing the break until the layout morphs once more.

In place of hearts, these beings have chambers—hollow trunk-space that resonates with each great wave of breath. Their communal air exchange swells, then releases in a ragged exhalation, creating that deep roar reverberating through miles of petrified corridors. You feel it through your bones as you stand there, a testament that, yes, they truly live. It is a cyclical proof of life for creatures that barely seem alive at all. Perhaps it is how they are tested by time, how their corridors remain stable, how they maintain a hold on existence without light or water.

No single trunk leads or commands the rest. They exist in a shared network of presence. Each passage, archway, or courtyard is an extension of their many-limbed body. If there is such a thing as a “center” to this place, it may be wherever the pulse begins—an ancient core from which each breath originates. But that center, if it is real, lies in such far reaches of shadow that it might as well be myth. It could be deep underground or perched in a lofty vault somewhere overhead, all connected by the labyrinth of braided vines.

Silent guardians of silence, the plants’ only sign of wakefulness is that cyclical moan, that collective inhalation and exhalation. In the midst of it, spores drift like distant stars in the swirling black, gently illuminating the edges of trunk and vine. The city’s dwellers need no eyes. They perceive themselves through that faint glow and through the shared vibrations trembling in their joined bodies. Their existence is an unbroken web: to be one trunk is to be the entire city, to have roots in every courtyard and arch, to hear centuries pass in the hush of dust accumulating.

These are not plants that yearn for the sun. Perhaps they surrendered that dream eons ago, or never needed it in the first place. They stole the skill for survival from a deeper law, one that honors the quiet, slow dimension of things. They learned to thrive in dryness, to transform the dearth of sunlight into a different sort of energy, to survive in a hush deeper than any night. And so, they remain, an entire population that is at once a single living being, embodied by walls of bark and pillars of braided vines, breathing in unison, dreaming to the rhythm of millennial pulses.

Were the ancient caretakers to return, they might find the city exactly as they left it—only subtly more refined, corners smoother, arcs grown thicker, surfaces polished by dust’s gentle scouring. But the moment of their arrival would be as foreign as a long-extinct star waking to find the cosmos changed. These guardians of the dark have created their own order now. Over indefinite centuries, they have become their own keepers, a labyrinth of chlorophyll ghosts who still hum with the memory of a living tapestry, their communal body resonating in darkness until the last echo of time.
