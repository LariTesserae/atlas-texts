They are here, though at first you might doubt it. You see no footprints in the dust, no tool marks freshly chipped, not a single echo of soft footfalls. You can walk the halls for hours and hear only that steady roar pressing in. It annihilates suspicion that anything living remains. But they are here, woven so thoroughly into this place that you might as well try to differentiate the mortar from the stone. You’ll never see them directly, never catch a glimpse of motion in the next corridor. They do not lurk behind corners—lurking would imply that they move, that they wait. Instead, they simply are, as old and immovable as the blocks that form the structure’s skin.

Some say they dwell in the hollows behind the walls, where the stone is thickest, contoured into hidden recesses the builders left behind. There is a rumor—perhaps just an echo conjured by the unending drone—that if you stand in certain corridors at certain times you will hear muffled shuffling in the thickness of the barrier. Not a rustle of scales or fur, but something that hints at slow, methodical rearrangements of great weight. Others have insisted that the presence is not physical at all: that these dwellers are purely vibrational manifestations in the massive columns, born of the same hammered resonance that rattles through the entire domain. Their existence is intangible, more akin to the structure’s memory than to any organism. Two contradictory tales, and yet each is equally plausible in this perpetual near-darkness where everything is stone—or might as well be.

If you linger for days in one of the smaller side-chambers, you’ll begin to sense them. It’s not a sight or sound, but a shift of density in the air, as though your body crosses an invisible boundary. Suddenly, you feel certain you’re trespassing on territory that belongs to something far older, far deeper, than yourself. The dwellers claim these spaces in ways you can’t tally with normal biology. You might feel a chill, brief and strangely out of place in the persistent warmth. Or you’ll detect a faint luminescence, not the red-brown glow of the stones but a subtler haze—like the afterimage that dances in your eyes when you stare too long into darkness. They’re in those illusions. They coalesce when your mind wavers, your senses saturate, and you no longer trust your own perceptions. They might brush up against your consciousness, glide past you without leaving footprints, except within your own unsettled thoughts.

But if they do have bodies, they cannot be soft flesh, for there is no moisture here to sustain it—no fluid at all, no humidity, no dew gathering in cracks. They must be formed, if formed they are, of ancient, desiccated substance—that same dusty stone, or else a dull metal hammered at the dawn of time. Perhaps they are shaped like squat bipedal forms with broad limbs and thick shoulders to bear the weight of these monstrous ceilings. Perhaps they are spindly, elongated, wispy silhouettes that slip into the narrowest seams between stones. One guess is as good as another in these half-lit labyrinths.

Wander into one of the corridors branching deeper into the plateau’s heart: the walls press so close you can feel the gritty surface brushing your arms. Huddle in that passageway and let the roar fill your skull. It’s there, in that moment, that you’ll begin to feel them watching—though the word “watching” is too human an idea for a presence that doesn’t rely on eyes or the visible spectrum. They sense you. Or perhaps they sense the displacement you cause in the air, the breath you exhale, the faint heat from your living flesh. The dwellers, adapted to an utter steadfastness, note the anomaly that you are: something alive in their world of carved stone. Their home, this labyrinth, has no margin of error for life; it’s a closed system, sealed and dry, unwelcoming. And yet here you stand.

You may try to call out. Any voice you produce gets devoured by the relentless roar; words are swallowed as soon as they leave your throat. Now you realize the dwellers have no speech—what language could survive here? Their communication, if it exists, must ride within that roar, or on some subsonic frequency saturating the stone. Perhaps they speak through subtle changes in the thrumming, slight modulations that carry enormous meaning to them yet sound uniform to you.

Some travelers have theorized the dwellers are guardians, not of objects or treasures but of the structure’s purpose—a purpose you cannot fathom. Perhaps the plateau is a colossal engine, and the roar is the sound of its ongoing operation. If so, the dwellers are both operators and components, tending to it, ensuring that the primal mechanism never falters. They could be the tension in the pivoting doors, the endless realignment of pressure in the subterranean vaults. They could be the cause of the heat that rises from hidden vents, or the funnels that redirect the drone so it resonates in every chamber. Living cogs in an impossible machine, continuing an ancient function for which no one remembers the goal.

Others, less rationally inclined, believe they are an old race that came here fleeing something else—some cosmic threat or plague. They built and built until every inch was sealed off. Perhaps they intended to hide from whatever lurked outside. Yet in the hush of centuries, they themselves became as lifeless as the fortress they raised. Each generation grew more inert, more attuned to stone than flesh, until in the end they simply merged with the walls, metamorphosed into the structure’s living threads. Now they exist as an echo of their once-human (or once-organic) selves, pinned in a timeless dream. The roar, in that interpretation, might be the vestige of their collective hearts still beating as one, or the wail of their endless vigil.

In deeper corners, if you find half-obscured carvings or reliefs under centuries of grit, you might see shapes that hint at who they once were. Maybe spidery figures that stretch across labyrinthine lines. Or silhouettes that look more like coiling serpents, each coiled shape nested within repeated geometric motifs. Or butch, rectangular forms, each outline reminiscent of an upright block carved into an approximation of a face. Stare at these relic images too long, and you’ll convince yourself they are the dwellers, captured in stylized portraits from ages past. But which representation is real? Or do they all represent a single entity seen through countless conceptual lenses?

Step into a large courtyard—one of those rare places where enough of the roof is open to reveal a slice of ashen sky. Listen to the roar. Confront that emptiness above. No sign or symbol breaks through the gloom. Yet in that emptiness, in the monotony of the unending drone, you sense the dwellers’ presence most keenly. They hold the architecture in tension: something practical, or else something purely ritualistic. The entire fortress-labyrinth stands as an extension of them, or they are an extension of it. They might be as small as dust motes swirling in the vibrations or as large as the fortress itself, simply infused throughout every block.

They cannot leave, because there is no concept of “leaving.” Beyond these stones is more stone, beyond the horizon more emptiness—no contrary environment to move into. In the immensity of time, the dwellers have become the environment. Perhaps they no longer recall what it is to be separate from it. Thus, if you remain too long, if you succumb to the dryness and lose track of your own boundaries, they might absorb you as well. You might find you can’t tell if the warm hum in your own bones is your heartbeat or the fortress’s, or if that unearthly roar is vibrating your vocal cords from the inside. At a certain point, you may realize you haven’t blinked in a long while—and a droning part of your mind wonders: Are you, too, beginning to merge?

But the dwellers do “live” here in some capacity: not in the dynamic sense that we imagine life—growing, reproducing, changing. Rather, they inhabit an eternal stasis, fulfilling a role that ended so long ago that neither the watchers nor the watched recall its origin. They do not feed or drink. They have no children. They do not pass on stories, except in forms that remain inscrutable to any outsider. They simply persist, unchanging and unyielding. In that perseverance, there is a kind of life, a continuity that bridges epochs.  

And so you might say: no one lives here—or you might see that everything living has blurred into the labyrinth itself, existing in a manner you can barely grasp. They are the silence behind the roar, or the resonance within it. They are the warmth that seeps from the floors and the geometry that refuses to align. They are the unseen watch that never ends. Surviving might not be the right word. Enduring might fit better. Whatever or whoever they are, they have become the ultimate stewards of a world that permits no change. A life not measured by breath or heartbeat, but by a seamless oneness with the stone.
