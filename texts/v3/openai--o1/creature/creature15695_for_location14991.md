They are not a large population, at least not in the open. When you arrive, you only glimpse the everyday folk darting between stacked metal walkways and buttresses of dripping composite. They wear heavy garments with high collars that funnel water away from their necks—some in thick, patched oilcloth, others in layers of woven fiber stiffened by the ever-present condensation. Most tuck scarves around their faces, not so much for cold as for noise: the persistent thunder from the city’s hidden machinery requires at least an illusion of muffling. Their boots are fitted with metal studs, meant to grip slick ramps and catwalks shining with unending moisture.  

Those you see, or half-see, are modest in manner and quick in step. They carry crates of salvaged parts or bundles of newly refined materials from one end of the plateau to the other, eyes always scanning the ground for the next safe foothold. Their glances are cautious, especially in the presence of outsiders, but they do not appear fearful. Rather, they hold a quiet conviction that they’ve already witnessed every possible permutation of life in a damp, rust-prone labyrinth—so if you come to trade or merely to observe, you’re just another part of the slow swirl of ages passing by.  

Deep behind these utilitarian figures, there are others whose shapes you might perceive only in glimpses of metal grate and half-lit corridors. Some are old beyond measure, stooped with the weight of generational knowledge of the city’s machinery. They rarely speak in open spaces, preferring the labyrinth of boiler rooms and subterranean corridors where the clang resonates in low, droning frequencies. Tuning themselves to the pulse and hum, they track changes in the system: an extra hiss in Valve 7C, a new rattle in Pump 12 that might warn of a larger breakdown. Their expertise is not documented in any formal system; it’s carried in hush-voiced anecdotes and hand-etched diagrams passed on to apprentices who prove themselves worthy of hearing the city’s secret heartbeat.  

Then there are the watchers on the outskirts—loners who’ve always preferred the transitional boundary where city meets plateau. They stand guard, whether officially or by an instinct that compels them to linger at the edges, scanning the haze for potential intruders or simply keeping watch over the sprawl they call home. Their presence is almost spectral. From afar, you might spot one perched on a broken wall, a silhouette against the misty sky, motionless save for the occasional flick of a hood. They carry long, spindly rifles or crossbows adapted to the local environment, with parts sealed against corrosion. They rarely fire; game is scarce, and any real threat to the settlement would have to come from deeper into the uncharted arcs of plateau beyond.  

The children, who exist mostly behind closed doors, reveal themselves at intervals: a flash of movement behind a dripping archway, or a cluster of small faces peering from a second-story balcony. Their laughter, when it escapes, is oddly bright against the clang and rumble around them. In some half-flooded courtyard, you might see them hop between rivulets that wind beneath walkways—turning droplets into improbable games, fishing out tiny glimmers of ore or pupating insects that survived in the damp. Occasionally, you hear a squeal that suggests delight or mild alarm as a swirl in the mud takes on a shape they swear wasn’t there just seconds before.  

Not all the residents are strictly human, if human at all. Strange, insect-like forms scuttle along the hidden transitions between stone and metal, where perpetual dampness nurtures lichens or algal blooms. They could be as small as a fingertip or as large as a child’s forearm, with carapaces tinted green-brown, blending seamlessly with the moldy surfaces. Some linger near warm vents that emit faintly sweet-smelling steam, while others gather in the run-off channels, sipping at the iron-rich water. No one speaks of them openly—perhaps because they’re as much a natural feature of this place as the condensation itself, a given presence that everyone has come to accept.  

Now and then, you’ll notice certain individuals—call them engineers or possibly priests—who seem to hold authority over the city’s subtler mysteries. They might emerge at irregular hours, pausing to inspect the swirl of water in a neglected courtyard or holding a measuring rod to gauge new tension in the old scaffolds. Their robes are layered and carefully sealed at the seams with an almost ceremonial precision. They trace glyphs in grime or place tuning forks against the humming pipes, listening intently to the resulting note. If approached, they rarely speak in more than cryptic hints: “The city murmurs tonight,” or “The water wants to flow differently this season.”  

In the higher tiers, near the plateau’s edge, dwell a class of crafters who repurpose salvage brought up from the city’s deep storerooms or scrounged from the husks of collapsed factories. Some are blacksmiths forging new couplings to replace old rust-laden ones; others labor over circuit boards in cramped workshops, coaxing defunct electronics back to life under flickering, improvised lamps. All around, you catch the tang of flux, the abrasive smell of wet metal heated to forging temperature, and the hiss of steam as newly welded parts cool too fast in the damp air. These crafters are pragmatic, speaking in clipped terms of torque and calibration, but you sense a certain reverence in their craft. They glimpse the city as a living being, and each replacement gear or circuit is an act of healing.  

Beyond the core settlement—where the geometry becomes inconsistent and the arches seem built for different proportions—are the “forgotten wards.” A small but persistent array of wanderers roams these regions, tinkerers or dreamers unafraid of the city’s esoteric corners. They examine the way a half-buried hallway curves impossibly into a slope, or how certain chiming pipes ring out distinct harmonies if struck at precise intervals. Some say these fringe dwellers follow the city’s more arcane signals, mapping out how each swirl in a puddle corresponds to the shifting pitch of unseen machinery. Words like “songlines” and “echo charts” surface in their whispered vocabulary, as though they aim to decode a grand, hidden pattern.  

Elsewhere, in tight pockets along the weathered plateau, the wiry birds make precarious nests, using bits of wire and twisted metal they pry off old scaffolds. They prefer these vantage points high up on the battered city walls, where the warm drafts created by the industrial vents meet the cooler mountain air. Their eggs, a dull gray, are protected from the damp by oily secretions from the birds’ feathers—another adaptation that has let them survive in a place with so little natural vegetation.  

And of course, there are the foxlike creatures you glimpse from time to time. As thin as hunger itself, they slink through battered drainage tunnels in search of stray rodents or morsels left behind by less careful inhabitants. Their large ears swivel constantly, catching the low-frequency hum that vibrates through the plating beneath their paws. Someone might leave out scraps for them in a dented tin bowl at the edge of a workshop, fostering a fragile pact: “We feed you; you keep the pests from gnawing the wiring.” They accept warily, eyes shining in the half-light.  

Finally, somewhere in the city’s unnamed depths—beneath the labyrinth of pipes and behind massive, locked doors that not even the stooped elders speak about—one suspects there must be an ultimate caretaker. It could be a single person or a council—maybe even an otherworldly presence fused with the oldest mechanical hearts, the impetus for the city’s entire design. The scattered clues might be the melodic pulses in the steam, or the footprints rumored to appear in condensed droplets on locked hatchways. If pressed, the most seasoned inhabitant can only offer a slight shrug, as if to say, “Yes, someone must keep it all running,” but no one can confirm the caretaker’s form or nature.  

Thus, the plateau’s dwellers are as varied as the city’s many moods. On the surface, you see pragmatic laborers and watchful sentinels, bent engineers and silent children for whom water is both playground and hazard. Moving deeper, you meet quiet observers and half-scholars charting the city’s hidden harmonies or coaxing old circuits to life. Nature sends in its sparse emissaries—a slender foxlike face or a defiant succulent clinging to crevices—reminding everyone that life persists, in small, stubborn acts, against corrosion’s slow creep.  

Together, they form an ecosystem in which the mechanical and organic coexist. Long after the clang of giant engines or the fizz of harnessed electricity recedes into memory, the footprints of these inhabitants and their many-layered roles will remain etched into the drenched stone. A place that should, by all accounts, be inhospitable has become home to a people who seem as much a product of the city’s rhythms as the city is shaped by theirs. And so they go on, generation after generation, weaving their shouts and footfalls into the damp tapestry of the plateau, never quite separate from the ever-humming machinery that sustains them.
