They travel in small, shifting clusters, these hardy souls who reside among the jagged ridges. You might not notice them at first; it’s easier to think the remnants of the old stone shelters are abandoned, that no one has inhabited them for many seasons. But in some hidden alcove—a crevice half-concealed by gnarled evergreens—several figures hunker around a heat source too faint to be called a fire, more an ember glow. They are lightly built, wrapped in dense, fur-lined attire. Layers of thick wool and hide shield them against the knife-edge cold. Their hands are calloused, shaped by labor rather than abundance, and they stare with guarded curiosity at any intrusion.  

They are not numerous, and that, perhaps, is their most striking aspect: a people as sparse as the vegetation. They know every rift and ledge. They can place a worn boot on a precarious slope without sending stones clattering. It’s a fluid, quiet existence—one in which hunts are done carefully, stealthily, so that the few animals here are not unnaturally spooked. It is said these folk follow the melt. When the sun warms certain slopes, coaxing water from the ice, they drift closer to that spot to replenish their stores. Then, when fresh snows drift in or the storms rage, they retreat to more sheltered hollows.

But these dwellers are only one thread in the tapestry of life. The mountains’ more permanent citizens move with a different rhythm entirely. You see it in the tiny scale-like insects whose shimmering carapaces catch stray rays of sunlight. They thrive where moss clings to sun-warmed stone, crawling in patient, methodical patterns. They have no need for hurry, resting in small hollows while the atmosphere shifts dramatically from biting cold to near-gentle breezes within a single day.  

Shyer still are the slight-bodied rodents that vanish at a flicker of motion—there one moment, lost in a craggy gap the next. Their fur is dense and speckled to blend with granite, sometimes tinted with the same pale hue as the lichen-dappled cliffs. The faint rustle of their frantic scurrying can startle you if you tread too near, the sound amplified by the echo chambers of rock walls.

Then there are the dwellers of the slopes: those animals with splayed hooves adept at clinging to sheer rock faces—mountain goats or a close cousin. Their coats are rough, layered with oily hairs to repel the wet snow. You’ll glimpse them in twilight just before they vanish behind a promontory, or in the midday haze of reflected light, picking their way along a trail only they can see. Sometimes they venture down to the scarce pools that form in shallow basins. You might hear the clack of horn against stone, resonance carrying across the still air.

High above, the birds of prey: large, broad-winged, coasting on updrafts that swirl around the craggy summits. Their screeches slice through the silence, reminding all below of the constant interplay of survival. Moons ago, one might have glimpsed a nest perched among the broken beams of a ruin, where the bird’s offspring tested their wings. These avian hunters rely on an unspoken alliance with the land’s scarcity; each success is critical for them. They remain watchful, and their gaze seldom strays from the scrabbling movements far below.

And then there is another presence—difficult to name. It’s hinted at by the old huts and the strange shape of implements dug into the earth by wind or exposed after melting snow. These dwellings and objects suggest that at some time before the current small bands of nomads, perhaps a different group tried to make a lasting home. Their memory clings more to the rock than the people do now. You see it in the way an ancient lintel door is carved with symbols. Or how certain walls are fitted so skillfully that the simplest stone holdfasts haven’t shifted despite the relentless freeze-and-thaw cycle. One might imagine them as ancestors, or possibly an unrelated culture altogether—those who mystically chipped away at the stone to carve vantage points that overlook precipitous drops. Whether they were forced to abandon this place or merely migrated with the seasons remains unclear. Their trace persists in the things left behind: an old, battered cooking pot lurking under a collapsed roof, a set of curious stone chisels, or a knife blade whose handle has long since disintegrated.

Some of the people living here now claim tenuous lineage from those vanished builders. Others say that the old dwellings are haunted by spirits that raise storms, or that the tall ridges themselves are alive with a presence that brooks no permanent settlement. Whichever you believe, there is a definite hush that envelops these half-buried walls. It’s as though the stone shelters are still listening for the echoes of their makers.

Beyond the humans, beyond the hoofed creatures, the insects, and the rodents, countless tiny forms flourish in the hidden cracks. In the damp gloom behind a mossy rock, there are minuscule amphibians—barely glimpsed but sometimes heard after a thaw, their soft calls bouncing inside a rocky alcove. Small populations of hardy lichen, half-plant, half-fungus, creep across the bare rock in fractal patterns. They, too, are inhabitants—slowly digesting the stone, measuring their existence in ages rather than years.

Life here seems to function on pulses: warm hours that awaken the insects, short intervals of sun-melt that feed small shrubs, brief windows when the goats descend to drink, or an afternoon lull when the winds subside enough for the robed wanderers to scale precarious heights in search of edible roots. With the next sudden shift in weather, everything hunkers down again—a choreographed retreat. The hush then returns, and the mountain retains its dreamlike stillness.

That stillness, however, is never empty. Each hidden gully houses a tapestry of sounds and movements, from the nearly silent skirl of a buried spring to the faint hiss of wind slipping through evergreen needles. Predators—both four-footed and winged—continuously scan the slopes for prey. In turn, the small creatures constantly test new hiding spots, forging winding paths among tumbled boulders. So many eyes peer from the shadowy undercrofts of rock, each belonging to something that has carved out the slightest advantage in this raw domain.

One might think of these beings as ephemeral, poised on a razor’s edge between survival and disappearance, but in truth they live here with a certain confident grace. The humans move as though guided by unspoken knowledge passed down from scarcity to scarcity. The birds curl their wingtips with perfect control, mastering the gusts that swirl unpredictably through the canyons of rock. The hoofed beasts find sustenance in unlikely patches of lichen. Even the insects, all glittering angles and twitching antennae, glean enough moisture from thin condensation to persist.  

They are all, in some subtle way, in conversation with the geology itself. The stone is still new, unscathed by time’s wearing hand. It invites daring and exploration—vertical illusions of safety where a slip might prove fatal, yet also offering the vantage points that only freshly uplifted terrain can promise. These creatures have learned to read the rhythms of melt and frost, to navigate shifting scree, to glean warmth from the bright glare of midday. From them, you sense no panic, only a watchful readiness. They trust the pulse of change, for they have woven themselves into it.  

If you sit quietly in the heart of that pale rock cathedral, you might feel the gravity of the mountains around you—an embrace that’s somehow both severe and protective. Within it dwell these self-concealed folk with their subtle traditions, the beasts that roam and soar, the boundless small lives in the crevices and moss. Together, they compose a living puzzle that reveals itself in fragments: a quick dart of movement here, a rustle there, and occasionally a glimpse of vantage across the broken spines of the peaks.  

In this freshly birthed terrain, life is a persistent tapestry of improvisation. Small though they might be in number, these inhabitants—human and otherwise—are forging their rhythms in tandem with the rock’s slow shifting. In each crisp, glowing sunrise, you see them continue on: building, scuttling, nesting, hunting, drinking, vanishing with the fog, returning at the thaw. A quiet, determined clan of survivors, all bound to this breathless land that still resonates with the memory of its own recent rise into the world.
