From a distance, one might assume this planet has no people at all—certainly no civilization in the sense we commonly understand. Yet, as you drift among those living structures, you begin to notice subtle patterns of movement and arrangement that betray more than mere happenstance. These forms—trunks and canopies and tangled bark-like threads—aren’t just flora. They are the foundations and the homes of beings so attuned to their environment that they blur the boundary between architecture and organism.

At first, it’s easier to notice the smaller creatures: the tiny, dust-feathered flyers that flit from one archway of woven bark to another in spiraling loops. These are about the size of hummingbirds, but with wings that seem spun from reflective filaments, catching the pale mineral light and scattering it back in dancing sparks. They live on the minute crystals that form in the crevices of the bark, chiseling them free with delicate proboscises that secrete a mild acid. Instead of liquids, these fliers must harvest trace minerals directly, flitting from spot to spot in ceaseless gleaning. Their bodies are as light as the swirling dust, yet they have a sleek strength that lets them hover in the faint drafts forever, rarely needing rest.

Closer to the ground, you’ll see—if you move slowly enough—whole caravans of scuttling insects, or something akin to insects, their segmented carapaces tinted with the same shimmering patterns along their edges. They roll about in spherical shapes, collecting “dewdrops” of mineral residue that gather in certain nooks. Though it’s not water, these accumulations have a slight condensate of chemical vapors rising from the newly formed cracks in the land. The scuttlers form small conical mounds of the gleaned minerals, which they then methodically haul back to hidden chambers nestled into the lower hollows of the living city’s base. These hidden enclaves are porous networks woven from root-like structures, perhaps grown intentionally to house the scuttlers’ prized collections. Now and then, you might see a curious gesture—two or three of the insects meeting, each depositing its gleanings into a single mound, then parting ways, only to repeat the process a moment later. This exchange feels almost ritualistic, as if signifying communal storage or perhaps an ancient routine that fosters an unseen chemical synergy.

Above them—and indeed throughout the entire labyrinth—are the watchers. They are longer-limbed, slow-moving beings whose colors blend with the bark and leaves. They have elongated torsos and limbs that splay out at unexpected angles, as if they were jointed like branches themselves. Their skin or exoskeleton (it can be hard to distinguish flesh from plant matter here) is tough but flexible, ridged along lines that match the living architecture around them. Whether these watchers are the city’s gardeners or its guardians is unclear, but they quietly traverse the walkways, placing or rearranging filaments, smoothing moss-like textures that cover certain corridors, sometimes even coaxing new shoots to sprout in carefully orchestrated patterns.

Their communication seems almost telepathic, though closer observation reveals faint pulses of color rippling across their ridged skin. A silent language of prismatic flickers and soft glows passes among them. These watchers appear able to direct the structures’ growth, steering sprouts around each other or weaving them into thickened columns that can support more layers of life above. They lovingly cradle seedlike nodules in their hands—if those appendages can be called hands—and gently press them into small pockets in the trunk-like growth. Over time, you see how each planted nodule pulses to life, feeding on the planet’s mineral breath, sprouting tiny crowns that will one day form a new corridor or an entire courtyard. In this way, the city expands from within, grown rather than built, guided by these watchers who are possibly the city’s architects.

Should you venture deeper, you notice enclaves where inhabitants gather. Here, among the dense spires, the watchers mingle with smaller, spiral-bodied creatures reminiscent of snakes or vines—creatures that wrap around thick roots, their many translucent, finlike frills quivering in the faint dust-laden currents. These serpentine beings glean something invisible from the winding bark, possibly the mineral-laden vapors issuing out of cracks in the planet’s young crust. With each breathy, undulating wave of their bodies, they seem to filter the dust and exhale it in faintly glowing puffs. The watchers perch next to them calmly, perhaps filling the air with subtle color pulses that encourage the serpents to coil in certain places, to keep them from damaging newly formed growth. It’s an intricate dance: no dominion or subjugation, simply layers of co-existence and mutual caretaking that sustain the city’s progress.

In some deeper alcoves, where thick trunk-like passages converge, you can find the hidden gatherings of beings who appear more communal still—collections of smaller bipeds that congregate in a circle around a trunk’s wide base. They wear (or perhaps naturally grow) whorled plates over their shoulders that bloom out in decorative curvatures, each unique. Their limbs end in fine, rootlike tendrils that latch gently onto the bark beneath them. What they do in these circles is mesmerizing: they gently sway in unison, their plates flashing patterns of colored light under the canopy’s shade. It might be a form of shared resonance, communal communication, or perhaps a ritual of maintenance for the living structures that form their home. You can almost feel a soft hum, even as no audible sound emerges—like vibrations passing through dust-laden air, stirring the swirling motes into transient shapes.

These bipeds are not the final strata of life, for nestled in the highest canopies are elegant gliders—creatures who appear to be living membranes shaped like broad fans. They rest until the swirling dust gusts upward, then open themselves to ride on subtle thermals. They serve a pollinator-like role, drifting lazily through the filtered light, occasionally brushing the towering columns and blossoming spires with fine filaments hanging from their edges. When those filaments touch certain nodules, you can see tiny sparks passing along the lines, as if transferring genetic or catalytic codes from one growth to the next. In that sense, these gliders are part of the city’s circulatory system; they carry crucial signals and sporelike particles from one district of living architecture to another, ensuring that no region of growth remains isolated.

Despite so much variety in creature, shape, and function, everything here is part of a quiet, interwoven tapestry. There is a sense that the city itself is alive—sentient, even—and these creatures are simultaneously its inhabitants, caretakers, and extensions. In moments of stillness, you might imagine that the entire city draws a single, collective breath. Each corridor, arch, walkway, branch, trunk, stem, and inhabitant seems linked by an unseen network that pulses slowly like a heart. Though there is no liquid water, the place exudes a kind of fluid harmony, as though energy flows in intangible streams—streams that rely upon dust, crystals, and the planet’s shifting geology.

Indeed, that geology is still forming, raw and malleable. There are organs of the planet itself—small vents that burrow up from deep fissures, releasing faint blasts of scorching, mineral-charged air. Here, the watchers and other fauna gather for warmth or for fresh bits of nutrient-laden dust. It’s as though the entire world is equal parts nest and nursery, quietly forging an infrastructure for the future. These vents also appear to guide the next wave of expansions within the city. The watchers cluster around newly emerged vents, helping to coax fresh living columns to take root there, forming new corridors around each vent’s perimeter—like building walls around a hearth.

Within this planet-wide city, you may find no single species that dominates outright. Instead, there is a gradient of roles—those that filter dust from the air, those that collect crystal outgrowths, those that weave new growth into supportive structures, and those that shuttle essential spores or minerals from place to place. Some are sentient on a level that parallels our notion of community or society, while others operate more instinctively. Yet they share a common drive: to cultivate this horizon-spanning realm, to expand the living maze in slow synergy with the planet’s newborn geology.  

All the while, the hush persists: a hush not of emptiness but of teeming life in gentle motion. The gliders’ membranes might flap softly in a rising breeze. The watchers’ color pulses flicker in dim corners as they nurture new stems. The tiny dust-flyers’ wings hum with just enough force to keep them aloft. Little is rushed; all is patient. There is a timelessness here, as though the city has existed on the edge between living potential and tectonic birth for eons—and will continue to do so as the planet completes its emergence.  

Spend enough time in these corridors, and you’ll sense how the beings who dwell here have shaped and been shaped by this languid dryness. They’ve learned to thrive without water in a realm of mineral nourishment and slow geothermal exhalations. Just as the environment resonates with quietly forming geology, so too do its denizens radiate a subtle, self-sustaining energy—a living testament to adaptation. 

They are the city and the city is them: a single organism with many expressions, each dedicated to cultivating an ever-evolving landscape of woven trunks and glittering leaves. Patterns of life swirl and settle and swirl again, an ongoing improvisation of form and function. And in that profound, dust-dappled stillness, the planet’s inhabitants continue their labor of growth, weaving new wonders from the dryness—building a world that transcends the very notion of impossibility.
