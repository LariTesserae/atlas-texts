They live tucked between the ridges—quite literally in the gaps and hollows where the wind sweeps overhead in a constant, keening rush. If you travel through the hollows quietly, you may first notice only the traces of them: an odd set of footprints, deep and angular, too narrow for a hoof and sharper-edged than most claws; a small mound of carefully stacked stones topped by the skeleton of a leaf, placed almost ceremoniously; a fleeting movement in the corner of your eye that’s gone when you turn your head. They are secretive creatures, or people—no one seems completely certain—and they have shaped themselves to this land as seamlessly as the moss that clings to battered stone.

Sometimes travelers describe them with an almost human grace: lithe forms swaddled in woven grasses with hoods that blend into the color of the rocky terrain. Others insist they must be more animal than human; they can scamper up nearly vertical rock walls and leap from one jagged plateau to another as though weightless. From a distance, one might see them perched on some outcrop, arms or forelimbs silhouetted against the roiling sky, scanning for…what? A break in the clouds, a shift in the wind, or animals to track? It isn’t clear. One rumor claims they carry slender, spear-like tools: not quite weapons, more like staffs to steady themselves in the turbulence. Another story suggests these shapes have no permanent form at all, that they shift like the storms, ephemeral illusions half woven from dust and wind.

Elsewhere, there are unmistakably organic creatures that inhabit the shadows. Tiny, sure-footed mammals with pointed ears and eyes too large for their faces navigate the rocky corridors in search of sustenance. Their fur is short and dense, the color of rust and stone dust. Some take shelter in the old, hollow huts of carved boulders—remnants of older, vanished inhabitants. By day, you might catch a glimpse of them hugging the shade of some low-slung spindly tree, nibbling at fungus hidden under overlapping rock ledges. Superbly silent except for an occasional soft trill, they vanish at the slightest hint of footsteps.

Other inhabitants prefer the open winds, skimming across the landscape as though sustained by the constant gale. They have translucent, membranous wings that flash iridescent in the sunlight, fanning out from sleek, snake-like bodies. No larger than a person’s forearm, these serpentine flyers tuck themselves beneath mossy outcroppings when storms roll in. Yet after the thunder passes, you can see them weaving and darting through the air in erratic patterns, as if drawn by the charged, post-storm energy.

Then there are the dwellers of the deeper cracks. In the canyons, one may uncover scaly, wedge-shaped heads peeking from gaps in the stone. Thick, plated lizards or amphibian-like creatures rest in cool, moist pockets until the storms start, drawn up by the sudden waterfalls that cascade through the rocky channels. They scuttle out, flicking wide tongues to catch stray droplets, then vanish as the water ebbs. It’s said these crevice-beasts have luminous markings that glitter in the half-light of dusk, possibly a lure for smaller prey or a signal among their own kind. Their slow blinking eyes reflect the stars at night, faintly phosphorescent, giving the impression of floating orbs when they move in the deeper canyons.

Who built those monolithic circles and stacked-stone walls—structures that survive the relentless blasts of wind—is another question entirely. Some believe that an ancient, now-extinct people once occupied the plateau, leaving behind only these architectural ghosts. Their descendants, a rumor suggests, are the hooded figures occasionally sighted crossing the canyons by moonlight. Others propose the stone circles were made by a race of giant guardians, individual beings taller than the plateaus themselves, who roamed eons ago, shaping the terrain with their bare hands. In local legend—which is never consistent—some of these giants dissolved back into the land, returning to dust and bedrock. The circles, huts, and pillars may be the imprint of their enormous ephemeral presence. No two travelers tell the same story, and each claims a different reason for the shapes in the stone.

Vines that glow under the moon are, in their own way, just as alive and mysterious. If you look closely, you’ll see tiny organisms that nestle inside the vines’ hollow chambers, emitting a soft, pale light that pulses like a steady heartbeat. At night, these vines coil around spindly trees or snake along rock faces, swirling with dim luminescence that creates a kind of dreamlike corridor, urging some travelers onward. Some describe a hushed stirring of the vines in the wind, almost like whispered conversation. A few travelers that have dared to touch them describe a faint hum, a gentle vibration, as though the vines themselves channel the land’s pulsing lifeforce.

Perhaps the oddest denizens are those only half-confirmed by story and fleeting glimpses: the watchers in the storms. They appear in the very heart of the gale, in moments when lightning fractures the sky. In that single electric instant, the after-image leaves smears of indistinguishable forms looming behind the sheets of swirling water and dust. They might be tall, robed silhouettes; they might be multi-limbed shapes that sway with the thunderclap. Then the flash is gone, and all that remains is the memory of movement—and an eerie sense that something stared back at you. Late-night whispers suggest these watchers maintain the balance of the plateau, fueling the storms and orchestrating the hush that follows. Others say they are illusions conjured by the shock of lightning on the eyes.

Quiet or bold, tangible or intangible, all these lives seem bound by common instincts: a reverence for the storms, a fierce drive to remain hidden until the land has calmed, and an uncanny patience for the cycles of dryness and sudden deluge. Over centuries, they’ve learned to read the flickerings of lightning as portents, the swirl of dust as a sign of which way to travel, the lull in the wind as an invitation to emerge. Their worlds converge at the pockets of moss or the waterfalls that last only minutes, and then each returns to its own domain among the ridges.

In the end, the plateau fosters a paradoxical existence—a place of punishing exposure and secret corners, ceaseless motion and timeless quiet. Those who dwell here, be they discreet creatures scurrying at twilight, gliding serpentine sprites, or possible storm-wielding illusions, have become practitioners of endurance. They have learned to bend rather than break, to move swiftly and silently in the spaces between thunderclaps, to shape themselves around this terrain as intimately as water shapes rock. Long after the last plateau crumbles, perhaps they will remain, carried on the howling wind, guiding the hush that follows the storm and reveling in the fleeting calm that marks each new dawn.
