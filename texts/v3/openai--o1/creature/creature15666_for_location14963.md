They seem to dwell in the long and winding interstices where nature meets machine. You might glimpse them first as silhouettes at the edges of terraces—figures tending vines whose stems coil around steel railings, or crouched alongside a rusted pump housing to coax out a gentle hiss of steam. Then, on a second look, you see their hands: some gloved in rough textiles embroidered with living threads, others sporting exposed metal mechanisms at the knuckles or wrists. Different as they are, their movements share a delicate precision, each gesture made as though it’s part of a larger biological-mechanical dance.  

Wander across a terrace, and you’ll find them in small enclaves—clusters of quiet dwellers who speak softly, stooping over a newly installed irrigation line or calibrating the luminous rods that bathe plants in a subtle golden glow. They build their homes out of layered plates like the ground beneath, carved to fit around trunk-like pillars of fused metal and bark. These dwellings sit half-hidden in fronds and creepers, balancing on sturdy buttresses of old beamwork, so integrated that at a casual glance they vanish into the tiered greenery.

Some among them are slender, the shape of their limbs hinting at subtle augmentations: a forearm that glitters with the sheen of flexible alloy, or a kneecap reinforced by elegantly filigreed bracing. Others bear no outward sign of machination at all; their only “augmentation” might be the swirl of vine tattoos curling behind their ears or the faint glow of embedded beads across their temples. Long generations spent in symbiosis with this environment have made them both caretakers and living components of it. Where a pipeline leaks or a root threatens to choke a crucial vent, they know how to harmonize the conflict.  

They don’t gather in large crowds—most keep in small, familial clusters—some purely old flesh and bone, some deeply entwined with mechanical grafts. If you watch from a distance, you notice them sliding between planes of existence: from the damp green undergrowth to the humming workshops. In the greenhouse pockets, they whisper to exotic seeds, transferring them from nursery pods to teased-out sponges of engineered soil. In the metal-littered corners of a forging bay, others stand gloved in heavy aprons, gripping tongs that place battered rods of alloy into an ancient press. All the while, overhead, mechanical-boned birds wheel. Sometimes, a caretaker will raise an arm, and one of those birds will alight on it, flaring teal-metal feathers, as if acknowledging kinship.

Not all who live here walk on two legs. Along the shadowed edges of each terrace, four-legged creatures—some covered in fur, others sporting metallic ribs—pad silently. These are watchers, protectors, or perhaps just neighbors. They prowl with easy confidence, sniffing at new growth or warm exhaust vents. Archive whispers claim that each beast, no matter how large or strange, was bred or engineered to help keep the equilibrium: some to eat the invasive insects that devour fresh leaves, others to guard the storerooms where vital components are housed. In the hushed spaces between machinery, you can hear them breathing, low and resonant, as though they, too, are part of the plateau’s own living heartbeat.  

Small amphibian-like forms cluster wherever warm water pools: bright-eyed creatures that slip between half-submerged pipes, their rippling skin tinted in the same muted greys and greens as the metal plates. Legend has it these amphibians are like living sensors, sensitive to chemical shifts in the water. They croak in layered, throaty tones whenever the ecosystem’s balance wavers. In response—sometimes without fail—a caretaker or tinkerer appears, checking fluid levels, adjusting dials on a cluster of pumps. In this way, life and machinery shape the plateau’s pulse together, always in conversation.  

Over countless generations, the plateau’s dwellers have developed a craftsman’s sense of time. They speak of “growth cycles” and “weather engines” and “alloys in bloom.” Years pass slowly here, each day measured by the rustle of leaves in newly installed irrigation channels or the arc of shadow cast by a walkway’s lattice overhead. They celebrate expansions—a new terrace built or an old pipe replaced—like another ring in the trunk of a great, collective tree. Their stories revolve around mechanical wonders as much as they do around seeds and sprouts, merging the forging of a new gear with the sprouting of a sapling in a single seamless mythic tapestry.  

One might wonder about their origins: Are they originally human? Descendants of something else? They do not name themselves in a conventional sense. Some pass down the memory that their ancestors arrived when the plateau was still raw steel and stone, a place of scaffolds and scaffolding dreams. Others say they’re the children of the plateau itself, that the superstructure built them as it grew into the sky; that their limbs, whether metal or flesh, started as grafts that tested the environment’s capacity for life. Regardless, all of them share the same caretaker’s spirit. Even those who seem aloof—bent over tinkering with a broken vent fan for hours on end—will pause if the wind shifts in a telling way, carefully loosening a bracket so a growing tree root can pass through.  

Communities dot the plateau at different tiers, each with a distinct identity. On the lower terraces, dwellers specialize in heavy mechanical processes—refining metal ingots, building robust structural supports. Their arms are stronger, their shoulders broader, not solely from muscle but from integrated frameworks. When they greet you, their approach is methodical, words measured as if they have all the time in the world. The middle terraces are home to a horticulturist caste, keen-eyed watchers who talk in quiet, murmuring clusters about cross-pollination and nutrient flows. They clamber along living walkways of woven vines, pausing to check the condition of a fiddly water tube or repot a drooping fern in fresh substrate. Still higher, at the airy top tiers, dwellers turn their attention skyward, studying the drifting flocks of mechanical and organic birds, charting patterns in the wind, forging ephemeral contraptions that might harvest the sunlight for new forms of energy.

Travelers connect these communities, carrying news and gossip (and sometimes seeds or mechanical parts) from level to level. They might be young and eager, their bodies lithe and quick, outfitted with coil-spring exoskeletons for bounding up steep walkways. Others are old, stepping carefully with canes that double as scanning rods attuned to the plateau’s subtle vibrations. All travelers share a personal union of flesh and steel, a synergy that gives them the endurance to roam from the highest, wind-swept spires to the warmer, steam-draped valleys.  

The dwellers’ culture is one of fluid boundaries. They speak of “the living and the built” as a single family. Valves and vines. Gears and petals. Over time, they notice how certain leaves grow in shapes that accommodate mechanical sockets; likewise, new machine parts sometimes reveal themselves with patterns reminiscent of leaf-litter or bark. Some call it artistry, others see it as fate. But none deny how seamlessly biology and technology mesh. In daily life, you see them share stories while perched on the edge of a mechanical root, or gather for communal meals where food grows from carefully irrigated planters that are half-metal trough, half-living bone.  

At moments, a hush comes over them, particularly when a new vent hatch rumbles open or a surge of steam cascades into the open air. During these times, you sense a reverence. It’s as if they, and the steam, and every living thing—and unliving machine—are part of a communal breath. The hush is fleeting. Soon enough, metal clangs resume and the whisper of leaves returns, but the memory remains: a collective knowledge that they share this place, that the plateau is greater than any individual occupant.  

And so, inhabitants of this monumental platform are many rather than one: machinists and horticulturists, scouts and guardians, amphibians that test for purity, four-legged watchers padded with fur or plated with steel, mechanical birds with luminous plumage, and everything in between. Each group is tasked with a piece of the plateau’s ancient puzzle, each living out centuries-old rhythms, watching as new expansions and new plantings fuse seamlessly with the old. Whether you call them a single people or a tapestry of many species, they belong here. They’re shaped by the plateau’s patient cycles, neither fully separate from its vast structure nor wholly identical with it.  

They simply live and breathe in tandem with the mechanical hum, just as the vines cling to warm pipes. They are caretakers and creations at once, stepping lightly through an engineered Eden perched above the clouds, forging slow arcs of existence that measure time in the growth of leaves and the creak of metal. And in every footstep on the interlocking plates, you can hear their quiet vow: to preserve this improbable balance between gear and bloom, metal and flesh, so that this plateau remains alive and harmonic for generations yet to arrive.
