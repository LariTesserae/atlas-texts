They are few, and being few, they have cultivated a way of existing that needs little broadcast. Step lightly along the stony terraces, and you might glimpse them in the peripheral flicker of a doorway, wrapped in layers of thick woven cloth dyed in subdued colors that mirror the moss and lichen. They have no name for themselves that outsiders would grasp—or if they do, it is never spoken beyond a whisper. Some say they are the descendants of an even older folk who vanished into the stone, or perhaps melded into the swirling winds. Others suggest they are wanderers who discovered this ancient plateau and took up the guardianship left behind. Still others believe they are none of these things and that what remains is only a reflection of the place itself: the land learning to animate its surface with living forms.

No single, unified identity holds them together. Instead, they seem held by the threads of a shared purpose: to endure. They move with measured steps, as if each action is carefully budgeted for the cold and thin air. Their faces, if you dare to look closely, show the etchings of wind-burn and unrelenting sun: brow lines worn deep, cheeks reddened from countless storms. Women and men—or some fluid variation of both—speak in tones that hush to blend almost seamlessly with the constant wind. Their language is clipped yet soft, so full of brevity that outsiders might think they speak in cryptic code. But for them, these short, muted words carry entire stories of how to patch a broken wall, which ravine has water, and what sign the sky gave at dawn.

In the earliest light, they emerge from their huts—blocky, ancient, and half-swallowed by moss—and move toward the edges of the plateau. There, they collect condensed water from cunningly carved grooves. They do this in silence or near-silence, interacting in modest gestures. Someone might pass a coiled thread of woven fiber to another, or offer an earthen jug filled with precious water. They wear simple boots or sandals embroidered with patterns reminiscent of lichen and ferns. Their hair is braided with strands of dried vines, not so much a statement of fashion as a method to keep their hair from tangling in the mountain gusts.

Despite the air of simplicity, they are not unsophisticated. The dwellings, aligned to the sun’s crawl across the horizon, betray a deep understanding of astronomy. They track the ecliptic path by subtle markings on stone walls. A rough calendar rises where a particular beam of light occasionally ignites a corner of a worn mural or sketches a fleeting shape inside a communal alcove. These are quiet celebrations, gatherings more recognized by shared breath than by any outward ceremony. In such shared moments, one catches the intangible link between them—an awareness that while the physical environment might appear stark, an unutterable continuity spans generations.

And there are those among them—whether elders, or simply watchers—who tend to the deeper mysteries. If you wander far enough into the settlement, you might find an unremarkable entrance to a cramped tunnel. Inside, these keepers trace lines with fingertips across ancient carvings, or place small offerings of wild seeds, bone shards, smooth river pebbles. Perhaps it is a calendar for rainfall or a tribute to the shape of their half-remembered gods. Glowing moss occasionally illuminates the contours of these cryptic carvings, and in certain seasons, motes of bioluminescent dust trickle through crevices to kindle a half-dreamlike light. Whatever these rituals mean, they are performed with gentle, deliberate reverence. One senses they believe it helps hold the plateau in balance with the storms and slow grinding of the glacial tides far below.

They do not hunt in large parties. The few creatures that live at this altitude are seldom taken except in dire need. Instead, they gather carefully chosen plants—moss, ferns, perhaps even the bright lichens that appear after the freeze recedes. They cultivate hardy root vegetables in sheltered pockets, layering stone upon stone to create an ephemeral greenhouse effect. Occasionally, someone descends the terrifying footpaths to barter with the world below: exchanging small crafts, resin from specialized plants, or intricately root-dyed fabrics for salt and flints. These journeys into the lower canyons become mythic ordeals, returned from with new tales and scars. The traveler’s stories feed the settlement’s shared knowledge: where new gullies have formed, how the winds have shifted, and what the outside world stirs with.

As for the haunting calls that echo in the night—some speak of watchers who leave their huts at dusk and roam hidden cliff sides. They listen for the resonance of shifting stones, the warning rub of glacial ice. Others claim the calls come from an animal more spirit than flesh, a guardian woven from the howling wind and the old bones of the mountain. Yet the inhabitants never appear startled; their faces, by the glow of a small firepit, remain composed, as though recognizing a familiar voice. When dawn breaks, a hush lingers, and one or two of them might hum a soft chant in answer—part lullaby, part morning call.

In quieter moments, they gather in small clusters near communal hearths. There, a faint sense of warmth spreads from the layered embers at the heart of carefully built fire rings. The conversation, when it flows, concerns the simplest necessities—how the trickle of water from the last storm has slowed, how a creeping fungus threatens an important patch of lichen, who has had trouble breathing in the thinning air. But occasionally, the talk shifts to older stories. Sitting around the fire, a few might recount dim legends of an age before the blocks of stone formed these dwellings, when great birds nested on the cliffs, or when the place was merely a stopping point for travelers on pilgrimages of unknown purpose. Like the wind’s endless swirl, the stories never stay in one place. They evolve with time, carrying new meaning as one voice finishes and another picks it up, weaving the old knowledge anew.

They are not immortal, these dwellers. Their bones sometimes fail, their breaths run short in the cold. But they have harnessed a remarkable resilience: a sense that every inhabitant—human, creature, vine—plays a role in balancing this precarious domain. For them, living here is less about conquering the elements and more about standing in ceaseless conversation with them. Even the huts themselves are part of that conversation. Some appear half-abandoned, their stones rearranged by wind or shifting ice. Yet no one demolishes them; the remains linger, occasionally repurposed, as though the settlement is alive in flux, an organism that grows or shrinks with the demands of time and survival.

In the light of the moon—especially a full moon—a sharper clarity descends. You might see a few of them watching the flickers of phosphorescent auroras dancing along the ridgelines, or waiting quietly for the rumored glow of secret moss. Their faces in that moonlight reflect more than just curiosity. There is something like awe, but also acceptance, for the plateau’s uncanny spectacles belong as much to them as to the land. And so they stand witness, unafraid, letting the hush and the swirl of wind conspire with the faint glimmer of natural sorcery.

Perhaps that is what truly defines them: a deep accord with the unpredictability of this high realm. One might call them a tribe of stone-and-wind people; or perhaps they have always just been the land’s own slow heartbeat, given form. Whether they are the direct descendants of an ancient empire, a new gathering of wanderers, or the entire mountain’s half-lucid dream, they live in a small geography that resonates with a vast, unseen tapestry. Bound by storms and meager resources, they endure by interweaving all they have with all that is around them.

So, when you stand at the edge of that chiseled settlement, feeling every breath drawn out by the altitude and hearing the groan of the wind across ancient grooves, know there are those who inhabit that raw hush. They pass through the corridors of tumbled stone, weaving baskets of mountain fibers, singing wind-tuned melodies beneath battered wooden beams, and keeping the old ways alive. They are watchers and listeners, silent architects of a precarious harmony, custodians of a place that balances precariously between emptiness and abundant life. They do not rule here; they simply belong, and in belonging, they become one more quiet current in the eternal swirl of ancient stone and unceasing wind.
