They seem, at first, to be only plants. Towering stalks with bleached bark, knotted trunks that cradle the fragile clay walls—creatures of cellulose and mineral, steadfast in the desert heat. At night, slivers of phosphorescence ripple across some of their surfaces, as if a faint, sallow blood courses through them. Listen closer, and the wind that scours the barren pathways offers a clue: these stiffened boughs rasp against each other not just from chance gusts but from a subtle, rhythmic pressure that hints at something more deliberate. Across the silent rows of mud dwellings, clumps of fibrous fronds pulse faintly in time with the shifting air, a slow, collective sway that looks almost communicative. They are the bulwark that’s taken hold in this waterless realm, but they are only part of the story.

Beneath the sun-baked soil, through the cracks and hollows that riddle the bedrock, dwell intricate webs of root-like structures, far denser and more active than the pale stems aboveground would suggest. These roots shift continually, grinding through the dry earth in fits and starts, following the subterranean world’s unhurried pulse. They wind around ancient fragments of broken clay jars, around bits of chipped stone tools half-buried, locking them in place or wearing them down to dust. By day, this root network siphons whatever heat or mineral-laden current it can tap. By night, it exhales a hush of gases, so subtle you might only detect them as the faintest swirl in the quiet air.

The single-mindedness of this underground root-mass all but erases the idea of individual “plants.” They share a collective drive: each trunk above the surface is an outpost, a column of living matter feeding from, and reporting back to, a vast web beneath. They are not meek or unconscious things—they respond to changes faster than a mere plant should. Should a half-buried scaffold or ancient wall begin to collapse, thick tendrils twist up to coil around it, applying a strange structural pressure. They seem to “sense” the settlement’s shape and aims, striving to preserve the manmade outlines alongside their own flourish. No one is quite sure how this merging began—whether some lost civilization introduced these species for reinforcement, or whether the seeds floated in on a scorching wind. Whatever the cause, they now stand as the city’s living architecture.

And yet, there is something else that dwells here—neither plant, nor purely mineral. Beneath the scoured ridges and plateaus of dust, if one listens past the wind’s roar, you might hear the faintest scraping. Some describe it as a chorus of subtle clicks, distant but growing momentarily clearer whenever a gust dies down. It arises from within the labyrinth of worn stone tunnels at the outskirts of the settlement, deep in the cracks that lead below the ridgeline. If you dare to peer through these narrow crevices, shining a dim lamp or using a handful of phosphorescent spores for light, you might glimpse a flicker of movement: shapes that recede almost as soon as they’re revealed.

Local legends—were there any locals left—would likely speak of small, heat-sculpted creatures that scuttle in the darkness. Their forms are rumored to be compact, segmented carapaces of mineral-tinged chitin that reflect the city’s improbable glimmers. They have spindly limbs ending in hooked claws that allow them to climb the soot-smooth passageways. Their eyes (or eye-like protrusions) might be translucent lumps that refract minimal light into ghostly images. Some say these creatures bask at the mouth of the tunnels when the hot winds strike, feeding on the radiant energy before fleeing back into the depths once the dust thickens again. Others believe the creatures’ shells are ridged with bizarre, funnel-like growths that capture drifting clouds of spore-dust from the plants above, using it as a nutrient or a breeding catalyst. No one wholly knows, for no living observer has studied them closely—and returned to speak of it.

Still, the clearest, most ubiquitous presence here are the strange “plants” themselves. Sometimes, under the swirl of dusty half-light, one can just make out the faintest motion that goes beyond the wind’s push: a winding, deliberate curve of a trunk or a shifting kink in a heavy root. It’s there and gone, so subtle that you could doubt your eyes. But stand in one spot long enough, ignoring the searing dryness, and you’ll see them orchestrate the city’s slow renewal. A collapsed wall, half fallen, will be discovered days later alarmingly restored, propped up by interwoven, fibrous tendrils. The illusions of emptiness fade as you realize how actively these inhabitants sculpt their environment. Over time, they have fused with the clay so thoroughly that to pull a single trunk loose would bring half a building down with it.

At the base of some of these plants, the thick bark merges seamlessly with lumps of hardened mortar. You can see how the earlier inhabitants might have cultivated this synergy—harvesting the plants’ spines for structural beams, layering the living bark into clay bricks to keep walls from splitting in the punishing heat. Indeed, the outlines of tools left scattered in the dust—stone blades, chisels of peculiar design—suggest knowledge of these symbiotic properties. Yet the city’s original builders seem to have gone, leaving no bones, no footprints in the dust, no definitive trace. Their civilization might have withered away when the temperature soared beyond what mortal bodies could endure, or perhaps they left intentionally, migrating to cooler horizons. Some whisper that the network of roots beneath the city consumed them, reabsorbing all that was flesh until only the shapes of their buildings and a handful of stone implements remained.

Of course, the real “minds” at work could lie deep below. Some suspect that, within the bedrock, where pockets of volcanic heat well up, there is a shared “heart” of biological and mineral layers fused together over centuries. From that heart, filaments reach out like neuronal branches, guiding each aboveground stalk. That might explain the near-sentient design of plant-latticed walls, the carefully maintained footpaths kept free of too much crumbled debris, as though the city’s superstructure were curated by an invisible caretaker.

For travelers who wander in—and they are few—this city of dust and bleached vegetation feels simultaneously vacant and alive. With a quiet step, you’ll notice the ground resonates underfoot, carrying vibrations of whatever stirs below. A wave of heat might roll through again, and from above, you’ll hear the friction-cacophony of brittle spines and hollow logs meeting in a clatter that mimics laughter or conversation. Every gust passes like a challenge, leaving behind a chorus of shifting, chattering growth. And in the lull that follows, the hush is so profound it raises hairs on your arms, as if unseen watchers hold their collective breath. Then there’s that echoing click—just once—hinting that something else shares the space.

They say that if you dare to sleep among the broken walls under a hush of still air, you’ll dream of voices. They speak in half-formed phrases—groaning timbres like earthen moans, all surging from the rocky depths. The dream-voices impart fleeting images: the city as it once was, crowded with figures wearing clay-packed clothing, forging stone tools in the sweltering gloom. Then you see them slip away into the tunnels, hand-in-hand with thick roots that guide them downward. You awaken in a cold sweat—despite the choking heat—to find the gloom unbroken and your footprints half-erased by drifting dust.

So who or what lives here? In a sense, the entire place breathes as a single organism. The tall, bone-white stalks, the thick, twisting trunks rooted in the rubble, the shimmering mycelium-like roots that probe every fissure in the stone, and the fleeting insectoid mysteries that scuttle out of sight—they form the city’s inhabitants. They move, slowly or suddenly, in ways not easily discerned. They keep the clay arches standing, the old footpaths passable, the stone ridges shaping an arena for wind and dust. Whether they feel a sense of time or history is uncertain, but their presence is pervasive—an unspoken vow that the structures, and whatever flicker of memory remains of the old builders, will not simply vanish into lifeless dust.

This is what endures: a layered community of botanical, mineral, and subtle arthropod forms, woven together by primal heat. The pale, fibrous spines that crown the city are just the visible aspect of an ecosystem older than any written record. They’ve anchored an abandoned settlement and co-opted it into their life cycle, forging a silent contract with clay bricks and half-buried stone implements. The city looks uninhabited, yet it’s humming: living matter saturates every corner, entwining with the skeletons of the past in a slow but tireless ballet of survival.

In that pocket world of scorching air and drifting dust, this conglomerate of life goes on, day after day, century on century—creaking, clicking, and glowing faintly in the gloom, waiting for visitors who never truly fathom their presence. They are patient, sturdy, serene in the hush that settles after each gust. And they are the city’s eternal residents—neither purely plant, nor animal, nor mineral, but a fusion of all three, bound by heat and dust and the deep, stony earth, persisting where no water can.
