They live everywhere the architecture and the water intersect, though at first you’d swear there is no life at all. Looming in the dark, those ancient megalithic blocks present a stage all but empty: no rushing shoals of fish, no scuttling crustaceans. Yet the longer one drifts, the more one senses the subtle heartbeat of occupation, a presence woven through the endless veneer of cold, submerged stone.

If you begin with the obvious, you notice first the greenery—the colorless algae and the softly undulating fronds that sprout from every chink in the masonry. At certain angles, these plants almost resemble curtains of paper-thin fiber, leaf after leaf layered until they form dense tapestries. They are numerous, and it is easy to imagine that they alone constitute what “lives” in this watery realm. If you listen through the hush, you will hear them flex and rustle, responding to slight eddies in the currents. It is not so much a chorus as an almost imperceptible trembling—a collective, rhythmic shift that moves across columns and arches. Invisibly, in each cell, the living tissue processes trace amounts of minerals and vestigial nutrients. Every leaf owes its existence to specks of archaic sunlight from above or from thermal currents rising from below. Entire families of algae and moss inhabit the cracks, some fractal and spidery, others forming broad, languid leaves that look like floating veils. These are the first inhabitants, easy to see and describe, though they seem as quiet and unthinking as the stone to which they cling.

But watch from the corner of your eye, and you begin to suspect something else entirely. Drift behind a tall, worn pillar, and the shadows themselves might shift—so slowly you could dismiss it as your own movement. Wait long enough, however, and subtle patterns emerge. Certain pillars grow pockets of vegetation that sway in ways at odds with the gentle current. Unfurling cilia-like threads drift out into the water, almost tasting what passes by. You might notice, too, a strange trembling out along the edges of the labyrinthine catwalks, as frond-tips touch one another and then recoil, like fingers brushing in the dark. At first, it appears wholly accidental—just overgrown weeds tangling in each other. Yet, with time, a watchful eye can perceive a shared intelligence among these clusters, a faint, communal sense of purpose. 

Perhaps they communicate through chemical signals that pass in slow pulses through the tangling vines, or via subtle light-produced vibrations. It’s impossible to be certain—and indeed, you may never glean a conclusive answer. But their synchronicity suggests a life-form that acts in concert, each patch of vegetal growth linked by a common impetus, an organism that has colonized every available surface. Their unified presence shapes the hush of the ocean, transforming it from empty gloom into a place with a secret occupant—an occupant with no singular shape but countless filaments, stalks, or leaves waving gently. One might call it a megalopolis of flora or a single, vast plant with countless nodes. 

Look deeper, and you’ll see countless micro-lives embedded in the translucent leaves, as though the “foliage” nurtures smaller symbionts: tiny beadlike spores or minuscule polyps that cling to the underside of each frond, feasting on whatever nutrients the mother growth provides. They, too, move little, drifting faintly when a subtle wave passes through. If you approach them—though you cannot truly approach anything swiftly here, for the water is so thick and still—you might observe the pale orbs of these spore-like creatures contract, sensing your presence. They seem aware, in that tentative, edge-of-threshold way certain microorganisms are: alive, reactive, but never in a hurry. Together, these smaller clusters of life form an ecosystem that calls each patch of algae “home”—and in so doing, they reinforce the labyrinth’s slow, contemplative movement, adding a nearly invisible hum to the silence.

So, are these plants alone in what might be called sentience? You might suspect not. There are hints of something older, something that dwells in the architecture itself. The geometry of these miles of corridors and arches hasn’t been wholly abandoned to the green. Now and then, a block of stone is suspiciously clear of algae, as though an unseen caretaker brushes it clean, an echo of the place’s original forms. You’ll never glimpse any caretaker in motion; there is no silhouette scuttling between the pillars. But perhaps some presence glides among the corridors at intervals so long they fall outside our usual sense of time. In a realm where centuries pass unheeded, it’s conceivable that a single entity, older than memory, roams the thoroughfares at a tempo measured in eons. If so, it leaves no footprints on the siltless floors. 

Stand at the threshold of a grand hall, your eyes adjusting to the persistent half-light, and you might sense—rather than see—shadows that are not cast by your own shape. If you peer closer, you can detect entire alcoves so delicately shrouded in leaf-filaments that they form near-solid screens. Now and then, these shrouds quiver without any apparent flow of water, parting just enough to let your gaze slip through. Beyond them lie partial glimpses of deeper passageways. You might spot the faintest swirl of what could be a limb or an appendage receding into the blackness, something simultaneously plantlike and architectural, as though a corner of stone had grown and moved of its own accord. Then the curtain of moss closes again, and the labyrinth is as blank as before. 

Whether these half-sensed presences are actual creatures or illusions conjured by the mind in a place so profoundly alien, none can say for sure. The labyrinth’s stony arrangement is so intricately carved that you could almost imagine it was sentient in itself—a monstrous intelligence shaped into cyclopean columns, dreaming through millennia of submersion. In this line of thinking, the “residents” of the domain might be the place itself: the towering geometry that so thoroughly occupies every inch of the sea floor. The columns, arches, and walls carry on an ancient, unspoken conversation through faint tremors, expansions, or settling shifts of stone. If so, the aqua flora are merely an extension of, or a partner to, that conversation, responding in their slow dance to calls too deep for ears to detect.

Indeed, you can entertain a mosaic of possibilities: a single, interlaced “reef” of vegetation with emergent intelligence; silent watchers shaped from the stone, patrolling eon after eon in a half-waking state; or perhaps druidic caretakers who left this place behind, yet whose spirit lingers in unseen pockets of architecture. In the hush, all these theories feel plausible. 

Several explorers—were there any to investigate—would come away with different accounts. One might claim no animate souls remain and that only the plants persist, purely biological. Another might describe glimpses of strange, translucent shapes in the corners of vision. A third could spin a tale of the entire environment as a single living organism, stone and moss heaving in slow unison. All could be true in their own way, for the labyrinth offers no single face, and the low-lit water conceals its truths behind layers of drifting algae and long-lost stone artistry.

Ultimately, who or what “lives” here is defined by the pace of existence in these waters—so difficult for a surface-dweller to perceive. Life emerges and evolves across centuries rather than hours or days. In such a measured continuum, the slightest sign of movement can be profound. The vegetation weaves a single tapestry, and whichever spectral inhabitants or half-sentient shadows lurk behind the next arch remain as difficult to grasp as a dream glimpsed on waking. No clamor disturbs them, no bright light chases them from their homes. In the hush and the cold, they simply endure, woven into the monolithic stone, part of an arrangement so vast and primeval it transcends any individual living thing. 

So, when one speaks of who “lives” in that silent ocean fortress, the only certain answer is that all of it does—every block, each wavering frond, every swirl of hidden presence. The place itself is the tenant, forever abiding in that watery hush, bound so seamlessly to its own quiet dwellers that, dissolved in time, they have all become one.
