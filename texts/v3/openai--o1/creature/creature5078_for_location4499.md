It can be difficult to pin precisely who or what lives here, because at first glance, the inhabitants blend almost seamlessly with the environment itself. Everything is so strangely organic—rock that’s barely stone, water that mingles with air, spires that reach upward like living trunks—and the creatures fit right into that paradigm. They seem born of the same forces that arranged these half-formed plates of the ground. If you watch for more than a few moments, though, you’ll begin to discern several distinct kinds of life, each adapted in a bewildering way to this half-constructed realm.

First, there are the spire-builders, or what might better be called the spire-growers. Their bodies have an elongated grace that echoes the tall, slender columns that tower over the damp rock. From a distance, they may look like an extension of the columns themselves: vaguely translucent, with curious patterns of refracted light coursing through them. It’s easy to mistake them for spire-shaped silhouettes when they stand still, heads tilted upward, their limbs folded into that same tapering form. Yet they move—slowly, thoughtfully. Rarely do they seem to rush. Their bodies glimmer with faint tones of rose or pale green, as if they contain hidden channels of energy that pulse in time with the environment’s resonances.

They appear to operate in small collectives. You’ll see three or four at a time inspecting the surface of a spire, or guiding new growth at its base. Their communication is subtle: a series of pulses or flickers seen in the glow of their bodies, accompanied by quiet percussive taps that resonate through the ground. When a group decides to raise a new column, you can see them pressing their hands (if one can call those multi-jointed appendages “hands”) to the moist ground. Something in the planetary crust responds—rocks shift, minerals liquefy, and a new root-like structure emerges. Then, over hours or perhaps days, it stretches upward, weaving itself into the existing architecture. It’s a technology that looks half magical, half biological. Watching them, it becomes clear that their dwellings aren’t buildings in the traditional sense, but living expansions of the planet’s own crust—augmented, coaxed, shaped.

Beyond the spire-growers, there’s a smaller cohort of winged creatures that are easily missed if your eyes aren’t prepared for their delicate forms skimming water’s surface. They have wings like folded glass, which catch the dim light and scatter it in thin arcs of color. When they perch for a moment on the ridges of damp rock, those wings fold back, and you see a soft carapace of pale opaline segments. They appear to feed on the mineral-laden rivulets that seep out of the rock plates, using elongated mouthparts to siphon up droplets. Occasionally, they gather in small swarms—an ephemeral shimmer that drifts from one spire to another. Their presence gives the region a curious buoyancy; for all the heavy motion in the ground, these winged beings provide a counterpoint of weightless grace.

A different sort of life lurks in the half-solid, spongy patches of terrain. These beings move along the boundary where the rock is still forming—a shimmering boundary that’s warm to the touch, exuding a mineral-laden steam. Their bodies are flexible, elongated, and they press themselves low against the ground, as if feeding off the heat or the chemical stew in the newborn stone. It takes a careful eye to notice them because their coloration mirrors the environment’s own palette: metallic streaks, dull grays, and occasional glints of greenish-blue. They slide between newly opened fissures, nearly vanishing in those wet cracks. Sometimes they cluster in small pools of heated mineral water, letting out soft whistles or moans that resonate with the sizzling steam. The local vegetation, meager as it is, seems to benefit from whatever byproduct these creatures exhale, for the wiry stems and fruiting caps always appear more robust in their vicinity.

And then there are the “heavy-movers,” hulking shapes that traverse the edges of this world, near the boundaries of sight. Bulky, broad-shouldered forms—yet oddly fluid in motion, as if their bones are hinged in more than one direction. Their skin (or plating) is mottled with thick ridges, reminiscent of the newly formed stone plates underfoot. They lumber in small groups, moving carefully along the abrupt rises and drops in terrain. At times, you can hear their low calls—reverberations that rattle through the air with a staticky crackle, as if their voices carry an electrical charge. When they gather around the trickling pools, these calls become a gentle rumble, and one might see them dipping elongated mouths or tentacle-like lips into the water, absorbing nutrients or minerals that the spires’ elaborate drainage systems have concentrated there.

Yet these large creatures aren’t as brutish as they appear; they exhibit a thoughtful deliberation in their movements and interactions. Occasionally you might see a softer side: a pair leaning their foreheads together, perhaps exchanging resonance. It’s as if, in each rumbling vibration, they transfer complex feelings or share knowledge about how the land continues to shift. Some speculate they share a close bond with the spire-growers, perhaps assisting in shaping the land on a more macroscopic scale—crushing down newly formed lumps or guiding the flow of water by shifting blocks of softened stone with their powerful limbs. In rare instances, you can catch glimpses of the spire-builders approaching these gentle giants, standing before them as though in silent dialogue.

Hovering at the margins of all this life, there is an even more cryptic presence. Flickers of... something in the twilight air, as though the land’s own newness has given rise to intangible entities. At times, you can see the barest outline of a shape—a suggestion of limbs or branching appendages—caught in the peripheral glow of the spires. A moment later, there’s nothing. The local creatures do not seem startled by these apparitions; if anything, they all but ignore them, as though such fleeting phantoms are a normal part of the tapestry. Perhaps these shapes are ephemeral life-forms that exist partly in another dimension or state of being, feeding on the constant flux of stone and water. Or maybe they’re some form of atmospheric anomaly the planet itself conjures, reminiscent of heat-mirages but with a more keenly alive quality.

All these inhabitants appear to exist in a shifting partnership with the place: drawing from it, fundamentally shaping it, woven so closely into it that it’s hard to tell where the land ends and the living forms begin. The spire-growers manipulate gravitational or geological forces; the winged gatherers pollinate or nourish micro-flora with their glimmering flights. The heat-feeders recycle minerals and moisture in the spongy areas, helping to create fertile pockets for those wiry plants, and the heavy-movers guide the grander steps of the terrain’s ongoing formation.

To observe them is to witness a living system so integrated that you sense no strict hierarchy—more a synchronized dance of varied participants, each adding to the place’s developmental surge. They seem unconstrained by time in the way we know it: as though they’re prepared to coax this realm into being over years or centuries, or to transform it in days if the conditions are right.

Even within their alien forms, you can detect something akin to wonder. There’s a quiet intelligence in the spire-growers’ luminous gestures, a primal yet gentle force in the heavy-movers’ calls, and a whimsical grace in the swarms of translucent wings. All exist in a living continuum, sharing pulses of energy that keep the environment not just alive, but in a perpetual state of becoming. When the ground quivers, they pause in unison and listen. When a crack in the sky flickers with an unknown energy, they turn their attention upward. The place and its inhabitants react together, move together, evolve together.

Ultimately, who lives here? Many beings, each half-born out of this land that’s itself still forming. Some appear wise and ancient, despite the newness of everything; others are ephemeral and almost playful, flitting through water and air with delicate grace. Yet all of them remain poised on a shared threshold, caught in that shimmering moment when the world hasn’t finished deciding what it is. And within that moment, they live not just as subjects of this realm, but as co-creators—eager, patient, and humming with the potential of what might come next.
