They are not numerous in any conventional sense. When you step into this shimmering landscape, you might suspect it is nearly empty of life, so quiet and immobile are those who dwell here. But the creatures—and the people, if “people” is the proper word—are indeed present, though they rarely reveal themselves fully at first glance. Let your eyes adjust to the bright dust, and you’ll catch a glimpse of them: figures half-blending with gray mineral outcroppings, or silhouettes flickering in the haze near half-buried machinery.

Some are best described as wanderers of the rock: their bodies encased in heavy servomechanical armor that appears fused with living tissue. The plating follows the segmentation of an insect’s exoskeleton, but you sense an intelligence in the way they pause at certain ridge lines and tilt their heads to scan the canyons. Their limbs are elongated, their joints wrapped in a dull, calcified veneer that merges seamlessly into mechanical joint-cylinders. Slender whisker-like sensors protrude from their faces, vibrating in the still air—these are listening rods, tuned to the subtle resonance of the planet’s tectonic hum. When they move, they do so slowly, as if the notion of haste does not exist here.

Along the undersides of rocky ledges, you see entire colonies of smaller arthropod-forms that seem more clearly biological: their carapaces reflect pale crystals embedded in their exoskeletons, shining faintly in the amber light. They anchor themselves to the coolest, shadowed minerals, feeding on microscopic crystal growths or filtering dust motes from the air through delicate cilia. These smaller creatures are communal—clusters of them bunch together, an unspoken choreography guiding them to shift, turn, and root about in near-unison. They live quietly but are integral to the ecosystem: their presence scours away old dust and reveals fresh mineral surfaces.

Occasionally, large, lumbering quadrupeds appear in the distance, so tall that their heads can crest the canyon walls. At first, they look like moving fortresses—great shells resembling fossilized coral meshed with sleek, alloy scaffolding. You can see the faint motion of enormous diaphragms along their flanks, as if they are breathing in ways entirely alien. Long, spined tails sweep the dust, leaving spiraling patterns in their wake. Some of these colossi carry clustered pods on their backs, harnessed with metal frames. You might catch a glimpse of smaller entities perched there—perhaps shepherds or attendants, or maybe symbiotic parasites. These watchers ride the giant creatures as the pair carry on a slow, patient pilgrimage across the labyrinthine terrain, stopping periodically so that the watchers can tend to the pods, which appear to be hives or repositories of nutrients gleaned from deep beneath the surface.

Then there are the winged shapes, rarely seen at rest. Large and dark, with wings that unfold in multiple layers, they soar on thermals, gliding effortlessly across the canyons. Come closer—if you can track one to its perch—and you might see that their wings are not leathery membranes but segments of shell-like plating that can splay open to different angles. Veins of luminescent tubes run along the wing struts, which pulse with a faint light as though pumping some glimmering fluid. Are they alive, or are they machines carrying cargo or data from one distant tower to another? Perhaps they are a fusion of the two—this planet does not seem picky about the line between organic and constructed.

Amid the canyons and high plateaus, machines of another sort prevail. Unclear whether they are inhabited, autonomous, or something in-between, these tall metal edifices creak and swivel with impossible slowness. Dark pylons stand upright at the edges of mesas, dotted with small metallic spheres that shift and click along tracks cut into the metal’s surface. Step close to one, and you might sense the faintest shimmer of electromagnetic forces washing outward from it, as though it is broadcasting or receiving signals from deep below the crust. Sometimes, you see a cluster of the pylon-spheres detach, rolling down slender rails to deposit themselves in a waiting container or drone. These pylon clusters may be components of the planet’s “nervous system,” linking site to site, maintaining the subdued hum that pervades the air.

Farther down in the valleys, you come across the watchers. They are shaped more like humans than anything else, yet each one is clad in modular plates that cover everything but the eyes—a single horizontal visor that reveals hints of motion behind it. Their posture is meticulous: they pause at intervals to press their gauntleted hands against the rock as if listening for subtle shifts in the planet’s tectonic chant. Their gestures are slow, their voices—if they speak at all—are seldom heard. Occasionally, they cluster around the towering pistons that thrust into the ground. You watch as one or two watchers connect thick tubes from the piston bases to some concealed port in their armor, as if siphoning power or resource from the planet’s mechanical heartbeat. When they finish this mysterious feeding or communion, they retreat to the shade of an overhang or into the plating of the nearest structure, leaving no sign they were ever there except for footprints ground smoothly into the dust.

In the sprawling complexes of skeletal girders—a labyrinth of walkways, pipes, and catwalks—something else moves with quiet efficiency: small, multi-legged drones carrying modules of ore or filaments of crystal. The drones bear the insignia of no company or faction. Instead, their surfaces are etched with swirling lines that might form the outline of the local creatures or references to the planet’s resonant frequencies. Some watchers, or perhaps caretaker-beings of unknown origin, guide these drones with simple gestures—hand signals, pulses of light from wrist devices. In these moments, you catch a glimpse of an ancient, practiced relationship: the watchers and the drones function together, each fulfilling roles clearly honed over millennia.

Some watchers are rumored to dwell in the spires themselves. Through metal-shuttered windows, you catch suggestions of shapes behind tinted glass. Low glow-panels flicker, large screens (or altars) sputter with data or symbolic patterns. The watchers move slowly inside these spaces—if they move at all—wearing archaic respirators or harnesses that link them to the building’s systems. They seem less to live in the spires than to integrate with them, part biological caretaker, part living component in a vast industrial system. Through their wired suits, they might sense every vibration in these towers, controlling the flow of resources to distant platforms, adjusting the timing of those colossal pistons, or connecting with the slow knowledge stored in subterranean data vaults.

Still, for all the silent city’s machinery, the original inhabitants—perhaps those massive crustaceans and arthropod swarms—hold their own dominion. For these ancient creatures, the metal outposts are little more than an abnormal flourish on the dusty plane. One can imagine them observing the watchers and the spires with a resigned acceptance, as though they have witnessed so many geological transformations over epochs that another layer of foreign technology hardly matters. Occasionally, you see a giant insectoid raise its head in the direction of a humming tower, then resume its deliberate grazing of mineral shards, unperturbed and unimpressed.

And lording above all are the timeless “leviathans”—those gargantuan quadrupeds marching with unstoppable slowness through the eons. Some watchers adopt them as living platforms for travel or resource storage. Others maintain a reverential distance. Legend might say that these behemoths were once part of the planet’s living core—formed in ancient times from the crust itself—before they emerged onto the surface to roam the canyons.

Even the sky-lords—those winged silhouettes—may have grown from mechanical seeds planted by an earlier civilization. Or else they are the last natural flyers of a world that long ago merged with its technology. In either case, they remain aloof, rarely descending unless drawn by the calls of the watchers or the flickering of light from one of the tall spires.

And so, life here marches to a rhythm that is not a heartbeat but a planetary pulse—a quiet, inexorable force from the world’s depths. To walk among these inhabitants is to witness an ecology that blends metal and meat, dust and sinew, ancient energy and modern design. Each being seems to carry a piece of the planet’s essence within, shaped by eons of dryness and slow, methodical change. If you linger long enough, you too might begin to sync with that endless hum, letting the drifting dust settle on your skin until you feel the presence of the watchers, the drones, the arthropods, and the quiet juggernauts all layering around you. Here, life takes forms unimaginable in greener realms, life that endures and mutates in tune with a world that moves at the pace of stone itself.
