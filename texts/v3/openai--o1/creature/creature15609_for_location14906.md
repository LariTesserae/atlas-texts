They drift and dwell in the currents, more like the subtle shifting of mood than any single, uniform population. At first, you only sense them as tiny flickers in the corner of your vision—the bare hints of scaled bodies sliding through corridors of drowned stone—or as faint movements along the walls where crab-like silhouettes scuttle in search of morsels. But as you sink deeper into this ancient city’s watery streets, you discover more of the fullness of its occupants. They are myriad, forging alliances with the shadows. Some are small as the tip of a finger, others as large as your imagination dares to conjure.

Many are fish, though “fish” seems hardly descriptive enough. They weave in and out of doorways and window arches, color sifting across their bodies in luminous patterns. Some spark turquoise and violet as they pass a momentary glow of plankton, while others look pale and ghostly, sallow reflections in the deep. They move in schools that swirl like living ribbons—one moment swirling down a column-lined avenue, the next darting through a broken vault long since crusted over with coral. These shimmering clusters seem choreographed, as if they share one collective mind that senses shifts in current or the faint approach of something larger.

Close to the corroded walls and hidden corners, you’ll find the encrusting creatures, the small guardians of the city who rarely leave their chosen spot. Barnacles grip the surfaces in dense constellations of ridged shells, fanning open to filter feed whenever the water stirs. Sponges, in improbable shapes and radiant hues, affix themselves to submerged archways, turning once-functional masonry into something organic and colorful. They breathe and pulse with slow determination, quietly filtering the city’s waters. Meanwhile, delicate anemones stretch their tentacles out from tiny alcoves, their translucent arms shimmering like faint, living lanterns in the murk, waiting for the smallest touch of food to drift their way.

Nestled within these nooks, nearly lost in the thick forest of coral branches and spines, countless crustaceans creep. You might see a small crab, no bigger than a child’s palm, boldly treading across a mosaic floor whose tiles have been largely devoured by algae and silt. Others are more elusive: shy lobsters nudging hints of antennae out of hidden recesses, sounding out vibrations with gentle, rhythmic movements. And in the lower reaches of broken towers, where little light penetrates, squat, ghostly shrimps gather in the hollows. If a gentle pebble rolls by, or you brush too close, the entire cluster flees in a chain reaction, vanishing in a swirl of drifting sediment.

Where columns stand half-toppled, eels thread themselves through cracks and cavities, slender forms melding into the gloom. They slide through window frames like ribbons of living night, heads peeping out to observe any intruder’s presence with an unblinking gaze. Some eels appear nearly luminous with pale stripes or spots, while others blend perfectly into mottled rock patterns. One might surface near your shoulder, inquisitive and unafraid, only to vanish in a pulse of movement that leaves barely a ripple behind.

Here and there, the seaweed stands tall—a scattered attempt at a vertical forest in this collapse of ancient architecture. Unlike the teeming shoals of scaled swimmers, the weeds sway almost languidly, their leaves shifting in slow motion like silent sentinels. Tiny, darting fish nestle among the fronds, safe from open-water predators. Sometimes, a larger herbivorous creature—perhaps a blunted, oval-shaped fish—will graze among those leaves, nibbling with deliberate, almost meditative focus.

And in the vast overhead spaces where the city’s grand halls once opened to the sky—and now open into the layering darkness of the ocean—shadowy figures roam. They are not always easy to see, but their presence is undeniable: heavier-bodied creatures patrolling the far edges. Sleek shapes glide with a lazy power, stirring schools of smaller fish ahead of them. A flash of silver fins might signify a hunting predator, or it might be a passing curiosity with broad pectoral wings that ripple in slow, purposeful strokes. Occasionally, you catch only the echo of their wake: a subtle disturbance in the water that sends a flurry of startled fish scattering.

Down in the thickest gloom, close to where the sea floor drops away into deeper chasms, there dwell stranger things—rarely glimpsed forms that thrive in partial darkness and warm upwellings from below. Perhaps they have adapted to this drowned city as a labyrinth all their own, unreachable except to those who can navigate the deeper crevices. Their presence reveals itself in shifting cloud-like shapes, pale or bioluminescent, gliding with sinuous grace. Or you may sense their approach as a series of pulses and vibrations echoing through the water, more a tactile impression than a visual one. Sometimes, only the faint swirl of a hot current—scented with unfamiliar minerals—betrays their lurking domain below arches long sealed by sediment and time.

And then, weaving through it all, there is the smallest life: plankton that glow in rare blooms, drifting as glowing motes in the darkness. When they burst into luminescent displays, they skim across the surfaces of statues, illuminating chipped stone faces in eerie half-light. In those brief instants, you catch glimpses of the city’s former intricacies—delicate carvings on what looks like an archway, or faint lines on a sculpted plinth that might have once housed a statue. A moment later, the glow dims, and those secrets slip back into obscurity, leaving the watchers—fish, crustacean, diver, or passing giant—wondering if they imagined those flashes of lost history.

Though vegetation is sparse, and there’s no grand forest of kelp here, the inhabitants more than make up for it in their dizzying biodiversity. Everything in the city is alive, multi-layered, and thoroughly fused. You see starfish clinging to what might have been a column’s ornate capital, their arms splayed as if they’re claiming the shape as their own. Brittle stars flit, quicksilver, behind them, losing themselves in cracks no wider than a hand’s breadth. Meanwhile, sea cucumbers inch along the floor with imperturbable calm, suctioning softly as they erode and recycle what the rest of the city’s dwellers leave behind. And at the cascading edges of submerged plazas, octopuses lurk in dens behind broken masonry—genius watchers in camouflage, absorbing new colors from their environment as if sampling them for a tapestry.

If you pause long enough, letting yourself feel the pulse and breath of this place, you realize each inhabitant plays a role. The shrimp crackle like static, scraping at algae growing on the remains of a marble floor. Small fish glean parasites from larger ones in a delicate dance of survival and mutualism. Pointed shells collect in corners, each carrying a hermit crab staking its claim to a portable fortress. An unseen orca or a curious sea lion—even if such stragglers from a different zone drift in—becomes part of this living mosaic, taking advantage of the labyrinth’s protective walls or hunting opportunity. Everywhere you look, life meanders, settles, or glides, each occupant finding its place, each corridor or tower a stage for a different marine community.

From time to time, the city’s deeper rhythms take on a sharper tone: perhaps an abrupt quake, or that feeling of molten warmth rising from fissures below. When that happens, an alertness ripples through its denizens. You’ll see fish scatter, warier creatures duck into hideaways, and the resonance of thousands of shells rattling as they shift. Only moments later, the equilibrium returns. This place is used to upheaval, accustomed to shifts in tide and the quiet fracturing of stone. Adaptation, after all, is the rule of survival here. In the end, no matter the rumblings from the earth or the drift of time, the city continues to host an uncountable array of marine beings, new arrivals swimming in to replace those departed, ensuring the cycle endures.

So, who or what lives here? They are the dwellers of a water-thick gloom where sunlight no longer rules. They are fish, crustaceans, eels, strange pelagic wanderers, and hidden cousins of all that the ocean can offer. They possess no memory of the time before submersion, no knowledge of the structures’ old purposes—only the reflex of survival and the strange gift of making what was lost a new home. They converge in layered communities that defy neat classification, each corner shaped by the synergy of wave, warm currents, and the quiet crumble of once-proud towers. Together, they have turned a drowned ruin into a living tapestry—each creature like a thread of color sewn through an ancient tapestry of coral and stone, echoing with the once-silent ghosts of a civilization, now wholly embraced by the vast hand of the sea.
