They don’t all belong to the same species, or even the same kingdom. Here, “belonging” is more a matter of enduring—of swimming in dark humidity, of taking in the choking air where rust mixes with leaf rot, and finding it survivable. Most of these beings live as though it has always been this way, as though the sky were forever black, and the horizon perpetually lit by sporadic sparks and the winking glow of damp fungus. If you want to find them, you have to look carefully, because so many of them hide in the nooks and gullies, the streaks of shadow between industrial glare.

In the lower reaches, at the base of the massive pillars where water drips in constant rivulets, entire draping canopies of lush fungi, moss, and vine create a realm of half-subterranean gloom. Down there, you’ll see the glimmer of insects—palm-sized and decked in carapaces that ripple with phosphorescent spots. They skitter across the slime-slick surfaces, weaving in tight arcs to avoid the hungry flicker of amphibian tongues. Bright-lipped salamander-creatures lurk in murky pools, their eyes reflecting the feeble glow of violent-orange hazard lights overhead. They’ve got skin that can go almost translucent when motionless, letting them blend with the water’s reflections. They wait for smaller prey: a snail with a glowing trail or a cluster of invertebrates gnawing on decaying leaves. Now and then, these salamander-like forms move in groups, spooking each other with hoarse little calls—barks you might hear echo from one dripping corner to the next.

Farther up the spires, where catwalks and rusted pipelines lace across yawning chasms, the life changes shape. Dense tangles of vines cling to the rock, though these are no ordinary vines. Some are muscled with fibrous cores, allowing them to recoil from passing footsteps or from the hiss of burst steam valves. Within their swaying forms, hidden pockets of fruiting bodies glimmer, feeding on mineral seepage and the metal’s slow corrosion. Move closer, and you’ll find it’s not uncommon to see tiny clusters of eyes—some belonging to spider-like arthropods specialized in the near-constant damp and darkness. They spin filaments not of classic silk but of a shimmering, eerie thread that catches any stray luminescence, marking intricate patterns in the gloom. Over time, abandoned webs accumulate the metallic dust that sifts down from corroding rafters, forming spangled lace across overhead beams.

High above, near the summits of the biggest industrial towers, winged shapes roost. Their outlines seem half bat, half reptile—membranous wings folded like cloaks against their spined backs. By day (or whatever passes for it), they hide in extended scaffolding arms or dangle from the undersides of giant ducts, absorbing the warm updrafts of steam. At odd intervals, they’ll lift off in slow, deliberate swoops to circle the humming power arcs. They’re attracted by insects that dance in the arcs’ faint radiance. But these creatures have also been witnessed hunting bigger prey—snatching a scuttling rodent or an unwary reptilian from a catwalk with surprising precision. Their calls are a chattering chorus somewhere between a bird’s screech and static-caught radio transmissions; they bounce from one steel tower to the next in vibrating echoes that linger eerily in the metal skeletons.

Humanoid shapes exist here as well, though not necessarily in societies you’d consider advanced or complete. Some are survivors of earlier colonization attempts—descendants of laborers or explorers who long ago ceased expecting rescue. They conceal themselves in battered bunkers or behind half-collapsed walls, using whatever meager generator or flickering lamp still works. Their clothes are patched together from old tarps, fungal fibers, and industrial scraps, and they carry improvised tools—perhaps a crowbar fashioned from a broken pipe, or a machete hammered out of rust-flaked steel. Their eyes glow with cunning or perhaps near-feral wariness in the gloom; the darkness has forced them to rely on other senses. Some claim to see infrared glimmers where the rest of us only see black. They memorize the melodic hum of turbines and the drips of overhead leaks so well they could navigate blindfolded. Their bond with this place is both intimate and uneasy: part caretaker, part scavenger, threading catwalks for salvage or new vantage points, ever mindful that a misstep means a plunge into the endless gorge.

A different breed of “resident” is purely mechanical, though whether it has true sentience is unclear. In the deeper labyrinths of factories—where half-functional assembly lines shudder and spark in the gloom—there are hulking automatons. Some are squat and rectangular, once designed to haul raw ores or maintain the turbines. Now, many wander aimlessly or have become stuck in loops, rattling along broken rails or belching steam in sporadic intervals. Others remain docile until you approach; they flicker with the last gasp of their programming, scanning passersby with sensors that flick and blink. Occasionally, you’ll spot one actively tending an ancient conveyor belt, carefully moving lumps of corroded ore from one end of a collapsed walkway to another, as if the centuries-old directive still pulses through its circuits. Vines and fungus cling to them too, merging biology with metal. One might come upon a giant mechanical arm covered in bioluminescent moss, the glow forming a living exoskeleton where the plating has rotted away.

Deeper in the labyrinth—beyond the watchful vines and rusted catwalks—rumors speak of more enigmatic denizens. They leave clawed footprints on dripping stone, or wide-lipped holes in the sides of industrial storage tanks. Some say these beasts resemble ancient reptiles, their armored bodies thick with overlapping scales that sparkle faintly in the soft glow of fungal spores. Others claim forms with too many limbs or entirely alien anatomies, bristling with sensory antennae that can detect the subtlest pulses of electrical current. Sometimes, low-pitched roars or keening howls echo through the labyrinth, ghostly voices that never quite match anything else known in the area.

And woven through everything is a vast tapestry of smaller lives: mold that sprouts in the delicate corners of cracked metal plates, feasting on chemical run-off. Wormlike creatures that burrow straight into living vines, using pockets of trapped fluid as micro-eco-chambers. Roach-like swarms with segmented shells that can flatten themselves to wade through tight seams of twisted steel. Predatory flowers that snap at passing drones or unlucky insects—if the vines are muscular, the flowers are all spring-like energy and subtle toxins, adaptively sealing shut over any morsel they can trap.

Despite the gloom, a sense of interdependence permeates. The enormous turbines churn the foggy air, dispersing spores to new heights. The winged predators keep insect populations in check. The salamander-like amphibians devour the mutant roaches and keep algae blooms from overwhelming the stagnant pools. Even the half-sentient machines carry on processes—draining water, pushing steam, providing new surfaces for vines to root in, forging new shelters for smaller creatures. The plateau is a giant, living, industrial terrarium, where growth and rust form a single system.

High above it all, storms loom perpetually, and thunder resonates through every inhabitant. Some cower in burrows at the slightest atmospheric shift, while others climb to vantage points to drink in the sudden deluge of rain. Here, apex species have learned to ride the thermal rises from steam vents, gliding effortlessly around the spires. Human-like scavengers, on the other hand, have patched together slender hang-gliders from scraps of tarp and mechanical sinew, launching themselves in leaps of faith across the void. If you watch from below, you’ll see them appear briefly in the spray of a hidden waterfall’s glow, then disappear into a swirl of warm mist.

In this labyrinth of metal and moss, occupants survive by fusing with the rhythms: rust and rot, drip and hiss, circuit spark and roaring wind, the cycle of scuttling insects and shuddering turbines. Each creature, whether flesh or steel or something in between, finds a niche. Nothing remains static for long; vines grow overnight, storms reshuffle the catwalks, valves burst and flood entire passages, opening new routes for those willing to adapt. The steady churn of industry—partially collapsed, half-forgotten, yet somehow still alive—invites all manner of opportunists. 

And so they persist, from the great, unnamed hulks that prowl the edges of roaring gas flares, to the tiny glowing mushrooms that colonize the cracks in steel. Life here is legion, simultaneously ancient and experimental, drawn like moths to the faintest flicker of light or the wettest trickle of water, forging a thousand quiet interconnections in a world whose sky may never brighten.

They are, each in their own way, shaped by the perpetual darkness, the unceasing moisture, the hum of unending machinery. You only ever see glimpses—an eye shine in a corner, a shape flitting past a broken lamp, or the curl of a vine around a piece of stray metal. But in the shifting half-light, you can sense them all around. They are the heartbeat of the Sky-Black Plateau, thriving despite silence from the skies above and forging small, vital existences within that labyrinth of sludge, thunder, and rust.
