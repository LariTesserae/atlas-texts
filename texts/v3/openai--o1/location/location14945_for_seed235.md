A first approach might be from a distance: you scale the final slope of a winding path that seems carved by ancient floods. The heat is overwhelming. The air itself trembles at the edges of vision, as if the entire place is a mirage. Yet the path is real, and leads to a vast plateau suspended at a frankly impossible height. Something about these cliffs defies normal reason: there are illusions of angles that do not match up, ribbons of color that slither across the rock face though it never moves, and intermittent warping of space that makes you doubt your bearings.

Water collects everywhere. The rock walls cry rivulets of condensation, animate with tangled vines and glossy moss clinging to the stone. Broad-leafed plants—long fronds, dripping cushions of green, translucent mushrooms grown huge—carpet ledges and crevices in exuberant profusion. The humidity is stifling, as if the air itself is saturated. And yet, the watery presence is undeniably there: you see sheets of water trickling from the top and into hidden channels below. Somewhere deeper in the plateau, a thunderous cataract (or hundreds of them) roars in continuous, deafening cacophony: so loud it feels like a physical weight on your ears and chest. It drones on without pause, the ambient soundtrack to this entire realm.

Above you, the plateau forms what might be called a city—but not in the usual sense, because there is no sign of any tool-making society. Instead, it’s like the crumbling skeleton of an ancient metropolis shaped by nature’s own hand, or by something older than humans. Towering columns of stone and vine-wood would pass for buildings, if not for the sprawling organic architecture that ties them together. Broad canopies weave overhead to form covered walkways. Arches made of wet, twisting root systems cross from one tower to another, forming causeways across impossible gaps. Where the vines end, the rock begins, pitted with age, carved by countless centuries of water’s slow erosion. Yet it all feels strangely stable—settled into place as if it has been this way for eons and will remain eternal.

However, there is also an undercurrent of impossibility here. Corners of walls flicker out of sync with the rest of reality. Sometimes when your field of view shifts, you spot a tear in the distance, like a seam in the sky or a mismatch in perspective. Edges of structures warp and twitch, as though concealed energies are pushing at the boundaries of ordinary matter. A faint glow pulses from certain spots within the walls—alien lights that nestle in nooks and crannies. Despite all these hints at something beyond the normal world, the plateau’s physical form never truly transforms before your eyes. It remains statically perpetual—a paradoxical collision of unstoppable strangeness and unchanging stasis.

No animals, no insects, no birds. The city is completely deserted of any animate creature beyond the lush flora. And yet the roaring water, the unrelenting heat, and the occasionally echoing cracks of rock expansion fill the silence with a tremendous, overwhelming presence. You might call it “loud loneliness.” Branches of colossal braided roots groan, pulling tight across dripping stone arches, as though the city itself is alive. A ceaseless wind—drawn by the extreme height and the temperature differences—howls through the open corridors made by trunk-like pillars and sticky green walls. The booming resonance of distant waterfalls competes with the wind’s roar, merging into a single deafening note that pervades everything.

Perhaps the entire plateau is the top of some ancient spire, a precipitous perch where the realms between matter and magic or dream have frayed. The smooth edges are worn, but not in the typical pattern of natural erosion. Instead, there’s a sense that the place was shaped by repeated cycles—stalactite-like growths on overhead beams, bleach-white ridges from mineral-laden water. And always that sense of cosmic tension, of an unreality half-exposed, like jagged cracks in a painting that someone tries to pass off as whole.

It feels large enough to be called a region in its own right: entangling corridors of open-air labyrinths, high terraces, dripping courtyards, labyrinthine grottos echoing with tremendous subsonic thunder. Walking it all would take days, even if time here did not seem caught in a stationary loop. The light is muted but functional—somewhere between a permanent midday that never shifts or dims. You sense that if you came here a century or a millennium from now, it would be precisely the same, vines in exactly the same positions, water following the same dripping paths, the illusions in the same corners.

In its own way, it is complete—a masterpiece of contradiction: a hot, overgrown citadel with no inhabitants; an equilibrium of unstoppable water and unyielding stone; a monstrous noise that leaves no space for the calls or rustlings of any fauna; and a creeping breakdown of physics that somehow never quite topples the place into chaos. It’s a hushless cathedral of vegetation and roaring emptiness, forever perched on the brink of unreality, yet never crossing it.
