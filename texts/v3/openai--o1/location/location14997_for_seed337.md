It begins well beneath the surface, in a vast stretch of newly revealed rock and sediment that extends in every direction—a place that feels at once embryonic, still shaping itself, and yet already brimming with lives determined to make their mark. The stone walls and ceilings aren’t old and eroded; instead they’re raw, as though pushed upward or hollowed out only recently. In many places, the rock looks freshly split, with jagged edges and sharp angles. There are hollows and alcoves in the stone where dampness gathers, forming rivulets that seep through the cracks. Water here is intermittent: slow drips echo gently in the otherwise hush environment, while runway puddles collect along meandering floor channels. Everything smells of cool moisture and fresh earth.

Despite being underground, the space is surprisingly open. Caverns unfold one into the next in a grand network, giving the sense of a subterranean valley or region rather than cramped corridors. Narrow gullies and wide, gently sloping expanses coexist in surprising adjacency. At times, the stone underfoot is smooth, as if sculpted by recent flows of water, but in other places, it’s rough, with flecks of crystalline minerals that refract what light is present. Beams of light reach many corridors through vents and angled shafts, where natural or hand-chiseled openings lead out to the surface above. The illumination is enough to consider it “day” down here, at least by local reckoning.

Sparse vegetation clings to the rock in small clusters: pale mosses, dull green lichens, and an occasional filigree of stringy fungus around water sources. These are the subtle signs of life in an otherwise mineral domain, giving an almost ghostly color to the stony walls. No forests here, no sweeping meadows—just pockets of persistent, hardy growth. The air, while cool, stays humid in places where water accumulates. Mists sometimes drift through the larger caverns, shifting with gentle currents of air that flow in from hidden cracks overhead.

Striking, however, is how packed the place feels with human or human-like presence. The settlement is dense. Homes and communal structures cluster in tiers, fashioned from stone blocks or rough-hewn timber braced against the rock. The architecture has a rudimentary, almost primitive quality. Walls are stacked from large, earthen bricks or assembled from logs dragged in from the surface. Simple rope nets, woven from fibrous vines or hand-spun plant fibers, are strung up to hold cargo and personal possessions slightly off the damp floor. Some dwellings even cling to the walls, projecting out on platforms anchored with thick pegs. Narrow lanes snake between these constructions, flanked by half-sunken pillars that serve as markers or supports.

Anyone looking closely would see that the settlement is layered not in finished sophistication but in partial attempts and abrupt expansions. It is a place of new arrivals or ongoing transformations. People here build step by step with limited tools—stone chisels, bone awls, and simple metal pieces hammered into shaping devices. Workshops are scattered throughout, their crafts lit by dim lanterns. The forging of metal is rare and rudimentary—often just small lumps of ore heated over charcoal fires. Most of the shaping and construction revolve around manual labor, resourceful use of the earthen surroundings, and an ingenuity born from necessity.

Despite its density, the environment remains hushed. Sounds carry softly: the gentle scrape of a chisel against rock, the labored grunt of a villager hauling timber, the drop-drop of water playing its steady rhythm against stone basins. Live creatures other than the settlers appear infrequently. Now and then a small amphibian—translucent-skinned, wide-eyed—lingers near puddles. Darting insects, colorless moths, and occasionally a skittering rodent might be glimpsed in the shadows, but animal life never overtakes the calm. When you pass by lower tunnels that lead deeper still, you might catch echoes of unknown creatures’ movements, but they remain shy specters in the darkness.

For all its quiet, the place teems with activity if one pays attention. Small groups gather to collect drippings from the walls, funneling water into rough ceramic jars. Trade inside the settlement thrives on an exchange of clay vessels, carved charms, and dried mosses that serve as spongy fuel. In certain larger caverns, rudimentary markets spring up, with craftspeople selling woven baskets, smooth stone implements, and lumps of freshly quarried rock. Regular condensation cycles create a subtle but important “weather”: some mornings see a thin veil of mist collecting overhead or swirling around torchlit corridors, which eventually recedes by midday when the rising warmth from torches and cooking fires drives it off. 

Though the environment is unsettled in a literal, geological sense—its formations still taking shape—those who dwell here seem confident in their new home. There’s a productive hum to their daily work, a sense of possibility amidst the earliest stages of carving out a life underground. No etched inscriptions speak of centuries of tradition; nothing suggests an ancient or deeply layered history. Instead, the stone surfaces are nearly blank, waiting for the marks of these settlers, for their story to be carved in. 

And above all, there is a pervasive sense of evolution. Newly formed corridors, fresh rock faces still studded with crystals, faint traces of water weaving in and out of cracks—everything hints that this setting remains in motion. The settlement itself grows outward, building upon each layer of erected walls and hammered pegs, responding to the natural openings that reveal themselves over time. It’s a living labyrinth, quietly humming, forging itself from the rock at the same pace its people discover how to use new tools, cultivate new spaces, and coax life from the damp stone beneath the earth.
