It is a vast, constructed expanse that seems at first to stretch into nothingness—a planar labyrinth of steel and stone, shaped by centuries of human (or perhaps not-quite-human) industry. The air carries a clammy coolness, odd in a region so close to sea level: one has the sense that water lurks just beneath the surface of every walkway, every rusting foundation. Moisture slicks almost every surface, beads of condensation trickling off pipes and girders, forming shallow pools along the uneven, pitted corridor floors. It feels as though a rainstorm has just passed, or is about to begin again at any moment.

The entire world here is built. There is no visible “ground” in the natural sense—only layered platforms, walkways, and mechanized causeways that appear to have been carved from monstrous slabs of metal or poured from thick, ancient concrete. The architecture is old and battered, as though it has been standing for millennia. Edges are worn smooth by unknown forces of time and battered by incessant processes of rust and chemical decay. Floodlights hang from tangled cables overhead, but most of them have failed, leaving long corridors of gloom. Where a lamp still flickers on, it reveals broad walls corroded to a grey-brown mulch of iron. Everything is drenched in shadows.

Despite how empty it first appears—nearly no signs of inhabitants or creatures—this place is anything but silent. A constant cacophony reverberates through the thick darkness: the hammering of colossal pistons somewhere in the distance, the hiss of venting steam, the clank and grind of heavy machinery cycling endlessly. Some machines whoosh and groan; others create percussive thuds that shake the structure underfoot. The din is almost unbearable at times, and it reverberates so powerfully that standing still, one can feel the vibration coursing up through one’s ankles and into the skull. The effect is disorienting, like dwelling inside a massive engine. 

Though the environment is largely deserted, it is not lifeless. Small pockets of spindly vegetation languish in the corners—drab, pallid stems creeping through cracks in the plating from unseen pockets of muddy sediment. These plants are hardy and half-starved of light, their leaves nearly translucent and their roots burrowing tenuously through crumbling seams in the floor. Clusters of fungus-like growth occasionally cling to the underside of ramps and the corners of corridors, feasting on the constant dampness. Yet there are no birds, no insects, no mammals—nothing that moves except the machinery and the meager stirring of these sad, color-leached plants in the cold, circulating air. 

This is an old place, a domain that feels far beyond any original purpose. The structure’s edges—if such a boundary still exists—are worn nearly flat, the corners eroded into gentle curves, the surfaces softly sagging from decades or centuries of corrosion. It is not quite a ruin, for it is still in motion: enormous riveted pipes funnel water from unknown reservoirs, sluices open and shut with sonorous clunks, and conveyor belts carry dark lumps of ore or waste to uncertain destinations. Within this grand artificial scape, the processes cycle on. They are as close to an ecosystem as this place has—machines feeding machines, vents feeding compartments, conveyor belts distributing materials that are then fed into hammering presses and molten crucibles, which in turn churn out half-finished shapes. It is a closed loop of mechanical life, ticking along with merciless efficiency and volume.

Everywhere, water seeps. Thick rivulets run down the sides of industrial pillars—where the plating is parted or compromised, beads of murky fluid ooze in. It is never outright flooding, but the environment remains perpetually soaked, as if the entire place rests on the threshold of some enormous subterranean lake that is pressing upwards. The air is humid enough for breath to feel heavy, and sometimes a chill wind gusts through large corridors, forming swirling pockets of mist that spin away into the darkness.

Yet the place remains vast and strangely open. The major halls are wide enough to accommodate dozens of parallel tracks of machinery. Doors, tall as cathedrals, stand parted to reveal further sorting floors or industrial bays. High overhead, enormous crane assemblies and bridging walkways fade into the dimness, their lights either out or faintly glowing like stars glimpsed in a night sky. One cannot see the final boundary of these chambers; it feels as though the complex extends across the horizon, a repetitive labyrinth of steel, scaffolding, and machinery continuing forever.

What is truly unsettling is the uncanny sense that it all makes sense in some strange way, as though governed by rules of function we only half understand. The lines of pipes vanish into the darkness, carrying unknown fluids to unknown destinations. At intervals, the entire structure shudders, prompting cyclical groans of metal, as if some giant mechanism far below is turning a gear that shifts everything a fraction of a degree. There is a profound sense of being inside a living, albeit synthetic, body: a skeleton of steel, veins of pipework threading water and lubricants, constant respiration of steam and exhaust. 

No animals scurry, no humans oversee it—this place seems to regulate itself. The faint vegetation only underscores the strangeness, nature’s last attempts to stake a claim in an essentially fabricated realm. And even those brave, pallid shoots are widely scattered and forlorn in the gloaming darkness, overshadowed by roaring engines and overhead clamps that shift cargo on rails.

There are times when the din crescendos—massive gears engage, and entire blocks of the structure tremble. At those peaks, the sound is deafening. Loose fragments of metal rattle on the floor. Reverberations bounce around corners to create fleeting echoes that collide and mix, forming a near-constant wall of noise. You could shout, and wave your arms, and nobody would ever hear you above the clamor. Yet, between these surges, there are intervals of lesser cacophony—still loud enough to set your teeth on edge, but slightly more bearable. 

Perhaps the strangest part of all is that in the occasional hush, you notice subtle changes in the environment: the far-off ring of a bell that doesn’t repeat or a new smell—oily, acrid, or sweetly corrosive—that had not been there a moment before. It’s as though the structure is shifting in phases, exposing different processes to the open corridors at different times. Everything keeps moving, keeps cycling, though to what end remains a mystery. 

And so the world is shaped: a dim, endless horizon of rusted floors and looming machines. A wet hush that is never truly silent. An ancient place, ground down by unimaginable spans of time and unstoppable, engine-driven momentum. Cool air slicked with condensation. Pockets of faint light only serving to deepen the gloom elsewhere. An absence of animals, but not an absence of life: the mechanical processes themselves glimmer with a strange vitality, and the sparse, pale vegetation testifies that something organic can still cling to existence. In the ever-present darkness, in the clanging roar that will not cease, this is a realm given wholly to machinery and the ghostly imprint of old intentions—still churning, still rolling forward, for reasons lost to the centuries.
