At first glimpse, one might assume it’s an oasis—a warm, richly colored pocket of life perched high on a plateau—but then one notices there’s almost nothing that could be called water. The air is dry, so dry that the slightest exhalation vanishes instantly. No pools or streams, no dew in the mornings. Yet the land itself teems with dense, vibrant growth. Great spires of fleshy plant-forms spiral upward, broad fronds and ridged stalks layered so tightly they almost touch, each one feeding somehow on the sunlight and on faint chemical traces in the atmosphere. From afar it might look like a jungle, but up close there is no glisten of water anywhere.

Though these lush forms are reminiscent of trunks and leaves, they seem to be made of something closer to living crystal or a hardened resin, each new shoot telescoping out to fill any gap. In places where the ground has split recently—jagged rifts better than a person’s height—it’s clear the soil is a light, porous rock, pale as chalk, newly revealed and scarcely eroded. The plateau itself feels newborn, as if it rose from below only a short time ago. Sharp edges catch the harsh sun at tangents, and only the smallest dusting of grated mineral suggests the land has begun the long process of being smoothed by time.

A settlement—more akin to an organically grown city rather than anything shaped by conventional tools—clusters along the edges of the plateau. Pillar after pillar of those resinous plants have fused together, forming walls, archways, and half-domed enclosures. Paths snake between them, close and cramped, more vertical than horizontal—one might think of them as corridors in some labyrinthine cathedral. Everything is warm to the touch, as though the rock and resin hold solar heat well into nightfall. There is no hint of metalwork or shaped stone blocks—no sign of tool-making. Instead, it all feels as though it has grown where it stands, or been slowly woven by creatures intent on building a hive-like metropolis. Indeed, it is a kind of urban sprawl, but not of steel towers—rather a dense organic warren.

A traveler walking among these twisted structures would see the presence of many creatures—some with plated hides, others adorned with translucent membranes, many quietly creeping or fluttering on nearly silent wings. Despite their abundance, they produce almost no noise; what passes for “chatter” is more subtle: the whisper of jointed limbs scraping and the faint rasp of resin growth being gnawed or rearranged. Occasionally, a faint soft crack might echo when one of the jagged fissures in the plateau shifts. But these sounds come only at rare intervals, maintaining a nearly complete hush day by day.

No rivers run here, no trickling streams, yet the “vegetation” remains thick and dazzling. In the world’s normal daylight, the crisp dryness makes silhouettes sharp. Colors are muted but present: dusty oranges, smoky greens, and the occasional flash of bright azure in the swirling spores. The air has a scent reminiscent of minerals—something stony and volcanic, tinged with a faint spice as if from unknown chemicals released by these unusual plants.

And there is a slowness to it all. These living structures, the plateau’s slow upward tilts, the creatures’ hushed movements—everything unfolds at a languid pace, the shifts measured in weeks or months rather than hours. There is a patient, measured cycle: the vegetation inching upward toward sunlight, the creatures nesting deeper into the labyrinth, and the plateau itself gently shifting with geologic exhalations as if only now settling into the sky.

It is a peculiar place, part desert, part abundant garden—no water to be seen, and yet all around signs of life, signs of a civilization grown without tools. One might wonder what hidden processes feed these quiet, resinous spires, how the fauna thrives in near-silence with no fresh streams. But the land keeps its secrets in the hush of thin air. The sharp edges of stone and the blooming fractal structures of the city stand almost as illusions—a newborn world whose next expansion will be measured in ages, quietly unfolding without the need for water’s flow.
