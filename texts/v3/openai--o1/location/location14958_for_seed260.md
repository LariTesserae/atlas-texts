Sometimes, it’s hard to speak of a place that shouldn’t exist at all. This one stands, or half-stands, in a dim half-light, so faint and diffuse that the landscape seems both slumbering and alert. Overall, it is a cool expanse—though calling it “air” doesn’t feel right. Everything tastes faintly mineral, as if the atmosphere is made of finely ground stone.

The ground does not slope gently or lie flat; it thrusts upward in jagged ridges and broken spires. Enormous, impossible precipices dominate the horizon. You might imagine it as a chain of mountains so tall they warp perspective—extreme peaks rising far beyond any proportion, some bent or twisting like half-melted candles. Their rocky skins are raw and rough as though they were formed only moments ago. Gouges and cracks suggest the terrain is still being forced upward from beneath—or else the ground is slowly tearing itself open and forming these ridges out of some older, half-built realm. The texture feels embryonic, as if the planet’s skeleton is exposed, yet continuing to shape itself.

Despite the dryness—no streams, no puddles, not so much as a damp patch of mud—there is a lushness, but it is not a green, leafy kind of abundance. These “plants” (if “plant” is even the right word) cluster in sparse collections, perched precariously on ledges and across half-formed plateaus. They appear as clusters of tall, tube-like growths, each made of a translucent crystal or glass-like chitin. They bristle with delicate, fractal filaments that wave slowly in the nearly airless wind. Though widely spaced and not numerous, these alien forms exude an otherworldly luxuriance—some soft glow from within, patterns swirling across their surfaces, an uncanny beauty that suggests a lushness of form rather than mass. They don’t depend on water; they draw in ambient minerals or some strange cosmic radiation from the stony air around them. Observing them, one has the sense that they’re newly seeded by fracturing reality itself.

There is no sign of animal life, no footprints, no flutters of wings, nothing that crawls or hunts. Yet it is not silent here—far from it. The entire place reverberates with a deafening roar, a constant, low-pitched thunder like an immense cosmic engine turning over at a glacial pace. It shakes the rock towers, sends the crystalline fronds gently resonating, and permeates any living ear. The sound might be the tectonic groaning of a raw planet still sculpting itself, or maybe it’s a breakdown of natural laws—reality’s feedback screaming in the open. At times, this roar shifts into sub-audible pulses that gnaw at the bones. At others, it rises to a howling pitch that vibrates through the spires, making them flicker and blur at the edges as though they are on the brink of fading out.

Throughout the landscape, hints of old, scattered structures loom. Most are nothing more than chipped blocks set into precarious perches on the ridges, or half-buried lumps of a metal too primitive to be steel and too advanced to be raw iron. A few stone columns, shaped and smoothed by hand tools, lean at impossible angles. One might spot an occasional artifact—a broken bowl carved from local crystal, or a small ring of scorched rock that could have been a fire pit. Each relic points to a group of inhabitants—long gone or consumed by the rifts in reality. Their technological imprint is minimal: simple stone hammers or sharpened shards of metal. These structures feel as though they have been dislodged by shifting ground, left behind in the unstoppable slow surge of new landforms.

Everywhere you look, space and distance feel unstable. The vantage might show a wide stretch of land, dotted only with a few shining crystal “plants” and jagged boulders, yet the background expands to an impossible scale. The mountains recede farther than your mind can comfortably track, as if each peak recurls into itself. There is a sense of emptiness here—vast kilometers of near-void between these scattered outcrops and winding canyons—yet it’s also undeniably “full” of that roaring, unearthly presence. Dim, cool half-light washes all surfaces in a gray-lavender gloom, lending each shape an unbalanced outline. You cannot tell how large things truly are; scale heaves and shifts from vantage to vantage.

Over time—a time that seems to stretch out like thick tar—nothing changes abruptly. The spires inch higher or fracture anew, but you must watch for days to detect a slight shift in the rock’s contour. The crystalline plants slowly wave their filaments, each shimmering fractal pattern adjusting with the subterranean resonance. Occasionally, you might blink and see a ridge vanish from the corner of your eye, only to reappear in a new, half-real arrangement. Perhaps that’s the dissolution of the rules of physical space; perhaps it is the land still being born.

Some corners of this realm hum with leftover energies. A faint sense that the boundary between real and unreal has frayed. You can sense a deep timelessness even as geology crashes into futurity—everything is building, forming, and raw. Meanwhile, the deafening roar hums and rattles on. Indeed, reality is cracking at the edges, but in a slow, stately fashion, so gradual you can almost believe it’s the only natural state of things.

This is a place of cool dryness, full of contradictions: lush life that blooms without water; towering peaks that may crumble away in the blink of an eon; half-made icons of a simple civilization marking a half-made world that may kill the laws of nature entirely. And behind it all, a relentless, quaking sound that drowns out any other signal that might yet be heard. It’s as if existence here is just beginning, on the threshold of collapse and rebirth, caught in the hush of its own primordial roar.
