A broad plateau stretches across the horizon, so high in the atmosphere that the air feels thin and gelid against the skin. Even here, at an altitude most would imagine as barren and icy, lush plant life cranes upward in vivid clusters, splashes of green and soft blues interspersed with pale, translucent leaves that catch the overwhelming brightness of the sun. There is a cold bite to the wind—icy crystals sometimes patter across the moss-like groundcover—and yet everything thrives in this contradictory climate. 

Algae-fed rivulets thread through the stony heights. In certain depressions, condensed water trickles into basins ringed by layered lichens that glow faintly under the intense light, creating ephemeral pools. The ground is damp without being soaked, dotted by pockets of water that evaporate nearly as soon as they form, leaving mineral-laced residues behind. The plateaus and cliff edges have that crisp, fresh-sculpted quality: spires and jagged overhangs that look newly risen from deep below. It seems the land itself hasn’t been subject to millennia of smoothing and grinding; instead, its edges remain abruptly sharp or precariously balanced, as if creation itself is still an unfinished task here.

This world stretches nearly beyond comprehension—vast in a way that suggests it could spawn its own weather systems or swirl its own pale clouds in the high vaults of the atmosphere. Every swath of land is jammed with living presences: sinuous vines hugging the crags, layered fungal domes across the low spots, and most of all, creatures. An almost chaotic tapestry of fauna parades forth wherever one looks. Tall, shaggy ruminants with thick fur to guard against the cold crowd at the edges of gullies, while brood swarms of smaller, rodent-like beings nest among the dense undergrowth. High above, winged creatures—colorful, bat-like flyers with mechanical-sounding rattles—fill the sky. There are so many tangled forms of life that open space is precious; nearly every crack and ledge is occupied.

Strangely, there are no obvious ruins or buildings—no stone towers or metal scaffolds testifying to civilization—yet an industrial presence thrums here in some other sense. On the ridges and hidden under rocky overhangs, one finds great, pulsing vents exhaling steam or metallic tang, rooted in the geology as if nature itself has invented factories all its own. These “machines” do not look manufactured, but they tick and hum with regular intervals, spitting out vapors and forging lumps of glittering minerals that quickly become home to ever more species of lichen and slime. Some vents release low-frequency rumbles, like the stuttering of colossal engines. Others puff out plumes tinted green, swirling away in the bright air, pungent and oddly chemical. The longer one stands by these vents, the more it feels like the entire plateau is a single mechanism—an industrial-era world that needs no engineers because it engineers itself.

The light is undimmed: the sky is so clear and the sun so insistent that every angle is free of shadow. When clouds pass, they flare milky white, scattering brilliance everywhere. One can feel the intensity even in the cold, as if the plateau’s altitude brings you closer to an amplified sun. There is no respite from the radiance. Vegetation has adapted, forming leaves and stems with silvery surfaces that reflect rather than absorb, and the animals, so numerous, move in clumps seeking micro-shadows under outcrops or behind tall pillars of rock.

It’s a noisy domain, too—the wind itself roars constantly in the high passes, and the combined chatter, hoot, and screech of life-forms near and far forms a clamor that never fully dies. Massive herds stomp in slow, crunching marches, while insect-like swarms click and thrum overhead, their collective movements reminiscent of heavy machinery. The entire place vibrates with life’s own industrial chorus, reverberating off the stony heights. In the distance, one can sometimes detect a deeper grinding beneath one’s feet, as if tectonic gears are moving below the surface—another sign that this is a young land, forging itself in real time.

Yet despite the constant sounds, change comes slowly here. The cycles of day and night seem long, almost glacial in their progression. Even the storms that roll in from the edges of this continent-scale plateau move languidly. Each event unfolds on a grand timescale, as though the land measures its transformations in eons. Vines creep at a pace barely perceptible to the naked eye. Pools fill, dry, and refill again, always in the same shallow dimples of stone. Those strange metallic outgassings never vary in pulse; the machinery-like rhythms remain steady year after year. It’s as though everything has found its place in a slow dance of unstoppable momentum, a monumental clockwork whose individual seconds last weeks.

All around, it feels untouched by any traditional notion of development, despite its strangely industrial heartbeat. There are no roads, no scaffolding, no fences—no sign of deliberate construction. The “factories” here are massive organic-mineral hybrids, producing energy and waste, fueling the teeming life, but they are nobody’s design. They simply exist by the planet’s own logic, forging a layered ecosystem so dense that each breath you take is thick with spores, flecks of ice, drifting seeds, and invisible motes of countless creatures. This place stands untamed, raw in its edges, grand in scale, shining in perpetual light, and echoing with the rattling thunder of life. It’s a realm at once mechanical and organic, perpetually cold, yet lush in its own strange way—a slowly evolving stage for the greatest pageantry of existence one can imagine.
