They call it the Sky-Black Plateau, though no sky is visible. Darkness lies over the landscape in a permanent shroud, broken only by clusters of dim industrial lamps, half-gone floodlights, or the orange blink of an exhaust flare perched high on a crag. The air is warm and humid, thick with currents of drizzling rain that patter ceaselessly against metal walkways. Water collects in rivulets along the cliff faces, hugging the edges of corroded platforms and dripping onto lush undergrowth far below.

In daylight—if such a thing exists here—you might call the mountains “towers”: tall, ancient spires sculpted by wind and time, their surfaces worn smooth into broad buttresses of stone. Yet the world is in darkness. Shadowy shapes loom out of the gloom: twisted ridges capped with moss and creepers, rock surfaces slick with a perpetual sheen of moisture. Vast canyons yawn between these mountainous columns, filled with swirling mists that glow faintly from hidden industrial sources—flickers of electrical arcs, perhaps, or pressure vents pulsing with some unknown energy.

It’s far more crowded than the emptiness might first suggest. Peer between the thick vines and watery gullies, and you see life: broad-leafed ferns, many-limbed vines that clutch at the stone, tangles of luminescent mushrooms feeding on mineral deposits leached out by the constant trickle of moisture. Strange insects scuttle through the damp gloom—chitinous forms with overlapping ridges and phosphorescent spots. There are foot-long salamander-like creatures hidden in pockets of stagnant water. Larger shapes, too, making deep, resonant calls that echo among the peaks: a sign the fauna is abundant, though hidden.

Closer to the plateaus, the signs of industry seep through. Rusted pipes snake around the edges of rock faces, bolted to surfaces long weathered and blackened by soot and dripping water. Narrow catwalks stretch precariously across voids hundreds of meters deep, pinned to the rocky spires like old scars. Here and there, you find giant turbines, half-buried in moss and anchored to the mountains’ edges. Their rotating blades churn the humid air with a dull, constant drone that mingles with the roar of distant waterfalls. Steam vents hiss from cracks in the ground, releasing bursts of heated fog that instantly condense on cold steel beams above, dripping in a rhythmic clatter onto metal rooftop panels.

An entire habitat of factory-like structures stands in partial ruins—barely lit, overshadowed by taller, more recent spires of metal. Some of these buildings still function, belching thick clouds of smoke and sparks, fueling an economy or process that no passerby ever fully understands. Others are only skeleton frames, half-collapsed, overtaken by thick webs of vines and spore-laden fungi that thrive in the darkness.

Amid the industrial hums and the organic drips and growls, there are deeper rhythms: storm clouds, swirling and piled high in the eternal black above, send torrents of warm rain every so often. Thunder reverberates off the stone towers, making the entire plateau tremble with a sub-bass vibration. It’s a place of ceaseless noise—fans whirring, engines chugging, metal structures groaning under their own weight, waterfalls crashing into unseen basins far below. In every direction, you sense a dense layering of processes, like a thousand living and mechanical hearts beating in discordant unison.

Exploration in this world is wrought with wetness and heat: you climb a crane that has rusted into the rock, cautiously stepping past the reptilian shapes lurking in crevices. High up on the ridges, the air is alive with the blended smell of primal vegetation and industrial fumes. Strange winged creatures—neither bird nor bat—circle the dim glow of a distant power arc, searching for insects or perhaps something else. The sense of scale is enormous, as if the plateau spans an entire planetary continent, each vertical cliff a nation of vines, metal beams, and creatures unique to its particular microclimate.

No one is certain how or why the darkness persists. Some say the planet never sees its star; others suspect a thick orbital dust ring that blocks out the sun. Whatever the reason, it’s a world of wet, living stone, overshadowed by mechanical expansions that cling precariously to the heights. Ancient erosion has shaped these mountains into stable pillars of rock; centuries of industrial tinkering have turned them into a labyrinth of catwalks, factories, and broken labs. And over the centuries, plant life has twined itself inextricably with the machines, forging a hybrid environment that is both evolving ecosystem and perpetual construction zone.

From a distance, if you can find even a small vantage point, you see a swirling black sky, countless twinkling factory lights, and the slow, luminous migrations of wildlife. All around, hidden forces churn the atmosphere—winds that whip in from secret caverns, vapor plumes that condense on the metal towers, spawning micro-climates of rain and fog, all adding to an unrelenting thrum.

In this darkness, you might stumble upon partial remains of earlier settlements: single huts carved into the stone or tiny sub-level bunkers built before the large factories took over. These scattered traces feel haunted, half-buried in vines. A defunct generator thrums with residual power for no apparent reason. A set of steel slides had once been used to transport minerals from the higher spires to the factories below. Now they lie twisted and rusted amid the ferns, the only sound the trickling sludge that ribbons down their length.

Time both stands still and rushes forward here. The plateau endures as it has for unknown ages—eroded yet constant, carved by rain and wind, by life and by steel. Ecosystems turn over rapidly, driven by pockets of heat and moisture. Predators hunt prey in the corners of the dark mazes, shrieking in odd tonalities that bounce from one industrial tower to another. Machines churn and whine, forging something that might one day lead to progress—or further ruin.

It is a place of colossal scale and dense life, a place of perpetual activity—metal meets moss, water meets darkness, and the night simply does not lift. Something about it is indescribably strange, as though the very rules of physics have slightly shifted. Maybe the gravity is a hint weaker, or the atmosphere is charged in subtle ways, or there’s a hush—beneath all that racket—of some deeper cosmic presence. Yet it remains an awe-inspiring blend of industry and wilderness, an eternal dance between creation and decay, thriving in its own kind of equilibrium under the shadow of its lightless skies.
