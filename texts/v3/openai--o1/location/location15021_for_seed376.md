It is a cold, high plateau wrapped in a cocoon of plant life. Everywhere you step, you find yourself pressed close by living forms—vines as tall as small trees, thick mossy drapes, leaf-shrouded pillars that might once have been trees but are now so overgrown with epiphytes and fungal mats that they’ve become something else entirely. There is no sign of built architecture; no walls or roads break the endless green. Yet roaming through this place, one can’t shake the impression of an industrial hum thrumming below the surface, as though some hidden mechanism churns and rattles beneath the layers of roots and mulch.

The air is cold but moist. Fog condenses on the broad leaves, and beads of water form and roll along twisting creepers. Shallow rivulets trickle between lumps of vegetation, weaving through pockets of saturated soil. There are no roiling waterfalls or broad lakes—just these slender flows and the occasional puddle glinting under a pale, normal daylight. Overhead, the clouds drift close, as if you’re near the ceiling of the world. Now and then a drizzle sets in, so light and so cold that it feels more like faint crystals of ice than proper rain. They vanish almost as soon as they land, feeding the sphagnum moss coating every surface.

The landforms here speak of great age. Eroded outcroppings of stone—flattened, half-buried in plant matter—suggest that this plateau has remained unchanged for centuries, letting wind and time shape it. A sense of timelessness hangs in the hush. The most persistent sounds come not from any animal or wind but from the faint crackle of stem fibers under strain, from distant tangles of roots twisting, from the gentle drip of water. Rarely, you might hear a deep, subterranean rumble echoing as though something large and mechanical shifts far below.

The density of plants suggests they have been left to flourish unchallenged. Some trunks bristle with layered bulbs shimmering in the cold. Others knit together overhead into archways and tunnels that enclose you in soft gloom. Underfoot, fallen leaves, fibrous mats, and humus create a spongy substrate that buries solid rock. The hush is nearly absolute—there is almost no wildlife to speak of, aside from an occasional glimpse of a quick, silent shape slinking away behind a root cluster. Small insects (rare in appearance) scatter over leaves, but larger animals seem absent, or perhaps push into the undergrowth and remain unseen.

Yet there is a certain turbulence here, a kind of barely contained frenzy. Strange bursts of energy crackle from within the colossal root networks; sometimes entire sections of foliage shudder and quake without any wind to prompt them. Newly grown vines coil across the ground with a frightening speed, creeping over stone shelves and smothering older growth. Gnarled trunks split, sending a tumble of seeds that rapidly take root in the rich soil. Then, just as suddenly, it falls still. It’s like a constant, subtle storm of life churning from within, an ecological machinery powered by some bizarre, cyclical force of nature. In places, you might stumble upon relic-like contraptions nested within the vegetation—silvery shafts, pulsing tubes, faintly humming gears that seem self-sustaining but have no obvious purpose. Nothing around them suggests they were built by hand; instead they feel grown, or spontaneously assembled by the forest itself.

When the clouds roil, the plateau becomes enshrouded in swirling gray, the temperature dropping further. Then you see the greenery rippling in chaotic waves, as if reacting to changes in atmospheric pressure. A swirl of spores lifts into the chilled air and glimmers in the muted light, only to settle again in new crevices, sparking swift, momentary bursts of growth. For a few moments, the forest is alive with a creeping mania, bristling with potential—and then there’s a lull, and the hush descends.

Cold, silent, overwhelmingly alive: here, the vegetation reigns, weaving itself into arches, towers, and thickets that dwarf any sense of human design. The sense of industry that hums in the earth below it all is never explained; it is simply a presence. Perhaps it’s a natural phenomenon unique to this plateau, or perhaps it’s something older, hidden beyond mortal reach. In the near-silence, you only hear the scrape of vine on bark, the near-inaudible hiss of watery channels trickling underground, and that low mechanical throb like distant machinery. In this place, nature is an engine, plants are the architecture, and the cold high haze wraps every leaf in a still, damp hush, punctuated by chaotic spurts of unstoppable, living frenzy.
