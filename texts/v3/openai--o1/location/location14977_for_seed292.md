A long, ragged plateau stretches out underwater, almost as though a series of craggy mountains had been swallowed by the sea eons ago. The water here is cool and faintly lit by a cloudy, shifting glow that filters down from somewhere above. At times it feels like standing in a cavern illuminated by soft daylight; other moments, when the current stirs sediment into great drifting veils, all grows dim and gray, and one can’t be sure which direction leads to open water.

Over the ages, powerful currents have gnawed at every exposed edge, scouring these submerged cliffs until their ancient rock faces have grown smooth in some places and oddly pitted in others, as if sculpted by a patient, unhurried hand. The overall impression is one of vastness, an open realm that extends well beyond the horizon in all directions. Indeed, looming silhouettes of elevated ridges and trenches vanish into the hazy distance, testifying to steep, sometimes abrupt drops. It’s as if the terrain has been carved and recarved countless times by unstoppable, continuous tides.

Yet despite this relentless washing-over, life abounds here. Towering aquatic vegetation—thick ribbons of kelp and fluid sheets of algae—clings to every surface. With the merest shift of current, these underwater forests sway in unison, forming dense, layered canopies near the plateau’s summits. Tangled vines cling to the edges of jagged shelves, and fibrous, twisting stalks sprout from pockets of nutrient-rich silt. Each cylindrical stem and leaf seems to host small crustaceans, spiny urchins, and a myriad of translucent fish that flicker in and out of sight. The plants are lush and healthy, their deep greens verging on near-black in the subdued light. In the darkest corridors, they cling to the watery stone with creeping root systems that look as ancient and knotted as venerable tree trunks on land.

All around, schools of brilliantly scaled fish dart in overlapping patterns. Some species cluster so tightly that from a distance they look like living clouds. Others swarm in distinct, angular shapes before scattering as they chase or flee. Odd, hulking crustaceans scrape the rock faces. Bigger, more mysterious silhouettes lurk behind the curtain of swaying seaweed, occasionally letting out resonant, echoing calls that carry for kilometers through the water.

Cut into the plateau, or sometimes perched precariously on ledges, stand the peppered remains of an industrial presence: massive pipes encrusted with lichen-like calcifications, half-buried rail tracks and tumbled machinery with fins of rust. In places, you can see the faint flicker of lights behind shattered glass portholes, as if some rechargeable or geothermal power source still sputters to keep these structures alive. Barnacles and coral have colonized the old metal, weaving living exoskeletons over every bolt and rusted beam. Twisted scaffolding pokes into the open water, partly smothered by kelp. Occasionally, great rotating fans or gears emit a soft mechanical hum, as though the place still stirs with a staggered heartbeat from an era gone by. In some corridors, where the machinery remains intact enough to function, you can hear the clang of hollow metal pounding or the hiss of escaping air bubbles. It melds with the pitch and roar of the currents, forming a background roar that rarely subsides.

Though the underwater domain is calm in moments—like a hushed cathedral—such tranquility never lasts long. Sudden shifts in the tide can turn the currents violent. In an instant, the calm sweep of vegetation starts whipping back and forth, and flurries of silt and debris cloud visibility. It’s as if the entire seascape is caught in a rotation of storms—turbulent pulses that churn through the region, pounding even the sturdiest cliff faces. During these spells, the environment becomes thunderously loud: waves crash in the distance overhead, resonating in the crushing depths; the swirling of water around hollow pipes and old vaults becomes a howl. Schools of fish scatter in panic, revealing new vantage points on the stone ledges when they part. Some old metallic fixtures groan under the strain, sending vibrations into the water that can be felt in the bones.

Despite all this commotion, there’s a strange sense of stability. You can feel that these cliffs have existed for immeasurably long ages and will continue to endure, no matter how fiercely the storms rage. They are nearly worn down in places, but also appear eternal, as if they’ve reached a final, settled form through continuous torrent and upheaval. Algae and corals exploit crevices in the battered floors, fracturing them further each generation. Small fish lay their eggs in the hollows, building entire life cycles into this watery sprawl of stone, silt, and steel.

In calmer pockets beneath ledges or behind massive industrial shells, schools of eels twist in intricate knots. Slender, luminescent shrimp dance near vents of warmer water that bubble from deep crevices, each swirl of their translucent bodies shimmering under the filtered light. Everywhere, the sense of density is palpable: life clusters upon life, building an ecosystem that seems to layer itself upwards, all while the unstoppable churn of water tries to rip everything away.

It’s a place of unstoppable motion and hidden resilience. The built environment may be in partial ruin—a scattering of old engines and half-sunken factories—but it has stamped its mark on the seabed, and in turn the seabed has reclaimed it. Even the soundscape feels alive: the roaring churn of storms, the industrial hum of half-functioning machinery, and the voices of countless creatures combine into a near-constant crescendo. It’s a realm caught between the unstoppable push and pull of water’s force, the persistent growth of life, and the deep echoes of an industry that once thrived here. And through it all, the ocean remains both a destroyer and a giver of life: shaping, eroding, christening this place anew with each fresh current that sweeps across its ancient plateau.
