Far beneath the surface of a planetwide crust, a vast cavern stretches out so far in every direction that it is difficult to tell whether it was formed by natural processes or intentional design. The rock around you is dark—almost coal-black or perhaps a bruise-purple—absorbing what little light filters from faint luminescent patches overhead. These patches could be mineral outcrops that glow from a slow chemical reaction or pockets of gas that flicker with eerie phosphorescence; they pepper the ceiling in broken bands, never forming neat lines, just sporadic washes of faint illumination. Between the patches, the darkness is thick enough to feel like a physical presence.

Despite the depth and the darkness, the air is warm—not uncomfortably so, but humid-less and strangely close. It’s an environment utterly devoid of water as you know it. There might be microscopic mists of unknown gases or swirling motes of dust suspended in the emptiness, but there are no rivers, no streams, not even a trickle of moisture across the rock. The walls and floors seem to be formed of raw mineral that has barely had time to weather. Edges of stone rods and columns jut up at sharp angles, ridges unrounded by erosion—this enclosed realm is newly emerged or newly uncovered, as if some cosmic shift peeled away an outer layer and exposed it. Everything is crisp-edged, the angles and surfaces fresh, unsoftened by time.

Though the rock and crystals are the only “terrain,” life here is unexpectedly abundant. In the distance, if your eyes adjust, you can make out scuttling shapes across the stone—fauna like no creatures you’ve seen on a surface world. Most are small, insect- or crustacean-like, with too many jointed limbs. Some coil serpentlike along the walls, clinging with sucker-feet. All seem oddly adapted to this dark dryness; they rarely rest, slipping in and out of nooks and crannies or creeping and gliding underneath mineral arches. Many species bear faintly glowing spots along their hides, a swirl of phosphorescent patterns that flicker in subdued oranges and duns or muted violets, guiding them through the gloom. Their movements are almost ghostlike, quiet in the still air.

In places, you notice scattered artifacts—very simple craftings that do not immediately suggest a recognizable civilization. Stone chips arranged in circles, a carved pillar with symmetrical patterns hammered into its sides, a floor area where the rock has been smoothed and leveled. These are not the trappings of an advanced society. The tools or implements that carved them would have been little more than sharpened bits of crystal, perhaps hammered by larger lumps of stone. There are no complex machines, no elaborate metallurgy, no signs of intricate architecture—just hushed suggestions of an intelligence that has the rudiments of shaping the environment. Even these faint marks look fragile, as though preserving them across centuries would be difficult in this place’s unchanging hush.

And hush is the prevailing characteristic. The air is still: no wind, no steady currents, just an ever-so-subtle movement of dusty motes when a creature darts by. Occasional clicks or rasping sounds echo as small life-forms communicate or scrape across rock, but then the silence returns, swallowing every noise as though it’s been banished to some echo void. Once in a while, faint rumbles can be felt through the stone underfoot—not a roar, more like the distant memory of a quake. These micro-tremors suggest some hidden expanses even deeper, or a geologic stirring that moves slower than any surface weather.

There is an unnerving sense of timelessness. This world does not seem to shift or degrade in the way a desert’s dunes might or a forest’s undergrowth would. The rock spires and pillars remain as they are, unsoftened by wind, water, or any shaping force besides those cryptic, low-level tremors. It is as if the entire subterranean realm exists in a locked equilibrium—an ancient, stable chamber that has always been this way and always will be. To stand here is to step into a place that defies normal rhythms of day and night, growth and decay.

Strangely, despite the quiet and the unnatural darkness, there’s a sense of overwhelming “density.” Under every overhang, behind every pillar, life—some with shining eyes or glowing chitin—presses in close. The interplay of faint phosphorescence from crystals and living organisms creates strange illusions in the near distance: you catch glimpses of shifting shapes, patterns that could be animals or shadows or both. The creatures move in clusters, entire gatherings of them on shelves of rock in the half-seen gloom. You might think you see the same shape over and over, or pieces of it reappearing at different scales, as if parts of the fauna share a fractal-like design.

All of it remains perplexing—like stepping into an alien cathedral or a timeless labyrinth. No water, no plants, no cycles of sun or moon, just an immense cavern of dark stone, freshly exposed shapes, subtle glows, and crowds of strange fauna. Primitive items of uncertain origin hint that something—beings or a single species—has tried to shape or interpret this place with nothing more than rudimentary tools. But no one is around to guide or explain. The hush reigns over all. It is a realm that has lived for countless eons or no time at all, an endless unbroken equilibrium, existing deeply tucked away from any surface world’s knowledge. Here, in a self-contained, unchanging hush, existence drifts in a slow dance of glowing fragments, soft echoes, and an enduring, elemental strangeness.
