There is no water in this place, at least not as we know water. The ground is hard and pale—almost chalky, with fine dust that turns to a corrosive grit whenever a gust tears across it. From a distance, it might look like a desert plateau perched far above some unseen lowlands, but scattered across this arid expanse are pillars of vibrant growth. These plants (if they can be called plants) have learned to flourish on something other than the liquids we usually picture; they draw nourishment from deep mineral veins, from flecks of crystal that drift in the sky, from the electric hum that ripples through the dust.

Tucked away in hollows and pockets are jagged spines and branching structures that sway as if stirred by a current we cannot feel. Their coloration runs from blackish-green to a fiery burgundy, and they are lush in the sense that they’re dense wherever they take root—splashes of living color on the otherwise pale, cracked plateau. Yet the vegetation doesn’t carpet every inch; it appears in discrete clusters, each cluster rising like an oasis in a sea of chalk. Where they grow, the ground has bulged and warped, as if roots push at underlying strata, warping and buckling the surface in gentle mounds.

The plateau itself is massively high—its edges drop off into a hazy, almost-sourceless dim. Clouds swirl perpetually overhead, but they offer no rain. They do, however, bring wind. The winds are fierce: they howl and shriek, reverberating off the stone walls and roiling air in constant turbulence. The dust lifts, forming twisting columns that tear across the plateau, scattering spines of vegetation, buffeting small creatures. It is never still here. Even in the sheltered corners of the plateau, a soft hum or persistent hiss remains in the air.

And there are creatures—lots of them. They range in size and shape, from fin-like gliders that ride the wind to large, scuttling mammals with leathery skin and luminous markings that glow faintly under the pale light. Odd, many-legged birds hop between plant clusters, their calls a bizarre, metallic shriek at odds with avian song. The constant commotion of all this life, plus the shrieking wind, makes a ceaseless roar that resonates over every stone and crack.

If you stand amidst the old worn pillars of rock that rise sporadically, you’ll see evidence of forges or hearths—small circles of blackened stone, or lumps of hammered metal. There are remnants of primitive dwellings here and there: outcrop huts shaped from the plateau’s own mineral plates, or hammered from the pitted bones of beast carcasses left behind. They form a haphazard circle in one of the larger clusters of vegetation, barely enough to house a small tribe. It is possible these structures have been inhabited for generations—weathered, patched, shaped again and again as the plateau’s storms allow or force them to do so.

The people here (for one must assume people, or something akin to them, built those huts) work with tools of sharpened stone or hammered scrap metal gleaned from some unknown source. Their implements and adornments are rudimentary: curved knives fashioned from the bony ridges of local megafauna, small chisels carved from some greenish mineral, net-like contraptions woven from fibrous strands plucked off the underside of those tall spined growths. These materials are surprisingly resilient, battered yet not destroyed by the relentless storms.

Light never grows bright: the day is a permanent twilight, cast through swirling dust. Occasionally, a break in the clouds reveals a dull orange glow from above—perhaps a hidden star, or something else entirely—lighting the plateau for a moment with a surreal, dreamlike hush, only for the wind to pick up again and the gloom to return. 

Though the land is harsh and the scenery stark, there’s great strangeness in how it all comes together. The plateau itself seems to hover on the edge of stability, crumbling a bit at the perimeter, shifting its shape each time a heavier gust of wind rattles the spires. Yet it has also existed here a long time, so well-worn that pebbles have been polished to smooth orbs, and the ridges on the pillars are so deeply carved that any further erosion takes them into entirely new shapes. It’s in balance with the chaos—somehow, despite the violent motion of the air, everything continues to stand.

And always, there is motion and sound. Huge flocks of insect-like creatures swirl overhead in bright, erratic orbits, releasing pulses of color or squeals akin to whistles. Occasionally, from distant corners of the plateau, you’ll hear the harsh crack of stone splitting when a storm drives an especially hard blast of wind. Along the rocky edges where the ground slopes downward, avalanches of chalky dust tumble off into the unseen depths. Bones of ancient beasts—or perhaps of ancient dwellers—sometimes resurface, momentarily exposed by swirling dust, only to be buried again.

Any visitor quickly learns that calm is transient here: the hush between gusts is brief, a pregnant moment before the next explosive rush of wind and bizarre, chattering life. In the shelters, dim lamps made from glowing crystals flicker behind tanned-skin curtains or plant-fiber doors, shining softly through the dusty gloom. People or creatures gather around these lights, hunched against the persistent gale. It might be a place of worship or a place of rest. Each structure stands on its own, with only the slightest suggestion of a central meeting area containing scorch marks and scattered remains of shared meals.

This plateau demands respect. Its dull skies, fiercely swirling dust, roiling storms, and dry, crackled soils initially seem inhospitable. But the patches of intense, non-watery growth speak of adaptation: life finds a home here, with its own logic, its own timing, fed by hidden streams of chemical energy beneath cracks in the stone. And oh, how it bustles with activity, each creature and plant playing a part in this unending, chaotic dance—booming, shrieking, rustling, scuttling across a landscape that keeps changing shape, yet remains steadfastly itself.
