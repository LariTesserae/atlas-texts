Even before you arrive, the heat presses on you like a weight. The air is thick, saturated with moisture—almost a fine mist, as though the world has never quite stopped raining. Low, wide expanses of flat, wet ground stretch out in all directions. There’s no greenery as far as the eye can see—no trees, no grass, not even a tangle of reeds. It’s all mineral flats, shallow pools fringed with salt or strange lichen-like crusts. The moisture beads on every surface, seeping from rocks, dripping steadily off iron stanchions, and trickling into shallow canals. You soon realize everything here is built upon watery soil, never entirely dry.

Yet for all its emptiness, this is not a lifeless place. Far from it. Every step stirs flickers of motion: creatures darting away in the shallows, amphibian forms slithering into cracks, and giant insects lifting off in startled, humming flight. There are so many small bodies crowding the edges of puddles and channels that it seems the entire land squirms just beneath the surface. The animals are everywhere, far outnumbering the visible structures, which only accentuates the eerie quiet. Now and then, a deep croaking call echoes over metal rooftops; otherwise, it’s a hush broken by the drip of condensation and the unsteady rumble of distant machinery.

That machinery belongs to a sprawling urban maze: a city that looks like it was built in layers over decades or centuries of industrial progress. Its buildings are low and wide at the edges, then rise into blocky towers at the center, ringed by scaffolding and snaking pipes. Long elevated walkways crisscross from one platform to another, designed to keep foot traffic out of the marshy pools below. Black rivets, steel beams, and networks of corrugated metal define nearly every edge. Occasionally, a dusty window or a glass-walled catwalk shows flickers of internal lights—orange glows that hint at factories or industrial plants busily humming away past midnight. Steam pours from pitted vents, merging with the warm, heavy air in twisting clouds.

Despite the evidence of busy production, the city feels subdued—dimly lit from a sky perpetually blanketed in low-hanging, moisture-laden clouds. Sunlight barely penetrates; instead, everything remains in a permanent twilight. Electric lamps hang from overhead cables in the narrower streets, their light feeble and flickering off the damp metal. If there is a roar of factory engines, it is curiously muffled. You catch the rumble of some colossal generator when the wind shifts, or the sharp hiss of compressed steam released into the open. But mostly, you traverse corridors of near-silence and stillness, broken only by the scuttling of wildlife.

As you move deeper into the city, you notice how time and weather have worn it. Rust creeps along the lower steel panels where the dampness is constant. Stone foundations have grown pitted and dark from centuries of water trickling down walls. Because there is no greenery to cover or reclaim these old structures, the signs of wear are stark and visible: a layering of mineral deposits, rust, and a patina that speaks of industry long settled into this place.

Every alley opens up onto wet thoroughfares or open squares where puddles reflect overhead pipes. Where you’d expect trees or ornamental gardens, there are instead stacks of crates, rows of heavy machinery, and fungal-like growths in cracks near vents—perhaps not true fungus but some other life-form that thrives on the humid heat. The rest of the city’s surfaces remain lifeless, deserted of typical flora. In compensation, the city teems with animal life. At certain corners, you see movements that might be reptilian creatures or large amphibians flickering out of sight. In the gloom above, winged silhouettes swirl around taller structures, occasionally swooping into the gloom beyond the warm glow of lamps.

It is a landscape of paradox: hot and dripping wet, expansive yet oddly sparse of living plants, at once hollow and bustling. You can sense the ties that hold it together—old roads built on top of rusting girders, half-flooded conduits connecting industrial zones, and a haze of steam that rises day and night, carrying the relentless pulse of the city’s engines. A subtle cycle emerges: the downpour condenses, trickles through channels, and is gathered or flows back out to the open plain which surrounds the urban sprawl. Then the sun—when it can break through—vaporizes water in shimmering waves, only for clouds to gather again and let another rain soak everything anew.

And through it all, the animal kingdom holds sway. You see small creatures hunched under riveted awnings, large insectoid swarms swirling in upward drafts of heat, and countless more hidden in the labyrinth of metal and water. Encounter after encounter reminds you that this place is theirs as much as it is the domain of heavy machines and human engineering.

In this low-lying realm of ever-present humidity and heat, the city seems self-contained, sealed off from any grand horizon by those hanging clouds and the salty haze. Yet it isn’t cramped. It stretches on for miles, a puckered expanse of corrugated metal rooftops, columns of steam, and watery basins. Overhead, the sky stays dim, offering just enough light to make out the silhouettes of distant smokestacks. Even with its sense of perpetual dusk, the city brims with active processes—factories churning, creatures scuttling under walkways, and water forever gathering and dispersing. It is a place with no need of plants, because every inch of life is in the creatures themselves; every surface is worn to its purpose but still stands. And in that balance of old industry, abundant animals, constant damp, and ambient quiet, the entire scene simply is—an equilibrium, long established, yet always in motion.
