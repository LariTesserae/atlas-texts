A low plain stretches out beneath a sky of perpetual gloom: the sun (if there is one at all) seems permanently locked behind thick ripples of cloud, so the daylight never brightens past a subdued twilight. The air is warm and damp, as though after a long, heavy rain, but this place does not quite have the smell of fertile earth. There’s no fresh tang of green plants here—indeed, it’s almost entirely barren of vegetation. Instead, the humidity clings to gritty, mineral surfaces. Moisture gathers in rivulets along the edges of rocky outcrops and in shallow depressions underfoot, feeding a constant sheen of water that meanders through the settlement.

Because yes—this place is undeniably constructed, and constructed on a grand scale. What begins as a scattering of squat, block-like structures quickly reveals a crowded sprawl of low towers, huts, and winding corridors that radiate out in all directions. The color palette is drab, mostly shades of clay-brown, dark slate, or dull basalt, gently glossed by the perpetual damp. One gets the sense that these buildings, though closely packed into an almost claustrophobic density, have been here forever. Yet somehow they also seem newly formed, as if the local geology itself rose in lumps and was hollowed out into dwellings and pathways, never once experiencing wind or water strong enough to wear them down. The walls remain crisp, sharply angled, and unblemished, as if no time has passed at all.

Empty cracks run through some of the thoroughfares, catching trickles of water that have never eroded nor deepened them. The ground is firm but has a raw, unfinished quality, like half-set concrete. There’s a faint mineral tang in the humidity that condenses on exposed skin or architecture, leaving everything slick to the touch. Occasionally, droplets coalesce into small pools against the corners of structures, but they never grow large enough to flood or drain away. It’s as though the world is in perfect equilibrium: the water that appears is the same water that vanishes or is used elsewhere, all processes locked into an unchanging cycle.

It is dim, but not quiet. Far from it. In fact, the sound is overwhelming—an unceasing din that reverberates through these labyrinthine streets. Much of it comes from the creatures that teem here. With little vegetation, the native fauna must rely on something else entirely for sustenance. Some scuttle up walls, scraping with tiny claws; others chirp, click, or hiss as they congregate near the water-channels that course through the settlement. There are no tall grasses or shrubs in which they can hide; instead, they duck behind slabs of stone or press into cracks in the clay-like walls, emerging in swarms when they sense a fresh patch of condensation to lap at or—perhaps—when they detect the presence of a different species.

Some look like pale amphibious things, frog-like yet elongated, their mucous-slick bodies shining in the half-light as they crowd together in convulsive clusters, adding to the claustrophobic density. Others move in packs, four-legged but with oddly segmented bodies, their eyes and carapaces designed for the constant twilight—perhaps no true daylight has ever been known here. Whatever their form, they exist in vast numbers, scurrying, calling, bellowing. Their noise, echoing off the fortress-like architecture, becomes a roar so constant and powerful that it blurs into the background, vibrating the rock underfoot. Trying to speak here is futile; no one sound can stand out for long, absorbed by the ceaseless tumult of life.

And yet, the settlement’s human or human-like inhabitants, if they exist, keep to simple ways. Crafted from local clays or hammered from brittle, unrefined metals, the tools and implements scattered around are crude. Stone adzes, chipped obsidian knives, pots shaped by hand—there’s no sign of advanced machinery. Despite the complexity of the layered dwellings, everything else about this culture, if it is indeed a culture, suggests a primal self-sufficiency. Sometimes you hear what might be chanting, or the distant thump of rough drums, but it’s hard to pick it out from the endless chittering, clicking, and scrabbling that fills the air.

Whether by decree of some unseen force or merely by the nature of this place, nothing changes. The stones do not wear away, the water neither increases nor dries up, the creatures remain in near-constant numbers. It is a tableau of ongoing life, locked in an eternal now. The dimness never lifts, the moisture never rolls into a full storm. Even the heat—which might, in other conditions, fuel thunderheads or cause waves of shimmering haze—seems caught in an unvarying stillness. No matter how long one stays, it’s as though you’ve arrived mid-sentence in a conversation that never ends, and when you leave, it will continue on, uninterrupted, in the same raw and timeless state.
