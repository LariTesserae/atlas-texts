Beneath a crust of sun-baked, coppery earth—so dry it cracks and curls like old parchment—there lies a vast, hollow realm of warm, living density. There is scarcely any true water here; nothing flows or pools, not as we would recognize. Instead, a thicket of tangled plant-like life dominates every available surface, so all-consuming that the very walls seem grown rather than carved. The vegetation is fleshy and fibrous, made of a peculiar chemistry that requires no moisture. It forms columns, intricate lattices, and broad spans of woven trunk, as if some grand subterranean forest had erupted from below and swallowed up the caverns in a single unstoppable surge.

The space is big enough to suggest towns or valleys if only there were distinct edges, but there are none. It fans out beneath the world above, sprawling through chambers that connect in labyrinthine configurations. Vines as thick as a person’s torso coil overhead, forming living rafters. Bioluminescent nodules hang in clusters; they cast a glow that, while adequate for seeing, flickers with pulses of plant-energy. The flicker is sporadic—at times steady, at times strobing—contributing to a constantly shifting ambience that never settles.

It is permanently warm here, a thick-robed warmth that makes the air feel heavy. Metallic tangs and resinous odors tinge every breath. The dryness is uncanny: though the place is full of life, it is a form of life that eschews water. There is a sensation of “dusty humidity,” an odd paradox—the dryness sifts across your skin as you move, yet the press of tangled growth envelops you and almost feels wet, though it never truly is.

Everything in this subterranean realm is in flux. The plant network is still in an embryonic state, its shapes and pathways actively evolving. Great roots push aside earth and stone. The floor underfoot heaves unpredictably as thick creepers expand, forging new corridors or collapsing old ones. You might spot a swath of bright, translucent fronds appear overnight, only to dissolve as the root systems shift their energy elsewhere. It’s a world of ceaseless rearrangement, like a grand living puzzle trying to piece itself together.

Scattered among the twisting pillars of vegetation are remnants of primitive craft—small platforms built from clipped vines hammered flat into flexible planks, or little huts perched precariously on knotted ridges. Each structure seems half-claimed by the flora, as if people tried to build safe havens but the plants reclaimed them faster. Ladders of woven fiber dangle from impossible heights, straining beneath the weight of creeping growth. Nothing is stable for long; you sense that no wooden or mineral artifact lasts more than a few cycles before the slow, methodical appetite of the vines consumes it.

For all the plant dominance, animal life is perhaps even more overwhelming. Creatures of every form—some insectile, some mammalian, many quite alien—surge in countless swarms through the undergrowth, across ceilings of braided stems, and into the half-formed hollows beyond. Underfoot, little scaled rodents skitter in droves, their chirps melding with the rhythmic scrabbling of exoskeletal legs. Higher up, gliding reptiles or winged arthropods spiral in columns, releasing keening cries that reverberate in the softly glowing air. Slithering beasts the size of men wind between roots like sinuous shadows, and multi-eyed birds—if that is what they are—shoot overhead, calling in voices like crashing gongs.

The noise is constant and deafening. Roars, shrieks, and clacking mandibles collide in a nerve-jangling cacophony. It’s as if the entire biosphere is shouting to be heard at once. Slaps of scaled tails against living walls, a constant thrumming drone of tens of thousands of wings, the scratch of claws on plant-fiber columns—it all merges into a rolling, thunderous background. When entire segments of vine or root break free from the ceiling and crash down, the boom echoes like subterranean thunder, scattering flocks of squealing animals in every direction.

The place crackles with instability. You feel a sense of urgency in every motion; the ground itself could shift on a whim. Thick cables of plant tissue sometimes bulge, then burst in a cascade of spore-filled air that swirls into new layers of growth. It’s a scene of perpetual chaos—like watching the world being born and destroyed in the same breath. Some plants extend themselves at impressive speed, finger-like vines creeping forward to hook around new outcroppings. Others wither in the same moment, their purpose spent, leaving behind papery husks that peel away in the swirling currents.

Here, “technology” might be a sharpened piece of root used as a knife, or a simple hammered-metal blade scavenged from minerals gleaned in cracks of the earth. Fragments of bone or chitin are bound together into tools. Evidence of these craft items is scarce—one sees them mostly in fleeting glimpses: a leaning spear improvised from a twisted branch, or a shield made of an immense beetle’s shell. Everything is ephemeral. Any sign of humanoid presence—footprints in the layer of dust or little rope bridges of braided vine—quickly gets swallowed by the unstoppable vegetative expansions.

Strangest of all is the way time itself seems fluid. On one side of a chamber, you see the vegetation in furious advance, absorbing calcium from broken geodes, forging new rib-like arches overhead. On the other side, a massive root structure withers, entire colonies of creatures scattering from it. The cycle never stops—turbulent, alive, and unbound by gentle evolution.

Down here, the air rings with a chorus of chaos, and the ground echoes with primordial growth. It is a realm forever under construction, a cradle of raw life where roots and bark replace stone walls, where animals are legion, and where every moment is an ear-splitting tumult of creation and collapse. Despite the dryness, despite the minimal presence of water, it is nonetheless an all-encompassing tangle of organic intensity—an underworld that is at once embryonic and ancient, alien and overwhelmingly alive.
