At first glance, it seems like a citadel of stone perched high in the thin air—yet upon walking closer, you realize every inch has been shaped by hand tools. The walls and floors are fashioned from meticulously fitted slabs of a faintly glimmering mineral, each tile cut to exact geometries and slotted into place without mortar. The entire expanse—stretching from one distant crag of the plateau to another—feels crowded. No space lies unused. Pillars, walls, wedges, ramps, channels for water: all press up against each other in tight, almost claustrophobic density. There’s not so much as a palm’s breadth of unoccupied ground. 

Though high in elevation, the atmosphere has a palpable cool dampness. Everywhere you turn, you see rivulets seeping from cracks and corners—patient trickles of water that gather in recessed basins or run down chiseled runnels. Some droplets condense on smooth surfaces, forging thin, glinting sheets before sliding off in slow arcs. Little terraces and canals guide these flows, as though the place were designed to capture and circulate every last drop across multiple levels. Despite the frequent wetness, there’s no lush growth. No plants cling to these stones, no moss softens the edges. This wetness is purely mineral and liquid—a watery presence shaped and contained rather than embraced by living things. 

With no animals lurking and no greenery softening the architecture, a quiet hush pervades. You hear only the occasional drip of water, the gentle hiss as it gathers momentum in narrow channels, and the subtle echo of your own footsteps across the polished floors. The air is bright—almost glaringly so. It’s as if the sun hits these high altitudes and reflects off the pale mineral surfaces with fierce insistence. Wind is minimal, and the entire setting feels surprisingly still, as though the landscape itself were designed to avoid unnecessary commotion. Everything changes so slowly here—water levels shift over the seasons, stone surfaces gradually wear from the trickling liquid, but the cycle unfolds across ages rather than days. 

No plant and no animal inhabits these colossal steps and corridors, yet it’s far from desolate. The place itself is alive in an architectural sense—a testament to some unknown craft, ancient yet somehow primitive. Evidence of tools is everywhere: chisel marks, scratches from vague implements, wedges hammered to split stone. There are no intricate metal joinings or advanced machinery, only rugged, hand-cut geometry locked into a seamless tapestry of mineral surfaces. It is impossible to say when this place was constructed; each feature appears fresh, as if the stones were quarried only recently, yet the scope and scale of the complex cityscape suggest centuries—if not millennia—of uninterrupted effort. 

Oddly, there’s a sense that the environment obeys a particular set of carefully applied rules. Each angle, each step, each trough for water is measured out just so—nothing truly random, yet not standardized in a manner we’d associate with modern engineering. It is more akin to an archaic blueprint repeated again and again, adapted to the slope of the highland, shaped to follow the subtle rise and fall of the underlying rock. This repetition lends a hypnotic quality: you round a corner only to find corridors that mirror the ones you left behind, continuing onward in a slow, methodical pattern. The brilliance of the sun reveals everything clearly, but the uniformity of the architecture still leaves you uncertain how far you’ve come or how far remains. 

The world here is strange but serene. Water is abundant—spreading across terraces, winding through gently shifting channels—but no life emerges from it. The sky is a sharp, dome-like clarity overhead, the distant horizon lined with more of these carved mineral silhouettes. Though the place is grand on a regional scale, not a single sign of animal presence or plant intrusion breaks the sculpted lines. Some subtle, unnameable hum resonates sometimes, as though the entire structure were humming in quiet conversation with the wind. Yet that too is soft and intermittent, a nearly silent testament to the slow, patient forces shaping this environment. You can feel it in the stones: a steady, ongoing process that will continue for countless ages—water seeping, surfaces wearing, yet holding together in secure, handcrafted perfection.
