It stretches out in every direction, a world lacking the shimmer of water yet brimming with living, green-tinged things—though calling them “plants” barely does them justice. They stand in for every surface you’d expect to be rock or soil or metal, forming an unbroken sea of living walls and buttresses, wide leaves interlocking to create roads, tangling vines shaped into corridors and machine cradles. You can’t even see the ground beneath that fibrous weave; the floor is an endless carpet of thick, knotted stems. It’s all vegetation, yet somehow it needs no moisture to thrive, its chemistry operating under alien laws or advanced engineering.

Cool, bright light suffuses this entire biosynthetic realm—no visible sun in the sky, but it might as well be noon all day. Where you expect shadows, you see only the faintest of them, edges that flicker as leaflike surfaces shift. It feels like a maze so vast it has its own horizons, its own weather patterns, slow gusts and stirrings drifting through the corridors formed by overlapping canopies. The dryness in the air is paradoxical in contrast to the sense of lush growth everywhere you look.

Every trunk, leaf, and tendril appears artificially shaped, as if carefully designed in a mechanized factory. Indeed, there is a sense that this entire environment is constructed: the “trees” are more like pillars or scaffolds, and the vines might be harness lines for electricity, humming with faint currents. If you look closely, you detect industrial struts woven into the living tissue—metallic ribs supporting fat stems, rivets embedded like seeds under flexible bark. Exhaust vents open in the sides of trunks, releasing warm vapor that condenses into glittering motes before being whisked away by near-silent fans. It’s a wonder of large-scale engineering, thoroughly planted with the chemical-stem “architecture” that all but eradicates the line between nature and machine.

The quiet is uncanny. Sometimes you hear gentle hisses from vents or a dull rumble far beneath the living floor, but nothing else: no chatter of animals, no dripping of water (there is no water), and almost no sign of fauna. In the distance—if you walk for hours along sinuous corridors of leaf and metal—there are said to be rare creatures that live in the high arches or sub-level vaults, though even seeing their silhouettes is uncommon. Their tracks appear occasionally where a spongy frond has been pressed flat, but there’s hardly a whisper of them in the air.

The temperature remains cool, as though regulated by some invisible system. Massive pipes—crafted from roots or rootlike pipes, it’s hard to tell—snake through the thick green substrate, carrying chilled vapors that keep the temperature low. You can sense movement beneath your feet and overhead, an ongoing cycle pumping energy through the living-mechanized tissue. Spore-like flecks breathe in and out of vents, dissolving upon contact with the bright, too-white light that saturates everything.

Time feels warped here. The environment seems ancient, worn in parts by intangible gravitational or dimensional forces, but also strangely new and alive. Edges of leaves show scarring, as if battered by aeons of strange storms, but the mechanical underpinnings never rust or corrode. Certain places flicker unnervingly: the supports might bend at impossible angles or shimmer around the perimeter of your vision, as if reality has grown thin. Where vines meet canopy, the geometry sometimes loops and folds in ways that defy logic, branches merging into each other without a clear boundary, leaves swallowing entire sections of walkway and depositing them somewhere else entirely.

And yet, in these bizarre folds, you may catch glimpses of industrial-scale processes: huge turbines rotating behind a half-fused membrane of bark and steel. A row of pistonlike growths might pump nutrients or raw materials toward some massive factory hidden in the living substrate. It’s an ecosystem and a factory line at once, transcending typical categories of “organic” and “built.” You cannot say where engineering ends and biology begins. High above, the canopy breathes and pulses with life, flashing bright green and glimmering with the reflection of unseen lamps. Occasionally, branches part to form great, arching windows, letting in a pure, unfiltered glow from some external source—though it’s never entirely clear whether it’s cosmic light or a by-product of the structure’s own tangled systems.

Despite this constant, gentle flux, the place remains eerily quiet. No wind whistles, only soft mechanical undertones, as if the hum of a planetary engine resonates through the woven skeleton of the world. In the distance, you might glimpse subtle flashes of movement: a single winged silhouette skittering along a trunk or vanishing into a tunnel of leaves. It’s one of the few fauna that survive here. There is a sense they only come out occasionally, ready to disappear at any sign of presence.

Over days—or what passes for days in this place—you realize these processes all serve a larger dynamic. Changes in color sweep across the canopies in waves, like the shift of seasons compressed into hours. Now and then, an entire corridor of living “architecture” recedes into the floor, only to open elsewhere, restructured, improved, repurposed. Strange swirling storms of luminescent pollen might drift, coalesce, and be vacuumed away into enormous vents. And through all these transformations, you feel that the world stands in a long-settled equilibrium, not at risk of imminent collapse but shaped by engineering on a planetary scale.

It goes on and on, continent-sized, perhaps even bigger. At times, you sense the fabric of this realm is fracturing at the edges—impossible angles beckon in the distance, or roots vanish into glowing fissures. Reality itself is pliable here, or maybe just maintained by the hidden systems pulsing beneath your feet. You can’t shake the feeling that if you wander too far into the horizon, you’ll slip through a tear in space, moving to an even stranger domain. Or maybe that’s just what passes for a horizon in an all-living, all-constructed, dimensionally uncertain plane.

Yet in this vast place, for all its humming industries and looming structures of leaf and steel, true silence is the norm. Every sound is muffled, each mechanical drone softened by the fibrous layers that form the substrate. No wind, no water, only the faint mechanical sigh and the hush of unstoppable growth, unstoppable innovation—an entire world made of living metal, industrial vines, and bright, baking dryness that somehow fosters an impossible, all-encompassing green life.
