High cold air drifts across the continent like a gently moving glacier of mist. The light that pervades this domain is steady, neither glaring nor dim, just enough to unveil the enormous sweep of terrain one can see in every direction. Whether one stands upon a crumbled overlook or at a lower terrace, the horizon never seems to come closer, and the land’s colossal scale hints at forces so large they defy simple human measure.

The ground here is ancient and heavily worn. Sheer slopes and battered ledges hint at ridges that may once have been mountain ranges, now eroded into bulges and low, undulating crests that extend for miles. In many places, the surface looks scoured almost flat, like the bottom of a drained ocean. Despite that, little pockets and basins remain damp: ephemeral streams or collections of condensation gather among jagged stone. Sometimes a faint drizzle ghosts across the sky, tapping softly against rock before ceasing as quickly as it began, leaving behind cool beads that clothe the surfaces in reflective droplets.

And yet, in the midst of coldness and thin air, lush vegetation takes root. It flourishes in surprising forms—some low to the ground, like broad patches of mint-green moss or dense clusters of what appear to be swollen, translucent stems reminiscent of reeds. Other growths cling to the rock faces in creeping webs of vine-like strands. Despite being woven with life, the land feels almost empty; these plants stand at a distance from each other, with wide intervals of stone or wind-razed gravel in between. No birds hover; there are no scurrying shapes in the underbrush—no creatures at all, merely the rustle of leaves shifting in the chill.

From time to time, swirling gusts blow across the plateau, making the entire canopy of unusual vegetation sway in a synchronized dance. The resulting hush is nearly musical, a long, drawn-out chord that resonates, then fades. Apart from this stirring, the world remains largely quiet. There are no roars or howls here—only the faint murmur of breezes gliding along ridges and the occasional drip of water into a nearby pool.

Within this expanse stands a sprawling urban construct, but it is nothing like a city of steel and glass. Large, time-worn structures rise from terraces carved directly into ancient stone, their blocky silhouettes reminiscent of megaliths. Made primarily by stacking huge slabs or shaping the bedrock into corridors, the buildings hint at a civilization that relied on basic tools—chiseled edges and simple supports. Archways truly old beyond record stand ready to collapse at the gentlest seismic tremor, yet they remain, anchored to the immensity of the landscape.

Among the ruins, worn causeways link grand courtyards. Some corridors shoot into the deeper labyrinth under the crust, carved with such painstaking detail that you can still see faint inscriptions—each stone etched with geometric shapes or symbols that distort if you stare for too long. It takes a moment to realize how the geometry keeps shifting, as though pieces of the walls flicker from one angle to another. Even the floors, smoothed by countless centuries of footsteps, sometimes give off a faint quiver, as if the ground can’t decide where it ends.

The air feels intermittently moist, pockets of condensation forming on the city’s steps, then sliding down in trails that vanish into cracks. Occasionally, half-frozen sheets of water catch the feeble sunlight and glimmer with a subdued glow. But in some corners, reality itself seems turned inside out: a door frame gives a view not of more corridor, but an exterior ravine hanging at an impossible angle. A vaulted chamber overhead warps the sounds of the wind into whispers that cannot quite be traced to their source.

Despite the cold, the planet-wide scale, the ancient erosion, there is undeniable movement here. Storms churn slowly across the distance, their clouds sometimes collapsing into the folded valley systems. Lightning pulses—brief, jagged lines—and then the hush returns. Trees with fibrous trunks swish in the wind; thick vines creep across the city walls with deliberate slowness, weaving cracks shut or prying them open. One might watch for hours as the entire ecosystem breathes, as though the land itself were a living thing—just one that no longer has animals to call or scuttle in its expanse.

In the quiet moments, it’s almost as if the far-off horizon is humming with a deep, cosmic tone. The place is vast, cold, old, yet oddly fertile, infused with swirling anomalies that question the rules of nature. Here, the land stays perpetual in its contradictions: primeval and immense, worn yet still shifting, sparse but laced with a quiet abundance of life, empty of voices yet filled with the echo of something that shaped these walls. It is a world on the cusp of reality’s edge—both settled in its final form and continually breaking, as if the act of existing in such a colossal space tears at the seams of what ought to be. And into that gap of strangeness, water condenses, vegetation thrives, and silent structures endure, waiting for someone—or something—to wander through again.
