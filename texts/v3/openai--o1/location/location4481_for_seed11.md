This place feels as though it has lived countless lifetimes. Broad swathes of land stretch out in all directions, nearly flattened from unimaginable eras of wind and rain until only ridges and knife-sharp plateaus remain. Somehow, in the hollows between these ancient ridges, life still thrives. In some pockets, the vegetation is so lush it stuns the eye—tall grasses bending in the constant gale, feathery moss hugging rock surfaces, spindly trees with serpentine roots clinging to the old stone. The air is warm, carrying the scent of damp vegetation and dust. It tastes slightly metallic and fades into the cool tang of occasional rain. Yet the dryness lingers like a memory, so that everything feels slightly parched even though condensation drips from broad leaves.

Jagged spires of rock are the few remnants of what must once have been towering mountains. Time and unrelenting erosion have smoothed them, as if an ancient sculptor has worked on them for ages, achieving an asymmetrical balance between sharp silhouettes and gently rounded contours. Stepping out onto one of the tall plateaus, you can see how the land stretches far, forming a chaotic, ridged geometry on the horizon. And despite this openness, the sense of emptiness pervades—there’s a volume of space large enough to swallow entire civilizations without a trace.

When the storms come, they come with tremendous force. Thunder reverberates through the canyons, echoing back onto itself in a ceaseless, overlapping roar. The winds howl from multiple directions, scouring the open land and stirring dust into swirling funnels. At times, there is so much noise it feels as if every stone is vibrating—an orchestra of storms raging, momentarily drowning out all thought. Ponderous clouds roil overhead, bruise-purple near the bottom, shining white near the top where they catch the brighter light. Rain might lash down for just a minute or two, enough to form quick streams on the rock faces, then vanish, leaving ribbons of water droplets clinging to everything.

The vegetated pockets flourish in the wake of these storms—ferns unfurl, broad-leafed creepers crawl slowly up angled stones, and bright clusters of ochre fungi bloom in the damp cracks. Even so, animals are surprisingly scarce. Occasionally, one might see a small, sure-footed creature darting between boulders—a flash of fur, or a glimpse of scaled hide. More often you’ll spot footprints in mud or hoof-marks etched into sandy flats, rather than the beasts themselves. Some say the wildlife here learned long ago to stay quiet and hidden when the storms rage, and to roam in the calm that follows.

Scattered through the ridges are hints of former settlements—rudimentary walls of stacked stones, curious round huts that appear chiseled from single boulders, or circles of monolithic pillars whose purpose is faded into legend. A few stone posts mark old pathways, often toppled by relentless winds but sometimes still standing, leaning at angles as if about to tumble. Whether these places served as dwellings, shrines, or something else is not certain. The technology behind them is simple—blocks fitted without mortar, wooden pegs rotted away and replaced by wads of dried moss—but the arrangement suggests a deep comprehension of the land’s fury.

Through it all, there is an undercurrent of strangeness. Vines sometimes glow faintly under the scattered moonlight, or shapes rise in the warm updrafts that cannot be birds. Lightning along the horizon might crack in unnatural patterns, fleeting geometric webs in the clouds. When the storms intensify, the heart of the plateau seems to tremble, and for a moment, one might catch glimpses of forms that defy logic—transient illusions dancing in the veils of dust and water. 

And always, the sound. It prowls through the canyons like a living thing: thunder that booms, wind that screeches, boulders shifting, hidden cracks collapsing. It’s a place caught forever between calm and collapse, never truly at rest, yet so ancient it feels as though it might endure everything. One day the storms might grind these plateaus to dust—but for now they stand, battered yet steadfast, a grand, turbulent stage on which lush life, whispered traditions, and a strange hush of emptiness exist side by side.
