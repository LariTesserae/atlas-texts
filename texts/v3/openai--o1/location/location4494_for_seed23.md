It is a high, cold place of constant wetness and startling brightness, as though the sky pressed closer and poured out more light than anywhere else. Moisture clings to every surface—thin veils of mist curling along ridges, droplets sliding down the smooth flanks of rock, and rivulets that collect in shallow basins dotted around the settlement. At times, you can see water condensing in midair, like a faint glitter that trembles before falling to the ground. Despite the cold, the precipitation is rarely snow; it’s more like an icy drizzle that never quite thaws, never quite freezes.

Everything is timeworn. The stone edges are no longer sharp, but softened by centuries of persistent wind and water. Places meant to hold shape—old walls, perhaps—have slumped and become as much a part of the cliff faces as the natural shelves of rock. The entire terrain feels as though it has reached a sort of equilibrium with the elements, having surrendered to them long ago. Here and there, pockets of stagnant pools are flecked with thin ice, reflecting the shimmer of the intense sunlight, so that the landscape seems alive with tiny mirrors.

Vegetation is rare—only a few hardy tufts cling to the stony soil, grey-green whiskers that hint at life but never truly flourish. But the animal life is somehow abundant, as if the creatures themselves don’t need thick forest or lush meadows to thrive. Small, quick silhouettes slip around boulders or dart across the water’s edge—creatures with pale, wet fur or shining scales, all well-adapted to the damp cold. Some have large, glimmering eyes, as if any advantage in seeing through the ever-present mist is precious. Others appear in flocks or herds, crowding so close that one might expect clamor—but there comes only a soft rustling, almost respectful, as if they know the hush of this place must remain.

The settlement that occupies a broad ledge near the heart of this plateau is packed tightly together, every building nearly touching the next—densely layered huts of stone and rough-hewn timber, with thick hide flaps stretched across entrances to keep out the chill. Despite the adjacency, there is no grand sprawl; it’s more like a single cluster, a cluster that suggests coordinated construction but without elaborate tools. You see evidence of very primitive methods: hammering stones together with smooth river rocks, chiseling at the rock walls with sharpened bone or simple metal edges. Yet there is a certain ingeniousness to their building—channels cut into rocky outcrops direct water away from living spaces, and small sloping grooves on rooftops funnel rainfall down into communal cisterns.

While overall it’s a quiet place—footsteps muted by wet earth, voices muffled by the thick, cold air—there is a sense of constant upheaval, as if something is restless. Gusts of wind can whip down from the crags without warning, knocking loose bits of shale or turning the mist into swirling, disorienting veils. In an instant, what was still and silent can become a chaos of motion: the scurrying of the many animals taking shelter, the huts creaking and drumming under the sudden pressure, droplets clattering on taut hide. Moments later, calm might return, the air stilled and everything hushed under that blazing sky.

The bright light itself is peculiar: it arrives with stark clarity one moment, casting sharp-edged shadows of every post or protruding stone, and in the next moment it refracts in the mist, making the entire settlement appear surrounded by faint rainbows or shimmering halos on the ground. Indeed, this strange interplay of dazzling light and thick wet atmosphere conjures illusions—making distant shapes ripple, bringing odd patterns of color glinting off the surfaces of creatures. It can foster the sense that the world is warping and reforming in swift cycles, as if each gust of wind or swirl of vapor conjures a half-real new shape.  

Despite the near-silence, you can feel the incessant dynamism. Water trickles in labyrinthine channels, animals drift in ephemeral herds, and the structures themselves seem to breathe with the shifting humidity. At times, deeper noise can be sensed rather than heard: a low groaning from the stone, as if the entire plateau is releasing or absorbing tension. In corners of the settlement, small gatherings of fur-wrapped figures kneel by shallow pools or feed fires with fragments of old wood; their simple tools—bone fishing spears, flint knives, hammered pan-like vessels—lie near at hand. It appears they live in tandem with these watery flows and sudden bursts of wind, as if resigned to the turbulence yet unwilling to be uprooted by it. 

Little by little, the place gives the impression of a hidden realm overlooked by the rest of the world: high, cold, ever-damp, yet radiant. Old but unbroken, in a perfect and peculiar balance built on both the abundance of life and the strangeness of an atmosphere that loves to change. In the hush, you sense an ancient interplay of stone and water, life and gleaming brilliance—a realm that stands suspended in a bright, quiet storm, forever on the cusp of becoming something else.
