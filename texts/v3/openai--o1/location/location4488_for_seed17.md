In every direction, water claims the horizon—an endless plateau of cold, dark liquid that lies at such a high elevation it feels like you’ve stepped onto the rooftop of the world. The air itself is liquid-dense, pressing in on everything with a weight that suggests you’re several thousand meters above what might once have been dry ground. Yet there is no apparent shore, no green fringe—only the steel-blue sheen of water that extends farther than the eye can see.

The surface is rippled but not stormy, and it appears young in a geological sense, its features still sharp: occasional crags of black stone, recently thrust upward through the water, plane-flat on top as if carved by a precise blade. Some of these formations are so recently arrived that they bristle with jagged edges and clusters of mineral nodules. Not a single trace of moss or algae clings to them. In fact, there are no plants at all—just bare mineral surfaces, glassy from the cold, reminiscent of volcanic obsidian or polished basalt. It’s a world stripped almost entirely of vegetation, as though plant life never gained a foothold.

And yet, within the dark water, forms move. Pale shapes glide below the surface—fast, sinuous, ephemeral. Their presence gives the impression of an ecosystem teeming with life. From time to time, a sleek creature comes up to breathe (or perhaps to feed on the frigid air), then vanishes again into the depths. Despite the water’s clarity, details are elusive: these animals slip back down with a twist of fins or frills or stub-like limbs, vanishing the instant you try to pick out a recognizable shape. Their very abundance comes from sheer numbers rather than close clustering—most of the time, they are mere flickers of motion set against the dark emptiness.

Overhead, the light is a cool white, reminiscent of a distant sun filtered through a perpetual overcast. It is neither dim nor harsh, evenly distributed, leaving few shadows to shape the sense of distance. This austere glow stretches across the vast plane of water, but it does not warm. The air is numbing cold, a wintry hush in terms of temperature—yet ironically, there is no hush at all in terms of noise.

Because the place roars. It’s not a single thunderclap or wave crash; it’s a mechanical, unrelenting clangor layered with the deep, pulsing hum of turbines or engines. The sound resonates through the water, reverberates in the black stone, and vibrates in your very bones. Some of the crags support remnants of industry: tall silhouettes of metallic spires jut from the mineral outcrops, dark and dripping with condensation. From these spires come the cacophony of pistons, pounding in rhythmic cycles, and flares of metal-on-metal shrieks. Any stride you take feels accompanied by a subsonic churn rolling through the fluid air.

Yet the industrial structures seem oddly abandoned. You might think the pounding machines ought to have workers, but no one’s visible on the open platforms. Instead, you find cables encrusted with frost, partially submerged cylinders half-collapsed with age, or industrial piping that glows faintly from within. You can detect a few smaller silhouettes—a hatch door rusted open, or a lonely crane arm locked in position—but for the most part, everything feels as if it was put here to run forever, unnoticed and unchanging, powered by an unending source. Jets of steam or vapor vent at intervals, condensing instantly in the cold air, sinking back into the water. The whole system is caught in a static loop, cycling without evolution.

Gargantuan shapes drift near the spires, too large and too slow to be mistaken for the swift pale creatures you occasionally glimpse. These drifting presences move with an almost mechanical purpose, half-living, half-construct, their backs or shells lined with embedded metallic plating. Some are ringed with swirling patterns of lights that blink in unison—blue, white, then orange—and their surfaces appear rigid, as though grown from both flesh and industrial alloy. They navigate this submerged altitude with unhurried motions, generating intense waves that reverberate across the water’s surface. The roiling patches of disturbed water betray the bulk of these strange fauna. When they pass by the smaller spires, you hear the clang of metal-laced fins scraping ancient piping. The cry of twisting metal swells into a deafening shriek, then slides back into the ceaseless industrial hum.

Meanwhile, the atmosphere hangs in a kind of tense equilibrium. However violent the noise, however grand the scale, nothing truly seems to change. The water level neither rises nor recedes; the roar of machinery remains constant and unbroken; the stony crags neither erode nor shift. Even the drifting creatures follow cyclical patterns, like carefully calibrated cogs in an immense mechanism. Familiar day-night cycles appear to have no effect here. The air never warms, the water never cools further. There is neither dawn nor dusk—only this uniform, diffuse glow that illuminates infinite cold water and industrial silhouettes, locked in their cycles.

If there are rules to this world, they are rules of constancy. It has the feel of a place engineered long ago for a purpose its present inhabitants no longer recall. Whether a planet-wide experiment or a cosmic anomaly, it spans a distance impossible to measure by normal means. It dwarfs any ship or structure you could imagine bringing here. For all its emptiness, it is also saturated with that cacophonous drone, as though the entire realm is an immense industrial engine churning just beneath the surface of your senses.

You find only scattered hints of who built this world: a rusted control console perched precariously on a basalt ledge, a tangle of tubes half-submerged, a raised walkway leading nowhere. No markings remain. No inscriptions. Just the soft hiss of valves and the faint flicker of half-buried lights continuing their unending labor. Perhaps once, a great civilization thrived here—or perhaps the machinery built itself, step by step, generation after generation of automated construction, all ignoring the passage of time.

All the while, beneath you, the deep sea teems with secrets: thousands of nameless creatures that have adapted to this closed-loop environment. Their presence is reminiscent of life teeming at a hydrothermal vent—only everything is scaled up, sprawling across a world’s roof of water with no bedrock in sight. Their existence stands apart from the notion of plant life; their food webs rely on mineral-laced flows and the drifting remains of some greater cycle. Pulses of fluorescent color spark across their bodies on occasion, but flick out just as quickly, leaving you to wonder if you imagined it.

Through it all, the colossal plateau remains inert. The cold never ebbs, the height never alters, the industrial clang never falters, and the sky—if you can call it that—never turns from its faint, gray-white. You can stand motionless, hearing the machinery pound against your eardrums, watching the silhouettes of creatures slip away beneath the surface, and sense that if you were to return a century from now, or ten centuries, or a thousand times more, everything would appear precisely the same: a vast, near-empty domain of cold aquatic expanse, dotted with unwavering pillars of forgotten industry, where thunderous machinery and silent, unrecognized fauna interact in unending, unchanging loops.
