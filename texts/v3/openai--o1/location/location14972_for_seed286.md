At first, it seems like nothing but a vast, watery expanse where everything breathes liquid. The water is cool to the touch, just a hair above cold—enough to give a sharp buzz of energy rather than a deep chill. It stretches out in every direction as though the entire world is submerged within it. And yet, within this liquid realm, the presence of gargantuan plant life dominates.

These are no ordinary plants. They ascend (or descend, depending on your vantage) in mighty swathes, in columns so tall that they could span entire continents. They form a forest so all-encompassing it feels as though the plant matter is the foundation of reality here. The stems and trunks—if you can call them that—are as thick as city blocks, woven in a tapestry of green, red, purples, and deeper colors that darken as you peer into their shadows. Strangely, though the vegetation is everywhere, there remains tremendous space in between. It feels sparse, as if each colossal trunk deliberately grants the others breathing room: towers rising in watery grandeur, surrounded by broad, undulating frond-like leaves that move slowly with the current. You can swim or drift for what feels like miles between any two major trunks and only meet lesser vines that loop between them, forming lazy arcs and catwalks of greenery. 

It’s high up—or perhaps the pressure is low? No normal sense of “up” or “down” applies here, because everything is afloat: you can’t find a seabed or sky, as though you’re near the top of the world yet still completely submerged. The water has a curious way of remaining still—like a permanent hush—and at the same time, roaring with an undercurrent of noise. That roar is an odd, industrial-sounding drone, reverberating through the water. It’s mechanical in feel: a combination of rhythmic pulses and vibrations that carry for incredible distances without any visible machinery to explain them. It’s as if the planet itself is the machine, an ancient engine thrumming gently in steady equilibrium.

Nothing manufactured or built intrudes upon this environment—no blocks of metal or glass towers or obvious contraptions stand out. Yet the place resonates with an industrial presence, a sense of large-scale hardware churning deep in the background. Imagine gigantic cogs below the horizon out of sight, or a never-ending set of pipes that process and recycle the water. You never see them, but the drone never changes. The entire realm feels like it was fashioned by some mind-boggling process eons ago and left to hum along forevermore, perfectly balanced, never deviating from its established rhythm.

Though it’s a stable, unchanging world—no day meets night, no seasons shift the water’s temperature—there is an odd vibrancy of life. Strange animals, if you can call them that, move about in abundance. You catch glimpses of them in the distance: silvery forms as large as locomotives drifting between the trunks, trailing odd metallic filaments behind them. They might be fish, or whale-analogues, or fluid-based mechanical organisms with softly pulsing lights along their flanks. They exhale bubbles that fizz upward and dissolve without rising far. Others glide low to the “forest floor,” rummaging among the root networks that coil in tangled, twisting webs. Not all of these creatures are large—there are slender needle-like swimmers too, darting in and out amongst the fronds. Some even appear plant-like themselves, sprouting fins that blend right into the surrounding vegetation, making them vanish if they so much as slow down.

The sound of life here is not a quiet chirp or gentle splash. Instead, it’s a resonant clamor of rushing currents, the thrumming industrial hum, and the occasional metallic bellow from the larger beasts. The underwater forest rustles constantly. Leaves and vines rub against one another in a subtle cacophony that can build to a volume that resonates in your chest. When a school of those large, silver creatures passes, the swirl of their movement creates a crescendo: water churns and thunders around them, rattling the leaves like wind through steel cables.

Because nothing changes—no tides, no storms, no morning or evening—the sense of time evaporates. The environment might be ancient, long eroded into its perfect shape. Curiously, for all its watery lushness, it has the look of a place that has found its balance and remains there. Edges of root systems and columns appear worn down, as though centuries or millennia of subtle water flow have gently sculpted them into smooth lines and graceful curves. If you stare at a single spot and come back ages later, everything would look exactly the same, only maybe a small detail—like a leaf turning fractionally—hinting that time hasn’t completely stopped.

It’s a contradiction made whole: no visible machines, yet a grand mechanical hum. No signs of civilization, yet a deeply industrial mood. Entire planet-sized oceans within an impossibly tall forest, suspended in a dreamlike stasis. And life thrives everywhere in that hush-loud realm: enormous, unhurried, constantly moving but never changing. A serene paradox—calmly and eternally at equilibrium.
