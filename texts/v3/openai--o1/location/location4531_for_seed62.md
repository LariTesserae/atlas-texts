The sky here is uncommonly bright, unfiltered by smog or thick clouds; instead, it has a piercing clarity that befits a high-altitude realm. The air carries a pervasive coolness, the kind that empties your lungs of warmth when you first arrive, though not so cold as to chase away the green life. In fact, the vegetation is strikingly lush—ferns with bright, folded fronds clinging to sheer rock walls, vines weaving across protruding ledges, and hardy moss filling every niche with emerald softness. You might expect to hear a constant chorus of insects or birds in such dense plant life, but here, all is eerily quiet. The hush seems to dull every movement; even small gestures feel amplified by the surrounding silence.

At a glance, the world appears both vast and intimately close. It is neither a sprawling plane that vanishes into the horizon nor a cramped hollow; instead, it has the modest grandeur of a sizeable mountain valley—a broad space surrounded by soaring cliffs whose edges carve the sky in jagged silhouettes. Because the rock is relatively young—newly thrust from subterranean depths or perhaps shaped by a dramatic shift of the earth—its shapes remain sharp and crisp. Sweeps of stone rise in angular planes, revealing fractures where fresh mineral surfaces glitter in the bright light. Occasional watercourses trickle down these rocky faces, shimmering like silvered threads. They appear and disappear suddenly, as if the underground aquifers decide on a whim where to emerge.

The temperature’s coolness mingles with the damp, forming pockets of condensation: fine droplets cling to broad leaves and cluster on metal surfaces exposed to the air. Everywhere you look, the plant growth is thick—layer upon layer of vines, mosses, and miniature sprouts push up through the cracks in stone, climbing over debris and each other. The density of life seems to press in on itself, forming complicated tapestries of overlapping textures. Yet for all this flora, there is little sign of fauna. Only a rare glimpse of a winged shape passing through the treetops, or the dart of a small mammal, hints that animals exist here at all.

Amid this green hush, a few industrial structures stand scattered. They are not towering smokestacks or sprawling factories—rather, they’re more like remnants of a larger system, evidence of a past or ongoing project. You might see a rusted metal walkway bridging two rocky crags, an abandoned pump station half-buried by creepers, or a half-collapsed, corrugated building perched perilously on a stone outcrop. The architecture is heavy with rivets and metal plating, reminiscent of older industrial methods, but some facility within it still flickers with electricity. Wires trail from place to place, bridging small canyons with a faint hum—though that hum is only perceptible if you press your ear to the cables. Otherwise, the environment is preternaturally silent, as if the valley absorbs sound into itself.

What industry remains active? It seems slow, methodical—perhaps minimal maintenance keeps certain machines running while the rest has been reclaimed by the thick vegetation. Harsh mechanical edges are softened by the relentless cling of moss and vine, and the entire system functions in a strangely patient cycle. Boiler vents let out a barely audible sigh once in a great while. At times, you can almost feel the hum of machinery through the soles of your shoes more than you can hear it.

The light is so vibrant that surfaces glow, and shadows take on a crisp contrast. Yet despite the brightness, the world feels subtly distorted. Odd refractions of light might bend around certain rock formations, causing illusions of floating motes or swaying shapes. The condensation on leaves refracts that intense brightness into tiny rainbows that shift as you approach. You might notice a vine that seems to coil in upon itself in fractal patterns—unsettling if you stare too long. Likewise, the roofs of the industrial buildings have curious angles that suggest they were not originally designed for this place. They have bent or warped over time, warping more than just physically—there’s a strange geometry at work, as if the rules here allow for a slow drift of the normal.

Nothing changes quickly, though. The forces that shape these mountains move on a geologic timescale. The vines grow steadily, but you can’t watch them stretch in real-time. The rainfall that occurs is sporadic but subdued: pockets of drizzle that fill natural stone basins, creating reflective ponds that linger before slowly evaporating in the bright air. The condensation cycles in the mornings and evenings create a layer of ghostly dew that washes over every object.

In this valley, time itself feels stretched. Days are long, nights are short but intensely starlit, and the transitions between them are quiet and gradual. Life here persists in layers of green growth, overshadowing dormant technology that might still wake if summoned. But for now, it all exists in a balanced hush—the faint drip of water, the brushed shimmer of distant machinery, and the tactile embrace of a thick, thriving plant world. It is a place that leaves you feeling both immersed and alone, where each footstep comes with a sense of entering sacred territory, newly emerged from the earth’s hidden depths, and yet touched by a human hand that has slowly receded into the lushness.
