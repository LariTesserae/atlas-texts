There is no true night and no true day in this place—only a dim, wavering twilight that never quite brightens. The air is hot, thick with moisture that beads along any exposed surface. The earth here sits low; its nearest horizon is just a shallow rise or the crumbling edge of some ancient foundation. Everywhere, water oozes upward, seeping from cracks in black stone, collecting in murky, glimmering pools. Beyond these pools stretches a landscape that might once have been a marsh, or a shallow estuary, but now is dotted with ruins and vast empty spaces, all under a pulsing, humid darkness.

Scattered vegetation clings to corners and seams, as though uncertain it belongs. Near some slow-bubbling marsh channels, thin wires of root and wiry reeds appear, but only in anemically small clusters: gritty tendrils that look half-dead and half-alive. They reveal no signs of bloom—only the promise that once there was more here than now. No animals rustle in these reeds, none scuttle under broken archways, because no creatures remain. At times, it feels as though something must be watching, but then you realize it’s only the hush of the atmosphere, pressing in on itself.

Despite the quiet, the environment isn’t still. There are deep, tectonic rhythms at work, and the water is in constant motion—tidal flows where tides only half make sense, like some subliminal push and pull. Occasionally, water drips syringe-like from overhead cracks—stone ceilings bridging collapsed corridors—where condensation collects in thick beads before slipping downward with a resonant plop into stagnant pools. A warm, acrid wind stirs these pools at intervals you can’t quite predict, sending eddies of hot, fetid air swirling in small spirals before dying away.

The city that once existed here is half-submerged. The architecture is layered, but in a way that defies simple geometry. You see walls that outline large, pillbox structures, yet they twist slightly off-square, as though the foundations have shifted over centuries or as though the laws of construction themselves have begun to bend. Bulwarks or gates—monoliths hewn from basalt—sag at their edges. The stones are battered smooth by eons of exposure and slow, destructive drips of water. The surfaces shine like obsidian in the perpetual gloom, reflecting a universe of shifting black water. 

The reality breakdown emerges most strongly in the edges of your vision: catwalks that spiral nowhere or vanish in midair, towers that seem upright one moment and leaning the next, watery reflections that show architecture that no longer exists in three dimensions. Sometimes, there’s a sense that the city might be repeating or folding onto itself, like an echo in a tunnel. You catch faint glints of recognizable shapes mirrored in the watery surfaces underfoot, but when you stare, the reflections become shimmering vortexes or bizarre angles that don’t align with what you see above. Time doesn’t run quite level here either: a single step might appear to take minutes, then a floating cluster of algae drifts by with unnatural speed. Sound, too, gets swallowed at odd intervals, accentuating the aura of emptiness. Each water droplet’s fall might echo for miles, or might vanish into the stillness without a trace.

Despite the place being almost entirely dark, your eyes adjust to a layered gloom, discovering subtle glows across the environment—luminescent mold or fungus clinging to damp corners. Their color is not the pale green of typical bioluminescence, but a dull grayish-blue, flickering to a faint scarlet if you stare too long. When that glow shifts, so, too, do shadows in the halls and the labyrinthine corridors between crooked buildings, suggesting that any corner might warp into a corridor of illusions. 

All around is an urban sprawl without current inhabitants—vastly open in some directions, sealed or collapsed in others. You sense that the original planners or builders, whoever they were, must have had a civilization advanced in structure if not in tools. There’s an organized feel in the layout: wide thoroughfares paved with a lacquer-like substance that’s cracked and potholed, channeling the slow-moving water. Yet there are no lamps, no metal rails or mechanical contraptions, no sign of a single tool left behind. It seems everything that might have been mechanical or crafted has been subsumed by stone or devoured by time.

Everything here has endured ages. The region’s erosion feels old and complacent—neither catastrophic nor gentle, but settled into something like a quiet stalemate. Stones have fused with vine-shrouded pillars; watery hollows have swallowed entire arches. Deep-lipped channels remain from countless storms that once deluged the land. Now, rain still comes. When it does, it can be abrupt, an onslaught of warm droplets that hiss against the submerged stone floors. And the downpour can vanish as quickly as it came, adding to the sense of cyclical unpredictability. 

No birds cry out, no amphibian croaks break the silence. The only sign of life’s remains are the small pockets of ragged plants you nearly overlook, and the low, brooding presence of fungi that parasites the stone. Yet the place moves in unseen rhythms, as if the city itself is breathing. You can almost feel it inhale when the wind drones through the half-collapsed corridors, and feel it exhale when the black water in the courtyard lowers by mere inches, leaving behind a slick, labyrinthine pattern on the flagstones.

Occasionally, the space warps in subtle ways that betray the “reality breakdown.” A distant structure might appear close at hand, then recede again like a mirage. Dripping water might rise, as if gravity loosened its hold for a second. The black sky overhead pulses with no discernible pattern, lit from within by flashes of dull color—brusque violet or bruise-brown flickers—never bright enough to shed real illumination, just enough to twist perspective and make shapes tremble at the edges of vision.

For all its bleakness, there’s an underlying dignity to this city—massive pillars and thoroughfares that hint at a once-thriving center of culture. Now, you can only wander its watery labyrinth by the faint glow of the fungus, hearing your own footfalls echoing through places wind and water have claimed. In that echo, you sense the faintest resonance of something ancient: a quiet pulse, as if the city’s soul endures, continuing its slow contraction into a realm that has forgotten time. 

For now, the hush forms a near-constant companion, broken only by droplets, the shuffle of water against stone, and the faint stirrings of that impossible wind. The warmth is ever-present on your skin, as if a dormant sun sits just behind that dark sky. Hours may pass—or only minutes—before you realize with a slight shiver that it might always remain this way: silent, submerged, and impossibly real, suspended in an unspoken labyrinth where the usual rules of day and night, up and down, life and absence, have twisted away from what you once knew.
