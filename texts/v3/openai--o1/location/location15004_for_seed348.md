It is an oddly temperate, vertical-walled basin of industrial clutter. The air feels lightly chilled, but there is a persistent dampness that beads on metal surfaces—condensation more than real rainfall. Everything around you suggests that once, long ago, some human or human-like community built these structures in neat lines, but now they are overshadowed by something else entirely.

The settlement is small in scale, almost like a single enclosed district of a once-thriving city. The “sky” overhead is just the exposed underside of a series of metal platforms and walkways, crisscrossing so densely that only narrow columns of daylight pierce through. Those sun-shafts shift slightly during the day, but otherwise the place has a timeless, unchanging quality. When you walk along the pocked streets, the ground underfoot is slick with the constant sheen of moisture. You rarely see big pools of water, but rivulets run through small channels etched into the metal floor. Rust has become part of the architecture—dark, corroded stripes and patches separate plates of steel, and rivets are eaten down by age. There’s a sense that the whole settlement has settled into a sort of equilibrium where it neither collapses nor recovers.

Despite the decay, the infrastructure here is dense—towers, half-toppled pillars, blocky enclosures of industrial machinery stacked into meandering avenues. The settlement forms a labyrinth of small factories, storage compartments, and clusters of cabled machinery. Thick pipes angle across the spaces overhead, some of them cracked or grafted onto old ducts. You can’t quite tell where one building ends and another begins; many constructions merge together as if grown from the same metallic seed. This is an “urban” place, but it’s not the typical city of tall glass towers. Instead, it’s a low-slung machine hive, all battered panels and stunted smokestacks.

It’s also loud—oppressively loud. Not with the roar of engines (though those rumble in the distance too) but from the overwhelming chorus of creatures that dwell here. No real plants remain, just the occasional fuzzy coating of rust-hued fungus clinging to a bulkhead, but the fauna is everywhere. So many living things are tucked into every shadowy recess, behind hatches, within pipe-lattices. Some of them chatter; others screech. You can glimpse thousands of small bodies scuttling under the walkways: tiny arthropod-like beings with segmented shells and bright reflective eyes. Squat, broad-backed rodents with peculiar ridged skulls nest in the corners. Larger shapes skulk overhead—sleek, many-limbed predators that move with fluid grace across the metal beams, letting out clanging calls that echo through the corridors. Some of them carry odd, metallic growths or barnacle-like lumps, as though having adapted to feed on the mineral or mechanical aspects of this world.

Though the place is loud, it never “builds” to anything. The noise is constant—clanks, screeches, and the hiss of unseen ventilation systems that always run at the same steady pace. You have a sensation that time in here is cyclical or inert, an endless repetition that never quite changes. The hammered steel walls are worn with centuries of use, yet they seem to have endured all that time not by getting repaired, but by existing in a constant stasis. At intervals, a dull clang reverberates through the corridors, perhaps from some archaic piece of automated machinery resetting itself in a repeating cycle.

No leaves rustle—there’s nothing green at all—but grayish filaments of unidentified biomass cling to corners and feed on the perpetual condensation. Once in a while, a stray drop of water splashes from overhead pipes, forming minuscule puddles that reflect the pale light into the gloom. In each reflection, you can see a quivering swirl of motion as small creatures come to lap up or nest in the fleeting bit of liquid.

What’s strangest is how purposeful the structures look, as though they had been built for some grand industrial process that’s still running. You can see whirring turbines behind half-peeled walls. Some produce electricity—a faint, near-constant hum of power lines can be heard if you press your ear to the metal. Perpetual, unchanging, it goes on and on in the background. Smoke plumes waft from battered chimneys, though the smoke dissipates into swirling pockets of moisture under the overarching metal platforms above. It doesn’t blot out the daylight entirely, but it lends a haze that keeps everything in a gentle half-light.

Despite (or because of) the thick presence of fauna, the place is strangely calm in its own ceaseless busyness. The chittering swarms know their routines, the factories and forging equipment revolve in their ancient loops, and water drips from overhead in precisely the same spots, day after day after day. There is no sign that anything—be it the scuttling creatures or the slow-turning gears—will ever stop. Time hovers here in a long, cool equilibrium. It feels as though it might carry on exactly like this forever, with no real sunrise or sunset, no major shift from quiet to chaos or back again—always a restless, mechanical lullaby that crescendos but never truly changes.
