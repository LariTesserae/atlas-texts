At first, all is dark—an unsettling, drifting dark. You are submerged in cold water that extends endlessly on all sides, an ancient, silent ocean without surface or shore. Even here, in the gloom, something vast and wholly constructed rises all around: enormous structures of arrhythmic columns, arches, walls, and floors. They spread outward, spanning contoured shelves of stone upon stone, each block carefully fitted in an epoch long since lost. No light penetrates from above. Only shadowy silhouettes of giant pillars loom in the distance. There is no silt on the floor, no drifting remains of organic matter—just the slow swirl of heavy water in a place that has stood unchanging for eons.

Yet for all of this darkness, the water teems with quiet, colorless vegetation. In the faint half-light, you can just make out thick ribbons of aquatic moss and tall, swaying fronds that cling to every constructed surface. They come up between the cracks in the stone blocks, gather in crevices, drape themselves over the edges of ancient terraces. Sheets of broad-leafed kelp, nearly translucent, lie pressed to these walls. Crystalline webs of some ridged, coral-like plant sprawl between the steps of a long stairway, though no animals move among them. There is a density in this growth—every inch of the architecture is overgrown with greenery that has adapted to a dim, cold life. It breathes faintly in the water’s slow currents, a leaf-walled labyrinth grown thick over centuries.

The cold is tangible, pressing in against the skin of whatever might drift here. It is a chill that suggests an environment near lifeless, yet these plants endure. The hush is profound: the water muffles movement, and without animal life, the only sounds are the soft shudder of currents through archways and the occasional susurration of rippling leaves. No fish dart by, no crustaceans clatter. The architecture is the sole occupant aside from the plants. No metal or advanced machinery breaks the pattern; instead, the structures look painstakingly carved from single massive stones—rising towers of polished rock whose edges are worn to softly curving angles by the seep of water and the unstoppable forward press of time. Every wall, corridor, and floor is part of an unbroken man-made realm, as though the bedrock of some planet had been carved and shaped into a continuous, watery fortress.

Despite its artifice, the place has grown so old it seems almost natural—like a fossil of civilization that has become the planet’s bed itself. The edges of the monolithic squares have been gentled by near-eternal erosion, corners softened into mild curves. The stone’s surfaces have lost any ornamental details that might once have existed; what endures is a final, simplified geometry. The rigid angles are partially lost to thick layers of creeping mosses and slender algae filaments that wave in slow unison if a current passes.

The scale is immense. Beyond each planar walkway or vaulted tunnel, more open spaces reveal themselves, each as wide as a canyon, the water stretching and stretching. It feels continental in scope, as though an entire world is made of these blocks upon blocks, carved out and submerged, no natural rock left uncovered. The thick vegetation that tangles across every surface only hints at the unimaginable breadth of these structures. Within any square mile of these half-lit corridors, there might be a maze of halls, pillars, and gracefully collapsing arches smoothed by the centuries.

Everything moves and changes only slowly. Algae coats a great stone causeway in a hush, its growth just noticeable over decades. The water's temperature stays cold, no sudden shifts; the stillness sometimes broken by gentle gravity-like flows that swirl pockets of colder or slightly warmer water across the labyrinth. The never-ending gloom ensures the plants have adapted to minimal light—no shimmering color or bright phosphorescence, just dull, pale foliage drifting in watery gloom.

There is something paradoxically dense about this expanse: every cubic foot of space is accounted for by architecture or by long curtains of underwater leaves. There’s little empty volume, as though every possible ledge and bracket has become a seam where new mats of plant life take hold in layer after layer. If you look closely, you can trace the vines meandering up the weathered columns and into broken ceilings, weaving curtains behind ancient gates, bridging corners of labyrinthine catwalks, all in an unceasing tapestry of pale growth. This layering is so thorough that in places, the plant matter nearly obscures the constructed shapes wholesale, making them appear as lumps and ridges of living greenery.

There are no advanced tools in evidence—indeed, all one finds are colossal slabs of stone, perhaps fitted by hand with unknown, primitive ingenuity. No metal plating, no complex machinery, no sign of forging or mechanical engineering. It is as if this boundless city was built with nothing more than raw manpower and simple chisels, surviving so far beyond its makers that it has become a new kind of deep-sea cave system.

And always, a quiet breath resonates through the water. An occasional soft groan as shifting currents round a corner, a gentle clap of aquatic foliage tapping stone. Otherwise, there is silence. No schools of fish or drifting invertebrates to break it. It is a slow domain, suspended between darkness and the subtle push of time. Nothing about it suggests it will ever return to the surface or that it was once above it at all. The final form it has taken is austere and eternal. In this cold, watered gloom, it endures—an entire world built and then drowned, now settled into silent timelessness under a forever-lapping sea.
