You first notice the roar. Long before you see anything, the air around you trembles with a continuous thunder that vibrates in the bones. It’s as though the cacophony is trying to shake this entire place apart. It’s deafening—so all-encompassing that you can’t tell if it’s wind or water or some stranger chorus of both.

When the view finally emerges, it reveals a high plateau ringed by oceanic torrents in every direction. The horizon is lost in dim haze. The light is faint, almost dusk-like, yet the atmosphere glows with a subtle warmth—there’s heat in the air, and occasionally the low clouds part with steam. The plateau’s edges are knife-sharp, as though formed only moments ago by some great upheaval; shards of rock angle outward, slick with newly formed rivulets that stream down into the swirling waters below. Everything about the stone looks raw, wet, and unsubdued by time.

Yet you are, in many ways, underwater here. A teeming vapor clings to the plateau, and at its fringes, columns of fluid seem to stand upright, curling around themselves in slow eddies. It’s as though the ocean has half-risen to the sky, or the boundary between air and sea has blurred. The entire scene is drowned in these half-liquid walls, so that sometimes you catch glimpses beyond them—massive, swirling waves surging up from below, reversing and crashing sideways in a strange defiance of gravity.

Scattered across the plateau are the remains of small structures. They look almost like ancient shrines or half-finished altars, hewn from the same sharp rock as the ground below them. No mortar, no metal, no obvious sign of tools—just upright stone slabs leaning precariously amid the relentless onslaught of wind and water. Some of these slabs shimmer with arcane fractal patterns the moment you look away, as though they shift in and out of existence with the roar of the storm.

There’s hardly any greenery—only the occasional patch of spindly, translucent reeds clinging to rocky gullies, their stalks quivering in the warm spray. A few stray strands of algae or wisp-like moss splay themselves across the stone, slick as seaweed, so faint and pale they might be illusions. Every so often a droplet-laden gust will pass over these fragile bits of plant life, making them bow low or even tear free and vanish into the swirling border of ocean-air.

The fauna, if you can even call it that, appears as fleeting silhouettes: a pale serpentine fin heaving through a standing column of water; a fleeting shape like a crab with glimmering, fractal limbs; odd, bulging eyes peeking from behind a stone slab only to dart back into unseen depths. Whatever living things dwell here are rare and shy—or perhaps they are ephemeral manifestations conjured by some break in reality that lingers around the plateau.

Because reality itself feels unstable here. Distances are deceptive. That cluster of shards only a few feet away might, with the next flash of roiling mist, stretch a hundred yards into the haze. You try to orient yourself by the lines of the rocky ground, but they distort, angles fracturing into impossible geometry. Sound arrives from all directions at once—a booming resonance that is part wind, part crashing water, part something else. It burrows into your mind until it’s near impossible to focus on any single thing.

Now and then the plateau itself seems to convulse, as though shifting up or down a fraction of a foot, sending a fissure or new spire jutting skyward. With each tremor, the watery screens that envelop the scene sway wildly, letting you glimpse another corner of this realm before sealing the view behind curtains of churning haze.

In the dim, thick air, the flicker of lightning—though not like any ordinary bolt—rips across the sky in serpentine arcs, producing enormous afterimages upon the stone. The flickers illuminate the half-sunken shrines, revealing natural cracks that seem painfully new. Perhaps this landmass rose only recently from the ocean’s depths, still half-submerged in a womb of swirling water that hasn’t fully decided if it will birth or devour it. It’s a place at the edge of formation, making and unmaking itself several times over as you stand there.

Time doesn’t feel stable. With each wave of thunder, a second or two seems to slip away from you. There is no technology to anchor the mind—no lights, no machines. The only testimonies to other lives are these broken altars or slablike fragments that dot the plateau. Maybe they’re remnants of an ancient attempt at settlement, or the result of random geological processes twisted by the place’s own bizarre energies.

All the while, the roaring never relents. One moment, you’re convinced it is the splash and crash of colossal waves. Next, it becomes a howling wind, too shrill to be wind as you know it. Then it’s a chorus of voices roaring and receding in unison. It’s as if the land itself is ringing a cosmic bell, calling out through time and space until the barrier of reality distorts.

And though the plateau is only about the size of a great temple courtyard—its boundaries visible when the watery veils blow aside—there is a sense that everything extends far beyond what you can truly see. Step in the wrong direction, and you might fall into unimaginable depths. Or land in another part of the plateau entirely. Space stretches and snaps back, refusing to obey normal laws.

The air remains warm and heavy, clinging to your skin. Despite the watery chaos all around, you find your mouth dry from the ceaseless shock of thunder. Beneath your feet, the rock vibrates with an urgent energy. The shapes at the periphery—fragments of possible creatures—swoop closer, only to vanish in the next wave of thick mist. Each swirl of vapor leaves a fleeting phosphorescent shimmer along your arms, as though you’re briefly stepping through another plane of existence.

This is a place still being born—or perhaps still deciding whether it wants to exist. You stand in the midst of this grand, cathedral-sized plateau hanging between ocean and sky, with water both above and below, and the roar of primordial chaos in your ears. And though the vegetation is nearly absent and the signs of life are scarce, the storm itself creates a profound, undeniable presence—a pulsing heart of turbulence that shapes the landscape moment by moment.

Some days, you suspect, the plateau might be nearly silent, or perhaps it lies buried under miles of ocean. Other times, it might tower higher, crystalline spires thrusting into a clouded sky. But here and now, in its newly emerged upheaval, this drowned, half-finished domain glitters with otherworldly energy. All around you, water and rock and noise and space collide, wrestling themselves apart and mending again, until reality itself appears on the verge of splitting wide open.
