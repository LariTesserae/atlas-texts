At first glance, it seems impossible that anything could exist here. The world is made of water, yet in every direction there are hints that we’re perched at a great height, as though these liquid plains have been raised atop ancient, soaring plateaus. It’s warm, but the light is muted, as if filtered through layers of haze or a perpetual dusk. Even in stillness, the air—or the water—buzzes with an invisible tension, a quiet hum that seems to resonate in the bones.

The aquatic expanses stretch on so far that they could be the surface of an entire planet, gently curving out beyond every horizon. Yet this is no ordinary ocean: the boundaries between air and water aren’t distinct. Heavy, warm mist flows in billows and curtains. Sometimes, the atmosphere thickens enough that you can’t tell if you’re submerged or standing on humid ground. Other times, you look up and realize the sky itself churns with waves, as if the whole world is afloat in the same watery realm.

Despite the abundance of water, there is lush vegetation—though it’s strange and fluid in its own right. Huge tangles of vine-like growths float and drift in the currents, their roots seeming to clamp onto ridges of rock that appear and disappear beneath the surface. The “leaves” are often soft-fleshed and translucent, glowing faintly from within, swaying in the waves like seaweed. Closer to some half-submerged ridgeline, you find spongy mats of broad, emerald membranes that cling to ancient stone. Their colors are vibrant—emeralds, jagged pink ribbons, even spots of black-and-gold swirling designs—yet all of it is shadowed by the soft, dim day. The plants press in close, layering over each other, forming dense ribbons and arches, tangling together as if each species is racing to claim space before some new wave of change arrives.

The terrain itself bears the mark of eons of erosion. Smooth pillars of rock and ancient ridges trace out what might have once been a colossal mountain range, melted down into near-flat expanses, punctuated by a few towering outcrops that have resisted time. Some of these outcrops have horizontal layers worn so thin they almost look like precarious stacks of paper. Others have become otherworldly monoliths kneaded into lumps by the eternal pressure of water, wind, and some deeper, more mysterious force. At times, you can glimpse half-buried patterns carved into the stone: curling lines, runic symbols, maybe the remains of a language so old that it predates the flood. Whatever civilization created these markings has long since retreated, died off, or drifted away.

Occasionally, as you explore deeper into this watery realm, you stumble upon partial structures—low walls waist-deep in brackish fluid, a solitary stone column rising out of the mist, or a series of archways that seem to continue far beyond what your eyes can see. These ruins are scattered at wide intervals, as though they were once part of a grand plan that spanned continents. Yet now, each stands alone, half devoured by plant tendrils, bleached by the warm, dim light, softened and smoothed by centuries of constant immersion.

What life remains is subdued, rarely glimpsed. Now and then, a ripple in the water betrays the shift of a hidden creature—a glimpse of dark fin or a dancing eye that vanishes as quickly as it appears. Sometimes you see enormous shapes, translucently lit from within, drifting high overhead as though they glide through the sky itself. Or perhaps that is a reflection you’re seeing, inverted below you in the mirrored surface of the water-laden air. As soon as you look directly, it disappears. This place’s boundaries of reality seem soft at best, capable of shifting with the angle of one’s gaze.

This reality-bending nature is clearest in the quiet extremities—where the watery environment merges with the horizon in swirling fractal patterns, as if perspective itself has begun to fail. Up close, surfaces appear normal: stone is rock, vegetation is flexible plant fiber. But at distance, edges blur and warp, and occasionally you can see arcs of the terrain rising at impossible angles, glimmering in and out of focus, dissolving and reforming. The water folds around these illusions, as if time and space are fluid here too.

Yet despite this pervasive strangeness, there’s a sense of deep continuity. Everything feels ancient and settled. No frantic upheavals or sudden transformations—rather, a slow, rhythmic pulse. The warm temperature never plummets, and in the minimal hush, you can hear soft creaks and burbles, as if the entire environment were breathing. Random surges of water massage old stone ridges, carrying bits of soil and seeds from place to place. Many of the plants cradle tiny pockets of water, collecting whatever nutrients drift by, their roots anchoring themselves so firmly into the drowned rock that only a deliberate hand could pry them free.

Overhead—or beneath if you dive deeper—clouds form and dissolve in slow, epic cycles. You might see a water spout swirling from some mass of clouds down to the oceanic surface, merging with the watery haze, or a sudden shimmering rainfall that merges seamlessly with the water below. Contradictory as it seems, the climate is deeply active, marked by roiling storms or drifting vortexes, though they pass in silence or near-silence. Thunder, if it exists, is muffled, like a distant heartbeat.

No technology stands here to measure the scale—no tools to catalog the movements or map them precisely. But everything suggests that this world is immeasurably vast. You wander for miles or days, and still the watery plateaus continue. Each stone ridge or meadow of twisted stems is just one more node in a continent-sized puzzle of water, rock, and living tapestry. The place is dim, the vegetation thick, the ancient stone all but consumed. Yet it hums with life on a timescale of centuries, building and dissolving in fluid cosmic breath.

And always, just at the edge of vision, you catch hints of the world’s final secret—places where the geometry of real and unreal flow together in mesmerizing waves, as though you’ve stepped through a dream. The few creatures that remain here navigate these shifting boundaries with ease. They appear rarely and vanish quickly, leaving behind only swirling eddies in the gloom. What is left is the hush and the faint drip of water, an echo in a cathedral too large to see, too old to fully comprehend, and too strange to dismiss as mere fantasy. This realm is both submerged and elevated, massive and intimate, incomprehensibly old yet humming with ongoing transformation—an entire reality suspended in the gentle cradle of warm, dim water.
