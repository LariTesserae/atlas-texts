At first, you notice only the brightness. The landscape is bathed in a clear, almost radiant light that seems to come from everywhere at once. There is no cloud cover to soften it, yet somehow it doesn’t feel harsh—just luminously open, revealing every detail and texture. As your eyes adjust, you see the world around you is high, precipitously high. The ground is a breathtaking upthrust of rock, showing ridges and abrupt drop-offs into canyons so deep you can’t see the bottoms. Mountains upon mountains extend in every direction, each face unevenly worn but shaped into a sort of steady equilibrium, displaying their age in striations and ancient scars. The stone underfoot—ever-cool, never seeming to warm—carries faint traces of moisture, as if the rock exudes condensation from within.

There are no roads, buildings, or even old walls here. It’s as if no human has ever set foot across these slopes, or if they did, they left no trace. There is no smell of burning or shaping of materials beyond the chiseled grooves that only rain, wind, and geologic time might have carved. That sense of minimal human presence suggests any “technology” in this world couldn’t be more than a few chipped stones or wooden pegs. Yet even those would be hard to spot in so vast an emptiness.

Despite the altitude and the dryness of the stone, there are pockets of greenery—well, not so much green as a spattering of dull, hardy scrub. A sparse grass or spiky succulent sometimes gathers around shallow basins in the rock, places where water collects from rare rains or from nightly snowmelt. These oases are few and separated by sweeping expanses of broken grey rock. Their presence is a quiet surprise—they don’t draw the eye immediately, until you notice small creatures darting around them.

And the noise. Wherever it originates, it merges together into an unbroken, deafening roar. At first, you might think it is wind—gale-force gusts rushing along the crags. But the air is almost still. It could be echoes of distant waterfalls, or water coursing through cavernous channels beneath the stone. Perhaps it’s the rock itself, vibrating in continuous resonance with some subtle planetary hum. Whatever the source, it sets stones trembling underfoot in a constant, low drone that’s correspondingly massive and intangible. It is so total that it almost becomes a silence of its own—too loud to separate from your own awareness.

Yet atop these heights, life is unexpectedly abundant. Small, swift predators skitter across ledges, their fur or feathers—some in muted colors, others in surprising bright plumes—marbled by dust from the rocky slopes. These creatures hop and leap with assured grace, taking advantage of the narrowest ledges or sudden cracks. Reptilian beings, too, with compressed bodies and wide feet, cling to vertical surfaces, hunting for insects that buzz near the half-hidden water pockets. Every ledge or outcropping seems to host its own small ecosystem, each one a cluster of surprising variety, as if life has found cunning ways to thrive in any nook that can catch the slightest moisture or droplet of warmth from the sun.

The light remains intense all day, shining across the stony peaks. The temperature, though, stays cool almost everywhere, rarely shifting more than a fraction. It is as if the entire system here has reached some timeless equilibrium—there are no dramatic daily swings. When night does come, it doesn’t arrive with a grand flourish of sunset colors; instead, the brightness simply fades to a sharper clarity of stars overhead, dotted onto a pitch-black sky, the landscape still illuminated in silver by the moon or by starlight reflecting off the stone. Even then, nothing changes in the sound—still that coursing hum filling every gap. The world feels static yet deeply alive.

As for humidity, it exists in ephemeral pockets—small gullies or crumbled hollows in the rock that gather condensation at dawn. The air is not exactly wet, but the stone can be slick in certain shaded corners. Tiny rivulets appear after a rare splash of rainfall, carving blackish marks into the paler rock. Looking more carefully, you might see these runnels expanded and deepened over eons, forging small canyons within canyons. It has all been shaped slowly, with cosmic patience, and now rests in a balance that feels neither precarious nor immortal—just settled.

In these high altitudes, flight seems impossible for large birds, yet you hear (and at times glimpse) swift shapes sliding through the thin air, screeching above the constant roar. They hunt the smaller life forms on the ledges, sometimes diving into shafts of echoing canyons. Songbirds, if you can call them that, have voices lost in the all-encompassing sound. Their calls become compressed into thin, piercing notes that merge with the saturating drone, leaving only faint flickers of tonality echoing from rock to rock.

There’s a subtle strangeness here, as though the land itself is conscious. Massive silhouettes of boulders perch precariously on thin spires of stone, never shifting. Things that should have toppled ages ago remain suspended in perfect balance. Some claim to see shimmering illusions in the scorching brightness—heat mirages that bend the horizon or invert cliffs to look like floating arches. The dryness, the thin air, the hypnotic power of the constant roar, all of these factors might feed a sense that time is paused, that every moment is the same. Yet the fauna and the scrapes and grooves worn by water remind you that change does happen, just slowly, on a timescale that dwarfs day-to-day life.

Nothing is built here, at least by mortal hands. If you find a flint or some hammered piece of metal, it must be from a time so distant you can scarcely guess its origin. Perhaps small groups once navigated these heights, carrying herbal knowledge or crude stone-echolocation tools. You might pass a circle of stones that could be an ancient signal fire ring—though it’s half-filled by dust and debris, so old it’s melded seamlessly with its surroundings. Apart from such dim hints, everything else appears in its own primal state, crafted only by the meeting of rock, wind, water, and time.

This landscape does not shift with passing clouds, or with changing moods—there are no clouds, and no mood but its own endless drone. The place is simultaneously hollow and full, empty yet teeming with hidden life, violently loud yet utterly still. And it is cold—perpetually cool, saving the occasional hot tingle of sunlight on rock. Circulating breezes might come off a ridge in the early morning, swaying the sparse grasses that cling to a damp corner, but they never last. Day after day, it remains much the same. A fortress of airy heights, luminous stone, and a presence that defies easy description, suspended between an overwhelming grandeur and a sense of near-void.

Here is a land that compels respect. It allows you in but doesn’t bend to your existence. It abides, seemingly without end, wearing away only through timeless, patient processes of water drip and cosmic hum, with its creatures weaving intricate survival stories among the ledges. And so you stand there, feeling minuscule, in an environment that is somehow both dynamic in its busy fauna and unchanging in the broader, colossal shape, accompanied by a roar so constant it has become a quiet, even reassuring presence.
