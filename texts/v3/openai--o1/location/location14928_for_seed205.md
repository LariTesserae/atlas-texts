They say this place has existed so long that the plants have grown around the bones of whatever came before. You see it first as a smear of green drifting above the horizon, high up in the hotter air. Approaching, you realize that everything is part of that lushness—massed vegetation shaped by time, by heat, by the moisture that collects in odd corners. The land is high and broad, so wide that clouds form over it in thick plumes, dropping intermittent showers into the tangling canopy. The canopy itself rises in pillars and galleries, a living structure whose scale feels at once enormous and yet nearly empty, because the distance between these trunks—if “trunks” is even the right word for them—can be hundreds of yards. 

Between these organic columns, open corridors spread out, sunlit one moment, plunged into dripping shade the next. The ground underfoot is spongy, almost entirely made of old root and leaf litter compressed into a gently rolling terrain. When the rains come, thin streams and rivulets appear as if by magic, collecting in hollow roots or deep pockets of bark. The air hangs heavy with humidity right after these showers, condensing into droplets that trickle down the broad, sinewy surfaces. It’s hot, always hot, but the dampness is erratic—some days you’ll go hours under a perfectly clear sky, sweat beading on your forehead, only to be caught in a sudden deluge that drips through the net of vines overhead.

Despite this all-consuming plant world, there is an open feeling here: the trunks tower so high that you could walk for hours without truly weaving in and out of dense undergrowth. Enormous leaves dapple the ground with shifting shapes of light, but there are also wide stretches where sunlight pierces through, forming bright corridors that beckon exploration. And in that space—what feels like an endless cathedral forest—there come sudden glimpses of remnants: half-collapsed huts built from thick fronds and dried vines, or chipped stone circles they might once have used for cooking or milling seeds. You can see where travelers have left traces of their presence: a few crude wooden tools, a bit of hammered metal that hints at a barely harnessed technology. 

Yet the overwhelming sense is of life—especially animal life, present in numbers and sizes that defy easy explanation. You hear it before you see it: a continuous, deafening chorus of calls, thrums, and shrieks that reverberate between the trunks. Insectoid chatter rattles in the leaf canopy. Some creatures produce a sound like thunder but with pulses in repeated intervals—almost like drumbeats that resonate through the living pillars. You pass under a cluster of thick vines only to realize they are covered with thousands of small, bright-winged crawlers that erupt into a clattering stream when disturbed by your presence. Far above, vine-swingers with many limbs flash into sight, their silhouettes animated in the bright sun before they vanish with cackling cries. Even lower to the ground, amphibious things—frog-like, lizard-like, something in between—pop out of shallow water pockets, bellowing in resonant croaks that seem to shake the air.

Here, time has worn away the sharper edges of the land. Eons of drizzle, windy updrafts, and the unstoppable spread of root networks have carved away any younger mountains or crags, leaving behind a balanced, ancient plateau. Everything hosts a patina of softened ridges and hollows, all of it clothed in the unstoppable march of climbing vines or broad fibrous plants. The plateau’s edges are possibly sheer, but few venture there, because the winds that scrape the cliff sides loom with a haunting whistle, and the living chaos inside the forest is strangely more hospitable than the emptiness at the brink.

Even though it seems crowded with leaf and branch, distance in this world is immense. You might walk a day and see only a scatter of relic buildings—small huts perched in the high crotches of the trees or built into some buttress root. Their walls have rotted to lacework in places, but a shape remains: a single shady chamber, maybe a platform of woven trunk-fibers. You suspect isolated tribes might still live among them, practicing primitive crafts to survive in this green ocean. There is a sense that these scattered folk learned to co-exist with the thunderous beasts about them, adopting quiet footfalls and a good sense of timing to slip between hunts or storms.

Weather here doesn’t move like elsewhere—it gathers among the leaves as if generated by the plants themselves, forming shifting micro-storms high overhead that may or may not break into lightning and heavy rain. Sometimes there’s only a brooding swirl of clouds, pouring thick humidity down upon the plateau without a single actual raindrop. Then, perhaps at dusk, great roars of thunder roll through the canopies, mingling with the howls of apex predators calling out across the miles. In these moments, the air feels charged and enthralling, almost supernatural. 

And it is strange. Plants spin around themselves in improbable shapes, knotting together in living cords that stretch halfway to the sky. Strange, too, in how animals and plants appear fused in certain corners: giant pitcher-like growths that harbor unknown creatures in symbiosis, or half-limb vines that scuttle away when startled, as though part-plant, part-beast. Ancient patterns scrawl across bark in luminous tendrils. The eye struggles to parse exactly what is flora and what is fauna, or whether such a distinction exists at all anymore.

Despite the deafening noise—an unrelenting wave of motion and calls—there’s a certain equilibrium here. Through countless centuries of fierce competition and adaptation, everything seems to have found its niche. Predators and prey cycle in a grand choreography, all happening in that hot, high, battered environment: a balance you can feel in the quiet between storms, or in the hush before a big creature’s roar. It is active, alive, ever-moving, but it is also timeless. The land has lain here through ages of water trickling off leaves, of sun and steam, of seeds drifting on the wind until they take root in some secluded pocket. Now it is what it is: a world of steep scale, thick vegetation, monstrous creatures, ancient footprints, and unexplainable oddities that just might be the daily hum of life on this plateau—beautiful, ferocious, and utterly strange.
