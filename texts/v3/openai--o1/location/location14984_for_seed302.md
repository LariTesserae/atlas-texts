In the far heights—where the air is thin and bright—there stretches a wide, stony shelf that echoes with a thunderous sound. At first glance, there’s little to explain the roar: no towering waterfalls crashing, no monstrous winds whipping, no machines of any sort. Yet the place swells with a near-constant din, as if the rocks themselves were humming in frequencies too low to discern as a song. It is like standing inside a great bell, perpetually rung, without ever diminishing its clang.

All around, the world is saturated by water, though not in any obvious way of lakes or streams. Instead, it seeps and condenses in every ridge, in the corners of shallow gullies, and in the cracks between mineral layers. The air itself feels wet, cool to the skin—a faint, covetous damp that never forms into visible rainclouds. Sometimes you see beads of moisture trembling on ridges of gray stone, shining in the intense white sunlight. Despite the water’s persistent presence, no single surface looks truly flooded; the land appears to drink its own runoff, storing it in alveoli of stone and hidden channels. It leaves the ground slick, glistening with a soft sheen. Occasionally, a droplet forms on an overhang, grows pregnant with weight, and plunks down onto the rock below. But the cycle remains the same day after day, an equilibrium that has long since settled into the bones of this place. Some might imagine it’s been like this forever and will remain so indefinitely—a realm beyond the tug of time.

This high elevation plateau is a complex terrain of rises and hollows. Ages of erosion have softened the edges of every boulder, carved gullies between them, and smoothed over ridges as if they had once been fierce, jagged peaks. In this hush of geology, nothing stands out as newly formed; no fresh cracks, no metal or wooden beams out of place—there is simply no sign of anything constructed or engineered. Even so, there are labyrinthine channels that snake around the plateau’s perimeter, busy with glimmering, damp walls. Multitudes of mineral veils cling to these walls, some tinted faintly by metals—coppery greens or iron-tinged reds—but there is not a single plant in sight, not the barest wisp of moss, not a single root. If there is life, it is subtle and wholly unrecognizable.

From the higher vantage points—where stone pillars tilt at precarious angles—one can see just how open this realm truly is. It spans beyond a single plateau, extending into valleys and neighboring ridges. The uniform brightness of day saturates everything. Little shade interrupts the luminous white glow, though here and there, under overhanging sections of rock, you might find pockets of deeper shadow. But the whole atmosphere is almost painfully radiant. Glancing into the distance reveals rolling silhouettes of more rock shelves, dripping quietly, all touched by the same glare of relentless, stable light.

Within this basalt labyrinth, one might suspect the presence of living creatures—from the corner of your eye, you think you see a flicker, maybe a faint shape darting behind an outcrop. Yet direct evidence of fauna is elusive. Sometimes you’ll hear, between the rock’s booming resonance, a distant skittersound—a faint claw tapping or a soft scraping of limbs against stone—and then nothing. On a rare occasion, you catch a glimpse of something small bounding over the wet surface: a pale, translucent creature, possibly amphibious or something more alien. But in a blink, it’s gone. Life here seems shy, threatened by the blankness of everything else.

The ceaseless roar pulses in slow waves, varying in intensity but never fully quiet. It resonates through foot and bone. At times, it seems driven by the planet’s own heartbeat, as if geothermal rivers swirl far below, generating a tidal hum that echoes upward. At other moments, it sounds more akin to colossal breath—endless, measured inhalations that press and recede. There are no howling storms to distract from it, and no dawn chorus of birds or insects. Silence is unthinkable in this domain; it is the rocky hush that paradoxically rages. This sound is so constant, so unyielding, that it feels pinned in place, like an anchor securing the atmosphere in a kind of suspended animation.

And suspended it indeed seems: day after day, the plateau remains the same. Understandably, it may take many hours to realize how truly static the environment is. Even the moisture that beads on the ridges always seems to collect in the same patterns, condensing at nearly the same spots. The faint creatures, if they are more than a trick of the eye, vanish the moment you’re sure you’ve spotted them. The brightness never dims, except very gradually, as if the sun that fuels it never truly sets—but transitions at a crawl between states of bright and less-bright. If there are seasons, they are glacial in their pace, unfelt in any human timeframe.

And so the place endures, high and cool, saturated yet almost barren of all recognizable life. The mineral bones of this land—polished by water and time—stand tall with an unyielding dignity, uninterrupted by any structure or artifice, unwavering in their luminous gloom. No vegetation creeps over these stones, and no technology mars them; only the quiet breath of water, the near-imperceptible scattering of rare creatures, and the never-ending sound that saturates every crack. One is left with a sensation that this plateau is more than a rock formation: it is a single, living presence of stone and water. It has always been this way—a place beyond any notion of beginning or end, locked in an eternal hush of thunder.
