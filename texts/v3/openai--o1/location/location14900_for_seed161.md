In the very center of this place rises a colossal mound of layered stone, its sides steep and fissured, as if some ancient hand shaped it in a distant epoch. The terrain around it is parched—the powdery dust underfoot carries no trace of actual water. Instead, the land exudes a dry, mineral tang that drifts on the wind. Yet, somehow, a lattice of strange, sinewy plants flourishes here. They sprawl across the stone and dust, forming tangles of dense, braided vines. Their foliage is a muted green-brown, slightly translucent and leathery, and they don’t seem to draw sustenance from water at all. Instead, they feed on the slow seep of heat that wells up from the bedrock, hungry fronds pressing themselves onto the stone to absorb radiant warmth.

Time itself seems to compress here. The storms or floods that might once have shaped the landscape have long since disappeared, leaving behind an ancient, weathered world. Cliffs and rocky shelves climb up and up, so high that the topmost edges fade into a dim haze. Their lines, once jagged, have been worn to smooth undulations. Cavernous hollows pock the cliffsides, old and stable, as if they’ve been the same shape for millennia. Even at the base, the dust has settled into thick layers, as though it, too, is just another stratum in this venerable geology.

All around that central stone mound, in the deep pockets of flat ground, sits a settlement—small by some standards, but laid out like a miniature labyrinth of interlocked structures. The buildings are raised primarily with rough-cut blocks of the local rock. Massive slabs, hewn by hand tools, slot into place. There is little metal here, mostly hammered scraps that form door hinges or the occasional ceremonial blade. Rope is woven from the same vine-like plants that cover the slopes, and it’s strung throughout the settlement to support scaffolding, rope bridges, and precarious walkways. Overhead, the sky remains in permanent twilight, as though the world has never known the brightness of a full sun. Whether the sky is naturally dim or the settlement lies in the shadow of the towering cliffs, the effect is the same: everything is veiled in a subdued glow.

Despite the subdued light, there is a great density of life. Small creatures make their homes among the vines and in the crevices between stone blocks. You hear them constantly: a raucous, overlapping chorus of chirps, hoots, guttural croaks, and hissing calls echoing down the cavernous streets and layered terraces. The volume is surprisingly high, reverberating against rock walls so that even minor sounds amplify into a booming roar. Creatures skitter across rooftops, leap from one precarious ledge to another, and scramble up the stems of the hardy vegetation. Their shapes are as varied as the staccato voices they add to the cacophony—some are multi-limbed with elongated necks, while others appear almost like hefty rodents, covered in stiff bristle-fur.

Where people dwell, they do so in close quarters. Passageways between homes are narrow and winding, some opening abruptly onto small courtyards that function as communal ovens or meeting spaces. The folks here appear content with earthenware pots, heavy stoneware, and simple woven baskets for daily tasks. You see rudimentary looms set up in corners, weaving cloth from coarse plant fibers. Ruined chunks of wall and structural expansions testify to a history of building, tearing down, and rebuilding, forming a layered effect—an urban nest set against the backdrop of ancient rock.

Heat is abundant, slow-seeping from the depths of the earth. In this land’s final form—long since past the tumult of tectonic shift or roaring rivers—change comes gradually. You can sense the centuries pass in each footstep on stones polished by countless soles. Eroded ridges and gentle plains stretch outward, giving the impression that time itself might have settled here ages ago, content to watch life play out in slow, deliberate cycles.

Although water is absent, the atmosphere feels far from barren. Instead of thirst, the air brims with a warm dryness that saturates your lungs. Perhaps it’s the mineral dust that lends a faint taste to each breath, or maybe it’s the constant hum of living things that make the settlement feel so vital. From high above the jagged canyon rim, weak light slants down—just enough to see color, though muted. Shadows creep around corners at odd angles, molding the settlement into dim labyrinthine shapes.

All the while, the loud clamor of fauna never quite recedes. At intervals, it subsides to a low, rolling chatter, only to surge again as creatures flush from one nook to another. Life here is woven thickly, pressed close against the stone and the vines, thriving in a place that should, by all logic, be inhospitable. Yet it endures, sustained by a kind of deep-time stability that has turned extremes into a quiet, abiding equilibrium.

Travel far enough in any direction, and you’ll find abrupt edges—cliff faces or deep chasms where the settlement simply cannot expand. In this place of limited scope, every shelf, every ledge is occupied. Houses cling to the precipices; rope bridges traverse impossible gaps; the communal squares bustle with gatherings of neighbors who sweat in the unrelenting warmth. Generations of inhabitants have refined their simple technologies and crafts, content to shape their daily lives by hand and vine. Tools are basic and functional: chisels, adzes, stone grinders. The settlement’s main thoroughfares echo with the rhythmic ring of stone on stone, the shuffle of rope suspenders, the braying and snorting of pack animals, and the fluttery alarm calls of small winged things.

It’s a place that has found its final form—a giant’s fortress of rock that’s been smoothed by the millennia, a city grown like a living tapestry of vines and dwellings. Lacking water, it nonetheless pulses with life, the hush of desert replaced by an all-enveloping thrum. And in this dusty twilight, everything—plants, animals, and people—has found a slow, steady way of thriving, taking the ancient, nearly motionless bones of the Earth and shaping them into a strangely vibrant home.
