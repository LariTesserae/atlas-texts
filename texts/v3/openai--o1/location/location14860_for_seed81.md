The heat soaks into everything, rising in shimmering currents along walls of black rock. The day—if one can call it that—remains dim, as though sunlight has to fight its way through an unrelenting haze. Despite the heavy warmth, there is enough moisture to coat every exposed surface with a faint sheen of condensation. Gravelly ridges and crumbling basalt are slick to the touch, their surfaces weeping small rivulets that gather in shallow pools, only to evaporate moments later in the sweltering air. The hot waters of this place never settle long enough to support true greenery; plant life, or anything that resembles it, seems absent. Instead, one occasionally glimpses shapes better described as fleshy mineral formations—pale, bulbous growths that cling to the porous rock walls, halfway between fungus and crystal.

Much of the terrain sprawls across a high plateau, though the land has eroded and settled over eons into a labyrinth of jagged slopes and gullies. Ancient water—or perhaps some other fluid—once cut winding gullies through the stone, giving the plateau a fractured appearance. Now, the channels are prone to sudden surges of hissing steam or bursts of obsidian-black sludge from deep below. Though the plateau itself is spacious, it feels strangely empty when one looks beyond the structures that cluster in the middle distance.

And there are indeed structures—a city built of dark metallic frames and broad sheets of reinforced glass, layered one atop the other. At a distance, it looks almost abandoned. Unlit walkways wrap around the exterior of tall industrial towers, interspersed with clumsy scaffolding and thick pipes that coil upward. The entire settlement seems to teeter on the edge of the plateau, hugging the rocky outcroppings to avoid the deeper canyons full of roiling heat. Several wide-bellied chimneys vent steam or acrid smoke, their vapors instantly joining the haze that pervades the environment. Flickers of light blink intermittently through high windows, like lonely signals in the murk.

Though the city is large enough to suggest a bustling population, one encounters few signs of life moving openly in the streets. Doors and windows remain shut behind thick, grime-frosted shutters. The roads—composed of interlocking plates of sheet metal—are nearly silent. A low, humming throb pervades the air, possibly generated by old factory engines deep in the bowels of the settlement. Occasionally, there is the clang of a gear or the hiss of a brake, but whatever activity goes on here is either hidden indoors or runs automatically.

In the cracks between the metal plating, moisture collects and then vanishes in the heat, leaving behind faint ghost-shapes of mineral residue. Metallic pipes wrap around the outsides of factories like vines, though any natural flora has long since been replaced or never took hold in the first place. Still, the atmosphere is not entirely lifeless: occasionally, one might spot a large, insectoid shape darting across a girder or see a pale shape drifting at the very edge of perception—a half-real phantom making its way through the architectural gloom. The sense is that these creatures, though rare, are well adapted to the city’s hot, dim environment, existing in the gaps and corridors where humans (if humans live here) rarely venture.

An odd sense of age hangs in the air, mixed with a forward-thrust of mechanization. The settlement feels ancient, though still churning with half-functional machines—great pumping pistons, reliquaries of spinning gears, half-submerged turbines fed by microscopic streams of black water. Vapors coil around these apparatuses, condensing on every gear tooth. A thin sheen of oil coats the water’s surface in the few open drains or catch basins where runoff pools. Everything—stone, metal, ephemeral fungus-mineral growth—is tinted by the dim, acidic light that filters through the ashen sky.

When the wind picks up, it scours across the plateau, scraping grit against metal. A sporadic moan might echo along one of the half-empty avenues, carrying with it the constant hiss of escaping steam vents. Somewhere, an unseen mechanism engages with a resonant clang, followed by a sputtering release of pressure. The overall quiet forms a backdrop, highlighting every distant clank or hiss, making them seem all the more mysterious and surreal.

All the while, the place seethes with a sense of slow but active processes—hidden flows of superheated water, quiet expansions of the metal shells of buildings, migrations of the strange mineral-fungal protrusions that sometimes glow faintly in the gloom. Weather might come as a warm vapor-shower from above, rather than rain, saturating the air with an electric taste. And then the vaguely metallic drizzle might bleed away into swirling mists that drift across the plateau edge, evaporating as they blow into the deeper canyons below.

At the city’s boundaries, precarious walkways lead to the distant edges of the plateau. There, one can look out upon a vast emptiness—a near-void of broken cliffs and swirling heat-fogs, stretching and twisting out of sight. The settlement itself stands like a bastion against this emptiness, layered in ridges of steel and glass, perched in a place neither inviting nor completely hostile. Here, the laws of the world seem to have coalesced into a persistent strangeness: water that seeps from hot rock, a haze so thick and dim that only diffuse rays peek through, and a hidden population that keeps its dreams and activities concealed behind dark metal walls.

It is not silent, but it is mostly quiet. It is not empty, but it is largely uninhabited. And in that balance—between the water-laced heat, the industrial hush, and the subtle movements in the shadows—one feels that this place resonates with its own cryptic life. The world here has settled into a slow, perpetual rhythm, carved by muggy weather, industrial machinery, and an eerie biological presence all its own. And for all its weirdness, it has found an equilibrium that persists, suspended in the twilight, high above the dark canyons, waiting for whomever or whatever may come next.
