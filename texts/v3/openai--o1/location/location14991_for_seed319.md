It feels like a plateau near the wet clouds, a place where the land’s edges turn downward in soft, time-worn shapes. The rock underfoot has been weathered for generations, scoured by rains and the patient push of wind across centuries. Even so, the surface is oddly wet, as though the ground itself sweats in the warmth. Trickles of water gather in shallow channels that snake through the city, while droplets glisten on dark stones the way dew might on a flower—except here, the vegetation is nearly nonexistent. Instead of vibrant fields, the terrain offers only small tufts of brittle grass and a few stubborn succulents clinging to cracks in the paving.  

From the edge of the plateau, when you look outward, you can see the shape of the region continuing in wide arcs. There’s a sense that the land extends well beyond this vantage, but mist hangs in the air, turning anything far off into a watercolor haze. Closer at hand, industrial structures rise in tiers, stacked across the gentle slopes. Some of them are built of corrugated metal and others from a composite of old concrete and local stone, patched and re-patched over the ages. They’re arranged more by functional necessity than by elegance, forming an urban sprawl both broad and tall.  

A labyrinth of pipes and vents threads between these blocky buildings. Much of the architecture seems older than the current inhabitants—so old that it has reached a comfortable equilibrium with the environment. Every exposed surface wears a film of condensation, which amplifies the shimmer of the mid-day light. Here and there, subtle rivulets run down the city walls, vanishing into storm drains that mumble with unseen currents. The place is never dry; at best, it is a persistent damp that keeps the air thick and warm.  

But what truly defines the atmosphere is the noise: a near-constant thunder of machinery. Giant mechanisms beneath the city’s surface churn methodically, though it’s never clear exactly what they power. Coal and steam might have been used once, but newer pipes hum with electricity, their coils vibrating. Even out in the open air, the throb of motors is enough to rattle the boards of walkways and hum in the metal guardrails. When a big generator cycles on, the clang resonates in your chest. People in the settlement—rarely seen outdoors but evidently present—seem used to it, carrying out daily tasks with practiced stoicism. Shouts must be bellowed over the engines; even footsteps ring out louder than usual in these echoing lanes.  

Most of the living creatures keep to the outskirts or hide in alcoves carved from rock. A few wiry birds glide across the sky, riding the thermals that lift off the plateau walls, but they rarely land. Occasionally, a single foxlike animal—thin and desert-colored—might dart behind the foundation of some half-ruined factory, presumably searching for scraps. Their presence is a surprising reminder that the city is not fully sealed to nature, just mostly disinterested in it.  

At first, it feels straightforward: an industrial town perched high on a wet plateau. But there’s something else here, a strangeness. Certain corners of the city appear architecturally inconsistent—tangles of arches and walkways that lead nowhere, as though a different set of rules governed them. Sometimes the machinery produces tones too harmonic, nearly melodic, in ways that raise the hair on your arms. Mud and puddles in the more forgotten courtyards form swirling patterns that rearrange overnight, despite the otherwise gradual pace of life—the day-to-day rarely changes, yet these subtle shifts appear like living Rorschach tests drawn by the water itself.  

Over long spans, the city barely transforms. It’s been standing for so long, humming away, that it seems almost eternal, as if the machinery that keeps it going runs at a geological pace. The water-laden air softly corrodes every surface, but just enough to accentuate the sense of perpetual endurance. Generations have come and gone, building layer upon layer, leaving behind high walls in some places and collapsed thoroughfares in others, yet the entire complex remains balanced in its own slow dance with time.  

You suspect that someone, somewhere, is orchestrating the heavy industry that suffuses every street, but they remain hidden in the compound’s depths. Meanwhile, trickles of water carve new lines in the stone. Most folks go on as they always have—shouting over the roar, weaving past scaffolds, living on the edges of the machine’s pulse. It’s a city that stretches beyond the immediate horizon, where steam merges with the warm, ever-condensing air, and the hush of lonely wetlands signals that nature hasn’t fully surrendered. The place seems suspended between worlds: half-living mechanical sprawl, half-forgotten plateau battered by centuries of precipitation, forming a strange, resonant tapestry few creatures dare call home.
