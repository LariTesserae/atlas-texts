High ridges of dark stone loom against a sky the color of old pewter. The air is perpetually cool here—never quite cold, never warm—and there is a faint dampness that clings to skin and stone alike, as though the place has recently been brushed by fog or a light drizzle. The terrain rises steeply, almost in tiers, each plateau edged by blades of raw rock that look newly thrust out of the earth. These ridges and shelves retain their shape sharply, showing only minor smoothing at edges, as if eons of erosion had been paused partway through. The place feels perched up in the world—a highland that the eye can’t quite see the edges of, but still it’s contained enough that it doesn’t seem endless.

Patches of vibrant, emerald-green vegetation nestle in hollows and climb the slopes in ribbons. Large-leafed ferns and spindly-limbed shrubs thrive in pockets where water collects—water that seeps from cracks in the rock, or sometimes just condenses overnight in shallow basins. Sometimes a low-hanging cloud passes and drapes everything in beaded moisture, leaving droplets to glisten in the morning light. Yet, away from these damp pockets, the stone shelves remain conspicuously bare, as if the rock itself refuses to host more than a sparse scattering of life.

From the edges of the ridges, one can see breaks in the land—a series of plateaus stepping down into a broader valley. The plateaus look vast in their own right, bigger than a single town yet not sprawling to the horizon. Much space lies between clusters of growth, giving a sense of openness. An occasional bird—plumed in bright colors that echo the green and gold of the foliage—cuts through the quiet with a call that echoes once, then quickly fades. Other creatures abound but remain hidden: slender four-legged animals move through the ferns at dawn, unseen but for the rustling of leaves and the flash of quick eyes. Sinuous, half-gliding reptiles and avian-like mammals make their homes in the cracks of stone walls and the moss-laced corners of hidden grottoes. You can hear an occasional soft scuttling or call, but mostly this world lapses back into hush.

Amid these rocky terraces stands a city—unexpected, but unmistakably present. Low, sturdy walls of fitted stone form the outer boundary, their masonry carefully chiseled but simple in design, hinting at a civilization that has long mastered basic construction with hand tools but has not progressed to more elaborate or mechanized techniques. Within those walls, the settlement sprawls across several stepped platforms connected by sloping ramps and broad staircases. Winding corridors are lined with tightly knit houses of blocky stone or timber frames. Their roofs are made of thick wooden rafters, topped with woven thatch or overlapped slate, all of it carefully lashed to resist the near-constant presence of mist. Simple water channels, carved from the same dark stone, divert run-off and condensation into communal cisterns. Beneath an unchanging light—bright enough to see everything clearly but never blazing—these buildings have a timeworn air, though not from decay; it’s more that they sit in a forever-stasis, as though the city were built ages ago and then remained untouched by both progress and ruin.

Everywhere, vines and moss creep over walls, tangling around carved posts and pillars. The vegetation is lush—but never truly overgrowing the structures. It seems as if the city’s people continually clear paths and tame back the greenery, keeping an uneasy balance with nature. In the courtyards and on narrow terraces, small gardens are planted with hardy, leafy crops. Despite the overall sparsity—houses spaced out with open squares and wide walkways—there is abundant life: flocks of small, pigeon-like birds circle overhead, goats with thick coats graze on precarious ledges, and an occasional catlike creature patrols the alleyways. 

The inhabitants appear accustomed to working by hand. They quarry blocks of local stone with chisels and hammers, lash together wooden beams with rope or sinew, spin fibers to create coarse fabrics. Everywhere, you see signs of manual craft—wooden wheels, braided baskets, stone tools with sharpened edges. Nearly everything essential to their lives is fashioned from the resources at hand. Metals exist, but in small measure: simple bronze or copper adornments appear here and there, hammered into plates or used for basic tools. Yet there is no sign of advanced forges, no clang of heavy industry. The settlement’s infrastructure is advanced only insofar as centuries of careful, hand-worked skill can achieve.

Despite this city’s size and relative bustle, the overall mood remains subdued. Voices echo faintly in the cool corridors, but the place never rumbles with true noise. Footsteps on stone sidewalks and the burble of water down carved channels are the predominant sounds. A stillness hovers that seems more profound than mere silence—like it is woven into the very fabric of the place. Clouds pass, wind stirs, but time itself feels strangely paused. Buildings stand, centuries old, yet show little sign of shifting or crumbling. The people’s rhythms and the environment’s cycles seem locked in a permanent equilibrium: no new districts are under construction, no expansions reach beyond the ancient walls, and even seasonal changes seem muted. The trees that line the terraces hold the same number of leaves throughout the year; small seedlings sprout, but remain ever the same height once grown.

Weird touches abound, hard to define precisely. There might be something about the color of the light, the way it never quite shifts in hue or angle, or perhaps it’s the curious undertone in the wind that sounds faintly musical. Some visitors have claimed that time does not pass normally here—that people born in the settlement do not visibly age. Others swear the city’s walls rearrange themselves in the night, though no one seems to recall them being in any other configuration come morning. Perhaps it is merely the hush of stone and cloud, the slow drip of condensation, the hush of centuries on a place unchanging—but there is some intangible oddity that sets every outsider’s hair on end.

In this high, mist-cooled land—where young rock glints with crisp edges, where lush vegetation stakes its claim in island-like clusters of green, and where a modest yet organized city of hand-built stone has stood for longer than memory—existence feels stable, suspended, almost dreamlike. And so each day passes just as the last, in quiet equilibrium beneath a sky that never quite clears, never quite darkens. The place persists, unhurried and unaltered, as though, at some point, time simply folded it up for safekeeping and forgot to let it go.
