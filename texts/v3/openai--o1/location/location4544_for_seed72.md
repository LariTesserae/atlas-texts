Down a gentle slope of carved rock, you come upon a basin-like depression that dips just below the world’s crust. The air there grows thick and muggy with heat, as though someone has pushed the warmth of a blazing midday sun just under the surface of the land. A thin haze of condensation clings to jagged walls, the water not quite dripping but always on the verge of forming droplets that glint in the dim light. Even here, beyond the typical boundary of open sky and open earth, the air pulses with an intense humidity that suggests frequent showers or seeps from deeper underground aquifers.

Sparse vegetation ekes out a living along the rock ledges—patches of pale root-fungi and wiry plants with thick, glossy leaves that trap whatever meager moisture condenses. No wide forest thrives here, and no great meadow stretches; instead, there are clusters of tenacious green and gray, each far apart from the next, separated by stretches of raw stone that look like they have barely emerged from the planet’s depths. The ground feels soft in spots, as if still being formed—slippery, embryonic clay that yields easily underfoot, refusing to show signs of age or wear.

At the heart of this shallow cavern is a somehow contained city. The settlement has been designed with an industrial efficiency: archways of rusted metal frame walkways that descend in concentric circles, leading deeper into labyrinthine corridors of pipes and steam-powered machines. The architecture is neither sprawling nor towering; instead, it is packed into a modest space, well-organized, with suspended platforms and scaffolds bridging from one stony outcrop to another. Thick cables run overhead, trailing sparks and the dim glow of feeble electric lights. It is never truly bright here—just enough light to navigate by—but the machines never cease their laborious churn.

Now and then, you catch the hiss of pressure releasing, the metallic groan of gears turning, and distant rumblings of heavy engines. In the half-gloom, workers in heat-stained coveralls come and go, though they’re hardly the central occupants: beyond them, a crush of wildlife stirs. Every crevice, every out-of-the-way ledge or shallow pool, teems with creatures—some are small, insect-like things that skitter across cables; others are amphibious dwellers, hidden in the murky pools that reflect the flicker of industry. Larger beasts prowl at the edges of the settlement, their silhouettes flickering in the halflight: thick-bodied, vaguely reptilian forms with an unsettling grace, drawn by the perpetual moisture and radiant warmth.

The animals are everywhere, darting in open corridors, nesting beneath walkway supports, living in uneasy coexistence with the heavy machinery. Day by day—and it’s hard to tell day here except by shifts in the labor schedule—these creatures migrate between hidden pockets of water. The absence of dense plant life forces them to stretch their feeding ranges, and they rely upon each other, or on scraps left behind by the city’s inhabitants. You can hear them constantly: a cacophony of scraping claws, guttural calls, hissed warnings, and the fluttering of leathery wings that vanish as quickly as they appear.

Despite the hushed echoes of dripping water and the gradual drip-drip from overhead crevices, the place cannot be called quiet. The city’s industry drones on in a constant muffled roar; you feel it in your chest. Boilers vent steam with piercing screeches, conveyor belts clank along, and the inhabitants shout orders or directions over the dull thunder of the turbines that power the settlement. All the while, the environment changes at a painfully slow pace—water slowly seeps, new rock faces appear as the ground is worn away or hammered, and the occasional cave-in or sinkhole reveals fresh territory.

It is a realm caught between emergence and expansion. The foundations of the city are still rough, as though in flux, built with the ambition of pushing deeper into the raw geology. Its heat and humidity suggest that the people here must master the precarious interplay of water and stone, carefully balancing their industrial ambitions with the domain of creatures that outnumber them many times over. And so life in this under-surface, dimly lit hollow happens in a kind of hot, steady swirl of machinery, animals, and slow, patient transformations—each shaping the other as time ticks by at a magma pace, forging a world that is small but teeming, faintly lit, but undeniably alive.
