It begins in half-light somewhere below the surface. Not far underground, but far enough that the sun’s caress is a memory. The air squeezes itself into a dim glow, helped along by scattered fixtures and organic luminescence, as though an unseen force decided that perpetual dusk should rule here. It is warm—a breathy, damp warmth, suggesting that water’s presence is intermittent but meaningful: rivulets appear in shallow grooves, condensation hugs the walls, and you never quite escape the thought that you might tread in a sudden puddle or be doused by a stray drip from overhead.

All around, living green dominates. It’s as if the rock and soil have been overtaken by a grand expansion of roots, vines, and thick-stalked creepers. Towering fronds line corridors like columns, while elaborate root systems dangle from overhead arches. Some places feel like halls of a cathedral where the “columns” are trunks of climbing epiphytes and moss-laden stems, each woven intricately with the next. You see no neat boundary between “plant” and “architecture,” because here, bookshelves, walkways, and actual structural supports are all grown from living matter. In the spaces where the vegetation bends away, ancient walls can be glimpsed—pitted and weathered, their original shapes forgotten over millennia.

An industrial presence asserts itself among the greenery. It’s woven in the same slow but determined way, as though the inhabitants have harnessed both the organic sprawl and old mechanical ingenuity to build their small civilization. Pipelines snake between trunk-like columns, their surfaces hammered and riveted from generations past, hissing soft bursts of steam or run-off. Monolithic valves creak open and shut, regulating flows of heated water. You might spot factories tucked in alcoves of living bark, belching faintly visible exhaust or plumes of thick, proportional to the scale of this underground city—small but set up for serious production. Gears and heavy machinery lumber within walled-off sections, their thrum reverberating through the vines. Wind-powered devices don’t make sense down here, so everything is driven by endless shifts of pressure and heat. The result is a chorus of clanking metal, the whoosh of steam lines, the slap of dripping water, and deep rhythmic engine churn. It’s loud, and it echoes mightily in enclosed corridors.

And yet the noise of machinery competes with the living creatures, for the fauna here is overwhelming. Shapes squirm in the semidarkness, some with iridescent hides that flash in the flickering light, others with slick or chitinous forms scuttling across every surface. A hundred different eyes reflect back at you from niches in the walls. Where a tangle of aerial roots lifts, you see entire colonies of lizards or arthropods—antennae twitching, bright with vestigial pigments designed for subterranean displays. Larger forms lurk beyond, half-seen among the broad leaves: heavy-limbed mammals or reptilian quadrupeds that brush aside the vines effortlessly. Predators and prey swirl in an ever-churning cycle. Screeches, howls, and bizarre warbles join the rumble of engines to form a cacophony that never settles. Nature’s own thunder inside a living labyrinth.

In places, the vegetation is so thick it forms natural rooms—large enough for a cluster of inhabitants to gather. These communities fuss over a mixture of industrial heritage and plant stewardship. The growth feels ancient, balanced with time: no sign remains of catastrophic collapse, only the quiet assumption that everything here has coexisted for ages. Even the way the enormous roots slip snugly around steel beams suggests an unspoken peace between the mechanical and the botanical. Erosion has sculpted the stone and substrate into smooth pockets and recesses; everything is worn into place. The “streets,” such as they are, wind through striated walls of earthy green, illuminated by flickering overhead lamps that flicker uncertainly between the vines’ luminescent blossoms.

Movement is constant: water trickles through channels, thick creepers shift under their own slow growth, factory parts whir and pound, and creatures dart or crawl in a living puzzle. The place feels self-sustaining, with windows (or vent shafts) high above offering glimpses of a cooler world. Hot drafts from deeper below ground occasionally gust upward, colliding with the moist air and condensing on every surface. It is a place of perpetual tinkering—by both mind and root, machine and beast. Strange, yes, but in its own way, peaceful: it has found a dissonant equilibrium of tangled vines, humming machinations, resplendent fauna, and endless, echoing life.
