There is no water, and yet the air is humid with something else—some chemical humidity that isn’t H₂O at all. The terrain stretches out across a vast plateau, warm under a golden-white sky. It is extremely high—so high that you can spot cloud lines far below, a hazy stratification of drifting particles. But here, the “lush” growth isn’t anything that would be called a plant in a usual sense. Instead, it is a churning proliferation of mineral-frond structures rising from the ground in curled columns, soft-looking yet distinctly inorganic. They weave together like a forest canopy but without leaves—smooth metallic fins that catch and reflect the light in subdued arcs.

Walk across the plateau, and the soil underfoot crumbles with embryonic brittleness. You can see in some places how the ground is still forming. It’s as if the land is newly cast, half-fused. Large fissures expose glinting layers of raw geology—unbaked striations that have a near-liquid sheen but remain stiff and immovable. The overall scale is open and broad: from a central vantage point, you can see miles of these metallic-vein forests, broken only by the occasional swirl of swirling dust that sweeps low to the ground.

There are no animals—no insects, birds, or lizards skittering around. In fact, no movement at all except the faint flicker of chemical growth along the edges of those mineral “leaves” and the swirling dust across the plateau. The world feels stable, as if it has attained some final equilibrium. The hush is profound: it’s not a natural hush of Earth-like desert, but a purposeful near-silence, as though all ambient noise has been siphoned away. You barely catch an occasional low hum that thrums deep within the ground. It comes in waves, reminiscent of industrial machinery in a distant engine room, yet you see no pipes or smokestacks. There are no built structures anywhere, nothing that a mind deliberately assembled. And yet the hum suggests something is at work beneath the surface—some massive, echoing process continuing at a slow, steady pace.

Large outcroppings of unknown composition jut upward, rising like half-formed towers. They seem more like geologic accidents than construction, but their repeated patterns—spine-like ridges, symmetrical bores—hint at some mechanical or engineered origin. Sometimes, you see flashes of faint chemical sparks in the crevices. It might be the internal friction of these newly forming layers, or perhaps an electric discharge that nourishes the crystalline “foliage” in place of water.

It’s warm—pleasant, even—though the windless sky offers no explanation for the heat. The sunlike light overhead is the only source, and it bathes everything in normal brightness, neither blinding nor dim. One would expect glare on the metallic surfaces, but the “leaves” seem to absorb and scatter the light in unusual ways, giving the entire plain a gentle, silvery glow.

And still, there is that sense of emptiness: a great, hollow quiet. Despite the lushness of these alien fronds, courtesy of whatever arcane non-aqueous chemistry allows them to thrive, the place remains wide open, almost voidlike. From time to time, you might see a swirl of faint dust devils among the columns, but they only emphasize the silent vacuum of the plateau.

It all feels suspended between states: newly made, yet timeless; lifeless, yet teeming with the slow, silent growth of metallic vegetation. No footprints or furrows mar the ground. No constructed roads or buildings interrupt the horizon. The hush is absolute, broken only by deep mechanical vibrations throbbing below. It is a land stable in its strangeness and suffused with a quiet, industrial hum that reverberates in the emptiness, neither advancing nor diminishing—just being, an enduring space in which things are perpetually coming into their raw and unearthly form.
