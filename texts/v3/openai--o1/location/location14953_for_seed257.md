In darkness, you move through warm water that seems to extend in every direction. Yet it is not a true ocean or open sea—every surface surrounding you is constructed. Stacked walls of polished stone, shaped columns, and carefully fitted blocks enclose the space so fully that no hint of natural rock appears. The entire environment—every ceiling, every floor, every pillar—bears the marks of some ancient hand, as though you have entered a submerged cathedral whose original builders vanished countless ages ago.

The temperature is oddly pleasant. The water feels faintly thermal, as though heated from deep below. But you are high—high above some distant sea floor or planetary surface. You sense it as you pass sealed windows or portals of thick glass: far below is nothing but haze and an occasional flicker of distant movement in the aquatic gloom. It is as though the entire labyrinth sits perched on a lofty plateau, sealed from the outside world, suspended in a womb of water that has not drained for eons.

No trace of vegetation exists here. No algae slicks the walls; no delicate fronds swing from the columns. Instead, the stone surfaces appear carved or etched with minute patterns—geometric lines, spidery whorls—decoration for the sake of decoration, or perhaps symbols of a lost language. Over millennia, the once-crisp carvings have worn smooth, the result of slow and endless water currents that erode all but the deepest reliefs.

The halls and rooms—organized in a close-knit grid—are neither expansive nor cramped, but hold a certain majesty, as if each space is akin to a cloister or small chapel. You move in near-darkness, guided by faint glimmers that might be stray bioluminescence from some of the creatures residing here. Abundant life scuttles and swims around you: strange crustacean-like organisms in clusters, eely shapes twining through tight corridors, large, slow-moving, gelatinous pods that cling to the pillars. It is a menagerie of silent shapes, gliding methodically among the shadows. Though the residents here are many, they utter no sounds you can detect—just the soft pulse of water as they drift.

Everything about the structure suggests an extremely old civilization whose technology never advanced beyond the simplest forms of metal or stone tool use. You find no trace of complicated mechanisms, but occasional hammered metal rivets or bracketed hinges testify to primal craftsmanship. Doorways hold heavy stone slabs hung on simple pivots, and wherever a joining of surfaces is found, lumps of hammered metal rods or pegs keep them locked in place. It’s as though this entire labyrinth was once assembled by patiently tapping chisels and forging rudimentary nails at a charcoal hearth—a place of heartfelt devotion or obsession, now consumed by time and water.

All the while, a soft hush pervades. Water dampens all sound; your movements create only the faintest eddies. Fish-like beings gently swirl about, occasionally brushing against your limbs. Their bodies are sleek and scaled in patterns reminiscent of hammered bronze or pewter, as if the environment itself bred them from mineral essence. Even the smaller arthropod creatures, with carapaces of milky translucence, cling to surfaces in thick clusters, their limbs clicking in near-silence as they pry morsels off the polished stones.

Despite the dense clustering of life, there is a patient stillness here. Changes unfold slowly—as if each corridor has quietly existed, nearly unaltered, for countless centuries. Walls have settled into their final forms, corners rounded by the unstoppable press of time. In these bowed corridors and semi-collapsed rooms, you find yourself sensing a profound solemnity, as if this entire place has relinquished its original purpose and allowed nature—animal life alone, in the absence of vegetation—to creep in and claim it as a final refuge.

The hush is nearly absolute. No shifts in pressure, no churning currents, no sudden floods—only methodical drifting. In this silent domain, you can almost forget the notion of day or night; external influences mean little so deep within. Perhaps a sliver of faint luminescence from a high window, or some hidden well of heated water, indicates a shift in unseen tides. But to your senses, it is only the shadowy architectures, the mild warmth, the hush of passing life. Everything suggests that if any transformation will come, it will be subtle, the product of centuries’ worth of drifting water and patient creatures scouring the last bits of mineral. It is a world in slow motion, an ancient, handcrafted haven submerged in darkness—its story nearly forgotten, yet teeming with quiet, abundant life.
