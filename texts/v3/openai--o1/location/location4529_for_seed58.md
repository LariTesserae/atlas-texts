Beneath layers of sunbaked rock, a maze of chambers and corridors extends in every direction. It is neither vast nor sprawling, but everything here is packed in close. Machine-built walls rise in tight angles, forming cramped streets and walkways that twist around one another in an almost claustrophobic arrangement. 

The atmosphere is warm to an almost blistering degree — the air itself seems to press in on you, pulsing with heat. In some places, you can feel the rock’s own internal warmth, as though the walls are exhaling. Flickering, low-power lights cast a dim, amber haze, never quite bright enough for total clarity. Operators of this place must rely on the faint glow of lamps and the occasional pulsing neon tube to wander from one cramped segment of the settlement to the next.

Pockets of condensation cling to the metal and stone. Dark rivulets of water sometimes gather in depressions along the corridor edges, forming shallow pools. There is a faint drip that echoes through the smaller passageways, but these bursts of moisture are rare and fleeting. Though the surfaces may appear parched most of the time, the occasional cluster of moisture stirs up patches of mossy film when given the chance. These splotches of dusty green are the only signs of plant life at all, making the ensuing dryness almost tangible.

The settlement feels newly hewn. Much of it is carved by sharp-edged instruments, leaving surfaces that have not yet been beaten smooth by time. The corners are fresh, seemingly cut only days or weeks ago, as though there were an ongoing excavation. Through narrow openings, you can glimpse half-finished conduits or partially assembled metal scaffolds. Faint blue sparks sometimes fly where metal meets metal, evidence of an unceasing industrial effort.

Structures cluster together in close rows, and many are stacked atop each other, connected by ladders or cramped ramps. Where the passageways intersect, larger rooms open up — small by any aboveground standard, but each one a network of overhead pipes, catwalks, and control panels. Despite the density of construction, the corridors carry an eerie hush. No crowds roam these streets. There are no animals here, and if there are any people, they keep to dark corners.

Massive cylindrical engines run in the background, their low grumble reverberating through the floors. Steam valves vent occasionally with a hiss. You can hear the rhythmic thumping of piston-like machinery somewhere deeper in the caverns. It is predominantly quiet, but not silent: a soft mechanical heartbeat pulses through the place, a sign of carefully managed technology thrumming away behind the walls. 

Every so often, a roving industrial cart trundles by on a narrow track, spitting out faint whirrs and squeaks. The smell is of heated metal and wet stone, almost metallic yet tinged with a rusty dampness. Tubing and wiring snake along the ceilings like tangled roots, feeding power from one cramped sector to another. Within them, liquids and coolants flow, delivering the resources necessary to keep this carefully engineered underground city alive.

The only hint of life—and it is more “presence” than life—might be found in the subtler motions of the machinery: the persistent reel of conveyor belts in the distance, the blinking lights on some control console, or the needle-thin mechanical arms that occasionally rise from well-concealed hatches, performing tasks with quiet efficiency. It is a settlement built for industrial functionality rather than human comfort, and it lays claim to the space with an almost organic intricacy.

From time to time, you catch sight of small, enclosed courtyards nestled amid the buildings — pockets of air that seem designed to cool or circulate the stifling heat. There, a faint deviance from the monotony can be found: a trickle of water pooling in a carefully placed channel, or a peculiar arrangement of panels that might serve as rudimentary fans. Strange that these spaces exist at all, as if the builders understood that living creatures would need some respite from both the intense heat and claustrophobic density.

Within the gloom of this subterranean city, the sense of strangeness grows. No scurrying creatures, no chirping insects, no sign of any wildlife at all. Yet the place is alive in its own way, driven by processes that chirr and hum in the background. Condensation seeps from one chamber to the next, adding the mild hiss of trickling fluid. Some sections are closed off, accessible only by heavy iron doors that shudder open with a groan of protest when the mechanism is triggered. You cannot see beyond these sealed areas, but you can hear faint echoes of grinding and steam beyond thick walls.

It all feels new: a settlement still forming and simultaneously operating at full tempo—an urban labyrinth of half-finished scaffolds, labyrinthine corridors, harshly cut stone, and the ever-present promise of further digging. In certain corridors, the heat intensifies, hinting at a deeper source of energy or purpose, compelling the whole place to remain in constant motion. The subdued darkness, the raw edges, the heavy machinery, and the conspicuous absence of living beings push this place toward the surreal, removing any familiar rhythms of life.

When you walk through, your footsteps ring oddly in the hush. There is a sense of expectation, as though something is about to happen or is always on the verge of happening. The vents might release steam, equipment might roar to life, or perhaps newly cut pathways will open, revealing yet another mechanical keep below the surface. And so it breathes and works, a subterranean city not meant for the living so much as for its own concealed purpose—hot, dimly lit, quietly alive with a thousand mechanical processes.
