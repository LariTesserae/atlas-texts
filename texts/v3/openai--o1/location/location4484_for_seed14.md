A dark, cold hush pervades this realm of water—an immense expanse where the liquid is not a thin layer at some coastline but rather a whole world below the surface. It stretches outward in pale, nearly colorless dimness for miles. The water feels thick, somehow, as though laden with sediment and the memory of ages. The sun—if there is a sun out there—doesn’t break through, leaving the region in a deep perpetual twilight. You might glimpse glimmers in the distance, whether faint reflections of hidden currents or bioluminescent flickers from some far-flung life.

Ancient stone formations, almost completely eroded into smooth lumps, poke out from the ocean floor. You can see that once, eons ago, these walls and plateaus must have stood tall, but they’ve been whittled down by unimaginable expanses of time beneath the weight of water. The traces of these old structures are everywhere, pressed close together: pillars collapsed into each other, all but turned into flattened ridges, so consumed by the surrounding fluid that they merge into a single, continuous mass. Every surface looks pitted and worn, as though water has slowly carved soft, swirling patterns into the ancient rock. Spare, translucent algae cling to rock faces in small patches, barely surviving in the gelid darkness.

The environment seems saturated—there is hardly a gap that isn’t filled by drifting particles, small floating creatures, or swirling ribbons of detritus. Faint streams of silt and plankton swirl in tidal pulses, carried by chaotic, fluctuating currents that jostle one another in a constant frenzy of motion. These currents generate a muffled roar, though it remains strangely quiet for all its turbulence, as if the water itself absorbs and damps any echo or resonance. Still, the feeling is one of persistent agitation; the liquid environment is never still. Sometimes, eddies spiral abruptly, stirring up fine sediment into dense clouds, making the water nearly opaque in places.

Life here is few and far between: an occasional silhouette in the gloom—a pale fish drifting by, a bottom-dwelling crustacean scuttling across the silt, or a cluster of tiny, nearly transparent jelly-like creatures pulsing in a sudden swirl. No vibrant reefs or bustling schools spangle the environment; only the rare wanderer coasts through the dim submarine labyrinth. Most creatures here seem small and slow-moving, conserving energy in the cold and darkness, subsisting on minuscule scraps carried by the currents.

No trace of architecture or engineering rises up from this submerged world; if any sentient beings ever laid claim to it, time has swallowed the evidence. Everything feels untouched, primordial—an unbroken continuity of cold water and crumbling basalt. If there is any sign of “technology,” it’s little more than the faintest suggestion of banded shells or rudimentary tools used by creatures scraping out a life in the gloom. Nothing seems sufficiently advanced to shape the terrain in any meaningful way.

And yet the entire place is alive with motion. The swirling water dances unpredictably, forming sudden surges and swiftly rearranging the sandy seabed. You sense an underlying volatility, an almost atmospheric turbulence that causes waves of sediment to roll like haunted clouds. It’s a paradoxical blend of silent hush and ceaseless, chaotic flows. One moment, you might see a ghostly calm as everything floats in suspended quiet. The next, a vortex comes from nowhere, twisting and re-shaping the drifting debris into new formations.

Time feels fluid, too. The primordial hush is broken only by the muted whir of hurried currents, the dull crack and rasp of stones grinding. Nothing here has changed for epochs, yet everything changes constantly as the water shifts. It’s ancient, and it’s in flux—an eternal state of slow chaos, warping the old rock, scattering meager life, stirring the dense gloom, and hinting at secrets lying deeper still in the cold, dark recesses of this submerged world.
