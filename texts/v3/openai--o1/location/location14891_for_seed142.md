Far above the rest of the world, on an expanse of land so eroded it has settled into its final shape, stretches a dry plateau with a quietly smoldering warmth. There is no sun here—or if there is, it is hidden behind perpetual darkness so deep you cannot tell night from day. The air feels strangely still and unmoving, as though time itself has slowed to a standstill, and what little warmth there is radiates from beneath the surface rather than from any external source.

In this high, ancient place, the land seems flattened by the passing of unimaginable ages. Worn-down ridges and plateaus stretch out in all directions, their edges broken and crumbled—sandwiched into forms that look like old scars on the land. The ground is as dry as bone. Any moisture that once existed here has long since departed, leaving behind a cracked crust of mineral salts and black dust that drifts lazily on the faintest breeze. When you walk across this ground, there is a soft crunch like the fracturing of delicate glass beneath your feet.

There is neither grass nor tree in sight; not so much as a whisper of green. In fact, the very concept of plants has faded into memory or is entirely unknown here. Any life that might once have thrived is replaced by stunted, mineral-like formations along the ridges—spires of translucent crystal or twisted pillars of volcanic glass that jut from the ground, reminiscent of fossilized flora but entirely inorganic. Occasionally, you catch the pulse of heat from beneath these mineral formations, as though the planet’s core breathes, but it is a nearly imperceptible movement. 

Despite the deep blanket of darkness, an entire city stands in the center of this plateau—as though a stubborn refusal to yield to these inhospitable conditions. This settlement is industrial in character: row upon row of towering structures shaped from dark metal and reinforced with unknown alloys. Pipes run overhead and snake underground, some throbbing with slow, methodical currents of energy. You hear the low rumble of hidden machinery, muffled by thick walls and the heavy stillness of the atmosphere. On platforms bristling with gearwork, colossal turbines creak in slow rotation, running on some ancient mechanism that never sleeps—perhaps harnessing the meager geothermal warmth that seeps from beneath the bedrock.

Harsh spotlights flicker here and there, trying to cut through the pervasive blackness, but they invariably succumb to a haze of black dust swirling in the narrow alleyways. When these lights dim or break—the bulbs last only so long in such dryness and constant darkness—it becomes almost impossible to see more than a few feet ahead. The silhouettes of rusted cranes and catwalks are barely discernible as they cross overhead. An occasional hiss of pressurized gas escapes from old joints in the pipes, producing small clouds that diffuse in the still, warm air.

In the midst of this urban sprawl, great steel factories stand spaced widely apart, each one a kingdom unto itself. They produce heavy machinery few outsiders could name. Nobody seems to come to collect these products; it is unclear for whom or what they are fashioned. There is a quiet but profound sense that this industrial pulse persists solely out of long habit, an endless maintenance of a world that has become self-sufficient and no longer changes in any significant way.

Strangely, though there is no water in sight, there are channels—dried canals, perhaps—radiating from the city’s center. They are made from a glass-smooth material that glimmers faintly under weak industrial lights. It is as if, at one time, these channels carried something akin to water or some other vital fluid. Now they lie cracked and empty. Some say they’re older than the city, remnants of a time when the plateau might have known a different climate. Yet the dryness here is absolute, and whatever the canals once contained has long evaporated or vanished altogether.

Fauna is nearly nonexistent. Once in a blue moon—if such a phrase even makes sense in perpetual dark—thin, skittering shapes might be glimpsed darting across the edges of one’s vision. Oddly elongated limbs, reflective carapaces, or floating motes of energy forming flicker-lives of their own. Sometimes you hear a faint, almost electronic sound: a chirp or pulse that could come from these creatures. Then again, it could simply be another whir from a far-off turret or the deep sigh of an ancient ventilation system. Their presence is so brief and ephemeral that one is never quite sure if they are truly organic or a trick of the darkness.

The atmosphere is thick with quiet. There is no wind to carry any song. The dark sky overhead is either a cosmic void or a shrouded canopy of black cloud that never disperses. If you pause and really listen, you might hear a subdued, throbbing pulse in the distance—the heart of some power plant or a factory forging who-knows-what, quietly beating out a steady rhythm in defiance of the emptiness. Even this hum is so muffled and slow you might doubt whether it’s real or just your own blood rushing in your ears.

Yet for all its bleak sparseness, the city has a certain magnetism—perhaps in its unusual geometry or sheer stubbornness. Its inhabitants, if there are any beyond the secretive figures who maintain the factories, keep to the shadows. More ghostlike than truly alive, they ensure the engines keep turning and the foundries slowly churn out their unknown produce. In the city’s intricate layers are corridors of metal steps that twist into sub-levels, hollow vaults that resonate with the echoes of footsteps never heard, entire underground complexes humming like a beehive without bees—a mechanical labyrinth supporting the plateau’s manufactured heartbeat. 

No one can recall a time the environment changed. It is always this warm, dark hush. Dust accumulates on centuries-old machines, only to be cleaned away by caretakers or by the large mechanical arms that slide silently along overhead rails. The dryness keeps decay in check: metal corrodes slowly, minerals form or shatter at a glacial pace. Everything endures in a prolonged, dreamlike standstill.

This is a world so ancient it has forgotten growth and water and the immediate rush of everyday life. Instead, it remains perched at its final plateau, in perpetual night, infused with the subtle glow of arc lamps and idle furnaces. A quiet industrial hush envelops it all, and only the rare glimpses of strange creatures or flickers of blinking lights remind you that, under the surface of this dryness and darkness, some form of existence persists: a patient, unchanging hum, forever forging the same shapes in the same patterns against the same silent horizon.
