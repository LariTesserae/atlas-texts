It hides within a low and gently sloping basin under a blazing white sun. From a distance, the place looks like a shallow shelf of parched minerals and pale stone, broken only by spires of strange foliage shimmering in the intense light. No sign of water glimmers anywhere—no river, no trickling fountains, not even the suggestion of dampness in the clay-like soil—yet an unexpectedly lush growth clings to every exposed surface. These “plants” bristle with rigid fibers, dusty pale or faintly iridescent, as if their stems and leaves were made of chalk or crystal. They flourish in this waterless pocket as though they live on pure sunlight or heat alone, bending and coiling in the warm breeze, forming a lively yet brittle canopy around the settlement that sprawls among them.

Such a city seems, at first, impossible. It is built of stacked blocks of hand-cut stone—sand-colored, flecked with bright inclusions that catch and reflect the overhead glare. Intricate wooden scaffolding ties the upper tiers together, though the wood must be gleaned from these tough, mineral-like “trees.” The architecture rises in flat terraces around a central open courtyard, where tall stakes and half-bent poles support overhead awnings of woven fiber. Despite the city’s large footprint, it feels curiously enclosed, as if it has been carefully tucked beneath a protective dome of blazing sky and swirling dust. It is an intimate world unto itself, ringed by more of that empty desert plain stretching out in every direction.

The air is stifling and hot, radiating from the baked earth. With the dryness, dust devils run rampant across the flat ground like restless spirits. The dryness is absolute; there is nowhere for moisture to hide, and the very notion of water is half-legend here. And yet the vegetation—those spiny, crystalline stems—grows in copious clusters, so the city finds itself half-ensnared by glittering bowers. When the wind sweeps in, the plants’ sharpened edges grind together with a nerve-jangling screech, rising and rising into an otherworldly din that echoes off the massive stone walls. On the stillest days, the actual city streets grow thunderous too: blacksmiths’ hammers, chisels on rock, and the ceaseless tamp of foot traffic create a cacophony that never fully dies. It pounds the air until you can feel it thrumming in your bones. At midday, that clamor can become outright deafening.

Life here is not abundant. Now and then, you might glimpse a wiry creature skittering through the tall spines near the settlement’s perimeter, or a flicker of something leathery gliding along the rooftops. Beyond these flurries of movement, though, the city feels nearly devoid of animal life. It’s as if those few creatures are ghosts—rare and fleeting shadows that vanish the moment one tries to approach. If they do feed on these chalky plants, they must do so discreetly, nibbling only at the fibrous bases hidden under swirling dust.

Daily existence thrums with an urgent, bustling energy—primitive technology harnessed through raw strength and countless hands. Large stone wheels roll along improvised rails. Crowds carry clay vessels filled with gritty powders or pockets of shimmering gems instead of water. Simple pulleys hoist blocks into place for the expansion of new terraces. Soot-covered artisans shape hammered metal plates over forges fueled by the petrified trunks of old crystal-trees. There is no gentle fluting of birdcalls or trickle of a stream, only the clang of rock on rock, the screech of mineral leaves scraping in the wind, and a relentless beat of labor echoing off the walls.

The strangest rule of this world seems to be that life can thrive in a place barren of liquid water. Whatever animates the bristling groves that cling to the city’s outskirts must be as alien as the city’s resolute determination to enlarge itself. The constant swirl of dust storms—sometimes tinted pale orange, sometimes white as if laced with salt—regularly blast the squat buildings, weathering edges into smooth curves and layering a film of fine grit over everything. But the dwellers here consider the dust as essential as air; it infiltrates every gap and stirs the plants’ seeds (if that’s what they are) from place to place.

During the brightest hours, the whole basin glows with merciless intensity—light reflecting off every surface, bouncing from crystal branch to shining stone to metal roof flashing back at the glaring sun. That shimmer can be mesmerizing, almost like a heat-induced mirage. And yet as soon as your eyes adjust, you see that the settlement is real, weathered, enduring. In the late afternoon, a deep roar rips through the streets—perhaps the wind colliding with the curved canyon walls beyond, or some subterranean pressure venting from cracks in the ground. Whatever the cause, it drowns out every other sound. The city vibrates, the rock grows warm, and the chalk-plants weave themselves into a rattling chorus. It is a land in motion: a clamorous, shining fortress in a desert that has no water, only brightness and heat.

In odd moments of stillness—a lull in the forging and the wind—one might glimpse the deeper strangeness here: the flicker of something peering out from behind a crystal trunk, the shift of color in a swirl of dust that briefly reveals a second horizon behind the real one, or the hush that comes when those skeletal stems quit scraping for just an instant. This place has existed for a long time, settled in its own equilibrium, and somehow the dryness and the abundance coexist in a precarious balance that defies ordinary reason. The city endures, generation after generation, forging a life from rock, sun, and wind. It is a pocket of inhabited strangeness in an otherwise yawning emptiness—deafening in its bustle, stifling in its radiance, and yet as rooted here as the unearthly vegetation that thrives on brilliance and dust alone.
