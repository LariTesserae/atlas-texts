The water presses in on every side. It is a cold, high place somehow—an expanse of liquid filling the hollows of a lofty basin, as though a colossal mountaintop lake had grown until it covered everything. In the dimness, one has the sense of a vast domain stretching in all directions, but it’s hard to see very far in the faint light. The only illumination seems to drift upward from silvery surfaces of strange aquatic plants. These plants are lush and numerous: tall, rope-like stems that sway in sweeping currents, broad translucent leaves that cluster in pockets of moonlit color, and long ribbons of kelp-like fronds that form drifting arches. They are the only living things visible—no fish, no crustaceans, no fleeting silhouettes of any other creature. 

Despite the lack of life, the place is far from silent. A pervasive roar reverberates through the liquid. It might be fierce currents clashing against submerged rock walls, or perhaps it is some constant underwater wind that groans through passageways carved in stone. The water is not still. There are powerful surges that sweep in waves, occasionally stirring large clusters of vegetation so violently that their leaves flutter and spines click. At times these currents carry ribbons of shattered stone or dark bits of sediment, telling of places deeper still—places unseen in the vast gloom.

As one drifts or wades (the boundaries of this environment are strange—sometimes it feels like open water, other times more like walking in a flooded canyon), occasional fragments of structures emerge. They might be half-buried under tangles of vegetation or they might loom from the darkness in odd angles. Many appear to be rough stone blocks carved in a distant past: squat arches, broken pillars, or small huts with half-collapsed roofs. The lines and carvings are worn down to smoother edges. Some patterns remain grace these relics: swirling shapes reminiscent of curls of seaweed, or geometric motifs that might have once had meaning. But no inscriptions remain legible—if they were ever there at all. The only sign that these were shaped by intelligent hands is their spacing and symmetry. They seem scattered at odd intervals, as if built haphazardly or left behind when the waters rose. 

Occasionally, a glimmer of metal can be spotted—crude lumps hammered into ring-like fittings or slender rods, set into the stones as if serving once as anchor-points or hinges. All of it is primitive: not the polished relics of an advanced culture, but rather the beginnings of craft—small cogs of a once-struggling, hand-tooled civilization. Now, though, no inhabitants remain. It is as if time has erased them as steadily as the watery currents have carved the stone.

There’s a chill in this watery air—enough that one can feel it even within the submersion, a pervasive cold that creeps into the bones. And it’s so dark. Only the faint, ghostly glow from certain pockets of vegetation keeps total blackness at bay. Sometimes a swirl of luminescent spores drifts along, leaving a glowing trail as the current carries it away.

The whole environment feels active and alive despite its lack of animals. You sense the cyclical push and pull of currents coursing through deep channels—like tides that have found a home in a mountain’s sunken hollow. The vegetation’s continuous growth, the scattering of ancient remains, and the unstoppable roar of water echo an eternal dance of creation and destruction. How can it be so silent of living creatures yet so loud in its motion?

Over time, a sense of strangeness grows. The water is simultaneously dense and airy, cold yet somehow swirling with warm pulses that come and go without explanation. One sometimes hears hammering echoes that might be leftover memories of those who built these structures, or maybe just currents hitting hollow pillars of stone. The deeper one ventures, the more the gloom turns electric with tension, as though this submerged world teems with unspoken energy, pressing outward but never quite erupting. 

And there is no sign of an end. The watery realm extends onward—a shadowy, half-seen landscape formed of drowned ruins and undulating flora. Despite the absence of animal life, the place hums with purpose. As if the water itself has taken over the role of the living. As if the stone, the plants, and the ceaseless currents are all that remain to witness whatever story began here long ago, in a frigid, high, and submerged world.
