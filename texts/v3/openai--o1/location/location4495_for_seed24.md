In the hush of darkness, the land opens up as a flat, dry pan—warm but starless and dim, as though it has turned its face away from the sun. The ground underfoot is a crust of salt-like minerals, softly crunching if stepped upon, though there are no footprints here. It looks as though water once tried to gather, then disappeared, leaving behind a pale, patchy sheen, broken here and there by deeper fissures. Each crack forms a crooked line that has been there for ages, unchanging and at equilibrium with the place’s stillness.

Small clumps of life cling to existence on the far edges of the salt flats: low, spindly shrubs with wiry stems or needle-fine leaves, spaced so far apart you can pick out each one clearly at a glance. Where two shrubs happen to grow near each other—rare as that is—they appear somewhat forlorn, standing just close enough to share a hint of company but never truly thriving. They have adapted to this nearly waterless environment over eons, their forms shaped by the dryness. There is no visible dew, no sign of moisture even in the night air.

The heat of the day has soaked into the ground, and though the sun is absent, the land radiates a gentle, pervasive warmth that hovers at the edge of comfort. A faint stagnant breeze, as if breathed by the salt flats themselves, stirs the mineral dust. Were there any animals to sense the motion, they might look up for a moment, but here no creature stirs—no rustling wings, no creeping insects, nothing. It is as though time has settled into a permanent lull, neither encouraging new life nor snuffing out those low shrubs that manage to survive.

There are no man-made structures, nor any evidence that a tool has touched these plains—a primeval stillness that suggests the place has simply existed in its own quiet domain for millennia. Although the environment is so stable and old, it does not feel like a ruin; it feels more like a secret: the empty stage before anything or anyone ever arrived. Traces of primitive technology might lie in forgotten corners—perhaps a stone chipped and left at the boundary eons ago—but even that is uncertain. If you search, you might notice a few bits of sharper-edged rock that could have been fashioned by some early tool-user, or they might just be naturally splintered stones.

Overhead, the air is dark enough that the inevitable glow of distant stars would be welcome, but clouds—even faint, high-level ones—block out the night sky, leaving only a dull, breathless black. What little light seeps through the haze makes the salt flats glimmer faintly, silhouetting shark-fin-shaped shrubs in a pale outline against the darkness. The phenomenon is subtle but hypnotic: a gentle ashen glow in a silent, unchanging realm.

No water flows here, and no wind carves new shapes in the earth. The land has found a kind of motionless treaty with the ages, each day and night so like the last that time itself feels stalled. There is nowhere for water to pool, no gullies or streambeds to trace. The far edge of the flats might gradually slope upward, forming slight rises or shallow gullies, but they, too, are ancient, carved when the climate was different and left to wear slowly away without drama or spectacle.

It is quiet. Not the quiet of a cozy room at twilight, but a deep, expansive hush that absorbs every sound before it can properly begin. Even your breath seems to vanish into the stillness. The atmosphere—more than physical dryness or absent water—imparts a sense that this place is a sealed-off stage, set for a performance that has yet to begin. Yet there is no hint that anything will change soon. The flats, the salt, the nighttime warmth, and the tangle of root and dust breathe together in equilibrium, in a hush that might last forever.
