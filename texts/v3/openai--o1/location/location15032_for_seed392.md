A blinding white radiance sweeps across the land, revealing a place that is both lush and silent—yet, on closer inspection, it becomes clear that nothing here is natural. The ground underfoot is not soil at all, but broad slabs of hammered clay or stone, baked reddish-brown by the sun’s relentless heat. Narrow channels of water run between these slabs, each channel so precisely placed that it is clearly the product of deliberate shaping rather than random erosion. The air is hot and heavy, thick enough that a faint haze hangs just above the ground, shimmering in the bright light.

Though the terrain is flat and low-lying, the humidity is almost palpable, as if water seeps just beneath every surface. Each slab is joined by cunningly mortared seams, still moist, as if dew or condensation has collected overnight and never fully evaporated. Small rivulets meander from one carefully placed channel to the next, dripping into shallow depressions that have the look of primitive basins—perhaps chiseled by hand. Such gentle trickling is nearly the only sound here. It might echo in one’s ears if they listen closely, though it never grows or fades. It simply remains, unchanging.

From these watery runnels arise plants—vines, ferns, large-leafed creepers—that at first glance look natural but soon reveal their mechanical or hand-fashioned origins. The leaves have a faintly metallic sheen or rough, hand-painted texture on their undersides. The vines sprout from hidden compartments within the paving, as if some artisan has tried to replicate nature but ended up with something more dreamlike and off-kilter. This strange greenery is exceedingly lush: each “leaf” layered, thick, and shaped to catch droplets of water. Occasionally, you might spot a tendril that flickers or glitches, as if the veneer of reality frays at the edges. Yet the vegetation stands unyielding, never withering or changing color under the merciless sun.

The fauna roaming this space—if “roaming” is the right word—seems similarly crafted. At the edges of the open expanse, small creatures dart between the artificial vines and the water channels. They move with uncanny precision: frogs that never vary their croak (though mostly, it is quiet here), insects with segmented bodies of hammered tin or carved bone, their wings faintly translucent. You see fleeting shapes that resemble birds, but instead of feathers, they have cunningly woven mats of dried reed or possibly hammered copper. Their calls, when they come, are faint: just single sharp notes that echo against the silence, then cease, as if they, too, are tethered to some timeless script.

Everything in this place has the air of newly formed, freshly built, yet it’s puzzlingly old in design—crudely shaped blocks and carved stone set in arcs or grids reminiscent of an ancient blueprint. Because the environment seems so fresh to existence, corners remain sharp, angles so clean that they feel impossible in a natural world. And yet, for all its orchard-like corridors of watery channels, for all its tapestry of vines and labyrinthine spaces, there is an odd quietude: you sense no cycle of day and night, no progression of dawn to dusk. The scorching brightness overhead never slants or dims. Shadows barely crawl across the ground.

It is an entirely constructed realm—a single, sprawling design of sunbaked plates and artificial flora that stretches to a horizon. Yet somehow, it feels spacious, as if you could travel a day in any direction and still remain in the same arrangement of blocks, channels, and creeping vines. Time seems inert, refusing to move forward or backward. The only sign of motion is the slow drip of water from carved spouts, the perpetual shimmer of heat near the ground, and the humming flight of strange, handmade creatures, all locked on an invisible track that never varies.

Occasionally, reality itself seems to slip: a vine will stretch too far, as though passing through massless air, or a reflection in the water will fail to match what is above it. It’s a momentary, hair-raising breach of physics or geometry—just enough to hint at deeper distortions in this constructed domain. Then the mismatch resolves, and everything looks normal once again, if one can call such an environment “normal.”

And so this place remains: hot, still, and glimmering with water; filled with carefully placed growth and heavily built surfaces. It seems an eternal monument to an old, primitive craft: the forging of clay, the carving of stone, the channeling of water. Abundant life flits through it—bursts of color and motion in an otherwise static, purposefully arranged world. Yet each movement, each droplet of water, each chirp or croak, feels enclosed in a vast hush. Time has no real purchase here. The illusions crackle here and there, as though raw reality is broader and weirder than the humans or creatures that fashioned these blocks could have dreamed. And in that hush, in that brightness, the land breathes in slow, unchanging equilibrium—forever on the cusp of revealing its secrets, yet never quite letting them shift.
