It’s a low, wide land, warm in its air but never truly hot, a plain that extends for many miles in every direction without a single man-made wall or roof to break it. There is no immediate sign of anything built, at least not in the usual sense—no towers, no factories, no roads. Yet there is an unmistakable industrial hum carried on the damp breeze, as though hidden engines labor in the depths of the earth, giving the place a persistent roar. The sound ebbs and peaks, sometimes rattling in the distance like far-off hammering, sometimes resonating just beneath one’s feet in a low vibrato, always present. It is loud enough that it defines the landscape as strongly as any mountain or ocean could.

The ground here is a hard-packed mixture of clay-like sediment and faintly gleaming mineral flecks, cured by endless ages of gentle, steady erosion. Rain has come often enough across the centuries to leave rivulets and shallow basins, but seldom enough that the land’s contours have settled into a stable equilibrium—neither severely carved nor truly flat. In these hollows, water lingers in pockets of shallow pools, each separated by long stretches of dry clay ridges. The water itself isn’t particularly clean or teeming with obvious life. Instead, it shimmers with a scummy top-layer of microscopic or near-microscopic organisms, possibly fungal or something simpler still. Sometimes the water condenses out of the air, beading into droplets along hidden seams in the clay, then trickling down into the pools to join the hazy film at the surface.

No recognizable plants grow here. Instead, faint, crusty patches cling to the ground—mineral blooms or spongy sheets of half-living matter that seem more geological than biological. Dark spots dot the plains, maybe fungal outgrowths that have the texture of fossilized bark or glassy mushrooms that never quite look fully alive. Their presence is sporadic and subdued, lending more questions than answers about what qualifies as “life” in a place like this.

And yet, for all its scorched, claylike bareness, the plain is not entirely without animal life. Occasionally, at the edge of one’s vision, something small and quick darts from one water pocket to the next—a furtive scrap of motion. Perhaps an insect, perhaps a lizardlike creature with translucent scales that merges into the dust. You rarely catch more than a glimpse; the land is otherwise hushed of flesh and blood. But the hush of living things is offset by that grinding, thrumming presence just underfoot.

At times, it feels as if the entire land is a single, ancient industrial machine—a machine whose parts are so worn and so large that what remains can be mistaken for natural ridges, pools, and crusty outcroppings. There are no discrete structures or visible turbines, but the mechanical undertone seeps into your bones and echoes in your ears. Perhaps deep beneath the clay, massive gears or labyrinthine pipes carry steam or molten vapors, fueling a never-ending process. The noise has been here for so long that the world around it has adapted, molding itself to the constant vibration. It is a stable system now—the clay contoured just so, the water pockets forming precisely where the resonance is easiest, the spore-like crust reclaiming the ridges worn away over time.

Oddly, despite the persistent hum, there is little shift or change. You get the sense that eons could pass, and the arrangement of pools, crust patches, and low ridges would remain nearly the same. The decibel level might waver moment to moment, but otherwise the hush of emptiness, punctuated by the roar of hidden machinery, never resolves into something more. Day after day the same low skyline, the same shallow basins, the same glints of moisture collecting around the same rocky ridges.

The air is neither bright nor dark, but gray-blue in a way that indicates ordinary daylight—no strange suns or twilit skies here. Whatever time it is, or however time passes, there’s little outward sign. It’s as if the light itself is part of the system. Warm, normal, unwavering. The horizon lacks any trees or structures to catch the eye, so one’s gaze is often drawn down to the details: the tiny glimmer of condensed moisture, a vein of dull metallic ore shining through the clay, the fleeting shuffle of some subtle creature. 

It is, in short, a place that feels both untouched and industrially driven, an impossible union of raw, unsettled hum and ancient, weathered calm. You sense that, although this corner of the world might be stable to the point of timelessness, it exerts its own quiet inevitability. Everything here has shaped itself to endure whatever mechanized presence lurks below. Nothing leaps out in frantic growth; nothing decays swiftly. The loud industrial undercurrent has become the heartbeat of this land, and time seems captured in that hum—persistent yet unchanging.
