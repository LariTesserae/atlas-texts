It begins with the distant roar of water. Not a gentle trickle but a low, resonant pounding, as if an ocean surge is happening just out of sight. There is dampness everywhere—rivulets seeping through seams in the rock, a cool mist drifting across the air. It isn’t raining, exactly; more like the sky wrings condensation out of itself, droplets slanting in a slow sideways fall before clinging to any surface they touch. Each stone in sight glistens with a thin film of moisture, so the whole world appears to be sweating under a heavy, dim gloom.

A first impression might be of standing near a marsh or swamp-edge, but there is not a single reed or blade of grass to be found. Instead, the land is forming itself from stark mineral edges. Shallow gullies carve half-sunken channels across an otherwise open flat. Some are sharp, as though newly created by tectonic upheaval or the violent release of groundwaters. Others are half-filled with dark water, so still it looks as though vast black mirrors have been laid upon the ground. 

No green breaks the monotony: no ferns, no moss. In place of vegetation, faintly iridescent fungal sheens or mineral crusts cling to the low ridges, flickering in the dim light. On closer look, the “fungi” may not be organic at all. Some drip from the undersides of ridge overhangs like wax. Others sprout in smooth pillars resembling layered glass or partially melted crystals. They bend or stretch slowly, almost imperceptible if you watch them for just a moment—but come back hours later, and the shapes have slightly advanced, creeping outward along the wet stone. 

Everything about this region feels ancient, yet the landforms look fresh, as though the ground has only recently emerged. One might expect bedrock so newly exposed to still be raw and jagged—and indeed, the edges are sharp where certain slabs have cracked or crumpled. The horizon extends in all directions, giving you the sense that you’re in a vast, mid-sized basin, a series of wide but shallow depressions overlapping in a pattern only half-seen through the perpetual haze. It’s open enough to walk for kilometers at a stretch, yet you’re closed in by the density of things. Every few steps you come upon new spires, cyst-like bubbles of translucent minerals, pockets of standing water, or columns of stone protruding from the ground at odd angles. 

Small structures occasionally break up the uniformity. They do not resemble buildings made by any industrial process; more like lumps of stone that have partially fallen or have been coaxed into curious shapes. Perhaps, ages ago, someone or something tried to build here but only managed to erect half-walls or solitary towers. Bits of worked stone—just enough to hint at an intention—punctuate the terrain. Yet no recognizable doorways, no regular geometry. If these are ruins, their original design has been swallowed up by time and whatever strange geologic process reshapes the ground. 

The air is cool, carrying a faint mineral tang. It tastes of wet metal, or of water that trickles for eons through subterranean veins. Light is never bright here. There might be a source somewhere above—some star or faint sun—but it rarely pierces the layered mist. Instead, the entire environment seems lit by a diffused, dull glow, just enough to see short distances. Shadows drift in slow, creeping arcs, never fully distinct. Everything appears partially in silhouette, half-lost in the wan haze.

Despite the gloom, there’s sound in abundance. Water resonates through subterranean caverns, sending up thunderous echoes when it collides with the hollow pockets beneath the rocky surface. At irregular intervals, you hear a muffled boom, like a distant quake or water surging against a cliff face. Now and then, something hard—like a rockfall—clatters, shattering the silence before the roars again take over. The effect is a constant, layered hubbub of liquid and stone. The noise is as dense as the place itself. 

Strange creatures tuck themselves into crannies, though true sightings are rare. A shape might dart out of the corner of your eye—long-limbed, slick with moisture, pressing its body into the stony ridges and then vanishing along a watery ledge. Only the scrape of a claw or the quick ripple in a puddle proves its reality. These beings never gather in groups; they seem solitary, each adapted to the watery mineral gloom, each a fleeting witness rather than a dominating presence. 

Time here unfolds slowly. Pools expand and contract across months—spreading a few centimeters and then receding again. If any of the spires collapse or slump, it’s a process of patient infiltration by moisture, layer by layer, until the structure simply melts back into the ground over years. The region is in constant transformation, but at a pace deliberate enough to go unnoticed from one day’s observation to the next. While the surface might suddenly echo with a crash, the actual shift in physical forms takes place over long intervals, giving the place an air of endless patience.

And there is an aura of the unusual, too: a tilt away from the ordinary laws of nature. As if something is stirring beneath all that sheen and shadow, coaxing these crystal pillars to twist in slow spirals. As if the rarely seen creatures hold onto a knowledge of this place’s shaping, gliding through the watery rifts and labyrinthine gullies according to patterns invisible to any casual observer. All the while, the gloom never fully lifts, and the resonant thunder of unseen water saturates the air, reminding you that though this terrain is newly formed, its primal energies have been brewing for far longer than you can guess. It is a realm that feels half-awake—not quite past creation, and not yet fully in decay—drinking in its own moisture and quietly building the shape of its future in the slow and patient dark.
