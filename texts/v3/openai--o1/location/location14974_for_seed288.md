A hush at first glance: the gloom of a cavernous realm extends in every direction, and one might imagine absolute silence. But there is nothing silent here. The air is filled with a low, thunderous chorus—a constant pressure on the ears, as if a thousand creatures have found a way to hum or cry in unison, each echo amplifying over unimaginable distances until it becomes nearly deafening.

The cavern itself is enormous, lying far below any recognizable surface world. You might walk for days (or what passes for day here) without ever catching a glimpse of true sunlight. The only illumination is a faint phosphorescence that runs in lines and patches along the worn rock walls—dim and ghostly, but enough to make out shapes and motion in an otherwise shadow-shrouded expanse. The stone underfoot has been worn and smoothed by some ancient process of erosion so complete it seems as if time has shaped these halls to a final, timeless form. Over eons, maybe rivers once ran here, or the rock was slowly dissolved by some long-lost chemical flow. Now it is fully dry: no puddles, no streams—just dust, mineral echoes, and a sense that water left this place behind ages ago.

Even so, there is life—lush in its own eerie way, but not the moisture-hungry green one expects on the surface. Instead, hardy, root-like fronds and fungal growths coil along columns of rock, forming thick nets and tangles in pockets where they can anchor themselves. Flecks of luminous spores drift through the air like tiny stars. On closer look, these “plants” are not plants in any ordinary sense: they are specialized to a chemistry that needs little water. They draw vitality from mineral deposits in the stone, or perhaps from the cavern’s faint energies. The overall effect is a labyrinth of sprawling fungal “forests” and curly, fibrous shrubs clinging to the walls.

Between these growths, vast spaces lie empty—wide vaults where the stone floor stretches out flat and plain, reminiscent of some ancient construction site. Thousands of footsteps, or something older and far larger, have scuffed and brushed them smooth. Here, communities of living beings have made their home. The city—if one can call it that—rises in layers, carved from the womb of the earth, with archways and honeycombed chambers that hint at grand intent. Yet there is no metal or machinery, no hum of electronics. Every door, window, and ledge is shaped from living or once-living materials: fungal wood, petrified branches, slabs of polished stone. Low-slung huts are fashioned from woven, dried root structures that curve into half-spherical shells. The denser dwellings cluster in the more stable pockets of the cavern, like small neighborhoods grown organically in the spaces left behind by time.

The myriad creatures—fauna abundant and often unseen—fill the gloom with their endless calls. Strange, tufted flyers flutter, colliding with the echo-laden air, their shrieks multiplied into a rolling, near-constant cacophony. Slender, many-legged mammals scurry along the fungal walls, sometimes sending shards of glowing spore debris drifting down like flecks of pale snow. Burrowing creatures, drawn by mineral scents, scrape and rasp beneath the stone, their movements dull thuds through the ground. When they move in unison, it becomes a drumming under your feet, pulsing like a huge subterranean heartbeat. Certain larger beasts, half-seen silhouettes, occasionally loom in the distance—towering forms that rumble through the blackness, prompting new waves of calls from the smaller life that flees before them.

Despite the bustling, loud presence of life, there is a strange sense of quiet to the place. Not in decibels, but in motion. Everything here shifts, grows, transforms at a patient tempo. The fungus slowly expands over decades, century by century engraving the rock with its intricate root networks. The city’s inhabitants go about their days with minimal fuss, using only what nature provides. They gather minerals and fibrous growth, shaping their abodes by hand or with the help of local beasts—no mechanical tools, no evident forging of metal or harnessing of chemical energy. It is a civilization in slow bloom, built on small customs repeated for generations without sudden leaps forward.

And overhead—if “overhead” is even the right word—there is the unceasing drone of life resonating in the high stone vaults. It can start as a moan or a whine and build to a roar, then settle again, like a breathing tide. Even when you stand still, the place vibrates in your bones. Eventually, you realize that your footsteps, your own voice, even your heartbeat, become part of that resonant motion. The land—dry, ageless, and so far removed from the sun—remains content to shift and erode at a geological pace, as it has for uncountable millennia. All the while, myriad creatures and dwellings dotting its immensity carry on with their quiet, unhurried living, beneath the ancient stone roof and within this hushed, roaring darkness.
