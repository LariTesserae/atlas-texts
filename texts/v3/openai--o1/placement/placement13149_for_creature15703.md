I step onto the damp stones, and at first, I am not quite sure who or what I am. I feel the soft glow of the moss underfoot, the vines brushing against my arms. My senses tingle with the pulsing breath of this realm, each echo of whirring insects, each drip of condensation from yawning leaves. It’s as though the world leans in close, whispering that I belong here, though I cannot recall a moment before now.

At times, I imagine I am one of the lithe, tall near-humans, clinging to a woven canopy-hut halfway up a spire. I can see it so clearly: I wake at dawn, dew gathering in my braided hair, my eyes flickering to trace the sprawl of vines that drape from one pinnacle to the next. A half-woven net sits on a wooden platform beside me, its loose ends trailing in the breeze. My thighs and forearms ache from yesterday’s climb up the steep trunk to collect fruiting pods. The memory of that ascent clings to me: the trembling in my muscles, the thrill of pressing my toes into rough bark, the sting of a slicing vine. Now, the morning air is hush and expectancy. Below, the green gloom awaits. I sense the day’s life stirring. In a few heartbeats, I’ll gather my net and slip down the rope-ladder to find breakfast—perhaps a cluster of succulent insects trapped gasping in a broad, sticky leaf.

Then I feel a shift, and I’m no longer human in the standard sense. My perspective widens, my limbs wrap around me. I take on the shape of a small amphibian nestled in a bark crevice. My skin glistens in the half-light with emerald stripes, well-camouflaged among the layered moss. A trickle of water cascades from overhead leaves and pools around my feet. A fleeting window of sunlight catches the droplets, turning them into shining diamonds on my skin. My belly rumbles with an ancient hunger, so I flick out a lightning-quick tongue to claim a passing insect, relishing the tiny crackle the creature makes as it slides past my teeth. The sweet, watery hush of this place wraps around me. For a moment, my entire existence is the gentle pulse of breath, the swirling hum of hidden life. I leap to another rootlet, the squelch of mud beneath my webbed toes as comforting as a lullaby.

Perhaps that is not the limit of who I am, though. Now I expand into something vaster, something that swoops high above all the spires. A huge, winged creature—neither purely bird nor bat—riding a current of warm air swirling between columns of ancient stone. My wing membranes stretch taut as I pivot, feeling every shift of wind under me. The world below opens as a patchwork of green and half-seen movement. I hear the shrill calls of others of my kind, a scatter of high-pitched tones that bounce off rocks and back to my ears. My vantage is magnificent. I dip low, skimming so close to a blossoming vine that my wingtips brush the petals. From these blossoms, I fill my jaws with pollen-laden nectar, a sticky sweetness that nourishes me for the next flight. A thunderhead brews at the horizon, but I sense no fear—only the exhilaration of the oncoming storm.

Niavé: that is the name I think I hear whispered by the vines—a name that feels like mine, whether I am a near-human perched in a shelter, an amphibian at the base of a trunk, or a winged giant soaring overhead. Niavé, the name tastes like damp earth and fresh rainfall, like an incantation from the spires themselves. It carries echoes of the swirling patterns in the stone outcrops and the half-seen shapes behind draping curtains of creepers. Maybe Niavé is a traveler’s name, or maybe it’s the hum of the environment made into a single word. Yet each time I recall it, there is a gentle warmth coiling in my chest—an acknowledgement that in this world, identity is fluid.

Then I feel the tug of something older. As the rumble of thunder recedes, I slip into the form of a caretaker—someone deeply woven into the spire community. My hair is thick with silver braids, interwoven with seed pods that clack gently against one another when I move. Sitting cross-legged on a platform of living bark, I am an elder among these vine-dwellers. Beside me rests a hammered copper bowl containing a small stash of precious pigments—ochres, greens from crushed vines, even a faint blue gleaned from rare blossoms that appear only under a certain moonlight.

I paint spirals on a rock face whenever the storms pass, each line echoing the patterns I sense in the shape of the spires, the coil of creeping roots, and the currents of migrating birds. I do not paint them as mere decorations. They are my conversation with the land. The spirals speak to something intangible that I no longer question. It is enough to feel the heartbeats of the vines and to know that they recognize the presence of my brush. Now and then, a younger spire-dweller stands behind me, watching intently. They might whisper in hushed awe, “The stone seems to move beneath your brush,” and I just smile, for I have felt it moving all my life.

Still, the caretaker is not all I am. Sometimes I slip deeper—beneath the root-mats and into the fissures of the stone. There, a hushed thrumming echoes in the wet darkness, older than speech. I become part of the vine-spirits rumored in whispered legends, my body half-formed of twisted growth, shoulders draped in tendrils, my eyes dark as the crevices that run through the spire’s core. I can feel that gentle push: the intangible hand that guides a lost hunter toward an unseen path or calms the winds to spare a precarious nest. In this form, I am the abiding presence that ensures the labyrinth continues—an ancient caretaker not separate from the environment but embedded in its very fabric.

There are other moments when I roam as a curious child, chasing the shimmering displays of the monstrous insect flocks. I scramble across the braided vines overhead, testing each new pathway, ignoring the warnings of older dwellers. My voice rings with laughter as I try to snatch a glittering creature mid-flight using a small hand-woven net. My stomach flips when I leap from one tower to the next, trusting my grip on a taut vine, each breath a mixture of excitement and fear. The distance to the rocky ground far below seems endless. Every time I land safely, I exhale a giddy relief and glance back to see if any adult saw that reckless jump. And if they did, I grin even wider.

Perhaps in the quiet of late evening, I become something humbler still: a drifting seed. One of many millions, carried on the breeze that surges through the canyon between spires. I float on a tendril of fluff, watching the world swirl below. I have no arms, no eyes, only the innate sense of wind and humidity. The rainfall that sometimes hits my delicate shape is not terrifying but life-giving, carrying me to a sheltered nook in the damp bark. There, I will put out a tiny root and test the stone. Over days and weeks, I unfold a pair of leaves, greet the next dawn. The hush of the environment guides me: the hush that arises after the roaring storms, in those moments of reflection.

Eventually, all these identities converge into one understanding: I am an inhabitant of this spired world, an expression of its enfolding tapestry of life. Sometimes I stand upright on two legs and weave huts from vines. Sometimes I crouch on all fours and leap with amphibian grace. Sometimes I stretch wings to meet the winds. Sometimes I reside in the hidden hush of the cliffs, an elder who leaves faint spiral carvings for others to puzzle over. In every iteration, I feel the warmth of the rain on my skin, sense the always-present breath of the land like a living heartbeat.

Every angle of the existence here is shaped by the watery air, the constant swirl of life. Some part of me remembers how the spires formed in an age of unimaginable storms, how the vines inched upward, clawing for the sun over centuries. Another part of me sees it anew every day, wide-eyed with wonder, collecting seeds and weaving a new net from fibrous bark. That is what it means to be part of this place: you are forever listening to the hush that comes after thunder, forever feeling the vines tremble when a predator passes, forever shifting your shape to match the next cycle of growth or rainfall.

In the end, if someone asked, “Where are you in this world? Who are you?” I’d answer: “I am everything that bends and flows with it—the caretaker, the child, the amphibian, the winged beast, the hidden spirit beyond the vines. I am all of it, and it is me.” And then I’d smile, because I know that here, identity is not a single form or a single voice. It is a chorus, echoing through the spires in the hush of the midday haze, carried on the spattering of rain upon leaves, nested in the slip of reptilian scales on moss. Here, place and being merge, and I find myself woven into every tendril of green that grips the ancient stone and calls it home.
