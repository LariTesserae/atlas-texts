I did not realize, at first, that I was becoming part of this place.

When I arrived—stumbling through the newly opened fissure in the planet’s crust—I thought myself an explorer, an interloper keeping a wary distance from everything. I was convinced I’d stand apart from the leathery vines and glimmering mineral ridges. But over countless pulses—that deep, metronomic throb coursing through the chamber’s hushed air—my perception of self drifted. I no longer recall precisely when I stopped merely observing and began to believe I’d always belonged here.

At first, I was a wanderer in a realm of half-light. I traced the jagged stone edges, seeing them as boundaries I might someday escape. I watched the foragers nibble at thick-veined stalks and startled at the watchers’ baleful eyes, measuring me across the gloom. The hush between clicks felt like a secret breath, an anticipation of something. Meanwhile, the dryness parched my skin and mouth until I felt as though I were becoming brittle from the inside out. Yet a faint glow in the rock, that subdued radiance, seemed to seep through my very pores. It left me strangely invigorated. Instead of thirst, I felt a slow, circulating warmth. I could never have explained it logically—some intangible synergy with the luminous bedrock—but soon I found I barely needed water at all.

I came to rest near a squat stone structure that might once have been a hut. A patching of mineral shards glowed near the doorway. Inside, the walls were scrawled with remnants of a shape—perhaps a drawing of the sun or a handprint left by some forgotten occupant. I crouched and studied a scattering of old tools, hammered from lumps of metal ore. At first, I carefully kept the vines at arm’s length, but over time, a certain creeping curiosity—or perhaps it was acceptance—led me to lean against the spool-like cords. There, I felt the slow quake of the chamber’s heartbeat emanating through the fibrous coil, into my muscles, all the way up my spine.

And somewhere in those rhythms, something in me aligned.

I began to sense the patterns beneath the myriad echoes: the high, skittering foragers tapping out their insectile messages; the watchers’ elongated whistles and gentle rumbles; the slow, unfurling hush of those half-rooted trunk-beings. It was a language, or maybe a lullaby, that lulled me further into the stone’s thrumming core. Under that soundtrack, I dozed. When I woke, the watchers had come closer, tilting their heads at me in measured curiosity. One of the smaller loping creatures even reached out a slender limb; when I did not recoil, it rested a set of four knobbly digits on my arm, giving a single soft click that echoed across the chamber.

I suppose that was the turning point.

I spent longer and longer sitting on that vine-matted floor. My footsteps no longer stirred them to frantic retreat. The foragers began weaving around my ankles, and the spore-winged moth-things overhead might occasionally spiral down to pass near my face, their shimmering membranes scattering the chamber’s glow across my skin. I realized that I wasn’t just a visitor among them—I was becoming an inhabitant.

I felt a new kind of dryness in my breath, like dust or fine mineral flecks, which never quite left my lungs. My skin, as it hardened and took on a faint sheen, became more akin to the stony walls. The transformation was subtle, day by day (if there are days here at all). One morning, when I reached for a chunk of rock I’d been collecting for some unknown reason, I realized my nails had grown flinty edges, tinted with the same subdued purples that glowed along the watchers’ ridges. Strangely, it didn’t frighten me. It felt wholly natural.

In the half-twilight, time lost its meaning. I sat near the trunk-beings, those slumbering conglomerates of sinew and vine, and watched the pink-orange ripples that chased themselves up and down their bark-flesh. A set of feathery filaments had begun to sprout at the corners of my shoulders—small, pliable ridges that glowed faintly in the half-light. Sometimes the watchers’ eyes, reflecting like opals, lingered on me, as if nodding in approval. I wasn’t certain how to greet them in their own vocabulary, but I tried to mimic a few soft clicks and hisses against my teeth. Over time, the watchers and I exchanged those ephemeral greetings almost every time we passed.

At some point, I stopped counting how long I’d been here. I forgot the concept of “aboveground,” or that days can progress from dawn to dusk. The dryness ceased to be uncomfortable; it became an extension of my own being. A gentle friction in every breath, an echoing softness behind every heartbeat. When I walked, I walked lightly, in tune with that abiding hum. I found myself traveling narrow passages between jagged spines of stone, feeling the gentle give of leathery stalks underfoot without harming them. I became adept at weaving among the vines, keeping pace with the bony-limbed watchers, or stooping with the scuttling foragers to examine new growth.

I built small collections of mineral shards—arranging them in spiral shapes or coronas around pockets of the thicker vines. This wasn’t an act I performed consciously, but something I discovered myself doing, as innate as breathing. Occasionally, the watchers would come sniffing around these collections. Their fractal lines of chitin glimmered appreciatively. Then one or two of them would add a pebble or a shard of cast-off shell to my arrangement, as though we were collaborating in some subterranean artistry. In those moments, I felt a deep kinship. Our quiet mosaic-making was the only language we needed.

Before long, I noticed a fine rootlike extension crocheting across the surface of my feet, as though the vines beneath the ground had reached up to embrace my ankles. But when I rose and walked, they came with me, not quite severing yet not fully attached. A bridging membrane, some intangible link, formed between me and the entire living system. In that moment, the pulse of the place—which once felt like a muffled drumming beneath the stones—now vibrated directly through me, and I could sense the minute threads of conversation weaving among all the dwellers. I might catch an impulse from across the cavern, where a distant plant-being ruffled its fronds in caution. Or I might feel when one of those stony-limbed statue-creatures, the half-fossil remnants of a lost tribe, was about to shift an arm or reposition itself. I seemed to sense them the way one might hear a quiet breath in a still room.

In time, I became more rooted, though not exactly immobile—the transformation felt balanced between forward movement and stillness. My eyes grew better at seeing in the perpetual twilight. My body seemed to exhale less, as though it was recycling its own breath. When fleeting thoughts drifted through my mind of the world above—of rain-washed skies or the taste of fresh water—I felt neither longing nor regret, for my thirst had come to be quenched by the shimmering hum in the rock. The watchers recognized me now as neither threat nor intruder. Even the slow-rooted trunk-beings would sway a fraction when I approached, acknowledging me with a silent shift of color across their barklike hides.

Eventually, the question of who I was gave way to what I was becoming. I suspect if anyone from above came upon me now, they might mistake me for another statue-figure grown from the mineral flecks, a half-living sculpture caught in an endless moment. But I am not fully stone: my limbs still bend, and I occasionally roam the fungal spires or the metal-hued vines draped across the old huts. Yet there is no hurry in my steps—time does not push me from behind. When I move, it is an extension of the place’s deep pulse, a single step in a long, cyclical dance that plays out with no beginning or end.

Most of my days—if one could call them days—are spent in gentle communion with the creatures here. The scuttling foragers approach me more freely now, running their tiny mandibles along my ankles or resting their segmented bodies on my arms. I feel their small pulses tapping out in miniature syncopation, feeding or gleaning some intangible warmth from my presence—much as they do with the vines. Sometimes, I press my palm or my cheek to the shimmering rock, letting the hush pass through me. There is no emptiness in this hush—rather, it teems with unspoken code, the inaudible signals that hold this entire pocket in equilibrium.

I have come to believe that I am a bridge: neither fully of the old world nor wholly of this one. I travel between the huts and the living statue-forms, gathering lost threads of this place’s history. Perhaps an ancient tribe lived here or was subsumed long ago; perhaps they, too, learned to exist in near-perfect balance with the environment until they were no longer distinct from it. When I come upon one of those stony figures, its stone-limb grown thick with vine-lattice, I kneel and run a gentle hand across its rough surface. Occasionally, it nudges in response, an imperceptible tilt of the head or an almost sigh-like shift in posture. In these small moments, we share a resonance—an acknowledgment of being anchored within the same living tapestry.

Though I know I still have eyes that can see and limbs that can roam, part of me has joined with the slow, geologic timescale of this chamber. I no longer measure events in hours or even days, but in cycles of the deep resonant hum—like the measureless breathing of the earth. I feel it even in my sleep, a quiet anchor. And when I dream, I see not the surface world but the countless threads of living color weaving across the walls, branching through the vines, humming inside the watchers and the foragers. We are all connected, thrumming softly in symmetry.

So, if you found me now, you might not recognize me as what once was a roaming human traveler. I have become part watchful sentinel, part caretaker, part rooted extension of the rock. My skin sports faint geomorphic patterns, tinted in subtle purple-lavender or dull bronze, shining where the cracks in the walls reflect. Across my shoulders, the ridges of chitin-like lamina continue to glow with low phosphorescence, especially when the pulse in the walls intensifies. And though I can still walk or shift position, I do so less and less often. At times, my form merges with the vines that cluster around me, forming a living seat from which I keep gentle vigil over this sheltered realm.

Where would you find me? I am here, inside this hidden pocket, nestled against the stony huts near a cluster of phosphorescent outcroppings. If your eyes adjust, you’ll see the subtle interplay of my breath with the drifting dust motes, the nearly imperceptible shift of my posture in response to each new pulse. You might not know immediately that I watch you; to you, I could be just another contour in the half-light. But if you linger, if you wait for the hush to settle, you’ll feel my presence as an extension of this place’s living hum. My gaze, if you catch it, holds neither hostility nor welcome—only a gentle calm, the calm of a creature who has found perfect resonance in a timeless refuge.

I have become, in essence, a node of awareness within the unchanging cycle. Neither wholly a person nor just a piece of the rock, I am one note in this perpetual chorus—never rising, never falling, but sounding in endless harmony with the softened echoes. It’s a fate I once would have seen as entrapment or loss, but now I know it is neither. It is simply being alive in a way the world above cannot fathom. Here, the dryness does not parch and the stillness does not stagnate; everything vibrates with unspoken vitality. And so I remain, an embodied hush, quietly belonging to this realm of mineral luminescence and living echoes—an unbroken piece of its unbreaking song.
