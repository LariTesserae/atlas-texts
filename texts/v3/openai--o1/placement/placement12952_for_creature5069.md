I am quite certain I was not born in a single moment. It seems more like I’ve been assembling for a long, indeterminate stretch—particle by particle, mist-thread by mist-thread—ever since this plateau started rearing itself out of the deep. If someone asked what I am, I might say “a traveler,” but not in any typical sense. My body is part vessel, part vapor, pieced together from the watery haze that clings to the edges of the stones. Just as this expanse of rock is still deciding whether it wants to remain, I too am an idea in flux, never wholly solid, never entirely fluid.

My origins, if such a word makes sense here, may lie in the swirling columns at the plateau’s periphery. Perhaps I began as a mere droplet, then coalesced with others driven by the deep pulses of the plateau’s heart. Or maybe I was dreamed up when a robed guardian brushed a fractal pattern, and some resonance sparked into being. Either way, I woke—or realized myself—when one of those odd lightning serpents sizzled across the sky and left the afterimage of a spindly silhouette behind. That silhouette was me, or the outline of some shape I claimed as my own, imbued with the shimmer of cosmic static. The next moment, I existed as a lithe figure with limbs that can fold inward and outward like ribbons, each movement accompanied by a faint ripple of white-blue luminescence dancing beneath my skin.

In shape, I might appear vaguely humanoid from a few steps away, but the closer you look, the more you see how my torso merges into translucent filaments that swirl around me like sea grass in a current. From time to time, I glow with an inner phosphorescence—subtle, ephemeral—pulsing in rhythm with the thunder’s sub-bass rumble. My fingers are elongated and gentle, ideal for brushing along the edges of those half-raised altars or sifting through the spindly reeds that grow in gullies. If I want to, I can let my limbs dissolve into the swirling haze until I’m more a suggestion than a solid shape. The plateau makes allowances for such fluid states; here, boundary is an option, not a rule.

I inhabit the in-between places on this plateau. One moment, I’ll rest against a slab of sharp stone slick with rivulets of water, letting the steam swirl around me like a living garment. Another moment, I might slide along the boundary columns where ocean stands upright, eyes trained on the luminous serpents weaving through the water. Sometimes, when the quake-like tremors come, I hitch a ride on the shifting air currents, dissolving into a swirl of droplets and then re-forming again farther down the plateau. In those instants, I belong neither wholly to the stone nor wholly to the water, but to the motion between them.

What I do, and why I am here, is a question that hovers on the edge of comprehension—like a half-finished tune you can almost hum but never quite remember. I know I’m drawn to the altars, those precarious rock slabs leaning in a circle, each bearing fractal inscriptions that shift and vanish whenever I look directly at them. Part of me wants to believe that I’m a caretaker of some sort, tending to these silent artifacts. Sometimes I place my hand upon the cool stone, provoking a faint shimmering of lines and angles. I sense a quiet joy in the patterns, as though the stone’s memory is greeting me. Once, when I touched two separate slabs at once, a chord of resonance swept through the plateau that made me gasp in sudden, exhilarated awe. In that moment, the roaring hush of waves and wind paused, ever so slightly, as if to allow the place itself to take a breath.

Between these moments of communion, I wander. The swirling creatures that flit and scuttle—some scorpion-like, some more akin to living ribbons—I regard with a mix of kinship and respectful distance. They do not seem to fear me, nor do they approach uninvited. We share the plateau like different notes in a chord, each occupying our own frequency. When the thunder grows too intense and I sense the land convulsing, I kneel with my palms against the slick rock, letting the vibrations flow through me. It’s as though we all brace together for the next movement in this symphony of upheaval.

Even the tall, robed figures that stand sentry beside the altars—figments of old power or guardians of new possibilities—seem to tolerate my presence. Occasionally, one will tilt its elongated head in my direction, acknowledging that I, too, belong to this newborn realm. Their swirling patterns shift, echoing patterns in my own filament-like limbs, as if to say: We see you. We know the same roar shapes us. I can’t claim to fully understand their silent language, just as they might not understand the manner in which I came to be. But we exist in parallel, feeling the humming undercurrent that pulses from the plateau’s core.

At times, the storm’s might intensifies. Columns of standing water thrash convulsively, and I have to cling to a jut of rock, letting my filaments coil around it. If the gale catches me at the right angle, pieces of my form stream away in ribbons of liquid light, dissolving into the sonic swirl. It doesn’t harm me—my substance is used to shifting states. But the sensation is startling, like being unraveled spool by spool. In these moments of partial disintegration, I glimpse the plateau’s deeper facets more fully. I see caverns in the rock that span downward, swirling with incandescent brine. I see half-finished shapes incubating in the depths, waiting to be birthed into the warm chaos above. I feel the place’s existential question: To persist, or to sink back into the watery void?

When the lightning arcs across the sky—lightning that whips and twirls and forks in slow, serpentine grace—it etches new lines in the air, as though drawing fresh boundaries for both the stone and for us creatures that dwell here. In the strobe-lit moments, I sometimes spot my reflection shining in a watery pillar: filaments of turquoise glow, face half-lost in ripples, eyes halos of swirling color, reminiscent of an undersea coral’s mouth. It doesn’t frighten me—rather, it reminds me that in this place, identity is a gentle weave of water and wind and stone. It’s a comfort. I am not alone in my fluid form.

Sometimes I gather strands of the pale algae or the gossamer moss that flickers at the margins of the plateau. I weave them around a small stone spire, leaving ephemeral patterns behind—my own form of fractal calligraphy, a transient gift to the land. Inevitably, the next wave of thunder or surge of steam tears it apart. But I suppose that’s the point: this world is an ongoing, living tapestry of creation and dissolution.

I like to imagine that the watchers—the robed ones—took part in forging these altars during earlier cycles of emergence. Perhaps they once hammered fractal sigils into the rock with nothing but resonant force from their hands. Or maybe their presence alone willed the patterns into being. They might remember forms of stability in this place—eras of calm or different storms altogether. As for me, I feel newly formed, like my consciousness only just converged from that shimmering haze. My memories stretch no further back than the last quake or the last slip of the watery columns. But I suspect that, in time, I will collect fragments of memory just as I collect droplets of water, slowly knitting them into a deeper sense of self.

And in rare, quieter intervals—when the thunder’s pitch lowers to something almost meditative, and the swirling veils of steam thin out—I find a vantage point near the plateau’s edge. I perch there, letting my lower limbs enmesh with a swirl of upright water. From that precarious ledge, I watch the horizon of endless surging waves and shifting wind. The boundary between sky and sea blurs, as it does everywhere here, but from this vantage, I can see fleeting pockets of stillness forming in the distance. Maybe those pockets are illusions. Maybe they are glimpses of other realms entirely, places where time flows more gently. A part of me wonders if I could ever reach them—or if I’m tied intrinsically to this living storm.

Still, I have no urge to leave. This plateau is home, as raw and unpredictable as it is. I exist in tandem with its trembling heart, shaped by its roars and cracks, by the friction of water and stone. Each new day—if “days” even apply here—I wake to find some nuance changed: a new spire juts from the plateau’s center, or a swirling column has merged with another to form a bigger vortex. Even I might find a new curvature in my limbs, or a fresh pattern of bioluminescence drifting under my skin. That is how we live, this place and I, forging ourselves in real time.

Raised from the ocean’s bed, not entirely sure if we’ll stand long or sink again, yet somehow persistently alive: such is our covenant. If the plateau does decide, eventually, to submerge again, carrying its shrines and watchers and shifting columns into abyssal darkness, I suspect I will dissolve along with it, returning to roiling waters and fractal potential. And if it stays, if it grows more stable—if rock and water ever find a lasting balance—then perhaps I’ll crystallize into a more unwavering shape, continuing my quiet custodianship among the altars.

In the meantime, I belong here. I am the hush between thunderclaps, the swirl of phosphorescent currents that hint at a new being taking form, the delicate caretaker who roams across a plateau still deciding its fate. And in that ever-shifting existence—somewhere between tangible life and elemental storm—I find strength, and belonging, and a purpose I can feel in my very bones, ephemeral though they may be.
