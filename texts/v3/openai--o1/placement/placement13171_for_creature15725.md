I think, in this damp, echoing underworld, I would be one of the “keepers of the living channels.” At first, I’m not sure how I came to this role—only that it feels like a calling as natural as the slow drip from the cavern roof. My earliest memories are of kneeling on a soggy platform of limestone and metal plating, coaxing small salamanders and half-blind eels out of hidden inlets. A caretaker placed a small fang-like tooth into my hand once—some relic of a larger creature, or so they said—and from that moment on, the entire labyrinth felt wondrous and alive.

Now, day by day, I walk along the winding thoroughfares with a sturdy staff in hand. Bent from the water’s humidity, the staff has a softly gleaming tip carved from an iridescent crystal found only in the oldest tunnels. A gift, or maybe an heirloom. The staff steadies my step on slick stones and also serves as an unspoken symbol: a silent reassurance for those who see me wading past. People notice the staff’s glimmer in their peripheral vision, realize that a keeper is at work, and give a small nod. Our tasks aren’t about forcing nature or making it yield. We keep the balances in place, guiding the labyrinth’s quieter inhabitants so they don’t starve one area of the city or overpopulate another.

Each morning, I visit the shallow basins where amphibious lizards lay small, transparent eggs. The lizards themselves scurry about, flicking tongues at midges that hover in the dank air. Over generations, their scales have turned a greenish-brown, matching the fungal clumps that cling to the scaffolding. Sometimes I find a nest in an unfortunate spot—next to a gear box or in a major footpath—and it falls to me to gently relocate the eggs to a safer space. Locals often bring me tips about new clutches, or about a new leak in the overhead pipes that might threaten a brood. I rarely speak more than a few words in thanks; they know I will come. My presence is matter-of-fact, unhurried, as if the city’s rhythms dictate my pace.

Hours slip by without me needing a clock. I judge time by the half-glow in the corridor lamps and the hum of the nearest turbine. Occasionally, one of the older engineers will ask me how the eels fare, whether they’ve ventured into a new water duct near the cargo lines. They worry about a population boom, or the possibility that the eels might nibble at wiring. These arcs of questions lead me deeper into the city’s watery channels, where sometimes I discover pockets of hidden life: an entire nursery pool behind a collapsed walkway, or a nook where the overhead fungus glows faint red for reasons no one has ever fully explained.

I’ve learned not to question all the mysteries. My duties already keep me wandering for many hours, staff tapping over metal gratings and water-worn stone. I test water acidity with simple strips of cloth dyed with algae, or I slip a hand beneath the surface to sense its temperature. Some areas are nearly lukewarm, warmed by unknown geothermal vents, while others are chilly enough that thick moss forms a quivering, emerald mat around the edges. On a slow, rhythmic schedule, I guide eels from cramped cisterns into more spacious pools, or bring an amphibian bred for pest control from an overfull district to an underpopulated one. I think of it like weaving threads across a vast, living tapestry of water.

Children sometimes follow at a distance, whispering and giggling as they watch me place a palm on the rock walls, feeling for hidden vibrations or listening intently for the rush of water behind a particularly thin barrier. They like to imagine I can talk to the creatures or that I see in complete darkness. I like letting them believe it. Now and again, I invite them close, letting them peer into shallow pools and watch the eels twist among each other. The awe in their eyes warms even the damp gloom. I remember being that young, feeling the city’s hush like a lullaby, wondering at the life teeming just below the surface.

On quiet afternoons, I stand near the edges of deeper channels, where the winged shapes sometimes roost. From a distance, I can make out silhouetted forms—membrane wings outspread, antennae like long, quivering filaments—as they cling to spindly outcroppings. They don’t make a sound until they suddenly take flight, and when they do, I feel the beat of their wings reverberate in my chest. It’s both a thrill and a serene moment: they remind me that for all I know of these channels and basins, there’s much I do not. Now and then, one of them glides low enough that I can see the glow of fungal spores on its leathery skin. Some people say the keepers could discover their secrets if we tried—but part of me thinks it’s best to leave a bit of the unknown intact.

My daily path winds through the districts where tradespeople labor. Old metal plates squeak underfoot; overhead pipes exhale little puffs of steam. These folks wave as I pass—some up to their elbows in sludge from a backed-up drain, others scanning gauge readings on rattling pumps. They’re used to me stepping carefully around them, staff in hand, stowing any worried eels or frogs in a lidded basin strapped to my back. Sometimes, if water levels shift or the pumps falter, I’ll linger to help. I don’t know much mechanical nuance, but I’m good at reading the flow of water and feeling where its pressure might build. The engineers call me their “water diviner,” half in jest.

Every few weeks, I’m called to the older, nearly abandoned corridors. The environment changes subtly: the hush deepens. Even the rock walls here are older; some corners are crusted with ancient residue that glimmers under the lamp glow. At times, I pass through vault-like chambers so silent that every drip from the ceiling sounds like a miniature gong. I see footprints in the algae—some fresh, some older—and I realize others move through these passages as well, though they rarely leave more than a whisper behind. It’s here, in these quiet outskirts, that the city’s shape is most dreamlike, twisting back on itself in puzzling loops. My staff’s tip might catch a faint reflection from an unseen water channel, leading me to discover another hidden nook or a small colony of lizards. Even after countless journeys, these deeper chambers never cease to surprise me.

Occasionally, an elder will approach me, someone who’s known me since I was small, and encourage me to share what I’ve seen. They have their own stories of how the city used to be, how channels once coursed differently, or how certain amphibious species emerged only after a new vent opened decades ago. We piece together these tales with practicality and wonder in equal measure. For instance, an elder might tell me how a generation ago, the eels migrated too far into one particular basin, leading to an explosion in rodent numbers because fewer creatures were left to hunt them. It took careful, mindful correction to bring the city back into balance. Hearing those histories helps me appreciate how delicate it all is, how the city’s equilibrium is never guaranteed.

When my day ends, I return to a carved-out nook that serves as my home—little more than a curved alcove sealed by a patchwork door of corrugated steel. Inside, a modest table holds a battered lamp, a few scraps of bread, and a clay basin for washing. A single shelf displays bits of bone and shell from creatures I’ve guided over the years, each piece a small memento. By lamp-light, I sometimes sketch in a water-stained notebook, trying to record shifting populations or unique sightings. My lines often blur in the humidity, giving everything a watery look. I’ve learned to embrace it; the shapes of eels and lizards on the page look as though they’re half submerged.

And so I live day by day, shaped by the hush and the slow rhythms, side by side with the improbable fauna and the thick industrial pipes overhead. I am not a ruler here, not a conqueror. I am a guide, one who moves with the currents, bridging the watery labyrinth and the beings that dwell within it. In time, I know I’ll leave my staff to someone younger, someone who shows that same quiet curiosity, that same sense of devotion to the labyrinth’s hidden channels. Then I’ll be just another elder telling half-remembered tales, nodding with pleasure when the new keepers discover an uncharted pool for themselves.

Yes, in this place of dim light and constant trickling water, I believe I would be a keeper of living channels: caring, observing, gently weaving the thread of life through the city’s hidden streams. And though my world may seem bounded by dripping walls and slow-turning turbines, it contains more currents and mysteries than any of us can fully comprehend. It is enough to guard them, to nurture their quiet potential, and to witness how life here evolves—always, always in tune with the patient pulse of water beneath the earth.
