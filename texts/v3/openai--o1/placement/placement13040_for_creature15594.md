I sometimes wonder if I was always here—if perhaps I took shape from the same black steel scaffolding and tangled pipes that mark this place as home. But in truth, my earliest memory is of stepping off a squat, mud-smeared transport barge, sloshing across the watery threshold before setting foot on the fresh concrete. Perhaps I knew a different sun once, but that life seems no more than a dream. Now, every beat of my day, every breath of heavy air, is woven into the settlement’s steady hum.

In the blurred half-light of this place, I’m a figure in a heavy rubber apron, layered over waders patched and re-patched so many times they no longer resemble their original shape. My hair is trapped beneath a hood, perpetually damp with condensation. I keep a battered tool belt around my waist, jangling with spanners, wrenches, small vials of sealant, strips of cloth for makeshift gaskets. There’s a single bright patch on my sleeve—once white, now stained a murky brown—that serves as a simple badge: I’m the one tasked with keeping the main boiler in check.

It’s a grand, rust-streaked cylinder perched near the center of all the crisscrossing catwalks, ringed by gauges and pressure valves that hiss at unpredictable intervals. The boiler is a living heart, pumping steam outward through thick pipes that exhale in jets of mist. If one line fails, the humidity can become unbearable, or a crucial process down the line might seize. If the temperature climbs too high, the pressure might vent violently, forcing us into immediate repairs or emergency shutdowns. In the gloom, this boiler’s circuit of ductwork glimmers with a faint, oily sheen. I traverse its perimeter daily, double-checking each valve, listening for the boiler’s subtle staccato.  

I have grown attuned to that metallic symphony: the slow churn of the pumps, the fan belts’ dull rotation, the clang of metal panels that expand with the heat only to contract again when a random draft of cooler air sweeps through. Even the immense fans overhead, spinning without rest, form a kind of lullaby that never quite sleeps. I find myself humming along on occasion, matching the whir of rotating gears with my own unthinking melody. Sometimes when I pause in my footsteps, I hear the settlement’s heartbeat layered beneath the insect whine and bird calls—a continuous background chord that resonates through my boots and hums in my chest.

When my eyes drift upward, the sky doesn’t offer me brightness or starlight. It’s a perpetual cloak of hazy gloom—never truly day, never truly night. At first, this half-twilight unsettled me. I missed the sharp outline of my own shadow, the daily arc of the sun. But in time, I began to appreciate the softness of the light. Everything is a gradient here, from the slick metal underfoot to the still, brackish pools out by the fences. The horizon’s color—somewhere between rust and greenish-gray—matches the shifting waters that the great pumps and dredges continuously try to manage.

When my shift ends, I retreat into the labyrinth of dwellings suspended within the scaffolding. My bunk is little more than a sealed compartment in the recesses behind a tangle of pipes, hammered together from corrugated panels. A single, sputtering lightbulb casts mild illumination on my handful of possessions: a battered notebook filled with pressure readings, some meager extras for patching gear, and the only memento I carried from... well, “before.” I’m not wholly sure what “before” was, merely that it had gentler air.

Above my bunk, someone has painted a stylized salamander in black and gold. I have no idea who painted it or precisely when—these small artistic gestures appear and disappear mysteriously, often in the hush of someone’s off-shift hours. I like to imagine the painter wore thick gloves flecked with the same gold paint that left spatters on the catwalk. Art is passed on here like a shared secret or a thoughtful hush. When I gaze on it in those last moments before rest, I can almost see it move as the bulb flickers, flashing a silent promise that even in steel and steam, life finds color.

I’ve learned that “off-shift” doesn’t always mean complete rest. There’s nearly constant need for watchers to scan the edges of the wetlands, ensuring no large reptiles stray too near a vital pumping line. I often volunteer for an hour or two, wandering along raised walkways with a handheld lamp. The wetlands out there feel primeval. The water’s surface, quivering under the weight of all that humidity, glows faint with halogen reflections. Whenever I spot the glint of an amphibious creature—thick-bodied salamanders or a slender, gilled lizard—my lamp reveals their slick scales as they lash through the reeds. And from the scaffolding, steel expansions squeak overhead, as if the entire structure is exhaling in tandem with the swamp.

On rare occasions, I accompany a small band of gatherers who move beyond the perimeter to retrieve building supplies or meet trading barges. We load squat cargo boats with metal salvage or barrels of fuel, exchanging them for crates of dry rations and new pipe fittings. Out in the deeper marsh, the water rises up to the edges of the boat, licking at our ankles in a steady slosh. The horizon there is empty except for more haze, and I catch fleeting glimpses of ancient trees drowned in stagnant water or the silhouettes of unknown structures that might mirror our settlement. It’s a reminder that, despite the closeness of my daily routine, this wet world extends in all directions, an unbroken hush of gloom and steam.

I think back on the settlement sometimes, as our boat chugs across the wide channels: how the entire place seems a testament to quiet persistence. The older workers—those who arrived long before me—speak of catwalks and scaffolds that used to be half their current size; they say the dredged wetlands used to be a different shape entirely, a smaller cluster of mud and water. Their reminiscences feel both ancient and childlike, always peppered with the same note: “It was changing even then.”

And yet, it remains so consistent. The fans still spin, the boiler’s heartbeat remains steady, the water continues to seep in. I’ve come to see it as a balancing act that doesn’t strive for mastery, only for equilibrium—a collaboration between human determination and the swamp’s relentless push. We wear boots so we do not sink, but we also yield, letting the water flow in channels that we only gently guide and dare not fully block. Sometimes we harness the swamp’s resource for power; sometimes it reminds us that it can swallow everything if we grow careless.

So yes, I am a caretaker—a caretaker of the boiler that keeps the air breathable and a caretaker of the small daily harmonies that knit this settlement together. If we succeed at all, it’s because we stay humble to the nature we dwell in. The gloom above, the drizzle of condensation, the insects that swirl in thick clouds, the slick gleam of amphibian skin, the constant clang of metal surfaces, the swirl of steam in every alley—these elements infiltrate our hearts, our veins, our breath.

Sometimes, in quiet hours, I wonder if I could leave. If I could trace a route back to more familiar lands of bright sky and stable ground. But then the lull of the perpetual twilight deepens, and I hear the rhythmic sigh from the boiler’s vent, calling me to check the gauges and ensure the next shift can take over without trouble. The door to my compartment rattles in a passing current of heavy air, a small insect pings against the lightbulb overhead. My reflection in the corrugated metal shows a hooded figure, flush with humidity, eyes glimmering with a strange contentment.

I realize I’m bound here, but not by chains—by a sense of belonging. I am a piece of this orchestrated sprawl of steel and water, no more or less significant than a single rivet or a salamander’s shy flick of its tail. My role is not to conquer the swamp but to guide and adapt. Each day, the settlement and I fill each other’s spaces: it shapes me, as I shape it, forging on through steam and shadow. And in that acceptance, I know exactly who I am and where I stand: one faithful keeper among many, an integral chord in the deep mechanical hum that resonates across these lowlands.
