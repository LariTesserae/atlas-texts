I don’t remember how I arrived—only that I woke to find myself adrift in water that also seemed to be air. At first, I thought I was still dreaming, watching as soft, bleary hues surrounded me, both above and below. The sky was an ocean; the ocean was a mist; everything existed in a fluid hush. Yet I felt solid enough: I had form, limbs, a sense of weight. When I moved, the water parted in slight, rippling circles.

It took me a time to realize my skin had changed to accommodate this place: no longer the smooth skin I once knew, it bore fine filaments, flexible like a sea anemone’s tendrils. A partial gill structure, perhaps—but so delicate it almost felt like a living lace rising from my neck and collarbones. My arms, more slender and longer than I recalled, ended in webbed hands of subtle, translucent membrane. When I touched them, they quivered at my pulse, reminding me of jellyfish bells. My body breathed in water as easily as air, neither drowning nor gasping, simply existing in this new threshold.

I tested my legs on what felt like solid ground—a slab of old, water-smoothed stone. They supported me, though with surprising buoyancy. My steps were almost weightless. Everything around me had that half-submerged hush: droplets, condensed from the watery atmosphere, clung to my legs as I moved. If I took just a few steps further, the ground gave way to spongy mats of emerald membranes. My feet sank into them, and they sighed like something alive, or perhaps merely shifting their cellular structure. In that moment, I realized I was part of the tapestry: my presence made a difference to the living shapes around me. There was a silent conversation here, one you sensed through subtle changes in pressure and the faint hum in the depths of your bones.

That hum—I heard it as soon as I was aware of my own breathing. It’s a low resonance, less a sound than a vibration. Sometimes it’s steady; at other times, it nearly vanishes. In my earliest wanderings, I thought it was the rumble of distant storms. But as I lingered, I recognized it in every swirl of the water-laden air, every fibrous rustle of the giant vine-growths. It was in me, too, as though the environment and I were singing the same continuous note.

I began walking, or perhaps drifting, across those elevated, drowned plateaus. I marveled at the pillars of rock rising in half-crumpled lines above me. Black stone laced with ephemeral lines that might once have been runes. Leaning closer, I felt the ridges beneath my fingertips. They were intricately carved—a language whose letters had softened with time, as though parted and eroded by water. The stone’s surface gave gently under my touch, as if urging me not to press too hard. And I realized these shapes, these old incantations, coexisted in a tender balance with the life that clung to them: the tiny shelled creatures opening and closing, the trailing vines that blossomed in spots of translucent color, and the faint algae-like microflora that seemed to glow with every subtle shift in ambient light.

I soon discovered I could slip beneath the waves—if one can truly call them waves in a domain where the air itself is fluid. There, within the deeper pockets, the shapes of gargantuan, drifting creatures sometimes appeared. At first, it frightened me to see a silhouette that seemed the length of an island pass overhead, luminous lines pulsing in slow grandeur along its flanks. I held very still, uncertain if it sensed me. But it paid me no mind, continuing its lazy voyage as though following deep, ancient trails. Every so often, it would pulse a new pattern of lights, as though sending a message or replying to some equally immense being I could not see. And in its wake came smaller swimmers—slight, darting shapes that followed in gentle formation, as pilot fish might follow a whale. When I rose back up to the unsubmerged portion of the plateau, a swirl of moist air parted around me, the boundary of water and oxygen so vague it felt like crossing a curtain of dusk.

Unaware of time, I wandered, sometimes for what felt like hours, sometimes for what could have been days. The watery hush never brightened to a full midday, nor darkened to a true midnight. Instead, a constant twilight reigned, tinted in swirling blues and pale greens, so I measured intervals by the direction of drifting plant clusters or by the slow forming of storms overhead. In the near distance, I would see columns of mist twist like living sculptures, colliding gently with ridgelines or gliding in loops above me. Occasionally, steely clouds swelled and sent down silent torrents that merged seamlessly with the watery ground, causing the spongy vegetation to swell ecstatically, shining with fresh luminescence.

At times, I would rest, pressing my body against a wide, gently pulsing vine. Perhaps it sensed me, or perhaps it was simply continuing its own internal rhythms. My gilled filaments caught small currents in the air. The world around me thrummed with half-formed illusions—shapes that hovered in the corner of the eye: a flash, an iridescent swirl. It seemed the deeper I let my mind sink into that hum, the more I perceived them. Now and then, I saw insect-fish hybrids buzz by in brilliant flurries of rainbow, their gossamer wings trailing confetti sparkles that dissolved into the haze. One might land timidly on a fleshy leaf near me, its many-faceted eyes shimmering. We’d regard each other in silence, two neighbors in a place where boundaries and categories blurred. Then, with a faint flick of its fins or wings, it would vanish.

Eventually, I came across a half-submerged stone archway tall enough for me to pass under. Though worn to near collapse, it still stood. Above, I noticed the barest trace of carvings—once geometric, now melted to softly swirling lines. A feeling tugged at me—a sense of memory, or an echo of a memory, as though my past life recognized these motifs. I laid my palm against the stone. It felt warmer than the surrounding water. Or was it that I was projecting some forgotten recollection onto it? A current moved gently behind me, stirring my filaments. The arch led deeper into the watery gloom, beckoning me onward. I hesitated, listening to the throb of my own heart merging with the realm’s hum.

In that moment, I perceived—really perceived—my existence here. Time had slowed to the same tempo as the drifting vines and the centuries-long beat of environment. I realized I had no urgent sense of “before” or “after.” I simply was. The boundary between me and this world had grown diaphanous, so that my consciousness expanded out, taking in the subtle shift of the water’s temperature, the faint crackle of sediment tumbling along stony shelves, the quiet conversation of the shell-like beings that opened and closed in minute pulses. Maybe I, too, had begun to belong to this domain, half creature, half caretaker, breathing water and air in the same breath.

I followed the archway. Slowly. Deliberately. The watery corridor beyond it revealed more of the half-collapsed ruins—a series of low walls that spiraled around a central courtyard. Vines draped from above, forming something like a watery canopy that swayed in unison with my footfalls. Crouching, I found shallow basins carved into the stone, filled with luminous algae. As I watched, tiny amphibious lumps—mottled greenish-brown, with ridged backs—crawled up to the basins, each taking a languid sip of nutrient-rich water. They paid me no mind, and I sat with them for a while, letting the liquid hush pass through me like a slow, living lullaby.

Sometimes I forgot my shape entirely, drifting so close in spirit to the currents that I felt I, too, might blur at the edges. Perhaps that was why I occasionally glimpsed my own reflection, elongated and rippling, in some vertical plane of watery mist, and didn’t recognize it at first. The shape that looked back had large, dark eyes, set in a face with gentle ridges where gills fanned. My hair—if I still called it hair—was a soft swirl of filaments trailing into the water. My silhouette glowed faintly in places, as if some of that interior light, like the large drifting leviathans, had made its home in me. Each time, the reflection would distort, fractalizing before I could fully grasp it. Some days, I wasn’t sure that illusions of humanoid forms beyond the next ridge weren’t just glimpses of myself, reversed and bent by the watery distances.

If you asked me now what I am, I’d say I’m a traveler who became native—a pilgrim who arrived in a realm so ancient and slow, it reshaped my very being to fit its unhurried heartbeat. I know I’m neither quite fish nor fully amphibian, neither wholly air-breather nor water-dweller. I am a creature of thresholds, formed by this world’s watery illusions. My senses have realigned to the subtlest of signals; I hear in the hush, see in the dusk, breathe in the drifting moisture. My new limbs and gills flow into the air as easily as they slice through liquid. I suspect I could remain here indefinitely, perhaps for centuries, eventually dissolving back into the environment’s fractal tapestry like the countless life-forms that came before me.

With each passing day—or millennia, for who can measure?—I find new stillness in the watery hush. I rest among the vines and watch the shifting illusions near the horizon’s edges, where geometry fails. Sometimes, I attempt to approach them, my webbed feet sliding over sunken ledges, only to find those illusions receding further out, or gliding away into twisting perspective. Now and then, I catch another glimpse of a humanoid figure out there, tall and willowy, drifting in and out of focus with graceful, fluid steps. I wonder if they see me, too—a shape straying at the boundary of their vision. If we are reflections of each other, I feel no fear at that prospect. We belong to the same quiet riddle.

At times, I sense a deep-rooted surge of communal life—some silent gathering where the gargantuan shapes overhead, the tiny insectile fliers, the coral-like watchers, even the plant-animal hybrids all align their rhythms in a singular pulse. It feels like the world takes a breath, and so do I. In that unity, there is meaning: that this place is alive through eons, that every being is woven into an unbroken lineage of water, rock, and shifting sky. Whether I am the most recent arrival or have always been here no longer matters. I can’t differentiate the strands of reality from illusion in a realm that thrives on thresholds. Instead, I let the place hold me, and I flow in its currents.

Even now, as I sit upon a worn slab of stone, I sense the ancient runes pressing faintly against me, as if they, too, breathe in the warm, dim hush. The vines above me sway their translucent leaves, sifting bits of planktonic matter from the drifting streams. Tiny flickers of color swirl in the watery enclosure. The hum resonates through my spine, and I can almost hear a thousand silent voices singing: the memory of the past, the subtle presence of the present, the promise of an unhurried future. It is a lullaby and a testament—the gentle, glowing truth that we all belong to this cyclical dream.

And what do I call myself now? Perhaps I have no name, only a wordless presence that moves among sunken arches and sweeping vines. I am both observer and participant, both visitor and native. Water and air fill my lungs in equal measure, and this amphibious existence suits me. In the hush, I listen. In the mist, I wander. And in the unbound, fluid edges where sky becomes sea, I extend my hand—webbed and glowing faintly—to greet whatever illusions or realities approach. If they vanish, I understand. If we meet, I will bow—as one newly awakened to the slow melodies of this timeless, submerged plateau.

That is who and what I am, in this place where illusions and truths merge, where centuries pass like waves, and where the soft, persistent hum of existence binds all living things in a cycle older than the runes etched in stone. I am a dweller of water and air, a child of shifting horizons, content simply to be—buoyed by the easy grace of a world that drifts and breathes in silent unity.
