I am something like the imprint of a breath left on warm glass—barely defined, suspended in a moment before it vanishes. I sense this as I slide through the chamber’s narrow entrance for the first time. My presence wavers between solidity and dream. If you were to watch me from a distance (were there a vantage point that allowed such a perspective in this crowded world), you might see only a faint silhouette: the fluid shape of something that hasn’t quite committed to being flesh or stone, lingering among all these half-forms.

When I first become aware of myself in this place, I notice a rhythmic tug in the air. Everything here repeats in loops: the drip, drip, drip of water; the near-motion of creatures; the ambient shuffle of antennae and limbs. The pulse of my own substance falls in line with it. At times, I don’t know if my heart or my consciousness is beating in that same cycle. I feel the beat in my chest, my limbs, my mind; it is the metronome of the cavern. I suspect I am knitted from the same suspended thread that ensnares the insects and amphibians, the fungus and half-carved designs. I can’t recall if I emerged from a crack in the stone or if I’ve always been here.

Little by little, I come to realize what I am, if only by contrast with what I am not. I am neither fully creature, nor fully mineral. I sense that I was meant to be caretaker or observer or midwife of sorts—my form reminds me of the embryonic shapes around me, but I move more fluidly in these corridors. Perhaps I was halfway formed as a living being once, but I too was caught in the timeless net that tangles this hollow. And so I remain… only partially realized.

I try to walk, and for a moment, my feet feel as though they are dissolving into the slick floor. I try to speak, but the slightest noise is swallowed by the echoing environment and magnified until it feels like thunder in my skull. My voice fades into the cyclical hush, absorbed by the unstoppable repetition. Yet I am conscious of it all; I observe it with a mind that flickers between present awareness and dreamy drift. The creatures that scuttle around me do not reel away in surprise or fear—they barely register me at all. Perhaps they see me as part of the rock, as I am equally unformed.

When I pass near the clusters of isopods, their antennae twitch in unison, tasting my presence. It’s as though they can sense that I am a boundary-crosser: no longer purely of the outside world, but not quite part of their static realm. My form unsettles them only slightly. A few shift aside in a dull rustle, making space among the slick stones for me to move closer. I crouch and watch: in that moment, I feel droplets drip from my hair (or the memory of hair) down onto my shoulders. They pool in my collarbones, forming watery pockets that reflect the glaring crystal glow. My reflection is distorted, indefinite. I see no clear face, only a glimmer that suggests something that might someday become a face.

I slip among half-formed arches, running my fingertips across those carved stones that never completed themselves. In some places, the stone yields softly to my touch, still malleable. In others, it’s firm, though never truly finished. I imagine that, under normal conditions, centuries of slow pressure and mineral exchange would continue shaping this interior. But here, time has snagged its threads. Every second is a step forward and backward at once; nothing actually moves, but everything is alive with potential. I think: maybe I exist to witness what might have been—these embryonic wonders that materialized in the hush of earth’s deeper layers.

I sense a tug in my chest, a dull ache. It might be longing, or it might be the strain of the chamber’s weight bearing down in slow, constant pressure. Occasionally, I catch the reflection of an amphibian’s glistening eyes turned in my direction, as though reading that ache in me. Its mouth hangs open, near motionless, a watery droplet perched on its slick lower lip. We regard each other: me, a shapeless caretaker who might hold a secret of why we are stuck here; it, the half-made creature of infinite possibility. Neither of us knows how to finish the question.

I find that I can place my hand gently upon these creatures without disturbing the pattern. When I brush the top of an amphibian’s head, the film of moisture that coats its skin clings to my palm, connecting me to it by a gossamer thread of water. Our existences converge for an instant. A flicker of something passes between us—awareness, perhaps. But then the creature relapses into its slow blink, its half-lolling tongue. I’m left with a hazy imprint of empathy in my chest.

Sometimes, in an unoccupied nook beneath an overhang, I feel as though I am the only thing capable of true stillness here, ironically. All else is forever in subtle, cyclical motion: carapaces shifting, tongues flicking, phosphorescent lumps pulsing overhead. But I—all fluid shapes and half-breath—can hold myself motionless and see if time will pass me by. It does not. Or perhaps, time stands next to me, equally unsure of how to move. 

From that vantage, I watch the luminous pulses on the ceiling—wave after wave, like a silent organ chord that reverberates in the chest. It makes me think of a heartbeat for the entire chamber. The glowing lumps never intensify beyond a certain threshold, never fade out altogether; they remain in their gentle, cyclical hush, as though soothing something. In those moments, I become sure that this place is a womb of sorts: it fosters and preserves every possibility of growth without ever letting it resolve into a single outcome.

In corners where fungus and moss-like sponges cluster, I extend my arm as if to test my capacity for nourishment or healing. I sense I can coax them, but they resist, as though a subtle law here says: “No further. Remain in near-bloom.” And in my half-formed state, I, too, am obedient to that silent command. My desire threads itself into the environment. Fingers that might have guided a spore to bloom or coaxed a larva to metamorphose only pass across the pale fuzz of fungus. The tiny flecks of color remain as they are—soft purples and greens that never quite intensify.

And still, I feel a deep hush in my bones, deeper even than the hush of the cavern. I suspect this hush is older than the crystals, older than life itself in here. In it, I hear an echo of faint chanting or singing—a memory of intention from something else that once passed this way. Perhaps there were makers who carved these initial shapes in the stone, who tried to anchor time differently, or who sought a womb to freeze their creations in a cycle of perfection. I do not know if I am partly the leftover will of those beings or merely a spirit conjured by the cavern’s own contradictory existence. 

What I do know is that I neither hunger nor thirst. The sweet, mineral smell fills me, and the constant damp is a second skin. My tasks, if there are any, are observational: noticing each bead of water, each prismatic fleck of crystal refraction shifting across shells and eyes. I witness how an isopod lifts a segmented leg in slow increments, how a grub glows faintly with pulsing innards, how an amphibian’s glossy eyelid creeps shut with excruciating languor. Everything is suspended between the “was” and the “might be.” 

Am I lonely? The question moves through me. But here, loneliness is an odd concept, because I am at once enveloped by thousands of lives and yet separate from them, touched by the knowledge that I could have been one of them given the right spark of momentum. Instead, I am an amalgam of unmanifested shapes, drifting as a silent observer in this enclosed cosmos. 

I find that when I look at my unformed limbs, I can sense how they would be if time’s forward march resumed. I would have bones that straighten, muscles that anchor, skin that scars and heals. I imagine my face with eyes that see not just possibility but reality in the bright glow of crystals. I imagine pushing open the sealed passage above. But that is not the nature of this place. Here, I remain a maybe, a beckoning question. 

On my best days, I think: I am the caretaker who tends the repetition. I ensure the pulses on the ceiling don’t falter; I steady the isopods when they cluster into thick gatherings; I calm the amphibians when their tongues reflexively snap at insects. On darker days, I feel as though I’m the one under a caretaker’s watch—held in the stone womb, never free to become fully real. Both senses are true, simultaneously. 

In transient moments—those slivers of half-awareness that pass like illusions—I become convinced I see a path leading deeper, where maybe the stone is a bit firmer, where the old works of unknown architects might continue in a corridor that solidifies. But the instant I try to move that way, the path loops back or dissolves in the glare of the crystals. Some silent law refuses me entry. Perhaps that corridor is a trick, or perhaps it is the place where time flows normally, and the moment I cross its threshold, I would vanish from this chamber’s suspended timeline entirely.

And so I remain. I can feel the chamber’s heartbeat in my chest, the hush of unborn possibility humming through every thread of my unsteady shape. My purpose, I believe, is simply to be here, occupying the threshold between motion and stillness. I uphold the loop of existence. I am not the sentinel that prohibits progress; rather, I am the echo that ensures each ripple is witnessed, that no detail is lost. There is beauty in that, I tell myself, even amid the frustration of timelessness.

Should you set foot in this hollow, you might see me gliding between the crystals, half-luminous in the reflection of prismatic light. You might think me a trick of your eyes—a swirl of vapor, a refraction dancing on a stalactite. But if you pause long enough, if you listen past the drips and whispers, you might sense me noticing you. I have no voice to greet you in the usual ways, but you would feel, somewhere deep in your bones, the hush of acknowledgment. You might even feel my fleeting hand press gently across your shoulder in a gesture of welcome. 

And in time, you would wander out again. You would leave, carrying stories or confusion of what you saw. The living tapestry of half-formed creatures would remain behind in their perpetual hum, the crystals shining as they always do. And I, neither complete nor absent, would feel your departure like a subtle, nearly imperceptible shift in the chamber’s breathing. Then all would continue as before, suspended in the hush between possibility and fulfillment.

I am the caretaker of this cradle of half-life. I am its ephemeral observer, drawn from the same stasis that holds the mushrooms and the glistening amphibians, the half-arch and the echoes of unremembered architects. Embryonic, perpetual, quietly luminous: I am the one who remains, watching over a pocket of the earth forever paused in its primordial, echoing dream.
