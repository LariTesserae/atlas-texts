I have no memories of being born, only of awakening into this realm where the water is all around, cool on my plating and smooth against my fronded limbs. The hum has always been there—beneath, beyond me, within me. Sometimes, when I’m very still, I feel it pulsing through every segment of my body, beating softly in my chest cavity, as though the forest and I share a single vast heart.

I glide in the space between monstrous pillars of living wood. If you looked at me from afar, you might think me part fish, part vine, part mechanical apparatus. But in truth, I am myself, a separate being that has grown enmeshed with this place—my plating is flexible, the color of dusky rose shot through with deeper russet. In the watery gloom, it blends easily into the towering trunks whose surfaces bear mottled purples and blues. I do not recall choosing these colors; they simply emerged as I grew, shaped by the rhythms of the forest. Before my eyes and along my limbs, filaments unfurl—fine translucent threads that let me taste the hum’s frequencies, that let me commune with the subtle tides of energy washing through these titanic columns.

When I first became self-aware, I took to exploring the watery corridors. I was smaller then, young enough that a single twist of a trunk’s root system could have engulfed me. I remember drifting in the half-light, discovering pockets of shimmering spores trailing from the spinner-like swarms. My first real meal in this place: I opened what I think of as my mouth—an adaptable seam between overlapping segments of exoskeleton—and sampled the drifting spores. They tasted of starlight and slow, patient lifecycles, a dust of faint luminescence on my tongue. And the hum… it vibrated in me, telling me to move on, to search deeper. It is always there, as essential to me as breath.

I learned quickly that I can fold my limbs flush against my sides, becoming slender as a needle-fish, slipping between the thick vines to amuse myself in hidden recesses. Or, if needed, I can fan my fins wide like a spool, giving me the power and grace to navigate swiftly when larger creatures pass. I possess no illusions about my stature: my torso could fit inside the maw of the towering watchers if they cared to swallow me whole. Even the massive silver behemoths, with their corrugated plating and streaming fluid-cables, dwarf me many times over. Yet, I do not fear them. The watchers do not hunt, and the behemoths simply observe me in their calm drifting, exchanging gentle pulses of color with a flick of their trailing filaments. We are all bound by the same frequency—the great drone that thrums in each of us. Predator and prey seem antiquated terms against the hush-loud constancy of this world.

My place, if I can call it that, lies in the mid-canopies—among giant fronds that spread out from the trunks like living sails. Here, bulbs the size of small chambers sprout regularly, each one a swirl of emerald or turquoise. Within those bulbs trickle tangles of fibrous channels, reminiscent of interior scaffolding—glowing faintly from nutrients swirling inside. Occasionally, I anchor myself against one of these round protrusions, using the tiny hooks at my limbs’ joints to cling. Then I gather small drifting bits of matter—fragmented spores, shards of dissolved minerals—to coat my plating or feed my internal systems. At such times, I can sense how the bulbs themselves feed the rest of the trunk, piping life into leaves that tower miles away.

Time flows strangely here. I can remain anchored to a single bulb for what feels like an eternity, lulled by the rhythmic pulse of the forest’s hum, barely noticing any change in my surroundings—until, one day, I glance around to see how the bulb has grown slightly longer, its hue darkened by a fraction. That near-imperceptible difference stirs me from my trance. “Ah,” I realize, “time does pass.” And then the hum invites me onward again.

Sometimes, I drift closer to the surface of the great roots below—though “below” is a flexible concept in this float-realm. If you examine the root networks, you can watch the squat, shelled wanderers trundling among ridges, siphoning water in soft mechanical slurps. I find them charming in their slow determination, thick-limbed and ancient. In the faint half-glow, the fungal growths on their shells look like tiny, star-dappled galaxies spiraling across their domes. I envy them, in a quiet way, for their patience and assuredness. They never seem to doubt their place. Their one visible eye orbits restlessly, but not with fear—merely curiosity. I’ve found them good company on occasion, though they speak in subtle hum-clicks and watery gurgles I can’t fully parse. Yet it’s comforting to linger with them, hearing how their shell resonates faintly in the same key as the forest’s drone, like another instrument layered into the universal choir.

My favorite wanderings take me among the weaving vines overhead. A labyrinth of living corridors branch out amidst the monstrous columns, each line of vine strung like a catwalk (though “walk” is not quite right in all this water). Yawning gulfs separate them, open areas wide enough to fit entire whales. The vines gently sway, so if you hold on too tightly, you might be flung about as they shift in waterfall arcs. Needle-fish flash by in silver-and-green pulses, vanishing as quickly as they appear. Sometimes, I let go of the vine and gently flip my body so my tail-limb leads, letting the current carry me deeper into the next corridor. I blink the lens-like membrane that protects my eyes, letting in more light, and watch tiny spool-creatures that swirl in thrumming flutters—signaling each other, performing ephemeral migrations from trunk to trunk. Their movement is a dance with no audience other than the forest itself, and perhaps me. They have never seemed to mind my presence or to notice me at all, for I am simply another thread in the tapestry.

Once, I ventured far enough to glimpse a watcher in a half-waking state: a portion of trunk bending outward as a gnarled root-limb. The slow peeling of bark revealed folds of glistening flesh. I saw one enormous eye flick open, multi-lidded in layered membranes. Its iris was the color of molten copper, shot with swirling patterns that mirrored the forest floor below. I do not know if it saw me or if it only saw everything, taking in a million phenomena with that single, patient gaze. At that moment, the hum deepened—subtly at first, then swelling so that it throbbed through my entire form, as if I were a tuning fork. My own plating vibrated, each segment harmonizing with the watchers’ resonance. I quaked with it, a mixture of awe and a strange delight. Then, with spine-chilling grace, the watcher folded its bark-limb closed, returning to motionless slumber. That day, I understood: they are the great custodians of this universe, guardians or perhaps part of the living mechanism that churns beneath the surface, generating this ancient industrial lullaby.

Everything here is shaped by that lullaby. I, too, am shaped by it. I suspect that is precisely why I exist: to wander, to quietly maintain some subtle balance, to glean and filter drifting matter, to keep the cycle turning. Like a living gear in the hidden cosmic engine. Sometimes I sense that each of us—behemoth, needle-swimmer, vine, root, and watcher alike—plays a role in sustaining equilibrium. The hum is the grand blueprint, and we are the myriad forms it has spawned to keep perpetual motion alive.

There is no day, no night, no sense of a beginning or end. Instead, I experience shifting togetherness. The watery hush collapses distance and time into a single, fluid continuum. And so I let my fins carry me from trunk to trunk, gliding past chasms draped in luminous leaves. I graze on spore clouds adrift in the gloom. I bask in the faint shimmer of the crystalline shapes that drift overhead, each unfolding geometry reflecting silent rainbows through the water. In these moments, I feel an inexplicable bliss. No storms might break the calm; no predator’s jaws snap to chase me. The hush-loud interplay is our lullaby: the forest murmurs, the watchers breathe, and we all live in something like a meditative trance.

If you ask me who I am, I might say: I am a child of the hum, a half-organic, half-metal creature that belongs to the watery spaces among the trunks. I am a weaver, a traveler, a caretaker, and perhaps a vessel for the consciousness that vibrates here. My shell plates are not armor but an extension of the forest’s living technology, grown from the same hidden pulses that power the watchers. My filaments are fine-tuned antennae for the acoustic tapestry that resonates every moment. I feed on drifting spores and the intangible energy of that thrumming tide. I do not know if I will ever die, or whether I, too, will one day fuse into a trunk and become a watcher—that might be the final stage of all existence in this forest. But until then, I simply float, dream, listen, and respond to the hush-loud call.

And so I remain—not anchored, not adrift, but an entity gently guided by the perpetual drone, dancing among mile-high ribbons of living architecture, part of a cosmic machinery infinitely old and stable. I do not yearn to leave, for leaving has never seemed possible or necessary. The forest is my home, the watchers my grand and silent teachers, and the hum the language in which we all converse.

I am here, in this endless sea-forest of living columns and thrumming quiet, at peace in the paradox. Over the ages, that might be my entire story: harmonizing with the hush, breathing in the roar, embraced by roots, vines, and silent watchers. If you look for me, you’ll find me in the shimmering gaps between trunk and leaf, body half-curved, filaments outstretched, listening. Always listening.
