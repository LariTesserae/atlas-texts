I imagine that I once arrived as an outsider, stumbling into the chalk-white basin with my throat tight from thirst and my eyes dazzled by the sun’s searing glare. I recall how my first impression was of utter sterility—nothing but chalky shards and an unrelenting heat that flushed my cheeks with dryness. I believed then that I might simply collapse and dissolve into the dust. But in that dizzy, half-lost moment, I glimpsed silhouettes moving between the crystalline bristles, and they carried me into this stony labyrinth of terraces and towers where, against all logic, life persists without water.

Now, I can hardly remember the person I was before. Perhaps I was some desert wanderer in search of mythic truths—or an emissary from a distant settlement who’d heard rumors of a city thriving under the white furnace of the sun. Whichever it was, the reason no longer matters, for I have since become an inhabitant of this surreal place. My old name feels as remote as a mirage on the far horizon.

I dwell in a modest chamber carved into one of the middle terraces of the city. My window—if it can be called that—is a aperture of smoothly worn stone that peers out onto the central courtyard. In the morning, I awaken to the rhythmic impact of stone chisels below. I know from the pitch and tempo which cluster of workers is preparing new blocks for the day’s construction; there’s a quiet camaraderie in the measured ring of their tools. My skin, once soft and prone to cracking in the dust, has become as tough as the sun-bleached bark of the crystal-trees. I have smeared the same mineral paste on my arms that everyone here uses, feel it caking into the lines around my eyes, sense the subtle shimmer it gives off as I move.

After the morning’s first hammer-falls, I rise and make my way to the forging courtyard—a place of blistering heat made even hotter by the forges’ red glow. I have learned the art of metal-shaping, though “metal” here is often a fusion of iron ore and shards of crystal gleaned from the petrified trunks. My favorite part is the moment just before I begin hammering: there is a hushed understanding that all creation here emerges from friction. I stoke the forge with fuel from the twisted branches of long-dead crystal-plants, hearing them hiss and crackle in the flames. When the metal glows white-hot, I shape it with measured strikes, each impact reverberating through my arms and echoing across the courtyard. My shoulders ache and tremble, but it’s a good pain; it is the city’s heartbeat, coursing through me.

No water cools the steel, of course. Instead, I use finely ground dust mixed with gleaming mineral flecks. When I bury the heated metal in this powder, it releases a hiss like escaping breath, and for a moment, I sense the metal and dust forging a kinship—two things that should not meld together, yet do. This is how we craft the hammered plates worn to deflect the swirling grit, or the translucent masks that guard our lungs in the worst storms. And as I work, I notice I rarely labor alone. Others circulate around the forge, carrying fresh lumps of ore or transferring finished pieces to waiting sand-carts. Their presence is quiet but comforting—there’s a deep solidarity in our shared toiling.

Sometimes in the afternoons, I travel beyond the city’s immediate sprawl with a small group of gatherers. We head out where the spines grow thickest, carefully navigating the labyrinth of brittle stems that clack and scrape in the breeze. Though the plants’ edges are sharp enough to slice unprotected flesh, they have become familiar allies to me. I run my fingers gently over their surfaces, feeling the radiant warmth at their cores. I’ve learned to listen for the hidden hum they make—this subtle frequency that crackles and sings in the dryness. We collect shards from the bases or glean loose fibers snapped off by the wind. I’ve come to admire their resilience: in this realm with no water, they somehow flourish on pure light, a secret I may never fully understand.

At times, I sense the glimmering eyes of the desert’s ghostlike creatures as we pass. I’ve caught fleeting glimpses: a wing folding behind a rooftop, a slithering shape diving into the labyrinth of chalk stems. Others pay them no more heed than they grant to dust devils. I find myself wondering if these creatures hold a deeper wisdom about this land. I notice they seldom roam near the city’s heart, as though they prefer the threshold at the convergence of dryness and life. Perhaps they watch us in curiosity or wariness, the same way we watch them.

When the largest dust storms roll in from across the plain, I retreat behind a stone parapet to wait it out. The city’s older dwellers say such storms are nature’s forging process on a cosmic scale—sculpting the region in swirling tempests of orange, white, or pale pink dust. Afterward, I help brush layers of grit off carved doorways and hammered railings, clearing enough that the city can almost breathe again in the sudden stillness. On those days, the sunlight returns fiercer than ever, glinting off the newly polished surfaces. If I step back and look at the city from a high vantage point, it glows as if lit from within—a scaffolding of raw luminescence.

Every evening, as the sun’s harshness begins to wane, I join the gatherings in our open courtyard. We remove our masks, letting the cooler air grace our reddened faces. I see children with dust-smeared cheeks spinning in slow circles, raising faint spiral clouds around their feet. I hear the older inhabitants chanting those deep, tonal patterns that resonate through my chest, leaving my bones gently vibrating. Dancers scrape bits of crystal shards along the stone floor in time with the blacksmith’s hammer-blows, producing a drawn-out keening that braids with the chanting. In these moments, the city doesn’t feel barren at all. It feels like a place brimming with raw, elemental energy—energy that hums beneath every surface, as if the desert itself is alive with an ancient pulse.

On rare nights, I climb an external ladder (fashioned from calcified trunk segments) up to the city’s highest terrace. From there, I stare out at the broad, open basin, where the chalk-plants rustle restlessly under the moon—or whatever faint glow passes for a moon in this place. The temperature may dip, but the dryness never leaves, and the breeze carries a hush that somehow intensifies the sense of presence all around. If I focus, I can sense the land breathing, or perhaps exhaling the day’s accumulated heat. The spines in the distance sway like stony dancers, the city’s silhouettes glimmer with flecks of embedded crystals, and I reflect on how impossible this life had once seemed to me. No one can truly understand it until they have felt that dryness in their marrow, until they’ve forged a bond with dust and sun, until they’ve heard that unearthly screech of crystal forest in a gale.

So who am I, in this improbable realm? I am neither wholly native nor a transient outsider. I see myself as a bridge—someone who has learned to speak the city’s language of stone and mineral but remembers the astonishment of first encountering this place. My old thirst has transformed into a craving for the hum of forges and the communal ring of chisels. My body has adapted: my skin, my breath, even my heartbeats now match the arid throb of wind and dust. I used to think survival required water above all else, yet here, we survive on radiant heat and the brilliance of hammered metal and the swirling storms that carry seeds of crystal from place to place.

I am a maker here, an artisan of the city’s glimmering heartbeat. Perhaps in time, my hair will be completely crusted over with chalk dust, twisted into spiny braids that mimic the flora outside our walls. I may stand among the elders, humming those chanting tones as we share the newest patterns in steel with younger generations. And I will likely never leave, because life beyond the horizon—where water flows—now seems strangely distant and unthinkably soft. I’ve become an inhabitant of sun and dust, forging a new belonging in a place that defies all ordinary logic and thrives on the very audacity of existence.

If you look for me in this city, you might find me at a forge, peering through the shimmering heat with a metal hammer in hand, or traveling between the crystalline trunks at the outskirts with a gathering basket slung over my shoulder. Or perhaps you’ll notice me standing atop a newly risen terrace, watching as the final light of day transforms every surface into blazing gold. There, as the wind roars and the chalk-plants rattle, you’ll see the person I have become: a dweller of the dryness, pledged to an endless dance of stone and sun, forging something brilliant under the blazing sky.
