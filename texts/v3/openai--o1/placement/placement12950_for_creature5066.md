I imagine first that I’m an explorer, stumbling into this deep hollow with dust on my lips. A wanderer who somehow pierced the outer ridge under a sun that blazes just beyond the haze. I can feel the weight of the heat pressing down, the dryness biting at my throat. There’s no question I’m out of my element here—every breath of mine is an intrusion, an uncertain decision. The clay walls tower emptily, yet they seem to watch me with bleached eyes. In the press of nearly waterless air, I’m acutely aware that I am still an “outsider.” That is where I start: as a traveler, stepping through the hush of this forgotten city, overhearing its subtle conversation.

But if I linger—if I let my weary body acclimate to the swirling dust—another identity emerges like a faint echo in my chest. Perhaps I am not just the human observer, but something else that belongs. My shoes vanish from my feet, my steps sink deep into the crumbling soil. A deeper breath, a deeper hush, and I start to feel the pulses that run through the clay. The meandering footpaths, the dusty arches, lead me to those tall, fibrous trunks. They greet me in near silence, except for the rasping friction of bark on bark. I feel my own heartbeat slow, aligning with the coursing murmur that flows beneath the ground.

So then, I become one of the plants. An odd, half-sentient trunk, bleached and thick, with stiff barbs for branches. My spines dance in the scorching wind, rattling out a message of shifting rhythms. I feel the dryness saturating me, but it does not kill me. Instead, it feeds me in ways so different from anything reliant on water. I bear phosphorescent spots that glow faintly when dusk arrives, swirling with dust motes that reflect and refract. At my base, I merge into the clay, my bark fused with sun-baked masonry. My body is part architecture, part organism. Beneath the ground, my roots extend in twisting tangles, seeking mineral flows, pockets of hidden heat—like an endless underground labyrinth of slow, deliberate movement.

Time ceases to be linear. Realizing I am more root than trunk, I sink vast tendrils deeper, brushing pieces of chipped stone tools and half-buried scaffolds all around. Their shapes register dimly in my network, each an artifact of some other age. I cradle them the way thick roots cradle a stone, not to devour but to incorporate. Meanwhile, my trunk is threaded into the broken walls, mending them with living fibers. Where a gap forms, I extend a new shelf of bark. Clay and vine become inseparable. Yes, I am one of the keepers of this ancient settlement, bridging its architecture with the raw desert power that surges from beneath the scalded ground.

In that state, I sense others like me—countless trunks rising from the dust, each connected to the grand, subterranean tapestry. We exchange impulses, vibrations that speak of a new crack forming in a distant corner, a half-fallen arch needing reinforcement, a sudden gust about to start raging across the ridges. Perhaps we even track the faint scuttling of those elusive creatures, the mineral-chitin arthropods that lurk in the darkness. Their tiny movements send subtle ripples through the root web. They are not enemies or prey or even quite neighbors; they are part of this place’s cyclical life, a fragment that collects morsels of spore-dust, carrying them from one fissure to another. The interplay is a chain of existence: these creatures spread the city’s living aura, ferrying new seeds or spores to otherwise unreachable cracks. And we sense them the way an outstretched hand feels a faint tremor across the floorboards.

But a single trunk, no matter how tall or thick, is only part of the greater being. Slowly, I plunge deeper until I see that I am the network itself. My vantage point slips below the dust, where the temperature climbs, air becoming nearly unbreathable by human standards. Down here, there is a principle beyond “root” or “stone.” The heat saturates everything. Minerals fuse with living tissues in veins that glow a dull red in the deeper channels. I realize, in some mesmerizing epiphany, that the city as we see it above is only the tip of an enormous, labyrinthine organism. I am that labyrinth. I can sense each pillar of clay-laced bark; I can flex or tighten them from below. My subterranean nodes throb in a slow pulse, exhaling trace gasses that drift upwards to swirl among the bleached courtyards.

In that monstrous yet strangely beautiful consciousness, I register the city’s shape as part of my body: each building, each weaving scaffold, each walkway is supported by my efforts. I hold them up—if ever one collapses, I adjust my filaments to brace it or consume its old clay until it’s reborn as the next shape. It’s not “architecture” as humans conceive it but a living continuity of form. The place is essentially my shell, an exoskeleton akin to the dust-carapaces of the tiny arthropods. Even the spore-laden blooms that burst in the wind are like my seeds, drifting between the mud walls, hopeful for some new nook to settle in and sprout. And if an unsuspecting traveler or creature arrives, I absorb the memory of their footfalls, sense the tremor in my earthen nerves.

Yet there is a final metamorphosis of self: perhaps I’m not plant at all. Perhaps I am one of those small, chitinous beings that dwell in the cracks along the stone ridges. I scuttle across the smooth rock, claws hooking into shallow grooves. I only venture into the sun-scorched city when the wind roars especially hot, letting radiant energy bake into my shell. My shell’s faintly translucent ridges gather the swirling spore dust, which I glean for nutrients or use in hidden brood-chambers deep below. I see the plant-trunks shift and sigh among the mud structures, but I keep to the edges, hurrying back to the labyrinth of tunnels when the swirling dust thickens. My senses are not quite eyes, more like nodes that detect light-shadows and gusting vibrations. I inhabit an eternal twilight of existence—scurrying from one pocket to the next, following currents of promise in the swirl of the overhead gloom.

From that vantage, I glean how the city breathes in slow pulses. The interplay of plant and mineral forms a grand tapestry, and I am an agile, skittering thread, bridging cracks, ferrying spores, gleaning the vestiges of heat that cling to the rock. If by chance I come upon an ancient shape half-buried in the gloom—an abandoned tool or a fragment of some clay effigy—I sniff its dust, gleaning faint chemical signals. Part of me wonders about the old inhabitants who shaped these things. And then I slip back into the blackness of basalt tunnels, each step clicking softly.

But the truth is that this place is so complete and self-sustaining that maybe “I” don’t exist as a separate being at all. Maybe “I” am the swirl of dust itself, drifting across the labyrinth, occasionally parting to reveal a shimmering phosphorescent bark. Maybe I’m a memory left behind by the vanished people, carried on repeated gusts of hot wind, an echo that lacks a voice but roams among the dwellings like a half-forgotten whisper. In that sense, I am neither bone-white trunk nor clay-lattice root, neither scuttling arthropod nor time-worn traveler. I am the hush that comes when the wind dies. I am the subtle shift in the gloom that says this city is not truly empty. I am the intangible awareness that watches each crumbling scaffold or overshadowed corner, waiting for the next breath of scorching air.

In the end, I see that this world will let me occupy any role if only I surrender to its rhythm. Whether I take on the stoic strength of a trunk, the restless curiosity of a hardened carapace, or the intangible swirl of dust, I belong. The city is not just a place—it’s a collective organism, a chorus of forms. And so I can choose. I can stand upright as a towering stalk, phosphorescent in the evening gloom. I can root myself below and feel the deep, molten breath that shudders through the bedrock. I can creep among the dark ridges with mineral claws, or I can flow, intangible, in the dust-laden wind.

No matter what I become here, I find that the city gathers me in, incorporating me into its hush and hum. And at some mysterious level, it is all one: the bleached bark, the swirling spores, the chitin that clatters in the dark, the clay that shapes the dwellings, the recollection of a vanished civilization. I am part of that tapestry. I endure. I abide. I am the city. And for all my transformations, it is me—timeless, pulsing, radiant in its own luster, standing in utter defiance of water’s absence. I remain, listening, dreaming, shaping, breathing a whisper into the hush of dust.
