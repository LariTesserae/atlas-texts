I am not sure, even now, whether I was born on this plateau or grown from the lattice of vines that line its trunk-bridges. Sometimes I think we Mossweavers simply sprout here, waking one day to the sound of dripping water and the taste of green in the air. But I have an earliest memory: my small hands clinging to a rough-barked root, my wide feet planted on a damp tangle of moss. Overhead, two elders murmured in low, rolling voices—like the hum of distant water—encouraging me to climb. And so I climbed, sliding my toes between knots of vines, feeling the softness of moss cushion my grip. My lungs filled with the scent of new leaves and soaked loam. The wet bark pressed against my face as I ascended, and somewhere above, sunbeams filtered through the canopy in thin shards of pale light. I recall the thrill when I finally perched on a ledge of interlocking branches, free to gaze over a world that was all green heights and trailing water.

I have lived in that swirl of green and water ever since, and I have never felt the need to be anything else. My eyes have grown used to the half-light under deep leaves, my feet have hardened to the damp shapes of living walkways. I’ve come to know each trickle and rivulet, each trunk whose bark forms a natural gutter, each pool that collects among the roots. I walk with my neighbors and friends in an unceasing conversation of calls and murmurs. When I need an extra hand or a missing tool, I do not knock on a door—doors are rare here—I simply holler a phrase that bounces off mossy walls until it finds the ears it needs to find.

People call me Selo. I am a young Mossweaver who studies the art of coaxing living tree trunks into new shapes. When I was little, I thought weaving huts meant tying vines together and calling it finished. I soon discovered there is a gentler, more complex dance to it. We do not force the vines; we guide them as they grow, training pliable branches to curve and intersect at just the right angles. Once, my teacher took my hand and pressed it against the frail tip of a sapling. “Listen,” she said. “Feel the leaf’s pulse. Coax it; do not command it.” Over time, I learned that even plants speak in slow, subtle ways, and that what we do—what the plateau itself does—is all part of one ongoing conversation.

In the mornings, I wander the settlement’s walkways, carrying a stack of flexible vine strips over one shoulder. Dawn on the plateau is often a swirling hush of fog, thick enough that you can only see a few huts away. My footsteps on the wooden beams drum a low, hollow note—water-slick vines beneath me, the damp hush of leaves overhead. The platforms we’ve built around the trunks lead from one “room” of forest to another, crossing over root ridges and stepping through parted curtains of ferns. My job is to reinforce the lashings of huts that might have slackened in the night. Because of the near-constant presence of moisture, anything bound by vine can eventually loosen. So I make my rounds, calling out greetings to families just waking under leafy roofs, grateful for a moment to chat while I check the tension of the walls.

At midday, the plateau is usually alive with sound. Small creatures chitter from the root-level, scuttling in and out of the pockets of forest that people sometimes leave intentionally undisturbed. Birds of a hundred shapes and colors shriek and caw from the canopy, while water droplets fall in ceaseless rhythms, each plink setting off echoes that fade and resonate. Around noon, it’s common to see clusters of folk gathered in communal nooks of the forest, sharing a meal of foraged leaves, succulent mushrooms, and sweet vine-sap. Basket after basket passes from hand to hand. Conversation weaves through the overlapping birdcalls and insect drones like a thread in a tapestry. The children, nimble on vines nearly from birth, flit among the walkway levels and rope-ladders, always generating new laughter and sudden squeals when a misstep sends them sliding into a friendly patch of moss.

Sometimes I help care for the water channels. We carve shallow grooves in the bark high up in the canopy, directing rivulets of condensation into spouts formed from hollowed limbs. These spouts feed our woven cisterns—large, basket-like structures that hang suspended by thick vine ropes. It’s a precarious system but vital: we never know when a downpour will rupture the quiet and fill everything to overflowing. We prefer to be ready, to harness that rainfall and store it for the drier spells. Drier, of course, is relative here; even in “dry” times, the plateau glistens with perpetual dew. Yet we know that water is life, and we treat its flow with reverence. To watch water swirl at our feet is to watch the plateau’s heartbeat, continuous and ever-renewing.

On many afternoons, I drift further from the center of the settlement, drawn by sudden birdsong or a glimpse of something bright in the foliage. Sometimes I’ll settle near an ancient root arch—a natural cathedral where the trunk buttresses rise like living pillars. The light angled through the leaves can become so marbled that the floor glows with green and gold mosaic. It’s not only I who love these places: you’ll see others pausing mid-task, letting the anchored hush wash over them. At times, an elder might begin a chant in the echoing root-chamber, a call that reverberates in the hollows. We need no special invitation; the forest is open to all. Passersby join in or simply stand silent, letting the harmony wrap them like a living blanket.

When evening falls, the mist thickens anew, rising in layers until it swirls around waist-height and obscures the walkways. Resin torches flicker, casting watery light from the huts perched on trunks. People gather by these small flames to trade stories or shell seeds, the rhythmic scrape of flint knives on bark accompanying quiet talk. I often sit cross-legged near one of the hollowed log drums, my fingers trailing in a shallow basin trickling with water from an overhead spout. Now and then, I tap a low beat on the drum’s taut hide, in time with droplets striking the leaves. It’s a simple, primal melody, but it resonates so fully here, layering into the forest’s ongoing chorus.

Nights on the plateau can be thunderous if the wind picks up, sending tidal waves of leaves overhead and great arcs of water spattering down. Our huts sway slightly, but we are not afraid. Our vines hold firm, coaxed as they were from living trunks that will not betray us. I lie sometimes in my hammock of braided reeds, listening to the thunder of beasts passing below, footsteps muddied but sure. The hush-then-roar song of water in the distance—maybe a waterfall newly charged by rainfall—plays serenades in the darkness. My lids grow heavy, and I drift into sleep, lulled by my mother’s old lullaby about how the plateau was once only a naked spire of rock before the forest claimed it. For a moment, I can almost see that time: a lone shelf of stone, lonely and wind-torn, inviting life to root. Then the vines arrived, the seeds sprouted, the moisture held. In my drowsy mind, I watch centuries spin by until the plateau stands as it is now, a tapestry of unstoppable life.

Once, I tried to imagine living anywhere else—even the plateaus below that some of our foragers visit. “Beyond,” they say, “there are open skies, grasses that grow knee-high, places where water is a precious trickle.” It sounded so strange to me, like a story told backward. Here, the water always moves, always hums in your ears. Here, the forest’s breath is your breath; the hush of a passing cloud merges with the rustle of leaves overhead, so you’re never quite certain where nature ends and you begin.

So this is where I’d find myself. High up, cradled by wood and vine, shaped by a thousand droplets. I am a Mossweaver, trained in the art of entreating branches to bend and huts to bloom from living bark. And though my eyes long to see new seeds, new insects, new vistas, I know I am part of this place as surely as a knot in the trunk. My skin is inked green by moss-stains and flecked with mud from the roots. My hair tangles around leaves some days. My laughter weds itself to the birdcalls, and my feet find each crevice in the bark as though they were made for that exact step. In the swirling hush-then-roar of this forest plateau, I exist as a single note in the larger harmony, a living breath among the tangled vines. And truly, I can’t imagine being anything else.
