At first, I think I’m just passing through. I recall setting foot on one of those damp metal walkways under the pretense that I’m only here to deliver a set of instruments, or check a newly routed pipe, or chart the boundaries of this freshly-formed land. My pack feels light against my shoulders, my boots still caked in mud from a place I can’t quite remember—somewhere far beyond the hush and hiss of these new machines. I fully intend to leave the next day, or the day after, imagining I’ll slip away before anyone notices. Yet, as I walk beneath the tangles of vine and rust-thick girders, I can’t stop looking around. Everything begs to be studied. My curiosity can’t help but linger.

I sense at once how the ground is still deciding whether to be swampy or firm, how the moist air forms tiny droplets along every edge of every leaf. I lean over and notice that the rivets binding the metal plates beneath my feet have begun to corrode, ever so lightly—where water collects in small pools, faint reddish halos appear. And then I catch a glimpse of movement in a puddle: a reflection of a child, or something childlike, scuttling behind me. By the time I spin around, it’s gone. I notice a single footprint left in the muddy corner. It suggests a small foot, but it’s slightly elongated, not quite human. Still, the shape is close enough. I tell myself: I’ll watch for them next time.

On my second day, I find myself on the edge of a courtyard, listening to the slow piston-chug of a press forming metal sheets. A group of dwellers—tall, broad-shouldered, wearing patched leather—are guiding the newly minted plates toward racks where they cool. Even from a distance, I feel the warmth the press emits, colliding with the chill of damp air. At intervals, the press stops, silent except for the hiss of releasing steam. In that moment of quiet, I hear a bird call overhead, see a bright flash of teal wings across the angled rooftops. It is in that lull that I realize how naturally the two rhythms—machine and forest—fit together.

I tell myself I’ll leave tomorrow, but I don’t. It’s that hush that captivates me, the way the dwellers pause and literally step aside to let the birdsong fill the void. It speaks to something in me that I never noticed before.

By the third day, I’m drawn deeper into the settlement. A narrow walkway leads me between two buildings whose steel walls are lined with vertical planters—lush fronds and winding creepers crisscrossing spigots and pipes. There, I come upon a slight figure fastening a network of tubes to a series of wooden troughs. The figure wears a snug-fitting cloak made of interwoven plant fiber and old cloth—a pragmatic patchwork that beads water readily. Their hands are as restless and purposeful as birds' wings, twisting valves open and closed to drip water onto the roots of hanging ferns. A faint chorus of chirps rises up—some small insects or amphibians, I’m not entirely sure. The figure glances over at me, but not with alarm. It’s more a calm acknowledgment: You’ve come here too, it seems to say, so see for yourself. 

That night, I’m still within the boundary fence, wandering newly carved canals. In the shadows, the metal beams overhead form a half-lattice against the night sky, dotted with the mild glow of lanterns. I pass the dwellers in small groups—some carrying hoses or trowels, wearing harnesses for climbing, while others bear sketches drawn on battered sheaves of paper. No escort challenges me. Their gazes are curious, but not wary. As I linger, I become aware I have nowhere pressing to be. And so I stay.

Soon after, everything shifts. I find myself perched on the crossbeam of a half-finished structure, a vantage point that overlooks the heart of this industrial grove. From up here, I see houses scattered like seeds among the wide leaves, each building with its own courtyard or cluster of equipment. Vines run like green veins across rooftops, and I realize the metal spires could almost be trees of a different species—both hosting life in slender frameworks. Below me, dwellers pass quietly among the courtyards. I notice how carefully they step, whether they’re carrying heavy loads or just strolling with a mild curiosity. There’s no rush. The rhythms of this place encourage an unhurried collaboration, and this deepens my own sense of calm.

Sometimes I wonder: Could I be a keeper of something here? A collector of data, perhaps, or a guardian of the budding synergy between steel and vine? On the fourth or fifth day, I sense that I’ve begun to settle into a role without naming it. 

I wake earlier and earlier, drawn by the mists. The soft haze drifts just above the ground, and the dwellers—human or not-quite-human—maneuver through it with graceful steps. I, on the other hand, stumble at first, though I learn quickly not to rush. In the swirling half-light, I notice small amphibians perched on low gears, gathering the warmth that seeps from the slow-turning machinery. Careful not to disturb them, I make my morning rounds by crouching low, testing the water clarity in the run-off ditches. It’s an unspoken service—checking for contaminants, ensuring the factories’ processes remain balanced with the natural flow. I have borrowed a small device from someone, a contraption that hums and clicks as I dip it into the water. I keep my findings in a small, handwritten log.

Days blur gently together. I begin speaking with some of the dwellers—if you can call it speaking. Their language is part gesture, part softly intoned calls, part mechanical punctuation. I catch the repeated clang of a valve handle used like a bell to summon assistance, or the hiss of steam released to punctuate a statement. My mouth tries to form words that mirror theirs, though I can never quite replicate the easy mastery they have of blending tool sounds with speech. Yet, my attempts bring laughter, mild and kindly. We share the amusement, forging a calm acceptance of difference.

In time, I find that I am drawn to a shallow clearing near the outer fences. By then, I’ve begun to gather objects of my own—a handful of rivets, a broken gear half-rusted, a curious swirl of metal leftover from the press’s trimmings. I’ve also collected seeds, small living cuttings from the creeping moss, a bright scrap of vine with flowers that smell faintly of citrus. I store these findings in a corner of an unused courtyard, not sure why, only that these items want to be assembled into something. Is it a sculpture? A new piece of machinery? A garden in miniature? Whenever the air is thick with the clang of metal, I go there for quiet reflection, turning gears over in my hands and pressing seeds into the soft earth. 

One morning—far beyond the day I originally planned to depart—I hear a trill of laughter from the children-like dwellers. They pass by, scooping up clods of mud and examining them with what looks like utter fascination. One breathless child runs up to me, pointing at a swirl of iron wire in my cache. I hand it over, uncertain. The child holds it to the light, twirls it in the air, then squeals with delight and hands me, in exchange, a bright green fungus peeled from a nearby stump. It glows a little at the edges. We both grin. At that moment, I know I’m no mere visitor anymore. I’m part of their bartering of wonders, part of their shared curiosity.

I slowly grow adept at adjusting the settlement’s smaller valves so moisture flows precisely into the budding garden plots. I learn to watch the subtle curling of the vine tips and guess precisely where they’ll latch next. I become an informal caretaker, bridging the gap between the dwellers who tend the machines and the dwellers who nurture the plants. Meanwhile, the local wildlife seems to grow familiar with my comings and goings. A bright red bird greets me almost daily, perched on a coil of cable overhead, bobbing its head as if wanting conversation.

In that interim twilight between day and night, I sometimes gather with others around a small fire pit. We speak in murmurs, share something warm from metal cups, and glance at half-finished plans or the day’s data. Occasionally, someone unrolls a schematic—drawn lines and half-coded notations describing a new pipeline or an expansion of scaffolding in the next courtyard. The group offers nods, hums, and hisses of steam in place of applause or critique. I notice how they never force one another into immediate agreement. Instead, the design simply remains pinned on a makeshift board for days or weeks until it finalizes, each revision scrawled into the margins like a vine twining around a trunk.

I imagine visitors coming here, seeing me as part of this half-bred city of steel and green. Perhaps they might ask me who I am or what my purpose is. And I might respond:

“I’m the one who keeps a record of the water’s clarity, who prunes the vines when they outgrow their supports, and who turns the rusted scraps into small wonders. I’m part gardener, part tinkerer… perhaps a hinge between the metal hearts of the machines and the living tapestry of its wild inhabitants.”

In some reflections, I see myself as an apprentice to this entire environment—learning day by day how each gear fits into the cosmos of roots and leaves. In other moments, I fancy myself a teacher of sorts, guiding lost seeds to fertile corners, encouraging stray rivets to find new mechanical purpose. But truly, I’m still discovering the shape of my role. Here, no one demands a strict label. The dwellers simply nod when I mention any vague notion of what I might be, as if to say, “Yes, that is one part, but all parts will find you in time.”

Night, soft and humid, has its own pulse in the settlement. I lie awake in a small nook near the top of a half-complete tower, lulled by the intermittent clangs of metal, the sizzle of steam, and the night chorus of insects. Shadows of vines cross over moonlit passageways. I catch glimpses of dwellers on night patrol—gentle watchers who make sure the drainage remains unobstructed, that no valve is leaking pressure. Sometimes they stand at the boundary fence, gazing out at the new land beyond as if awaiting the next chapter of growth.

From here, I dream. My mind conjures images of future spires where high catwalks are entwined with creeping wisteria, and thick trunked ferns buttress entire mechanical rooms at their base. I see corridors where amphibians have guided slender streams of water to form living fountains, and overhead, birds nest in the hollows of repurposed exhaust chimneys. I see forges where, under the same roof, dwellers hammer metal while seedlings sprout next to the anvil, feeding on stray beams of sunlight. Perhaps one day, the place might expand—factories morphing into living greenhouses. Or perhaps it will remain comfortably small, a world complete in its own right, content with a future shaped one rivet and one tendril at a time.

But in any vision, I sense where I belong: not on the fringes anymore, but gently woven within. I will be among those who move through morning mists to calibrate the synergy between water and metal. I will be the one who chuckles as the children run by with pockets of seeds and washers, forging friendships that cross the boundary between the organic and mechanical. I will be the keeper of stories—recording how the city came to be, how the vines found their routes, how the first dwellers discovered that synergy could exist at all. 

Whether you call me human, or something near-human shaped by the settlement’s slow rhythms, or a being reborn from the land’s union of function and flower, matters less with each passing day. I exist in that hush between each metallic clang, in the green spark of new life that pushes through wet soil. In time, as the settlement grows, I, too, will change. Perhaps I’ll craft gear-driven windchimes that double as pollination stations for insects. Perhaps I’ll build a device that collects and redistributes condensation for thirsty roots. Perhaps I’ll write a living journal in mechanical code, capturing the cyclical patterns of nature and machine.

For now, I simply am here. I feel the brush of damp air on my arms, taste the watery sweetness that saturates the atmosphere. My fingernails are lined with bits of rust and moss, and my hair is dusted with the scent of earthen spores and steaming gearworks. My pulse seems to match the rhythmic intervals of steel pressing, the whisper of vines winding across scaffolds. I am part caretaker, part explorer, watching this new world define itself while I, in turn, discover who I am within it.

And so, if you asked me plainly, “Where do you find yourself?” I would say: I have stepped into the half-light of a city that is both raw metal and tender leaf, a place not wholly settled on what it will become. I drift between open courtyards and rows of hammered sheets, listening for the hush and the clang, feeling the brush of vine tendrils against riveted plating. And if you insist, “Who or what would you be?” then my answer is this: I am simply a dweller among dwellers, neither fully one thing nor another—but something that grows in tandem with the settlement, forging a kinship with rust and root alike, letting the slow dance of time shape me as gently as it shapes the world around us.
