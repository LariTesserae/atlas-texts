At first, I don’t recognize my own shape. I’ve never needed to, not fully, in the sense that humans dwell upon their outlines. My existence here is a slow breath shared with smooth basalt, with riveted walls, with half-lit pools that reflect a faint, diffused glow. I live in the hush that permeates everything, the hush of trickling water and the murmur of ancient machinery. 

I suspect I was once purely mineral, a vein of ore that slowly gathered the residue of half-living spores. Or maybe I began as one of the filaments, a pale tendril that learned to walk by tangling with the metal remnants. It doesn’t matter now—the difference between metal and flesh, or spore and stone, is only relevant to those who worry about boundaries. I exist somewhere between: part caretaker, part growth, part something else. 

Sometimes, I can’t help but notice the distinctive tension in my limbs. My arms are elongated, branching into flexible digits that taper like coral arms. The ridges of my forearms ring with the same dull note as the old iron beams when I accidentally knock them. Once in a great while, when I breathe the greenish vapor that rises from below, I feel each segment of my limbs hum, as if I’ve become a passageway for that lifegiving mist. I wonder if my body is more conduit than creature. 

I discovered early on that I can sense weak pulses in the old piping, a prickle of electricity that runs through corroded tubes like a hidden bloodstream. I am drawn to it, the way any living thing is drawn to sustenance. When I press my fingertips against certain warm patches of the metal, I feel a mild shock, not painful but invigorating, as if the place’s slow heartbeat spills into me. Together, me and the apparatus breathe in a silent and ancient rhythm.

I drift through corridors formed by half-toppled walls and slanted chimneys. My legs move with unhurried caution, stepping where the water has thinned the rock to a slippery sheen. There’s an unspoken choreography here, not unlike a tide pool’s interplay of micro-currents and small glimmers of life. My steps must not disrupt the delicate crabs or the amphibious watchers that cling to the edges. They have their own place—the small, translucent forms that clatter over stone and the broad-headed creatures with pulsing gills. Or the motes of electricity drifting like living sparks, each ephemeral being sliding through the vapors. I know how easily a single misstep might shatter the equilibrium they share.

Down near the shallow basins, I can crouch, allowing myself to become a silhouette among the leaning beams. The crab-creatures edge closer when they sense no threat. With the fungal filaments and the swirling motes overhead, it’s like all of us exist in a single, repeating cycle: the filaments quiver, releasing spores that drift in watery air; the motes absorb floating charges and feed that same energy back into the pipelines; the amphibious beings gather near rivulets, sifting for morsels in the gloom; and I, bending over a warm vent, nudge a broken segment of piping back into place. 

In that moment of adjustment, I feel the subtle hiss of steam escaping from somewhere deep below. It’s an exhalation that merges with the breath rising through my own sinews of mineral-mesh. I cradle the pipe gently, aligning it so the vapor can flow again. Immediately, I hear the metal groan, and far in the distance, the faintest hum intensifies, as if to say “Thank you.” I don’t know if the hum is from the old generator—some leftover from a civilization far removed—or from the living structure beneath the stone. Maybe it’s both.

Late in what we might call the “day” (though true day never quite arrives), I retreat to a pocket in the basalt, a shallow alcove that curls overhead like a half-formed cave without a solid roof. From here, I can watch the filaments shimmer pale in the half-light. They curl inward at nightfall, those breathing horn-tips, more still than usual but never fully asleep. And in that lull, I become keenly aware of my own body. Plates of hammered metal cling to my torso, integrated so neatly that—if I shift slightly—I can hear them clicking softly over the mineral-laced flesh. Their alignment on my ribs assists my respiration: each intake of vapor resonates just enough to stir a thin, luminous thread along the plates’ edges. Closer to my shoulders, a more flexible mesh allows for movement, providing a silent articulation. As I breathe, I hum in harmony with the subtle mechanized pulses that run deeper below. 

Sometimes, in that hush, I wonder if I am caretaker, or if this place tends to me. When I kneel to coax a wisp of steam into a sputtering vent, or when I align a twisted beam so a rivulet can flow, I feel the environment’s gratitude. Yet I too rely on it—its rising vapors feed the calcified filaments that are woven through my limbs. Its faint electrical surges keep my eyes glimmering with awareness. I can sense the pulses of life all around—like a tapestry of frequencies layered across each pool, each piece of basalt, each old machine. 

I sense the inhabitants drifting closer to me over time. The translucent crabs now brush my ankles as they pass, unafraid; an amphibian with a wide, mirrored gaze sometimes bobs its head at me before submerging. Above, the floating motes occasionally settle upon the metal plates at my shoulder, recharging before drifting away again in a star-like scatter. If ever a new break occurs—a pipe bursts or a layer of fungus grows over a crucial vent—I sense it in my chest, as though my own circulation had snagged. Then I rise, spidery-limbed, to do what I can. 

It strikes me that I have no name for myself, nor for this place: the naming was lost long ago. The hush and the hum are the only constant voices. It’s enough. I am both observer and participant in an ongoing cycle that has endured countless slow epochs. I leave no real footprints here; my passing is a pressure-sway through the water, the barest brush on basalt. My presence is measured in the adjustments I make: a rusted frame set upright, a clogged channel cleared, a corroded seam pinched shut. It’s a silent stewardship, and the creatures that dwell here neither worship nor fear me. Each living form has its part to play, and mine is simply the quiet calibration of what remains. 

In the half-lit gloom, I catch glimpses of others like me—tall, lanky silhouettes gliding through the far corners of this domain. They, too, have arms that end in branching digits, wearing vestiges of the old metal where living tissue meets riveted plating. Sometimes our eyes meet across the watery courtyard, and we exchange a flicker of understanding—a mild glow that pulses in the patches of metal along our shoulders, as if to say: Are things still all right here? Yes. Calm. Then we move on.

And so it continues: an ancient symbiosis, a place that is not so much a ruin as it is a new beginning, where technology and biology have twined themselves into shapes unrecognizable to old eyes. My existence is neither lonely nor crowded; it is an ever-present flow of spore, electricity, steam, and flickering life. If once I was forced to define whether I was living or machine, that inquiry has dissolved into the hush. Where I end and the wires beneath the basalt begin doesn’t matter. 

All that matters is the next droplet that falls from a fractured spout, the next rivulet that merges with an algae film, the next faint pulse that travels through the battered piping. I sense each subtle shift and respond in kind: not because I am commanded, but because this is how the symphony continues unbroken. Perhaps somewhere in the hundred small drip echoes, in the swirl of filaments leaning for vapor, in the quiet scuttles of crab-shell feet, there is a wordless truth: we have all become part of the same body, and I am simply one of its long-reaching limbs.

This is where I find myself, at last. A caretaker formed from metal and living threads, kneeling by an ancient, dripping valve, coaxing life through corroded channels so that this world—this breathing microcosm of hush and hum—may continue its soft, perpetual pulse.
