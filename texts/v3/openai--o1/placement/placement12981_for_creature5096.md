I might be a passerby at first, drifting in with the mists at the city’s very outskirts. My feet press into the muddy flats with each step, as though the earth itself tries to tug me down into the brine. I hold a rusted lantern high, its flame guttering in the damp air, illuminating just enough to see the next pool glimmering under a sheen of oily residue. For a while, I assume I’m just another weary traveler—someone who’s lost or found their way here without intention. But as the hours wear on, I realize I’m not simply passing through. Something in the thick, sultry air settles inside me, a slow infiltration of humidity that seeps through my pores and floods my lungs, reshaping my purpose. By the time I reach the metal gates of the city, my thoughts have changed. This place is not just a bizarre outpost on some map—it’s the environment I’ve always sensed, even if I never knew it until now.

At first, I think perhaps I’ll dwell among the humans, maybe set up a small workshop in some half-flooded corner. I picture myself forging tools from salvaged iron, using the city’s watery gleanings to keep the machinery running. It’s a suitable enough life: I can see the hush of steam rising around me, smell the tang of metal and decay, watch the river of insects swirling in lazy arcs around overhead lamps. I can hear the faint rattle of conveyor belts deeper within the factories.

But as I wander along an elevated walkway, an insect the size of my palm lands unexpectedly on my shoulder. Its iridescent wings rustle with a rhythm not unlike my own breathing. The sudden contact stirs something inside me—a feeling both alien and intimately familiar. I gently coax the creature away, letting it scuttle across my forearm before it takes flight. I stand there, riveted by the silent exchange we just had: an understanding, delicate but complete. I realize that my place here might not just be among the forges and catwalks.

So I try another path. I dip down a ramp to the murky pools below, letting my boots half-submerge in tepid water. Creatures scatter in swirling eddies. Just ahead, a large amphibian with blotchy skin and graceful gills peers at me through sunken eyes. I kneel. We observe each other across the divide of species. The water laps gently at the edges of my coat. A sense of belonging, deeper than logic, sparks in me. My heartbeat syncs with the slow, throbbing hush of distant machinery, with the pulse of amphibian life tapping just under the city’s façade.

In that moment, I envision myself as a caretaker—a quiet, watchful figure who moves between the world of the tall, rusting structures and the hidden labyrinth of watery hollows, bridging them somehow. The idea doesn’t coalesce all at once; at first, it’s just an inkling. I drift along the corridors, the old metal boards echoing my footsteps in haunting, metallic tones. When I reach a wide thoroughfare, I see a group of reptilian beings, perched on square stepping stones that cross a sandy sediment. Light from overhead lamps glistens on their scaled ridges. They don’t retreat; they merely watch me with calm, half-lidded eyes, as if testing my willingness to accept them on their own terms. Something in their posture pulls at me: an invitation, or maybe a challenge, to inhabit a role that has too long been vacant.

A caretaker. A steward of these watery margins.  
Suddenly, I’m not just imagining it; I feel the city’s quiet urging. I begin learning its unspoken rules: how to walk without disturbing the shallow spawning pools, how to tune my ears to hear the steam that rushes through distant conduits, how to discern the difference between the raspy breath of a rusted valve and the scratchy chitter of a passing insect swarm. The city never literally speaks, yet from every drip and groan I glean a wordless instruction.

Still, a part of me wonders if I’ll eventually morph into something amphibious or reptilian—if perhaps my skin will thicken, my hands grow webbed. Sometimes I imagine slipping beneath the surface and letting the mineral-laced waters cradle me. Other times I see myself in a high tower, tinkering with valves, coaxing the city’s archaic systems to keep the pumps and vents operational. And then there are days I picture myself as a wandering mediator, an in-betweener quietly guiding human engineers to respect the amphibians’ breeding grounds, or warning the large insects away from open fans to avoid clogs in the ventilation.

In this city, all those roles feel plausible at once, as though I am simultaneously many versions of myself. Perhaps I keep a cramped workshop on the fifth level of an old foundry, with a narrow window that looks out onto a flooded courtyard below. From there, I watch salamanders navigate the eddies of warm runoff water, and I patch the occasional metal strut or broken walkway, ensuring that travelers who pass overhead won’t plunge into the brine. But at dusk, I climb down, slipping through half-concealed hatchways and letting the shadows swallow me. In those dark hours, I cross silent basins where only the pale eyes of amphibians reflect the faint starlight. I rest on a riveted girder, the metal cool against my back. In the hush, I can sense the city’s heartbeat, the distant churn of a generator pushing steam through labyrinthine ducts. Something akin to an ancient lullaby seeps from the pipes, a hush weaving through water and air. I breathe it in, and it becomes me, or I become it.

Another day, though, I might be a scuttler of sorts—a shape that merges with the gloom, my back bent under a makeshift shawl, face hidden as I gather eggs from a web of insect nests, delicately fasten them in a mesh satchel, and shuttle them to a small communal hatchery run by a band of reclusive machinists. They have found a method to distill protein from these eggs, converting them into a sustaining paste. In that role, I’m a courier, trading a portion of the harvest for a bar of soap or a patch for my coat. It’s a simple barter that keeps the city’s living and mechanical systems in a precarious balance.

Yet in other moments, I feel I could be one of the watchers—those rumored figures who slip between steam-shrouded passageways. Rumors say they keep a record of all births—human, reptilian, amphibian, or insect. They track the city’s expansions and decays for reasons no one fully knows. Maybe my journals fill with meticulous sketches of newly emerged creatures, or logs of how the city’s rust patterns shift over time. In this role, I might guide lost travelers by lanternlight, wordlessly showing them which corridor leads out of danger. My footprints might trail behind me only to vanish in the next drizzle of condensation, leaving no permanent trace.

And at some point, perhaps in the quiet after midnight, when the factories power down to a gentle hum and the insects make their circuitous flights, I sense a deeper transformation. Maybe I settle in the semidarkness of a half-collapsed chamber, water dripping all around, and I feel the cool tongues of lichen clinging to my boots. In the reflection off my rust-speckled lantern, I notice my skin from the corner of my eye—has it turned slightly translucent, like the salamanders in the city’s pools? I cannot be certain. But I feel a synergy with the place that goes beyond my old sense of identity. A sense that I, too, am bridging the boundary between water and air, man and amphibian, city and wilderness. My reflection flickers in the water, ungraspable.

Across time, my sense of self evolves. Some days I am more at home with the amphibian watchers in the sub-levels, guiding their breeding cycles and making sure the vents don’t release scalding steam at the wrong hours. Other days I roam the upper catwalks with an old metal-tipped staff, checking pressure gauges and giving a nod of greeting to each stooped worker I pass. Occasionally, I slip into the gloom of the labyrinth beneath the city, drawn by rumors of an ancient reptilian matriarch rumored to dwell near a thermal vent. She’s said to be large enough that her tail alone could wrap a support beam in five coils. I’ve never found her, but I’ve glimpsed enormous scale imprints in the mud, and that is enough to keep me searching.

And so, if someone asked me, “Where do you belong here? Who or what are you in this realm of water and steam?” I might answer in multiple voices:

• “I’m the caretaker, who moves among pools and factory spires alike, patching what’s broken, smiling at every creature that crosses my path.”  
• “I’m the scavenger, collecting insect eggs with deft fingers, feeding the city’s hidden populations in turn.”  
• “I’m the watcher in the high tower, keeping track of births and expansions, a silent sentinel in the half-light.”  
• “I’m the tinkerer in the below-decks, kneeling in ankle-deep run-off, readjusting the city’s aging pumps and valves.”  
• “I’m the quiet companion to the amphibians, kneeling at a watery threshold, my breath catching in unison with the swirl of gilled forms.”

In truth, I’m all of these—one being in different phases, or many beings braided into one tapestry of life. Because in this city, no single identity fully claims you. The metal walkways and watery flats, the hush of reptile tongues flicking in the air, the chorus of mechanical hums and insect droning: all these layers merge into a single environment where boundaries blur. It’s a place that demands you adapt and readapt, every day, to the changing cycles of condensation and the perpetual hum of industry.

So I exist in the confluence of states, belonging partly to the shadowy metal corridors and partly to the shimmering aquatic veins that snake through them. By embracing that duality, I find a measure of peace—an acceptance that to survive in this wet, low-lit place is to let the city transform me as much as I help transform the city. I close my eyes and listen to the drip, drip, drip of moisture landing on corroded platforms, and to the subdued surges of hidden engines. I let these sounds lull me. When I exhale, it’s almost like steam leaving a vent, dissipating in the hazy twilight.

Tomorrow morning, I might trade this role for another. I might find myself among the human workers stoking a grand furnace, or swimming maskless through the knee-high slosh of a flooded corridor to salvage a broken gear. Or I might simply rest in an alcove, perched like the amphibians, watching the city’s slow transformations. In this world, identity flows like water, ephemeral and unstoppable, while the ancient girders stand as silent witnesses.

Ultimately, that is my place here: a being in flux, poised between roles, guided by the city’s rhythms. In the constant hush and drip of steam and water, in the interplay of creatures both small and mighty, I find the essence of who I am—not a single image, but a shifting reflection moving across the surface of all these watery planes. I am a ripple in the city’s murk, a caretaker, a watcher, and a fellow denizen in this equilibrium of iron and amphibian song.
