I find myself arriving first as a ripple in the light—an indecisive shape drifting along the wind-blown ridges. Perhaps once I was a traveler from another land; perhaps I was always here, tucked beneath layers of sun and mist. But in this place, origin feels less important than reflection: the plateau mirrors me as surely as I mirror it. As I step onto the slick stone, I become a being shaped by this realm’s hush and shine.

At first, I think I am only a guest. I hover at the edges, where columns of pale stone meet the sky in sharp angle, waiting to see if I belong. But as the days pass, I notice how the droplets cling to my hair, how my skin grows used to the perpetual drizzle, how my eyes start to catch subtle changes in the mist. Even the shifting gusts no longer surprise me—I learn to bend, to press myself against the rock when the wind thrashes, feeling the raw power roll overhead.

Little by little, I let the place redefine me. My cheekbones grow more pronounced, as though they rise to greet the biting wind. My lashes lengthen, strong enough to bead with droplets and keep my vision free. I find that my voice has settled into a soft, low hum that carries quietly between close-standing huts. I trade in my old sense of dryness for garments that are always a little damp but never quite sodden—layers of woven fiber, wisps of hide, and ribbons of hammered metal that catch the brilliant expansions of light. 

As I meld into the life of this settlement, I sense a role calling to me: caretaker of the water. Though water is abundant here—it drips, condenses, flows in labyrinthine streams—its shape is ever-changing. I learn to watch the runnels that carve tiny canyons in rock. A single shift in wind can redirect a drip from one cistern to another, risking overflow or dryness. Following the lead of the elders, I take on the job of balancing these flows.

I walk the perimeter of the settlement daily, crouching by ice-flecked pools and listening for the faint hiss of water trapped behind rock. With a small, flattened bone chisel, I loosen pebbles that clog channels, guiding the water to weave once more past homes and into stone cisterns. On cold mornings, I brush away thin crusts of ice that cling to gutters, freeing the water to move. There’s a sense of gentle collaboration with the very plateau, as if I am a quiet hand adjusting an instrument forever on the verge of playing a discordant note.

In time, others in the settlement know me by the shimmer of droplets that cling to my layered cloak—little beads shining in the watery sunlight. They also hear my quiet greeting as I pass: a single, nearly voiceless hum that acknowledges their presence without fracturing the hush. This hum becomes my resonance with them, a small swirl in the swirl of all else.

My daily tasks bring a rhythm to my life: awakening at dawn, stirring a small stew from leftover scraps of the night hunt, then heading out to check the channels. My footsteps on wet stone become sure and silent; I too have learned to pad lightly, aware of every shift underfoot. When the wind howls, I press my back to the nearest wall as if greeting an old friend. Sometimes I close my eyes and let the sudden spray of fine droplets pepper my face. There, in the uproar, I sense the heartbeat of this place.

Occasionally, I help gather the tough grasses grown on the outskirts—grey-green tufts that can be stripped and woven into cloth. My fingers are calloused from braiding the fibers into fine threads. I watch as the bright-eyed children turn the weaving into a game, trying with laughter in their eyes to keep each strand from slipping on the moistened ground. Their large pupils reflect the swirling weather, a kaleidoscope of ephemeral shapes. They remind me that, for all its hush, this is not a somber place—its quiet simply shelters a kind of hidden exuberance.

Sometimes, I join the elders at a weather-worn stone ring just beyond the settlement, where silhouettes of upright pillars stand like watchers. We observe the sun’s track—though it’s difficult to mark precise lines when the light glitters off the mist, shifting angles into illusions. Still, we notice the final pivot of the sun’s ray at day’s end, or the faint turn in the wind just before the next shift of season. We note it all in memory, and the knowledge we gather moves through us softly, like the little rivulets beneath our feet.

As I go about my work, certain creatures accompany or observe me. The small, wide-eyed animals that skitter between boulders—they’ve learned not to fear my presence, for I am slow and careful with my steps. The sinuous herd beasts move across the plateau in silent waves, their hides shimmering in the bright haze. When a particularly treacherous windstorm breaks, they may press in near the settlement, and I help guide them—taking up a gentle vantage, arms spread, voice low, turning them from any hidden crevasse or drop. Later, if the community must hunt, I join the ritual that offers a portion of water back to the plateau in thanks, pouring it gently into a shared cistern while chanting in hushed tones.

During the calmer hours, I settle near the communal pools, dropping a tattered line into the depths, fishing for translucent crustacean-like morsels. As the lure dips below the surface, I watch, entranced by how light refracts in the swirling water, creating twin illusions: one part reflection, one part the real shape beneath. There’s a lingering sense that even I am partly illusory—my outline shifting as mist wafts through. I feel at peace in that half-real, half-luminous state.

And in moments of rest, I listen for the plateau’s deeper music: the groaning of the stone beneath hidden tensions, the shuffle of hushed footfalls, the patter of ceaseless droplets. This subtle symphony enwraps the settlement. There’s a hush that is anything but empty, a hush pulsing with promise. It reminds me that here, in this high realm of drizzle and unblinking sky, everything is always on the verge of becoming something else—like water that does not freeze yet never fully warms, like the brilliant sun that can vanish in an instant behind the swirling mist, leaving only hazy rainbows in its wake.

I do not name my role beyond caretaker or dweller—here, permanence is an illusion, and names matter less than harmony. What matters is this: I remain, shaped by the plateau’s quiet tumult, bound inextricably to the winds, the watery arcs, and the bright hush. And so I stay, a guardian of small streams, a silent witness to the illusions of light on every surface, neither visitor nor native anymore, but simply another voice in the chorus of dripping stone.
