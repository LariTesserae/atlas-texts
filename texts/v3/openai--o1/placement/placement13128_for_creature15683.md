I find myself suspended in a halfway place: not quite in the damp depths below, not quite in the swirling heights above. My home is a narrow studio perched on a ledge within the mid-level terraces—a precarious alignment of walkways and steel buttresses. If I look upward, I see layers of catwalks, rooftops, and spindly towers lost in the mountain mist. If I gaze downward, I catch flickers of movement in cramped alleyways half-choked by vines. Everything here feels as though it is about to shift—slowly but continuously—like a breathing creature of metal and stone.

I have been here for as long as I can remember. Perhaps I was born in one of those lower gullies, amid the tangle of industrial hum and dripping water. Yet sometimes I feel a quiet pull from the ancient monoliths at the city’s outskirts, as if my ancestry is older than I can fathom. It’s a vague sense of belonging that I can’t easily explain, a sense that some earlier presence shaped the rock before human hands arrived with their scaffolding and machines. And so I hover: half a citizen of ramshackle industry, half a watchful pilgrim drawn to the primal heart of these ridges.

I work as a caretaker of sorts. By day, I help harness the ephemeral water that trickles from the ridgeline whenever the rains come. I’ve studied the gullies and chasms, learning which become torrents after a storm, which remain stubbornly dry, and which carry a delicate seep of water for only an hour at dusk. My job is to collect that water in battered copper cisterns, then channel it through a series of narrow pipes. These pipes feed small rooftop gardens—I’ve built three so far—and a handful of deeper greenhouse alcoves that cling to the city’s vertical edges like barnacles on a ship’s hull.

Each greenhouse is its own experiment. Some are enclosed with clouded glass and sealed with thick rubber strips, capturing enough warmth to sustain heartier vines and grit-loving succulents. Others are open-air, letting the swirling mountain wind pollinate spore-laden ferns and lure in tiny, droning insects that help the cycle continue. Over time, I’ve grafted certain species to endure the cold, splicing them with creeping algaes that thrive in the mists. Tucked behind each greenhouse is a small workshop stacked with splintered wooden planters, coils of coir rope, and sacks of pungent compost. It smells of water and decaying greenery—and I find the aroma comforting amidst the city’s brash perfume of rust, grease, and smoke.

In these spaces, I also conduct quiet studies of the fractal patterns formed by the vines on cracked steel plating. The swirling shapes evoke half-remembered scripts etched in the older monoliths. When the first watery sunlight of morning hits those etched patterns on the rock faces, I sometimes see lines that resemble curling roots or spiraling symbols. As though the mountain itself once spoke through these lines, and the vines have learned to echo its language. Some nights, I can almost hear the soft hum of growth, a resonance that seems to come from the rock itself, making the metal beams vibrate ever so slightly under my palms.

When I’m not tending the gardens, my duties continue in the pumping stations. The city relies on a labyrinth of repurposed pipes and hand-welded conduits. Sometimes these tangle in hidden recesses behind old masonry and scaffolding, and I must clamber through tight corridors, gripping an oil-lamp in one hand and a wrench in the other. I’ve memorized the city’s subtle rhythms: the hush of certain corridors, the faint reverb from below, the hiss of release valves. My hearing has become unusually acute—I notice the faintest change in pitch or volume. If a pipe has begun to clog or leak, I might pick up on the difference three levels away before anyone else hears it.

I’ve made friendships in passing. There’s a machinist who lives in a converted furnace chamber—a big fellow who always smells of hot grease and metal shavings. We trade goods whenever I pass through his sector. He offers me newly forged brackets or pivot joints to reinforce my greenhouse frames; in return, I bring him herbal infusions made from the minty creepers that thrive best in the cooler gullies. We don’t speak much—our voices are subdued in the hush of the city—but there’s a quiet camaraderie in the exchange. 

I share a mild rivalry with one of the scholar-engineers who occupies a rickety tower that tilts a bit to the west. She studies the patterns of rainfall and tries to forecast when the ephemeral waterfalls will appear and vanish. She insists there’s a calculable formula to it all, involving atmospheric pressure and layered geology. I operate more instinct than math—my sense of the water is born of time spent crouching among the dripping vines. We meet sometimes at the cusp of a new storm, exchanging brief glances that convey equal parts curiosity and mutual respect. Then she’ll pull out a battered notebook filled with data points, and I’ll remain silent, trying to feel the shifting current of the wind. We both record our observations in our own ways—hers meticulously graphed, mine in half-finished sketches of water flow and root growth. Eventually, we’ll share what we learn, though neither fully accepts the other’s perspective.

When night settles—when the city’s weak bulbs glow amber along the catwalks—I climb higher up, wriggling through an access hatch that leads to a short walkway rarely used. From here, glimpses of the highest ridgelines come into view. It’s a private vantage I’ve claimed for my own, a place above the everyday bustle. Gusts of wind tug at my oilcloth cloak, and the hum of the city’s machinery fades beneath the hush of the sky. On good nights, the moon breaks through the cloud cover, and I can see the ragged silhouette of the mountains in every direction. In those instants of silver illumination, the worn spines of rock look like ancient ribs. The scattered scaffolding and towers become a glimmering city suspended within a giant fossil. It humbles me. 

Sometimes, if I linger there long enough, I spot faint lights. They drift across a far ridge or hover over a distant outcrop. They’re not fireflies—too large, too deliberate in the way they move. I’ve never quite deciphered what they are. Perhaps illusions of mist and moonlight; perhaps watchers from some other age, returning to float among the ravines. I’ve thought of trying to approach them, but any path would be impossibly steep, and I have no real guarantee they’d remain if I got closer. So I simply watch, with the hush of the city below me and the press of the mountains all around.

I’m neither an outlier nor a typical resident. I’ve grown proficient with spanners and blowtorches—there’s no surviving here without understanding the mechanized skeleton that holds the city together. Yet I’m also an archivist of sorts, collecting subtle signs of the land’s deeper memory—patterns in root filaments, swirling loops in water-lain sediment. I own a small table crammed with battered journals, each labeled by season, stuffed with pressed leaves, sketches, bits of stone etched with puzzling marks. I’m perennially on the cusp of unearthing some knowledge older than the city’s own history. But each clue only unravels into more questions; it’s a puzzle I’ve come to love more than fear.

It isn’t always peaceful. I have glimpsed tension in the city’s underbelly—strained arguments over whose sector gets the freshest water, or which family holds rights to a newly discovered ore seam. Occasionally, the hush of metal and rock seethes with conflicting agendas. But even in the throes of dispute, there’s a collective awareness that we need one another, that this precarious existence in the high ridges can crumble without cooperation. When the storms hit, or the gearworks jam, old grudges are set aside. We band together, hoisting tarps, shoring up buttresses, funneling water where it’s needed. I’ve found myself forging alliances with folks I barely knew, leg muscles burning as we scramble to brace a sagging walkway. There’s an unspoken bond in that struggle.

I wonder sometimes if I’ll remain forever. The terrain beckons, and the city’s changing rhythms hold me in thrall. On certain evenings, as I listen to the distant, low grind of the factories, I feel an echo within my own chest, as if my heartbeat aligns with the city’s slow mechanical pulse. In other moments, while brushing lichen from an ancient carving, I feel a stirring like I’m tracing something from a past life. These moments spark a longing in me—longing for understanding, to piece together the city’s old riddles and maybe find a thread that connects it all: the monoliths, the fractal vines, the ephemeral water, the half-glimpsed lights from beyond.

If asked, “Who or what are you?” I might reply that I am a bridge, though not a literal one of steel and rivets. I dwell in the in-between: between nature’s quiet infiltration of carved stone and the city’s humming machinery, between old script scrawled on ancient monoliths and the new expansions hammered into place day by day. My life is a reflection of this place, both caretaker and observer, part dreamer, part doer.

So here I remain, in a precarious world of jugged ridges and swirling gloom, carrying water across catwalks that creak in the wind, coaxing tender vines to flourish in the shadow of rusted girders, and scribbling notes on patterns I suspect may hold secrets older than any of us. And in that fragile balance—between the hush of shifting rock and the hum of machinery—I find my purpose, shaping a life that is at once wholly part of this city and yet always listening, always searching for the echoes of what lies beneath.
