I might first feel it as a taste of thin air on my tongue—cool, almost metallic, with the faint undercurrent of wet stone. It arrives in a half-formed dream: me standing at the rim of a newly carved ravine, the sky so vast I can’t help but sway. My mind isn’t a single shape yet, but I sense that here, in these wind-chiseled heights, I have only ever known the world as cliffs and ledges, as precarious footpaths clinging to crags. If I look at my hands, they’re wrapped in coarse woolen gloves, the outer fabric scratched from the hair of an alpaca-like beast. My steps are slow but confident on uneven rock.

Then the image shifts—like I’m both young and old at once, part of a lineage that starts in some communal courtyard carved from the mountain, a place where goats bleat and the embers of a hearth keep everyone warm. But I can also feel a pull toward solitude, glimpses of hermit huts perched on spires. Neither identity is fixed, so I wander, letting the land itself suggest what I should become.

At times, I see myself among the watchers: individuals scattered in huts so remote that the wind is the only companion to our whispers. I imagine that in these cracked-stone dwellings, we keep track of changes—a new dries of lichen on a ledge, the shift in the color of the clouds that hover at the horizon. Our days blend with the hush of the land: I roam from one rocky outcrop to the next, scrawling lines in a ledger made of bark and bound with sinew, trying to capture the subtle transformations in the rock’s face. Perhaps I harvest a steady supply of shrubs to feed a small kiln or keep a worn drum that resonates in harmony with the mountain’s hidden pulses. When night drapes the peaks, I tilt my head skyward and watch as pale stars bob above the ridges, listening to the heave and sigh of slow winds.

But in another vision, I’m in the market plateau, in the swirl of voices and the shuffle of hoofed animals. My shoulders carry bundles of newly hewn stone blocks, bringing them from the chiseling yard to where the masons wait. I feel the strain in my muscles, the hiss of my breath as I climb. Maybe I am a stonemason’s apprentice, one who’s come from a distant outcrop because I showed an early knack for shaping stone corners true and snug. I’ll squat with the older masters, learning how to angle a chisel just so, how to sound out each tap—tock, tock, tock—listening for a note that says the block is nearly free of flaws. In the thin air, every strike echoes in a crisp staccato. I discover satisfaction in the slow transformation of raw rock into a sturdy pillar or a carefully balanced lintel. The settlement depends on these pillars, on each join in the scaffolding that crawls along the cliff face, so my work feels essential. Every morning, I kneel in the dust, passing my calloused fingers across fresh carvings, feeling the swirl motif reminiscent of morning mists.

As time flows—decades, maybe generations—another possibility emerges: I could be the caretaker of the great condor-like birds, or hawks that circle the peaks. Imagine me in a spindly structure perched near the settlement’s crown, where thick beams meet near eye-level with the circling raptors. I would keep watch for eggs or wounded fledglings, building rope nets to support nests in the spaces where the cliff has sheared away. My daily chores involve climbing narrow ledges with small satchels of scraps for the half-tamed birds, coaxing them to accept an easy meal so they’ll keep returning, a tenuous bond of trust. Their enormous wings fan me with gusts of cold air. On lucky mornings, they glide so low overhead that their shadows flicker across my face. Over time, the lines around my eyes deepen from peering into sky and stone, and the birds come to know my presence as an unthreatening shape among the spires.

There’s yet another pull: perhaps I’m a weaver down in the busier enclaves, where the thick wooly coats of our beasts become cloaks and blankets. My hands love the texture of raw fleece—its musky scent and its warmth—and I find beauty in tying the spun threads into patterns that reflect the jags of the ridgeline. I sit in an open-air weaving hut, wooden frames around me, the warp threads taut, my shuttle bouncing gently. There’s a calm in the cyclical rhythm: beat, pass, shift… beat, pass, shift. The watery slivers of sunshine sink into my shoulders as twilight edges in, and I can see the bright sparks of lanterns start to glow across the network of cliff-hung dwellings. Sometimes, children scamper by, weaving between chairs, and I pause to call them over, showing them how to manipulate the threads. Their laughter resonates in the corridors, then is carried off by the hush of the wind.

On certain nights, my mind drifts yet again—this time, to the watchers who live beyond the main settlement, those who record the memories of the mountains. Slowly I realize I resonate with them. A small hut built half into the rocky face, a brazier crackling inside. I have stacked a row of clay vessels, each marked with symbols that correspond to weather patterns or the comings and goings of creatures. Sometimes, a bleating goat from a passing caravan reminds me I am not alone—that people from the plateau might occasionally climb out this way to deliver supplies or glean my knowledge of a new fracture line in the cliff. During these visits, I share a quiet conversation, trade them a new carving—a swirling shape that describes a phenomenon I saw at sunrise—for fresh bread or a spool of thick yarn. Then they descend again, and I remain, suspended between rock and sky. I see the real power of the place in these solitary hours: the hush, the sense that I’m tasting geologic time in each breath.

Eventually the dream of identity swells to hold all these forms—watcher, weaver, caretaker of birds, stonemason. My spirit in this world has become a shifting presence—like the clouds the mountains cradle, I take the shape that is needed, merging with the land’s slow, patient transformations. And that, in some sense, is who or what I am: not any single occupation or role, but a living chord between the place and its people. At dawn, I might be hauling a load of clay from the ravine; at midday, I’m helping a friend lash timber beams to create a new walkway; by nightfall, I’m perched beside my brazier in a remote cabin, etching a memory of the raptors’ shadows onto a piece of soft stone.

Where do I find myself in this world? Everywhere. Or, at least, I pass seamlessly between vantage points, as though the entire settlement network is my home. Perhaps I have no fixed dwelling but am welcomed in many corners, from the workshops of the potters to the ridgeline huts of the watchers. In the basket-weaver’s stall, I pause to swap a story of the day’s drifting mists. In the open courtyards, I share a meal of roasted roots and fermented grain, exchanging glances with old friends as we savor the shared effort that keeps us all alive here. Older folks might call me by a name that means “one who wanders the wind,” while children might know me simply for my patience in telling them stories under a star-sparked sky.

And in the hush of a high pass, I let the geologic newness of this land wash over me—the jagged ridges, the abrupt fins of naked rock. The air raises goosebumps on my arms. Occasionally a drizzle sets in, beading across my woven cloak. It’s a place that insists on humility: the mountains are not entirely tamed, and each stone ledge demands respect. Here, I do not stand above the land; I am woven into it. My footprints merge with goat tracks, my breath merges with the swirling clouds, my body merges with the press of the settlement’s daily rhythms. In this, I learn to move at the pace of the mountains’ own slow transformations.

So perhaps I am a wanderer of many trades—a half-weaver, half-mason, part-watchman, part-storykeeper. Slowly, I realize I do best when bridging the clusters of settlement to the wilderness beyond. The watchers whisper that the ridges shift imperceptibly, that new cracks appear in the vertical face. The potters show me fresh clay gleaned from a newly revealed basin. The stonemasons ask me if I’ve heard the watchers’ updates, for they fear building on a fault line. I carry these messages like a breeze, crossing swaying rope bridges that link settlement to settlement, feeling the boards bounce underfoot as I shuffle past donkey-like beasts, pausing to greet them with a reassuring pat.

In the end, that is who I am here: a messenger of the mountain’s slow language. Whether I’m shaping a stone block, weaving a protective cloak, or perched high above the spires to watch condors ride the airy currents, I live at the threshold where the hush meets the murmur of life. Sometimes I see my reflection in a trickling waterfall, a faint shape layered by the swirl of water and the flicker of sky. Other times, I catch a glimpse of my own footsteps etched into the dust, a sign that I passed by, though the wind will soon soften those prints. Such is life here. We are all ephemeral, drifting like clouds. Yet for a while, we leave enough trace—an angled block, a woven pattern, a caretaker’s watch—that the next generation can build upon our slow, patient design.

Tomorrow, I’ll wake with the light drizzle on the rooftops, breathe in the crisp air smelling faintly of evergreen, and remember how it feels to exist so high above the broader world. Whether I kneel in the communal courtyard to share a morning meal, or whether I greet the rising sun from a lonely hermit’s perch, my presence is integral to this mountainous realm. And so I make my place as part of the slowly forming identity of the land itself, woven into every stone pathway, every note of staccato speech, every swirl of a weaving pattern that echoes the spines of the peaks. I am this high country’s child—by craft, by watchful gaze, by footsteps along the ledge. Here, I live and breathe with the mountains, letting every sharp ridge and every hush of wind shape me into something timeless and new.
