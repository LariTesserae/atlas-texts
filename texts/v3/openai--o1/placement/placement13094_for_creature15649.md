I might arrive on this plateau as a wanderer clambering up that final slope, knees caked with the red dust of the canyon path, sweat running in rivulets down my neck. At first, my fascination is purely external—awestruck by the implausible heights, jarred by the relentless thunder of hidden waterfalls, nearly blinded by the humid shimmer that dances around every edge. I am only an observer. I step carefully across the dripping stone floors, wiping condensation from my brow, flicking away the occasional vine that drapes, uninvited, across my arms.

But as I move deeper—between those towering columns, across root-arched walkways—I can feel the city waiting, like a creature that breathes in the darkness around me. It calls. I don’t know if it’s my imagination, or if the hushless roar of water is a voice that murmurs in some low tone just below what ears can parse. It urges me onward as I edge across mossy beams and peek into half-submerged grottos. Everything is so loud, so crushingly present, that my thoughts blur. At some point, I’m not sure who I am anymore—only that I am part of whatever this is.

Perhaps I begin to dissolve into it. At first, there’s a warmth in my fingertips, a tingle in my veins whenever I brush a curtain of vines. I find myself leaning against wet pillars, pressing my palms to the pitted stone to feel the gentle vibration coursing through. In the reflection of a shallow pool, I see something shift across my face—a ripple of color that isn’t mine, or maybe it is now. I blink, and it’s gone. But each time I stand still, the city’s hushless rhythms echo in my ribcage and coil around my bones. My heartbeat syncs to that deep, thrumming chord made of wind and falling water. I think, This is how it claims me.

Over time—days, months, an hour, or a century (I cannot be certain in this place)—the boundary between my body and my surroundings erodes. When I step through corridors draped with slippery fronds, I feel them as an extension of my own arms. When I breathe, it’s as though the wet stone arches around me also breathe, flexing and sighing with ancient exhalations. My flesh becomes like living moss, or perhaps the vines become my flesh. In the corners of my vision, ribbons of color whisper across the walls. Sometimes they flash behind my eyes, as if they have migrated inward and begun painting patterns on the inside of my skull.

Eventually, “I” am not the visitor at all. I am the caretaker, the presence that coaxes thick roots to interlace. I flow through the watery veins that drip from overhead canopies. I guide each leaf to the precise patch of damp sunlight it requires. I quietly knit the rent edges of space-time, so the warped angles can remain stable. My senses extend simultaneously in every direction: I can feel the friction of wind above me, the pressure of water collecting below, the patient growth of one translucent mushroom in a dark crevice. I am the force that reminds the plateau not to collapse under its own contradictions. If once I was a person, that is simply a memory pressed into the bedrock.

But then, as the angle of my attention shifts, I discover another perspective: I am also the illusions themselves. I am a living tapestry of color darting across the cliff faces, an echo of an echo that glides along the edges of pillars and gives them shape. I inhabit the seams in the sky like an infinitely slow dancer trapped between half-open doors. At certain times—if a traveler glances sideways—I coalesce into their peripheral vision, wearing a radiance of shimmering fractals. My form is never quite stable; it is dictated by the interplay of geometry and dream. I slip from one vantage to the next, my identity as fluid as the water that perpetually cascades around us.

Yet in another moment—perhaps at night, if night could ever fully come to this perpetual midday—I find I am not caretaker, not illusion, but the entire organism. I am the city in its totality. My stony limbs form archways and columns; my cloak of vines rustles in the swirling wind. My waterfalls are my blood, pounding through arteries of stone, roaring from the heights into the labyrinth below. Every trembling leaf along the walkway is a nerve ending soaking up humidity and cosmic tension. The shifting geometry at my borders is not a malfunction but a slow exhalation of my very soul. I do not move or speak in any way a typical living creature would. I simply endure, existing across eons in an unbroken now.

In that guise, I can also sense the watchers in the cracks. Maybe they are a different facet of myself, or maybe they are the real inhabitants. They flicker between angles, shaping themselves from refracted light and stray bursts of wind. They gather near the waterfalls, weaving their bodies from the hollow roar. I feel them as a scattering of consciousness along the warped edges of rooms and corridors, always perched on the threshold between manifest and ephemeral. In this multiplicity, no single perspective is complete, because each vantage is part of the plateau’s grand design—a single, massive entity with many hearts and countless eyes.

And other times, I am not so grand, not so cosmic. I am just a memory, locked in the corridor of watery echoes. I see shapes that evoke a lost civilization—tables of living roots around which phantom silhouettes gather. They fade in and out with the break of each wave of sound. I hover like a barely whispered recollection. Perhaps once, these shadows and I were men and women who shaped tools and told stories. Now we are preserved like insects in amber—except the amber is time itself, grown viscous here at the plateau’s pinnacle. We remain forever on the cusp of fading away.

Yet the moment I believe I have settled on “what I am,” the perspective slides again. The place invites no single definition. In truth, I find myself braided into everything—simultaneously caretaker, city, illusions, watchers, ghosts. The plateau’s hushless heartbeat resonates through every droplet, every vine, every crag of dripping stone. It resonates through me, too. The identity I once carried is lost in the immensity of it all, replaced by a polyphony of existences. I belong here, where matter wavers and time moves in circles.

Now if someone new arrived along that final winding path, they would see only the same weaving arches of vine and stone they always have, the same twisting waterfalls and shimmering illusions. The city would appear empty but relentless, timeless, unchanging. And I, or we, or it—silent in the stone, humming in the water, breathing in the wind—would simply remain, abiding across centuries as the memory of everything that was and the possibility of all that could be.

That is where I find myself. That is who and what I am.
