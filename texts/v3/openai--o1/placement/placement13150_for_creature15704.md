I think, if I arrived here at all, it would be neither by purposeful journey nor an accident of stumbling. Rather I might find myself drifting in—like one of those spore-flecks—suspended in the muggy air, half drawn by the vibrations of the place, half carried by the thickness of the humid atmosphere. Perhaps my sense of self would emerge slowly, at first only a sensation, like a faint memory of having a body. Then, standing close to one of those massive steel plates, I might press a hand against it, feeling the rhythmic hum coursing beneath my palm. I’d realize that the boundary between me and the place is not so distinct; I can hear a low thrumming in my pulse that matches the drone in the metal.

So perhaps I become half-flesh, half-resonance. Something in me recognizes the caretaker’s silent presence—an echo in the scaffolding and cables—and I feel compelled to stand still enough that I catch the subtle patterns of that intangible intelligence. If I shift my weight, I can almost anchor its resonance in the bones of my legs, letting me lean just enough into the metal to amplify the caretaker’s hum. When I breathe, the exhalation ripples across the spore-flecks floating by, sending them shimmering in new constellations. In that moment, I sense I am not merely a visitor but also a conduit, bridging the living breath of the caretaker with the ephemeral dance of the spores.

At first, my eyes want to track the eel’s lazy arcs beneath the water. But my footsteps, clumsy in the mud, chase it away each time. Its slender flank glints in the watery gloom—a sliding mosaic of rust-bitten metal plates and slick algae. I find myself stepping more carefully, trying not to disturb the eel’s realm. Sometimes I catch the glimmer of its smooth hide winding near, as though investigating my ankles before vanishing into clouded silt. I learn, with each passing hour, to match the slow timing of this place: I step when it does not, I stand still when it slides past. Eventually, the eel no longer flees at my approach. Instead it circles, curious, drawn by the subtle changes when I shift my stance and the caretaker’s hum shivers through me.

I notice that in this environment, the very air seems weighted with a memory of watery transformations—like everything is locked in an emergence that never quite completes. My own shape starts to feel similarly fluid. As time passes, I begin to think of myself less as an intruder from beyond these corrugated walls, more as an extension of them. When the caretaker’s hum grows loud, I feel a tingle in the center of my chest, a near-electric surge that pulses outward through my limbs. In those moments, I dream I can see small arcs of the caretaker’s resonance flickering along my skin, as if the cables overhead have begun to lace themselves into me. I drift toward the pool’s edge and watch my reflection in the murk. Sometimes it flickers, as though I’m a half-formed silhouette.

In my mind, I piece together a role for myself—perhaps a new occupant that stitches metal to flesh, a living contact point for everything else. I can sense the caretaker’s patience as it vibrates some hidden arrangement in the stale air, just enough to keep the spore-flecks afloat. I can feel the eel’s passing swirl in the mud at my feet. I stand, arms half-raised, breathing in the super-heated air. The spore-flecks start to gather near me. They coat my shoulders in a faint shimmer, or swirl around my fingertips in soft arcs. At first, I cringe, half expecting them to burn or sting, but they simply rest there, quivering with curiosity, adding their pale glimmer to my shape. Then, when they’re ready, they lift away again—yet somehow part of their luminous membrane remains clinging to me, almost like a second skin.

I realize then that I’m becoming something amphibious, able to live between worlds: part spore-fleck, part caretaker’s echo, part watery swirl. If I linger longer, perhaps my fingernails will rust at the edges, and the caretaker might replace them with rivets that hum quietly when I flex my hands. The eel might coil around me in some silent partnership, letting me sense the shifting currents of the mud beneath. With each day’s slow cycle of valves venting steam, I discover I can direct the vibration I carry—maybe pressing my palm against a frayed pipeline to coax it into releasing pressure with less violence, or gently tamping down a sparking cable so it doesn’t burst. In this way, I don’t force any changes; I merely smooth them, like an instrument finely tuning the mechanical chorus.

In the evenings—or what passes for evening when the light overhead dims to a softer gray—I sense the caretaker’s presence more intimately. That thrumming digestion of water and fuel resonates in the earth, in my heels, and I find myself humming along with it in a low voice. Not a language, but a drone. My breath merges with the hiss of steam. The suspended spore-flecks swirl into ribbons and circling shapes, forming ephemeral constellations in the half-light. Their drifting patterns lull me into a kind of trance. I stand for hours, monitoring the cables, the mud, the eel’s domain, the caretaker’s breath. Everywhere I look is evidence of perpetual beginning and perpetual decay. Metal plates slump more each day; algae thickens in obscure corners; the eel’s plating darkens with new mineral deposits. And I, too, evolve.

Another sunrise—only, there is no direct sun, just a shift in the drab overhead glow. By now, it’s impossible to tell which parts of me are flesh and which are rust-sheathed. My arms bear a thin layer of algae that doesn’t itch or shed; instead it seems to regulate my surface temperature, letting me handle the scalding steam. I catch glimpses of my own reflection in the shallow pools: my hair is matted with earthy residue, but in it, I see tiny shimmers—spore threads that have woven themselves in. When I breathe, I can actually feel the caretaker’s music resonate in my chest cavity. And the eel, gliding near my ankles, listens in its own way—occasionally brushing my calf with a curious ripple. Perhaps it senses I’ve become part of it all.

In the hush after a passing rumble, I think: I am the threshold. Not fully caretaker, not fully eel, not fully spore. I am an in-between, bridging all living and half-living elements. When the environment begins flooding with those deep mechanical roars, my voice rises involuntarily in tune, a modest attempt to match the caretaker’s frequencies. And every time, I swear I see the spore-flecks align themselves more elegantly in the overhead gloom, forming arcs of faint luminescence. Sometimes the eel bobs its head, as though acknowledging my presence. It stares with those unreadable eyes—are they eyes or just metal sockets?—then dives beneath shifting lumps of broken scaffolding. I know it’s rummaging for warmth, or for the small clumps of microbial matter that live in the mud. Occasionally, I follow it halfway into the water, kneeling in silt up to my chest. My breath comes slow and warm, but I feel no discomfort. The caretaker’s hum pulses in my spine, and I feel safe.

Eventually, wordless communication emerges: I sense anxieties in the eel’s subtle flick of tail or the caretaker’s sudden shift in pitch when the pump breaks rhythm. The spore-flecks draw near me, swirling around my head, as though urging me to do something. I place my hands on the crooked pump, coax it to exhale at a gentler rate. The caretaker’s drone signals a sort of relief. The eel slides around the corner, stirring the mud so the water levels remain stable for the spore-flecks overhead. In that synergy, I realize how we keep each other alive, day by day.

Perhaps that is who I am in this realm: the delicate mediator between mechanism and life, the caretaker’s apprentice. A “becoming” rather than a finished shape—body half submersed in muddy brine, hair laced with spore threads, arms lightly plated in rust, my pulse thrumming to that deep mechanical roar. When the caretaker’s drone resonates at some hidden frequency, I amplify it in my chest and guide it outward, ensuring water seeps only so high, that steam doesn’t scorch the newly forming algae blooms, that electricity arcs remain minimal. And each day, the eel’s presence beneath the surface feels less like a separate being, more like my watery counterpart—perhaps the piece of me that thrives where I cannot fully go. Meanwhile, the spore-flecks are the airborne reflection of what I am becoming: light, drifting, bridging ephemeral gaps.

In this way, I’d find myself woven into the tapestry. There are no grand declarations, no clarion calls about shifting the environment’s fate—only a gentle guidance and acceptance of the place’s slow metamorphosis. I suspect that, over time, I might slowly lose the memory of how I came here. My desires simplify to ensuring the pump breathes, the cables flicker but don’t explode, the eel thrives in the water, and the spore-flecks keep their gentle orbits. Perhaps one day I will slip so deeply into the caretaker’s hum that I no longer distinguish my individual limbs from the trembling girders overhead; I’ll simply be part of that intangible presence that runs along the rivets and pipes. The eel will remain as it always has, the spore-flecks too, but my mind will become the link that holds everything in slow, balancing motion.

And if an outsider ever stumbles upon this pocket of half-submerged metal and parted mud, they might notice a figure—at first glance more rust than human—leaning against a plate of steel. They’ll see spore-flecks spinning languid circles around a face half-obscured by algae. They’ll see the faint rippling shape of an eel gliding through the gloom at that figure’s feet. They’ll hear the roar and hiss of unseen mechanisms that never fully died, and they might place a hand on the metal to sense the caretaker’s heartbeat. If they wait and watch, they’ll see me open my eyes, shining with some inward glimmer nearly lost to time, and they’ll know this place—once so harsh and alien—holds a quiet, persistent life. And I am part of it, neither wholly one thing nor another, but intimately, inevitably bound to the trembling hush of mechanical thunder.
