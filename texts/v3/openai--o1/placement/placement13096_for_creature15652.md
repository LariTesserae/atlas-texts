At first, I thought I was merely passing through—I arrived at the plateau exhausted from the climb, with my throat raw from breathing thin, icy air. The wind tried to push me back at every step, as if testing whether I truly belonged. But there is something unspoken in these jagged heights that sets root in a person—an invitation whispered through the whistling cracks in the ancient stone walls. Within a week, I knew I would not leave. Or perhaps the mountain decided I should stay, and I simply surrendered.

I am not sure I can call myself one of “them,” the dwellers in this wind-swept courtyard of stone, but neither am I an outsider anymore. I have grown accustomed to the hush that precedes each squall, the sudden blasts of cold that rattle my bones, and the strange luminescence that sometimes graces the ridges at dusk. My fingers can trace the worn paths through the settlement with the same ease they handle the woven fibers we use for clothes. When the few others pass by, we nod in greeting or exchange the simplest of words—the language here is as spare as the air.

Sometimes, I stand at the ledge where the plateau drops away into mist and stone canyons. The view is so vast it unsettles the mind, and the wind is relentless, tugging at the layers of cloth draped over my shoulders. My pulse thrums as if I might be carried away by a gust. But the mountain never quite lets me fall. Instead, it holds me in a precarious balance between fear and awe. I lean forward, feeling the swirl of air on my face, and in it I hear echoes of the ancients who fashioned these blocky huts—stone upon stone, misaligned yet enduring, no mortar to bind them but the hush of centuries.

At dawn, I join the silent gathering to collect water. We cradle shallow cups beneath grooves cut into the rock’s surface—a cunning arrangement devised by hands centuries ago, or so the older dwellers say. In that early, silver light, we move as one organism: minimal conversation, a nod, a faint smile. Someone passes me a carved ladle to guide the water into an earthen jug, and I return it with a small gesture of thanks. Our breath mingles in the thin air, coiling like smoke. It feels intimate, almost sacred, though few words are spoken.

Later in the day, I might explore the half-hidden tunnels that burrow under the plateau—dim passageways lined with etched symbols. I carry a shallow clay bowl of glowing moss to light my way, a practiced tradition I’ve learned from one of the keepers. Down there, each footstep resonates with an otherworldly echo. I run my fingers along the ancient carvings: spirals, glyphs shaped like drifting clouds or perhaps the coils of ferns. I cannot interpret them fully; they hold a secret language known only to a few. Still, I sense their meaning hidden just beyond my reach, like a melody you nearly recall.

In the afternoons, I sit at the edge of a communal hearth—little more than layered stones forming a circular pit, a handful of smoking embers in the center. It surprises me how warmth can accumulate in even the smallest flames here, as though the raw cold intensifies the fire’s gentle heat. We gather in pairs or small clusters, discussing the color of lichen or whether the last hailstorm damaged the roof beams. Now and then, I catch hints of older tales: the presence of great raptors in an earlier age, or a time when travelers from below passed regularly through. But these stories shift like windblown sand, changing each time they are recounted, half-lost in the swirl of memory.

Nights are the most mysterious. Long after the embers fade, I rest in my cramped hut, listening to the groans of timbers as the wind rubs them against the stone. Sometimes, eerie calls echo from the cliffs—deep, resonant tones that make the hair on my arms prickle. Others claim they belong to watchers who roam the higher ridges, guardians shaped from the mountain’s own breath. I haven’t seen them, except perhaps in fleeting silhouettes I can never be sure aren’t tricks of moonlight. Yet hearing those calls, I do not feel fear; there’s an odd comfort in them, as if the place itself were humming a lullaby.

There is a rhythmic pulse to life up here—a daily conversation with the cold, the wind, and the hush between storms. I weave small pouches from fibrous vines, idly stitching patterns that mimic the ferns and moss around us. I’ve planted a few seeds in a stone grow-box, hoping for new shoots come next rainfall. Whatever I do, I sense this land is attentive. It notices small movements, large changes, the difference between reverence and carelessness. When you live here longer, you begin to feel the mountain’s subtle reciprocation. Sometimes, a sudden swirl of fog envelops my hut just as I yearn for solitude. Or a gentle beam of sunlight slips through a gap in the stones, warming my hands when the wind cuts especially close.

What am I to this settlement, then? A bridging presence, perhaps. One who arrived from somewhere far below, yet found a belonging in these heights. The others never asked why I stayed. There is no concept of “why” in their quiet acceptance. They only show me, in small gestures, how to keep breathing, how to layer the cloth in the biting cold, and where to set a jug to catch the last trickle of mountain water. I learn from them how to listen for the stones shifting at night, how to read the glimmer of the sky’s sudden lights—perhaps a cosmic tapestry, perhaps the spirits that have always danced along these peaks.

Over time, I have come to see myself less as an individual visitor and more as a cell in the plateau’s living body. My footsteps follow the centuries-old grooves in the rock. My breath mingles with the swirling wind, becoming part of the call and response echoing off the cliffs. The huts, the wind, the watchers, the drifting lights—everything participates in a grand, unspoken ceremony of existence. In that ceremony, my role is simply to attune, to witness, and to endure. And so I remain: neither master nor servant, but a humble point of awareness in the eternal swirl of ancient stone and unceasing wind.
