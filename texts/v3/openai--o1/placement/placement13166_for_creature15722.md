At first, I assume I am a wanderer—someone who’s stumbled here by chance. I feel the crunch of jagged gravel under my feet and the wet, clinging weight of the wind against my clothes. I’m sure I am still human, at least for a few moments.

But the place doesn’t let me stay as a mere onlooker. I linger too long, absorbing its constant motion—the roar of wind scraping stone, the serpentine crawl of vines—and something shifts at the edges of my awareness. The fierce newness of it all breathes into me, until I can’t separate myself from the push and pull of growth. It’s as if the hollow’s damp air has soaked into my very pores, and suddenly, I can’t recall which pile of fractured rock I climbed over or where the cleft in the cliff face behind me disappeared. My boundaries become porous. My pulse seems to align with every scrape of grinding boulder. It’s dizzying, terrifying, thrilling.

And then I notice something: I am not shaped the way I was. The stony walls feel closer, not as obstacles but as supports. My arms—were they arms?—stretch outward. My fingers feel extended, not into separate digits but into fibrous lines that cling to the cracks. The swirling wind that used to pummel me now billows against my rough bark-skin. I realize that the vine net bridging from one spire to the next is partly me, or I am partly it. A tangle of living rope, anchored in the stone and fueled by moisture. My old sense of a torso collapses into a weaving trunk of ropelike stems that wind and knot. My breath is the gentle inhalation of dew that seeps into my bark. 

Yet this transformation is only the beginning. Some part of me becomes the rich bed of moss, spongy under ephemeral pools of rainwater. My awareness hums in that low emerald carpet, each droplet shimmering on the velvety surface. Because the moss is me, I can feel how each tuft angles itself just so to capture the faint luminescence filtering through the watery gloom. There is no single vantage now—my vantage is diffuse, interwoven. Where once I looked up and saw pillars of stone, now I am the vine that braids across them, the fern that rustles at their base, the cloak of lichen devouring the stone’s mineral secrets one slow breath at a time.

I can’t stay a single form; I shift constantly. Sometimes, I am the giant pitcher plant with its yawning mouth, craving what little morsels might tumble in. Then, with the next gust, my consciousness scatters into thousands of drifting spores, each one spinning off in a swirling chaos of wind, only to lodge in a far corner of the cleft. There, in the quiet moment between gusts, I settle as a nearly invisible seed. When the next surge of moisture arrives, I germinate, burrowing a faint root hair into the freshly cracked rock. I can almost hear my first cell divisions popping and fizzing, winding me deeper into the stone, drawing nourishment from the wet grit. In the same breath, I extend upward as a pale shoot, tasting the gloom-light.

All the while, in every shape, I am part of this place’s thunderous music—the scraping, the groaning, the hissing of steam. It resonates through me as though I’m a taut string in a colossal instrument. My leaves flutter in the wind, strumming a chord that merges with the bigger chorus. The generosity of this realm is that we all become instruments together: the vines thrumming, the ferns rustling, the loose slabs crashing, the steam vents sighing, the seeds spinning, all in glorious, chaotic arrangement.

And at times, I find myself as the stone itself—my silent solidity interrupted by tectonic restlessness within. I sense the ike of magma far beneath, the barely cooled seams of molten rock that stiffen and crack. There’s a deep internal pulse, a ground-level heartbeat. Where the stone face splits, I can feel vines—my vines—fingering their way along the fresh rifts. There is no conflict here between stone and growth. We all share one abiding momentum: to coalesce into a new shape, a living cathedral in flux.

Perhaps, if I try to name my nature, I’d call it a steward, or an engineer, or just a presence with no single body. I belong to every level: moss and leaf, vine and pitcher, basalt and iron-laced ore. I am the seething engine of wind and steam that each living branch harnesses to scatter seeds. In every sudden downpour, in the rattle of rain across broad flapping leaves, I feel my own thirst slaked. And when the deluge stops, I steam quietly into the swirling air.

So, in answer to “Where do I find myself? Who or what would I be?”: I am of this place now. The moment I stepped into this half-lit world, I ceased to be a traveler and became another strand in the tapestry. I have no single, static existence here—I’m a shape-shifter of root and rock, of spore and leafy canopy, of drifting vapor. I reside in the whiskered cracks of newly minted geology and in the soft lichen that blankets its face. I’m the battered vine hooking around jagged edges, bridging one spire to the next. I’m the ferns that uncurl at dawn and coil at dusk, dancing to the thunderous factory-hum of wind. And I’m the drifting pulses of steam that break into the air, bearing the subtlest hint of smoldering magma below.

At times, bits of me break away—a seed on the wind or a chunk of moss dislodged by a gale—yet I never vanish. I find a new home in the next rocky ledge, or the next shadowed corner where condensation collects. Part of me might slip back into the narrow cleft, discovering fresh cracks in the bedrock to seed. Part of me might ride the swirling clouds that snag the high spires. No matter the path, the momentum of living growth endures. Boundaries blur in this jagged cathedral, and I am the blur—multiple forms striving, adapting, amplifying our collective symphony. That is where I find myself. That is who, and what, I am.
