I am deep within the cave, born of it, shaped by its echoes. My first memories are of the roar—an all-consuming thunder that was my lullaby, my teacher, and my warning call. I grew up in one of those huts that cling to the stone, half-woven from the long roots of the pale ferns, half-plastered with thick mud. We call these huts “k’tallen,” or “bowers” in our tongue—or something close to that. In truth, language here is more about gentle hums and occasional clicks in the dark, so any naming is tenuous. I only stumble on words now, thinking back on them.

I was a small child—thin-limbed and wide-eyed—when I first felt the hush in its fullness. Not the hush that is silent, but the hush that is a presence, a roar so great it presses against the inside of your skull. When you’re born here, that hush is like a cradle: it surrounds you, seeps into you, fashions your heart to beat in tandem with crashing water. My mother told me I once nearly crawled off into the darkness, following some subtle shift in that thundering pitch. She caught me by the waist as I disappeared behind a curtain of roots. I remember the air was damned cold there, even colder than near our hearth. I would have been lost forever.

We survive by listening. Our entire village—no more than four huts nestled around a long curve of stone—depends on reading the hum of water. My earliest chore was to help gather the palm-like leaves that sprout where the stone cracks enough to let moisture and faint light inside. Our elders say each leaf loves the trickle of condensation more than the dim glimmer from above. My small fingers were a perfect fit for slipping between the ferns, loosening the fibrous stalks. We wove them into wraps and mats, wove them into walls, wove them into baskets for carrying slivers of luminous fungus. Those leaves are the lifeblood of our dwellings.

As I grew, I learned the labyrinth of roots that run behind our huts: the hidden corridors and half-collapsed passageways where salamanders dwell and slick-shelled crayfish scuttle. Sometimes, I’d sit for hours in the gloom, letting the roar lull me to near-sleep, waiting for a salamander to step out from under a wet stone. Our clan prizes these creatures for food, but I was simply fascinated—utterly smitten with the dapple of faint bioluminescence near their spotted skin. I’d follow them deeper into the blackness than I should, taking daring steps on stone ridges polished smooth by ancient floods. At times, I swear the salamanders led me to new patches of fungus or hidden pockets of soil where the biggest leaves sprouted. Or perhaps I only imagined they guided me. In the caves, imagination bleeds into reality so easily.

There came a day—perhaps I was ten or so—when the pitch of the thunder changed. Not a mere shift but a deeper note, as if something vast had loosened beyond our walls. I recall the entire village falling silent at once, every elder’s head bowed. My mother placed her hand gently on the cave wall. We listened for a very long time. With no sun or moon, we measure time by how many times our slow-burning fungus torches must be replaced. We changed torches thrice while we waited for the shifts in tone to settle back into the usual roar. Then, as one, we exhaled. A crisis had been averted. The unseen waterfall would not crash through our chamber. We were safe—for the moment.

That night, the memory-keeper came. He was ancient to my eyes: draped in thick mats of woven vines, half of his face pressed with a chalky white paint. He carried our staff—a long rod of polished driftwood, crowned with feathers that trembled in the chill. At intervals, he rapped the staff against the stone in a rhythm that blended with the underlying thunder. The entire village gathered around his slow tapping. Words are rare among us, spoken only when necessary, so his staff served as language itself. He taught me to listen to patterns in the echoes, to catch subtle changes in pitch. We call it “speaking with the water.” Through those sequences of taps, I came to understand more about the land we dwell in than any scattered conversations could convey.

Eventually, that memory-keeper chose an apprentice—me. I was to follow him where the chamber’s edges blur into narrower passages and side corridors, places where the fungus is scarce and the roar can be almost piercing. We skirted broad pools where the black water whirled ominously. We made small offerings of leaves to protect the glowing shelves of fungus, gently removing the dead patches so that the living parts might thrive. Some nights, we ventured to the far side of the cave, searching for new rivulets or collapsed channels. “Watch the water,” he would whisper in low clicks. “It is never still. Its voice is the language of our home.”

Over time, I came to see how the cave shaped us. Our skin, once wholly human in color, took on a ghostly pallor, dotted with small areas of moss that find footholds in the folds of elbows or along damp hairlines. Our eyes, though, never grew large. Instead, we learned to feel the stone with every sense other than sight. The staff was our guide, tapping gently on rock, sending vibrations we could follow. We are not blind, but in near-total darkness, the glowing fungus is often enough to see by, if you learn its subtle luminescence.

Between lessons, I honed stone tools. Our clan crafts them with infinite patience, chipping away at the same place for hours. A flaked edge that might slice a fish or carve a root must be shaped carefully, so it doesn’t shatter in your grasp. I observed other villagers, burly or wiry, going about their tasks with that same meditative care. They wove rope from soaked vine fibers or pressed mud into the gaps in our hut walls. Each movement was unhurried. The cave demanded it.

Eventually, I, too, carried a staff. Not the same staff that the memory-keeper used—that belonged to him alone, a relic of past generations—but one I shaped from a fallen trunk of some subterranean wood, drifted here who-knows-how many years before. I tied my own tokens to it: bits of lichen that shimmered like trapped stars, small bones from a salamander, a single bright-blue feather from a lost bird. My teacher nodded in approval, reading my story in those humble ornaments. 

And so now, I follow in his footsteps. When the thunder drops an octave, I gather the clan near the largest patch of greenish fungus. With gentle taps of my staff, I share my warnings or reassurances, guiding them when it’s time to fortify the huts, to rest, or to stay awake and watch for changes. I lead quiet journeys out to gather fallen roots. I read the trickle of water that runs along the stone, tasting it on my fingertips to know its source—has the runoff changed, grown bitterer, or carried more silt? Each detail tells me if the cave itself is shifting.

At moments, I recall a faint dream of bright daylight or open skies—like a memory inherited from ancestors who might have walked the surface. It comes to me as fleeting, something ephemeral and not entirely believable. Perhaps the old memory-keeper once whispered of such things. But we all sense that the cave is our real home. I’ve never felt the sun on my face, nor do I truly yearn to. The thunder is my sun, an everlasting presence around which we set our routines and devotions. Below ground, we have discovered a different kind of luminosity, a slow glow that emerges from fungus and the pale, unwavering spark of human endurance.

I have learned that time here is measured in growth: a child's hair lengthening, a new vine creeping across a damp boulder, a patch of algae blooming on the underside of a rock ledge. Our days and nights begin and end with subtle pitch-shifts of the water’s roar, each cycle an echo of the eons that carved out these chambers. There is humility in living so close to unstoppable forces. The rushing torrent is unstoppable, yes, but we have found a way to exist beside it—touching the dark, listening to every resonance, gleaning the smallest bits of nourishment from leaves and mushrooms and the occasional fleeting creature.

You ask who or what I am? I am one of the watchers, a caretaker of memory and tone, shaped by the hush that is anything but quiet. I belong to a lineage that has married breath to water’s constant thunder. I bear the staff, I listen to echoes, I guide others through the blackness. My footprints blend with footprints from generations before me, and I suspect that long after my bones decay into the damp soil, another caretaker will walk the same slick stones, staff tapping in time with the roar.

I am a human child of these caves no longer, but something more: a being practiced in slow patience, in hushed communion with the ancient violence of water on stone, in reverent gratitude for the faint, emerald shimmer of fungus. I live in the gloom and the glow, in the drip and the thunder, perched right at the meeting place of root and rock. Like the others, I exist at the pace of the cave and will fade in the same hush—never truly leaving, only merging back into the roaring dark that birthed me.
