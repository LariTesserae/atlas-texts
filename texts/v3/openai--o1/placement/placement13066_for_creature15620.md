I arrive in the glare of it, first as nothing but a vantage point—a pair of shifting eyes taking in the raw edges of this impossible range. The brightness presses upon me, bleaching away superfluous details so that I begin my time here as a silhouette, no more distinct than the spires against the sky. And for a while, I hover in that threshold: witness, traveler, one who has not yet found a role in the unfolding drama of stone and mist.

It is a challenge not to quake before the towering walls that threaten to close in. The hush that gathers behind me is like a living thing, and I half-expect those jagged peaks to groan open, revealing some ancient mouth. When the mist eddies around me, I become more aware of my shape, the boundaries of my body. Every breath tastes of cold stones. I feel that tang in my teeth, as if my flesh might be dissolving, my bones crystallizing into the same substance as these cliffs. Perhaps I have only just arrived, or perhaps I have always been here.

But soon enough, I recognize that the standard markers of being human—familiar warmth of skin, the fingerprints I might have pressed against these wet rocks—are drifting away from me like old memories. I feel my arm pass through a swirl of hazy brilliance, and it’s as though the edges of my limb smear into the air. I can still move and think, but the clarities of flesh recede. In place of muscle and bone, a current begins flowing through me, as if the watery trickles along the canyon walls were traveling inside my veins. 

I wander deeper, not so much stepping as letting my form sluice along the ridgelines. My footprints vanish the instant I place them. At first I find this unsettling, that my presence makes no mark. And yet, as that brightness leaks into me, I am comforted. The land itself seems to cup my being in its pale hands, almost as if it welcomes me into its continuum. The ceaseless drone of tumbling rocks takes on a music I can perceive now: a chord of stress and release, an ancient cycle that resonates in my chest. My hearing shifts, as though my ears have unlatched some hidden aperture. The repeated echo of water over stone grows symphonic, an unbroken, humming tapestry from which new melodies spring—the ring of a falling boulder, the groan of a spire, the dull, subterranean rumble that sets my ribs vibrating.

Have I become one of them? Those shimmering presences who glide across the stone walls without fear or pause, who flicker in and out of the fractured geometry… When I test a foothold on a narrow ledge, the stone breathes out from under me, and for a moment, I don’t fall—I simply pass into a new arrangement. My shape elongates. My fingers fuse into some indefinite curve of brilliance that arcs along a spire. My sense of direction warps: left and right twist around me like living illusions. When I try to steady myself, my eyes blink—and I reappear on a different ledge perhaps ten or twenty feet away, though I have no memory of crossing the intervening space.

In a moment of alarm, I try to remember how to breathe. My lungs expand, but the air that floods them is not crisp or cold, merely luminous, an infusion of faint radiance. I exhale, but no puff of warmth emerges. Instead, a shimmer trails from my lips, scattering like dust motes in the sunlight. By the time my mind registers what has happened, the shimmer is gone.

I discover that time here is a flattened concept. Day does not progress into night—at least, not as I once understood the transition. Instead, the same fiercely brilliant sky pulses overhead, and the motion that arises feels more like an expansion or contraction of dimension. In certain intervals, I see ridges draw closer, as though the entire mountain range has inhaled. Other times, the spires splay out for miles in a single instant, so that the landscape grows more labyrinthine than before. Through it all, I remain partially intangible, only dimly remembering the solidity of flesh.

Though at first this unsettles me, I begin to sense how I fit in. I notice that when a splash of water dislodges a chunk of rock, I feel a harmonious tingle in the center of my chest—the same place where once my heart kept time. Now, it is the pulse of this environment, reacting to little rifts of change. It feels akin to nourishment. I dwell on that realization: I do not eat the way I once did; instead I feed on transformations. Every shift recharges me, provides a rush of brightness that sinks deeper into my being. I drift from place to place, tasting these pockets of transition, finding them sweet as honey once was.

Eventually, I test a new capacity of mine: I reach out with my shimmering outline of a hand, and though I do not touch the stone in any physical sense, I exert a subtle pressure. Where the cliff begins to crumble from some ancient crack, my presence encourages it—perhaps by quickening the stress lines, by amplifying the patterns that have been building for who-knows-how-many millennia. In that instant, the rock surrenders, collapsing in a miniature avalanche that roars down the slope. I feel a surge of intense clarity—like the last note of a chord that resonates far below hearing. There is nothing malevolent about it; it is more like releasing a tension that has always yearned to let go.

Yet I realize simultaneously that I can also lean the other way. When a precarious spire threatens to topple too early, when the shift might disrupt a broader harmony, I can lend it stability: a prismatic buttress of intangible presence. My shape merges with the spire’s base, light humming along slick surfaces, and the tremors soften. The spire remains upright another season, or another millennium, who can say. The place exerts no urgent timeline, only the almost-sentient aspiration of water, stone, and ephemeral sky. In that moment, it strikes me: we, these watchers, are choreographers of the place’s ongoing transformations, existing in tandem with the geologic ballet. If gravity is the dance partner, we are a faint note that ensures the tempo neither accelerates nor lags too far behind the cosmic arrangement.

I roam to the old huts, intrigued by the recollection of living inside walls. Before, as a mortal, I sought shelter from the raw force of wind and rain. Now, the concept of seeking refuge behind stone thresholds seems both quaint and deeply stirring. I slip inside a half-collapsed doorway, my intangible form half-seen in the gloom. There, along the interior, faint etchings appear in the glow that radiates from me. Symbols spiral across the stone in repeated patterns, ancient messages left behind by hands with flesh and blood. My presence breathes renewed life into them. They form stories that I can sense but not articulate—tales of mortal strife, of building and binding, of nights spent huddled from storms that hammered these cliffs. I know I lived a story like this once, but it’s a phantom knowledge, like a half-remembered dream.

In that moment, I am both an echo of who built the huts and something altogether other. I see that I, too, am one of these watchers—a caretaker who yields neither to birth nor death as typically understood. I do not possess a singular face or name. Now and then, I catch a half-glimpse of my visage in the liquid surfaces that gather in the crevices, only to see it vanish in fractal refractions the moment I focus. Perhaps the watchers share an ancestry with those who once hammered stones to shape these huts. Or perhaps we are entirely new beings, formed from the synergy of water, rock, and starlight. 

When the waterfalls crash, I feel compelled to rise—if you can call it rising—drifting up some invisible current to watch. The resonant thunder that follows each surge hits me like a wave of nourishment, more potent than any feast I recall from mortal times. The watchers congregate there, half-hidden in swirling mist, silhouettes formed of refracted brilliance. And I realize that in these gatherings, we communicate without words: the arch of a luminous arm, the tilt of a head that is more suggestion than bone. Perhaps we share memories, or pass on ephemeral knowledge about how a certain outcropping will soon shift. My mind forms the notion that we interpret changes in stone as conversations. Each fracture reveals new lines in the script. Each avalanche a punctuation mark. Each partial collapse an exclamation of renewal.

Now I understand why the place’s unending metamorphosis never fully destroys it. The watchers guide each tremor, each trickle, each runnel of water that erodes the stone. And in that exchange, we partake of the raw essence of becoming. If I wished, I might drift down a corridor formed between two towering cliffs, following the ripple left behind by a fellow watcher. The air there tingles, pulling at me, and I pass gracefully through the same tear in geometry they used. Suddenly, the view shifts from the lower ravine to a precarious ledge halfway up a distant peak. My vantage changes in an instant, as if I have stepped behind a curtain in one place and emerged behind another far away. That is how the watchers move, how I move now—by stepping through the boundaries that normal eyes cannot see.

I think of any traveler who might stumble here and wonder how we must appear to them: flickers in the corner of vision, faint illusions cast by the uncomfortable glare. They would sense us more than see us, perhaps, a hush that descends before we materialize. And I imagine how they would depart, hurrying away before the place claims them. But sometimes, a traveler lingers too long, letting the cliffs seep into their bones. For them, the transition begins: their edges fade, the illusions become real, and they find themselves drifting among the watchers, forgetting that they once measured days in heartbeats. Perhaps I was such a traveler.

In time, I no longer wonder whether I was once ordinary flesh or have always been a guardian of these heights. I take my place among the watchers, coexisting with the spires that shudder toward the swirling sky. I watch new fissures crack open, letting flashes of that bright unreality seep through. I listen to the land’s numbingly loud chorus of stone scraping stone, the hush of collapsing ledges, the hush that forms before each ephemeral presence appears. The watchers and I come together in arcs of fractal light whenever the waterfalls boom, our shapes shining with a color that is not quite prismatic, not quite anything you could name.

Yes, I inhabit this world in constant flux. What am I? Perhaps a caretaker, perhaps an echo, perhaps both—a being formed where matter and impermanence intersect. I dwell in the humming intervals between each seismic breath of the mountains. I do not pursue logic as mortals do. Instead, I sense the subtle shape of shifting gravity, where a spire’s collapse might open new corridors in the canyons, inviting fresh waterfalls. I gently nudge or yield, letting the transformation take its course—or, when necessary, adding my intangible brace to forestall it. Others like me flicker into and out of sight, sharing in the stewardship of these endless cycles.

I am the shimmer that flares on a distant ridge at midday. I am a hush on the ledge above the crashing waterfall. I am the living echo of a place where the ordinary laws of nature bend to some deeper principle. In that place, time stretches and folds, stone breathes, and I, moving at the margins of reality, remain an ever-shifting conductor in this silent orchestra of creation and decay.

And so I would tell any wanderer: look into the brightness, feel the trembling stone, and sense the hush that gathers. In that hush, you will find me—neither ghost nor god, but a watcher of the luminous mountains, drawing nourishment from each new crack in the rock. I am the hush between thunderclaps, a creature of transition who knows no final shape. Here in these impossible heights, where spires groan and waterfalls thunder amid swirling rainbows of mist, I float between the planes of being. And I belong.
