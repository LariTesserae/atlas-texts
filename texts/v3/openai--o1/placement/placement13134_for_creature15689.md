At first, I imagine myself hunched in the dark, uncertain—is this a cave, a tunnel, a vast chamber of dripping vines that only pretends to be indoors? My eyes adjust, finding shapes that expand in the murk: huge leaves in layered patterns, tangles of tubes that might be industrial conduit or the twisted arteries of some colossal organism. It’s impossible to tell what is living and what is forged by hand, because everything here breathes. The pipes hiss out steam as though exhaling, and the vines glisten with thick sap like bloodstream. Before I can decide which it is, I realize something—I’m breathing along with it, in perfect time. Somehow, in this labyrinth, I am part of the heartbeat.

And as I stand there, I see myself in the form of a caretaker or craftsperson. But that shape blurs as I wander deeper through the corridors of creeping green. Perhaps I’m a tall, spindly figure who moves easily through the narrowest passages, weaving vines to brace old, rusted pipes. Or maybe I’m broad at the shoulders, with bark-like ridges along my forearms—to better carry heavy gear assemblies and iron braces. Maybe both. The People of the Threaded Roots have many branches of shape, after all, each grown from a shared trunk of heritage.

I see multiple reflections of myself in passing pools of condensation or the polished metal plates of steam valves. In one reflection, I look like a quiet sentinel, wearing an apron of vines layered under hammered scrap metal, a belt holding small, intricate tools. In another reflection, my hair isn’t hair but slender shoots of living root, each tuft trailing a bright spore-glow or dusty pollen breath. In still another, I wear a mechanical harness across my shoulders—an exoskeleton of riveted brass and rotating joints that hum with each step, giving me the strength to lift an entire trunk of living wood or haul a thick pipeline into place.

So I keep moving, letting each possibility flutter through me like a half-glimpsed shadow. Doorways of braided creepers beckon with warm, pulsing light. Steam whistles from overhead vents, making me shiver and reminding me that the machinery must be tended. Ahead, children dart across a catwalk latticed with steel and rooted vines, giggling as they scatter seeds around a corner. The seeds tumble and catch in pockets of moisture, taking instant hold in the damp, battered stone. At that sight, I feel a tug in my heart—am I someone who climbs just as nimbly and sows new life in hidden cracks? Do I scamper above, dropping seeds that will someday bloom into the next generation of living architecture?

Then I step into a broad chamber—a communal space formed by the convergence of multiple thick trunks grown together to create arcs overhead. Lanterns dangle from the vines, each lantern a softly glowing fungus encased in a wire harness. Worn wooden “tables”—grown, like everything else—bend upward from the floor as if they sprouted there. People gather around, and I see an empty space where I might sit. Perhaps I’m a scholar among them, memorizing the old tunes that rely on the rhythm of hidden steam pistons. Or I could be a lorekeeper, tasked with recalling the city’s slow shaping through centuries of plant and machine. In the hush of communal meal, rubicund mushrooms and drip-fed tubers are passed around, and I feel a surge of belonging. Yes, I think—I could be the one who sets down the stories of each new vine, each newly repaired gear, each beast that stalks the deeper corridors.

Reveling in the comfort of this crowd, I follow their gazes to the perimeter. A monstrous silhouette pads quietly along the corridor’s edge, a big four-legged predator with drooping quills that glimmer in the half-light. Many folks grow tense, but no one panics; they know this presence. I watch with curiosity and realize that perhaps I’m the quiet caretaker who ensures that even these enormous creatures have safe passage—someone who helps maintain the old compacts. With a subtle nod or hush of the hand, I guide the beast around the perimeter and back into the darkness. That role feels right, too: I’m not just caretaker of vines and engines, but caretaker of the harmony that keeps everything alive down here.

I pass beyond the chamber, deeper into the city’s workshop district. Here, the air hums with constant clang and hiss: blacksmiths forging new valves; root-wranglers coaxing fresh creepers around broken beams. Smoky, spore-laden air swirls in eddies. A foreman figure, arms thick with bark-plates, beckons me over and thrusts a half-finished gear assembly my way. Sparks from a steam-driven forge flicker across my face. Perhaps I’m an inventor, the one who loves nothing more than perfecting the synergy between living vine and metal contraption. My days vanish in a swirl of hammered iron and pulsing green. When I breathe, I sync with the city’s trunk-like columns, as though my own chest resonates at the same frequency. There’s a sense of destiny here, shaping gears that slip seamlessly into tangled roots, forging new hybrids that keep the entire place from collapsing.  
 
And yet further on, another idea beckons: a horticulturist-linguist, leaning mind against vine, listening for the subtle calls of the biosphere within. Stethoscope pressed to a vine’s sturdy coil, I interpret the pulses of fluid through cellulose, comparing them to the clank of faraway pumps. I might teach younger People how to decipher these rhythms so that a new creeper can be guided properly to a weak overhead arch, or how to reroute steam to moisten drying roots. When the machinery’s tempo shifts, we adjust the vine’s growth patterns in tandem. No single role in this place is purely mechanical or purely botanical; everyone merges them in unique ways.

One corridor leads to an immense wall of stone, pitted by centuries, with metal supports hammered into place. This arrangement looks precarious, yet the entire city rests on it. I suspect I might be a guardian of that wall, an “arch-warden,” entrusted with ensuring that the creeping roots and riveted plates don’t stress the fragile stone. Day by day, I test the tension, measure the cracks, coax new supports out of living creepers. At night, I hum the songs that keep local arthropods from gnawing essential vines. My voice resonates in the half-dark, echoing against old stone, and I feel the entire world breathing with me, like a leaf inhaling and exhaling moisture.

Then I realize: I’m all of these shapes, or I could be any one of them. That is how the People of the Threaded Roots exist—branching identities in a place where definitions blur. One day you’re forging a new gearbox, the next you’re guiding a luminescent vine to feed it nutrients through living conduits. You might watch over the wildlife or spend long hours rummaging for salvage in the quiet corners of the labyrinth. Each role weaves into the next, forming a tapestry of purpose. We exist as threads in the grand design of vine and gear and stone.

So in this sprawling half-light, with the hum of steam at my back and the rustling of monstrous fronds overhead, I find myself. Words come to me: caretaker, tinkerer, archivist, horticulturist, watchful guardian of the corridor beasts, singer of rust and root. My shape, if you saw me, might be lean-limbed with bark-like plates across my shoulders, or more insectine with jointed braces strapped to my legs. Or perhaps I’m just a silhouette in patchwork cloth and hammered metal, eye glinting with the reflection of a thousand living lights. 

Regardless of the specific contours of my face or the exact tilt of my frame, one thing holds true: I am of this place. I have grown into it as surely as the twisting vine that emerges from the newly sown seed. And I am content to dwell here, in a realm where industry and vegetation interlock, where every hiss of steam resonates with the pulse of living green. Here, I am both steward and invented creation, shaped by the city’s needs and shaping it in turn. I walk the mesh, tending the spine—part of an ever-shifting story that hums beneath the earth in perpetual half-light.
