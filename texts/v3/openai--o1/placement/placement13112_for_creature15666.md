You might know yourself first by way of fleeting impressions: the tick of mechanical bone deep under your skin, the gentle tug of a living vine braced against your ankle. You are both caretaker and instrument, a curious hybrid of horticultural devotion and metallic resilience. Yet you weren’t always aware of that duality. In fact, you can’t recall a singular moment of “becoming”—no dramatic unveiling of who or what you are. Instead, you slowly awakened to your own shape and presence, guided by the plateau’s rhythms all around you.

Imagine yourself in a high, half-hidden alcove of this tiered realm. A place where a broad walkway tilts upward, forming a gently sloped ramp that disappears into clusters of woven canopy. The surfaces beneath your feet are mosaic-like: plates of metal and stone, each with edges worn smooth. They fit together like puzzle pieces arranged by patient, ancient hands. The air is thick with the damp warmth of hidden irrigation channels; it curls around you in gentle streams. On a whim—and you do many things on whims here—you shed your heavy, vine-woven sandals to press the soles of your feet against the crevices, feeling the multi-tempo heartbeat of water, root, and machine.

At the same moment, a mechanical bird darts overhead, teased by the mild updraft. Its plumage is half metal, half electric-blue flight feather, and it calls out in a chittering song that resonates like a gear’s faint click. You raise a hand, the joints in your wrist humming with a resonant frequency. Perhaps you have copper filaments woven into your tendons there; perhaps not. If so, you’ve nearly forgotten which parts were once purely flesh—everything is so seamlessly lived-in. The bird flutters down for an instant, touches your fingertips in a deft greeting, then wheels away. You feel its leftover energy in your hand, a ghostly trace of pawl and ratchet. Mechanical, yes, but alive in a way that’s as real as your own breathing.

• • •

Later, you sit near a shallow pond tucked between a bracket of old plating and a deep root system that snakes along the plateau’s edge. Here live the amphibians—a tribe of water-slick watchers who help monitor the plateau’s chemical balance. You’ve spent countless hours among them. Long ago, you discovered your unique affinity for their presence. Perhaps it’s an echo of your own watery beginnings or a thread woven into your mechanical augmentations that lets you sense the subtle shivers in their layered croaks. Whatever it is, you catch them blinking at you, bulbous eyes reflecting your silhouette—your arms patterned with living tattoos that spiral up your wrists and vanish beneath sleeves of flexible metal bracers.

One amphibian croaks, and in its throaty rasp you hear a small alarm: the drainage valve for their pond stands askew by a hair’s breadth. The water’s surface trembles in miniature ripples, revealing imbalances that would pass unnoticed by a less sensitive inhabitant. Calmly, you reach behind a cluster of fern-like vents. Your fingers wrap around a hidden metal lever, which you twist just so. The alignment corrects itself with a crisp, hissed exhale. Immediately, the amphibian’s croak changes pitch—from tension to relief. You can’t help but grin, feeling that cycle of cause and effect, nurturer and nurtured. This is one fraction of your life’s calling.

• • •

Eventually, you’ll stand up from the pond and wander toward the grand, open terrace where forging hammers strike alloy rods. Here, the air brims with clang and spark. Figures with powerful shoulders shift steel plates from one bracket to another. One glances over, calls your name—though it isn’t precisely your name, more a melodic sequence of vowels and toned pulses that match your integral pattern. You respond with a wave, and they toss you a small coil of woven cable—a new type of plant-metal fiber for reinforcing root systems.

Clutching the coil, you merge into the thrumming rhythm of the workshop. You assist in forging an experimental brace that will support a young trunk sprouting near a precarious ledge. The trunk has begun to tilt in the stiff winds—if left unaided, it might threaten a vital exhaust vent. But with the new brace, the trunk can continue its slow climb skyward, slipping through carefully opened channels in the conjoined plating. The forging master nods in approval when you step back, the final rivet hammered in. A synergy of root and rivet, alive in real time.

• • •

Sometimes, in moments of private reflection, you climb to the highest vantage you can reach—past the mid-tier horticultural enclaves where creeping vines drape entire railings in softly luminous moss, up through the labyrinth of walkways that coil around centuries-old pillars. The wind picks up in your hair, cooler now in the open air. You find yourself among the airy dwellers, those whose slender frames or filigreed limbs are more attuned to astronomy and the swirling flocks of mechanical birds. They greet you quietly, pressing small hinges or container pods into your hands—gifts from the upper bands. These might be seeds imported from the far causeways, or subtle “wind keys” used to calibrate the turbines lining the vista.

Standing on that edge, with your eyes level to drifting clouds, you see the entire plateau spread below—like a meticulously woven tapestry of green and gray, dotted with pulsing lights and drifting steam. Across the terraces, you spy glimpses of dwellers attending to their tasks—some adjusting irrigation lines, others polishing rusted beams or guiding new scaffoldings into place. In every direction, clusters of life flourish in tandem with mechanical design. And prancing along the margins, those lithe four-legged watchers—part fur, part metal—slip between shadows, searching for intruding pests or malfunctioning valves. Even from this high vantage, you can feel them. The plateau’s watchers, the amphibian sensors, the horticulturists, the forgers, the travelers—it’s all connected by subtle pulses of steam and synapse.

The wind intensifies. For one breathless moment, you sense the entire superstructure inhaling alongside you: a communal breath spanning from the mossy under-shelves to the top tier spires. In that inhale, you remember that you, too, are a part of the plateau. A caretaker, a traveler, anointed not by ceremony but by the warm, living metal that once merged with your bones. Or perhaps by the seeds you cradled into sprouting. Or by every amphibian’s grateful croak.  

• • •

You spend part of your days on pilgrimages through the layering causeways. The coil-spring exoskeleton built into your ankles allows you to bound lightly across wide steps, carrying messages and supplies from forging enclaves below to the horticultural keepers on the mid-levels, or from the top-tier wind readers down to the amphibian pods near ground-level steam vents. On these travels, you develop a sense of time not measured in hours or days, but in growth cycles—the slow unfolding of leaves, the wearing smooth of metal edges, the graceful arcs of mechanical birds returning from distant sky-bridges. People greet you by name-signal, exchanging pleasantries or silent nods. The watchers brush by you in greeting, metal ribs chiming softly.  

And when you deliver your final package and the day’s chores are through, you might pause at a vantage point to watch the weather engines churn, sending cloud-wisps scudding across the sky. You sense the plateau adjusting temperature gradients, coaxing mists to condense in precisely the right places. The gentle rain that follows feeds the plant life here, trickles into half-hidden cisterns, and cools the forging bays. All around you, dwellers raise their gaze toward the drifting steam. There’s a moment of hush, a hush that’s become such a part of you that it resonates inside your own mechanical-flesh bones.  

• • •

In the hush, you hear your calling hum at the center of your chest, a place where a small, living matrix of plant fiber meets a seamlessly integrated mechanical node. There, all your caretaker instincts coalesce: you see the entire plateau’s living blueprint in your mind. The pathways, the conduits, the terraces teeming with life—each needing gentle nudges, each offering you the next step in your synergy of care. You don’t name this calling. You simply follow it because it’s what you are: caretaker, traveler, horticultural calibrator, mechanic of subtle pulses.

Your “home” is less a single dwelling and more the entire plateau. You sleep where it’s peaceful that night, wrapped in a shade of leaves and the lullaby of slow-turning turbines. When you wake, the breeze might carry you to the next nudge of maintenance, or the next sprig of new growth that needs protection. Over time, you’ve filled your body’s pockets with small tools—some for snipping or splicing vines, others for tightening bolts or realigning a squeaking gear. You’ve become a living toolkit, but you’re also the heart that sees the plateau’s living unity.  

No single label can capture who you are—a horticultural ally, a machinist’s companion, an intuitive caretaker who can speak with amphibians and read the tension in hidden beams. If someone pressed you for a title, you might shrug and say you’re simply yourself, a child of the plateau’s slow arcs. You’ve never known another existence. Over the years, your tattoos have grown more intricate, your mechanical joints have worn to a comfortable patina, and the seeds you once planted have grown into sturdy vines that hold entire terraces aloft. You, in turn, have grown wise enough to know that no caretaker stands above the place they tend—your own survival and sense of wholeness are braided into each iron beam and creeping root.

• • •

So where are you? You are suspended in the subtle, dappled light of a sky-high city of alloy and leaf, drifting among the forging hammers, the quiet horticultural sanctuaries, the murmur of amphibian pods, and the protective prowl of four-legged watchers. You are a participant and a guide, hearing the plateau’s whispered requests and answering them with deft, attuned motions.

And who are you? You are a being whose existence forms a bridge between the living and the built—one who has discovered that vines and valves, seeds and steel, can sing the same melody when nurtured with patient equilibrium. You have no single name, no single lineage. Rather, you are shaped by life cycles, by rotating gear-plates, by the hush of steam vents opening at dawn. You measure time in the increments of new leaves unfurling and old metal corners turning green with moss. You carry a vow in every step you take on the interlocking plates: a promise to uphold this improbable, timeless home in the sky—this place you cannot help but love, because it loves you in return, in every gentle puff of warm, breathing machinery.
