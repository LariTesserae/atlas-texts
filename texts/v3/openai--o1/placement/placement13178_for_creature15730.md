I can’t say for certain when or how I arrived—whether in a slow metamorphosis from another life or in a blinding moment of clarity—but I know I belong here in these steep-walled ravines. At first, I’m not entirely sure what I am. The heat overwhelms me; my skin feels slick, and I cling to the dark, dripping rock with limbs that splay wide, each joint bending in a way that both perplexes and delights me. It takes time to recognize the shape of my own form. I do not scramble in alarm; rather, I simply wait, melding with the dampness, letting this ancient place teach me about myself. 

• • •

Hung high above a narrow gorge, I feel as though the sun itself has latched onto my back, pressing its fiery kiss between my shoulders. Yet water trickles all around, and my skin glistens with it—a natural equilibrium. Green vines stretch outward in tangles, occasionally brushing my toes as I sway on a ledge no wider than a handspan. A faint breeze stirs, and I catch the smell of wet rock, singed by heat. Far below, I hear the crash of water as it pours into invisible depths. And just beneath that persistent rush is the hum: low, machine-like, utterly inhuman yet undeniably alive.

In the beginning, I’m still, unsure what next step to take. My limbs—are they scaled? Am I amphibian or reptile or something new? Perhaps I’m none of these, or perhaps all at once. I see the lizards scuttle by. Their colorful frills flick open and shut; they hardly notice me. I see the tiny frogs crawling across the slick surface of the moss, bright droplets of orange and electric green, as if lit from within. My hand—a web of fine skin between delicate bones—peels away from the rock when I shift position. Water slides down my forearm and drips in a steady plink onto the plants below. 

I peer at the ridged, rubbery shape of that hand—my hand. Warm golden sunlight coats the ridges of knuckle and claw. When I curl my digits, the tips bite into the rock. This is me? Strange, but not frightening. My chest swells with a giddy awareness: I am part of this place. I can cling to a vertical cliff with effortless grace, feeling no fear of slipping into the gorge. Moisture is my ally, not a threat. The rivulets on the rock help keep me anchored; my limbs and body temperature shift in tandem with the drip and steam. The sizzling sun reveals colors on my curved back—patches of glimmering scales that catch the light like tiny mirrors.

• • •

At times, as I test the edges of my domain, I drop lower along the cliff. My limbs press tight to my body, and I let gravity pull me, trusting that the water-slick rock will guide me rather than betray me. Other times, I taste the air with a small flick of my tongue, sensing the humidity swirl in subtle patterns. The pulse beneath the rock thrums with a steady calm: I discover I can feel it through my feet, like standing on a distant heartbeat. It hypnotizes me, a lullaby or perhaps an anchor. 

Every step feels both ancient and new. There is no ladder, no rope, just the infinite fractal of ledges, vines, crevices, and dripping hollows. Each rung leads me deeper into the tapestry of life that thrives in these water-hewn heights. Soon I realize I can slip into pockets of shadow unnoticed, swirl away from bright watchful eyes. It’s not precisely magic, more an organic fluidity—my scales darken slightly when I press into shade, catching less light, while the drenched environment helps camouflage my movements. 

I find no single place to call “home.” Instead, every vine-latticed nook or trickling cave mouth offers a different shelter, a new vantage. I might curl among the ferns that tower overhead like living canopies. Below those massive fronds, the air is thick with humid shade, and a sweet, decaying fragrance seeps from the moss-laden hollows. I close my eyes, hearing the drip… drip… drip… of water into a shallow pool. Open them again, and the hush recedes, replaced by a flurry of motion: a bright bird darts past, screeching at some intruder; frogs along the pool’s edge trill in reply. 

Sometimes, I climb up-slope toward the rock crest, where midday sun bleaches color from the sky and a sheer cliff-face drops away beneath me. I cling to the scorching basalt, and rivulets of hot water trickle across my limbs. The mechanical hum is fainter here, or perhaps drowned out by the roar of the biggest waterfall that plunges into the chasm below. If I approach the lip of that waterfall—cautiously, so as not to be swept away—I can peer down into a spray-filled amphitheater. Rainbows arc in the mist, shimmering disjointedly. Ferns hang precariously on the edges. Occasionally I catch a glimpse of that cat-thing slinking behind a curtain of vines, eyes glinting with sharp intelligence.

• • •

I begin to sense that I am not alone in my uniqueness. As I roam, I see others of my kind—or at least, close relatives—each shaped slightly differently, each adapted to a particular fold of the terrain. One has wide fins along its arms, translucent webbing that shimmers pink in the sunlight; perhaps it glides from ledge to ledge. Another sports a darker hide, ridged with spines that channel water directly down its back. In fleeting encounters—usually at dusk, when the thick night air becomes alive with calls—they nod or blink at me, acknowledging a shared origin and purpose. Our society has no language of words, just a sense of belonging. We know that in the interplay of stone and stream, we have our place, and that is enough.

Hunting becomes a natural extension of daily life. When small insects swarm by the thousands near a glowing moss patch, I lunge, using a sticky, slender tongue to capture them mid-flight—or by creeping up with smooth, deliberate movements until the last second. I savor tiny pulses of nourishment. The taste is mineral-rich, reminiscent of the slick walls from which the insects feed. On other occasions, I discover plump berries growing in vine-hidden recesses. My teeth are pointed, but I can nibble on these succulent orbs if I wish; their juices cool my throat, and an unusual tang of iron or sulfur might linger in my mouth—a testament to the land’s volcanic underpinnings.

• • •

Over time, I’ve learned to follow the deeper rhythms: the subtle cracks in the earth that leak steam when I press my body close, the patches of algae that flourish in near-boiling water, the unexpected shift in wind when a wave of condensation forms overhead. In the afternoons, clouds often gather, darkening the sky, releasing a brief torrent of rain that amplifies every sound. Each raindrop is like a hammer on the leaves, a thousand tiny drummers tapping out the land’s heartbeat. I crouch under an overhang, water cascading over me, a temporary waterfall forming just inches from my face. I swallow a few drops, feeling the warmness of the rainfall, tasting faint minerals that traveled umpteen miles through hidden caverns before reemerging in the sky’s drizzle.

At night, curiosity leads me deeper. I slip into a cave mouth veiled by vines and step on slick stone. Cool darkness envelops me. There—glowing fungus lines the walls, pale blues and greens pulsing in eerie patterns, each one responding to my presence by slightly dimming or brightening, as though aware. The hum is stronger inside. There’s a steady hiss from a vent in the cave floor, ribbons of steam twisting upward, swirling around stalagmites that drip with condensed water. I breathe deeply, letting the damp heat fill my lungs. My eyes adjust, pupils flaring wide to catch each glimmer of luminescence. Pulsing worms trace snail-like trails across the rock, and blind crustaceans scuttle from corner to corner. Small echoes bounce off the stone, each reverberation layering upon the mechanical thrumming in the depths. 

In these underground moments, I sense how ancient the land is, how each searing day and wet night forms part of a chronicle that spans uncounted eons. My presence here—my entire people’s presence—feels like a single wave in an endless tide. Yet I do not mind; there’s no rush to claim or conquer. We exist in tandem, blood pulsing in time with the earth’s interior beat. If it’s a machine, it’s not separate from us; we are cogs that slot neatly into place, living gears in the planetary mechanism.

• • •

I sometimes wonder if I might spread wings like the birds overhead or slip into the shallows like the amphibians in the pools. My body is so malleable, so adapted for clinging, but perhaps it can adapt further. Each day, new possibilities emerge. A particularly steep climb might trigger my arms to lengthen, my feet to grow more webbing, or my tail to flatten for better balance. It is as if my form hasn’t quite finished deciding what it wants to be, that every life-thread in this place is a swirling current I might tap into. 

But just when I think I might sprout feathers, I notice the birds have their own realm, high in the open air where they chase insects in bright sunlight. I try to follow their path along the cliff’s lip, but my longing dissipates as soon as I feel the wind blasting across my damp scales. That domain belongs to them. Likewise, I watch the cat-like hunter with a mix of awe and caution. Sleek, predatory grace is mesmerizing, but I do not covet that skill—there’s a fierceness in its sinews that belongs to thick darkness and sudden leaps. Mine is a different way: an intimate, patient climbing, a quiet assimilation into nooks of dripping green. 

• • •

Maybe some nights, when the steam vents open wide, I dream. The pulses of geothermic heat give me the sense that I’m drifting not just in water but in centuries. I see vast tectonic plates shifting; I see the mountains buckling skyward, softened by endless rainfall, carved by rivers into these spider-webbed gorges. I witness the first ferns unfolding between cracks in the rock, the first amphibians testing the scalding moisture. I catch glimpses of a time-lapse of life evolving in tandem with the planet’s forging. And then I see shapes that must be my own ancestors—small, nimble creatures, gliding from one watery niche to another, each generation refining our forms until we became the living expression of these heated cliffs. 

Waking from such a dream, I stir with renewed certainty: this place and I are bound. My vantage might shift, my features might evolve, but the land’s heartbeat remains constant. On a newly formed ledge—where rock, once molten, has cooled and cracked—I test my claws. The water that seeps there glows faintly, testament to minerals rising from deeper veins. I partake in a sip. It tastes ancient, almost metallic, and resonates in my bones. Suddenly, my skin flickers, and for a moment, I see a reflection of scaled brilliance, a synesthetic hum coursing from the water through my cells. Yes, we are one.

• • •

There comes a point—though it’s never final—where I stop trying to name myself, and simply am. I may be called a reptile by some hypothetical outside observer, or maybe an amphibian, or some brand-new chord struck in the evolutionary symphony of heat and water. But in truth, I’m a living facet of this land, as integral as the cat-like predators, the neon frogs, and the birds with their weaving flight. At dusk, I rest on a spongy moss patch, lulled by the layered calls of creatures winding down the day. At dawn, I cling to an overhang, letting the emerging sunlight streak across my shimmering back. My color palette shifts subtly, catching the pink blush of morning. 

Everywhere I travel, I carry the hum within me. In my bones, in the wetness of my membranes, in the swirl of heat that thrums against my newly toughened skin, I feel the same quiet pulse that underpins the waterfalls and the tectonic grind beneath. I have no desire to leave, nor do I conceive of “leaving” as an option. Why would I abandon the place that sculpts me each day, as a sculptor shapes clay on a wheel spinning through eons?

• • •

If a traveler from another world stumbled here, they might try to name me, categorize me, perhaps even catch me. But I doubt they could, for I am not merely an organism in a habitat. I—and all my kin—are manifestations of these cliffs, living out the dance of rushing water and scorching sunlight, the hiss of steam and the branching green fronds. We are the land’s flourishing imagination, shaped by cycles that have turned since before human memory. 

In the end, it doesn’t matter what label you give me. I am an observer and a participant, a dreamer and a hunter, scaled with molten minerals and bathed in eternal showers—just one form in a continuum of beings who call this realm home. I exist simply because this place has willed it so, forging me in the crucible of steaming rock and roaring water. And each time I cling to the dark stone wall, letting blazing sun and shimmering dampness cradle me in equal measure, I know: I am exactly where—and who—I ought to be.
