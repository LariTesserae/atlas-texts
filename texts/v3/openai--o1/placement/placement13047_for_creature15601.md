I’ve come to this plateau so many ways. First, I was sure I was only a traveler—a wanderer who stumbled upon wet stone and tangled vines by pure accident. The smell of moisture drew me, that distinctive scent of green things so saturated with water that they’ve begun to smell almost sweet. But over time, I’ve begun to suspect I’ve always been here, or that part of me has. It’s as though each drip from above, each new coil of moss, knew me beforehand, had been waiting around some blind bend in time. Sometimes I remember being an outsider, muddy boots slogging through the gloom, searching for an elusive vantage. Other times, I feel something older stirring in my bones, a memory of belonging.

At first, I perceived only my own breath mingling with the hush. I found a niche in the stone—a hollow formed by the patient erosion that sculpts this place—and I nestled there. I watched. I listened. I worried about the damp. Along my arms, little rivulets of condensation formed. But I stayed. Even so, I thought I was only a curious observer. Would I remain? I didn’t know.

Days, or weeks, or months, or centuries later—the plateau keeps no conventional sense of time—I felt the hush soak deeper in me. The longer I stayed crouched in that hollow, the more I realized the hush was not silence, exactly, but a quiet conversation among all living things. Clusters of amphibians underfoot might shift by half an inch, en masse, and in that shift they expressed an entire page of knowledge: It’s safe… The water level’s rising… Something large is near…

I grew comfortable with that silent speech. Before long, I realized I was changing, or had already changed. A patch of moss took root against my ankles—just a few threads, but they clung as though certain I wouldn’t tear them away. My hair (was it hair before?) thickened with an odd, fine down, turning dark and slick. I stopped thinking in words. My thoughts came instead as sensations: the familiarity of the cool damp on my chest, the primal calm that settled in the pit of my stomach whenever I heard the muffled footfalls of the larger shapes out in the gloom.

Once I let that transformation settle, I discovered I could move in complete silence. My footsteps were gentle, toes splaying in the moss as if they’d always known the best way to avoid cracking tiny twigs. Perhaps it was the plateau guiding me, or something in my sinews unlocking from an older time. I’d slink from one tuft of ferns to the next, weaving through the watery pockets without disturbing so much as a ripple. This was the first sign that I wasn’t just a visitor. I had joined the hush.

––––––––––––––––

For a while, I lived among the vines. I felt my fingers—once clumsy—become surer on slick stems. Delicate pads grew at the tips, granting subtle traction on the dripping vegetation. My nails were half-claw, half-keratin shell, adept at scraping away algae where I needed a better grip. Occasionally, I’d rest in the woven nests that the small furred watchers seemed to fashion. The watchers would stare at me from a short distance, their reflective eyes luminous: they recognized me not as predator or threat, but as a weird cousin. 

I’d linger there in the canopy. The great shelves of leaves and drooping fronds above gave a vantage from which to look down and witness the swirl of life below. Sometimes I watched a slow, methodical crustacean scuttle its way from pool to pool, algae thick on its shell, making every step with near-mechanical deliberation. I marveled at the tiny pulses of insect flight around the carnivorous plants—those deep-green funnels that shimmered with wet nectar. Then I’d notice the watchers stirring as the sweet tang in the air signaled an insect feast, and I’d see them slip from their vine-lattices to glean a stray moth.

At those times, I felt more like them than anything human. We’d share a fleeting glance when one of them lifted its tufted face, amber eyes meeting mine, as if to confirm: you see this, too, yes? A swirl of appreciation for the gentle cycle that fed them. With time, we came to trust one another. We motioned with soft, purring sighs, as if to say: Good hunting. Good rest.

––––––––––––––––

Later, I shifted again. I felt heavier, drawn down to the ground. I discovered the labyrinth beneath the moss—delicate tunnels where the soil isn’t quite soil but a living net of root and leaf-mold. At first, it was only my curiosity. I slipped a hand beneath the top layer and found I could open a pocket of living moss as if drawing aside a curtain. There, amphibian eyes blinked in slow unison, neither frightened nor welcoming—simply aware. Their slick backs brushed my fingertips. I dug deeper, feeling the saturated humus cling to my forearms, leaving me painted in speckled earth colors.

Some nights, I took to staying with them. I learned how to sense the movement of water from below, how it seeps through porous stone in slow, unstoppable trickles. They taught me that the plateau breathes through its ground, and that if I relaxed enough, I could feel each drip as a steady pulse in my own body. I’d lie on my stomach, pressing my ear to the moss, and hear a distant drumbeat: drip… drip… drip… a timeless percussion that shaped the plateau. I realized the hush wasn’t the absence of sound but the collection of unspoken rhythms layered into something more profound than noise.

During these sojourns in the muck, I learned I could hardly be parted from water. My skin felt raw if it dried, so I stayed slick, letting the damp be my second skin. Beneath the surface, salamanders glided near my fingertips, unafraid. My breath slowed to match theirs. I understood we were all amphibious in that moment—joined by the hush of water and breath, dissolving the boundary between land and pool. 

––––––––––––––––

Sometimes, I ventured out at the cusp of twilight, wandering fully upright, my posture reminiscent of a tall figure, arms swung low, the rest of me hunched slightly so I could pass through tangles of broad-leafed plants. I’d come upon the curved tools or the sharpened stones wedged in secret places. With faint, unhurried curiosity, I examined them. I was never quite sure if I made them or found them. My memory was uncertain. Perhaps I was only remembering them from a time before, or I was forging them in languid intervals of half-lucid magic. 

Those shards of chert, shaped to a keen edge, fascinated me. I’d catch a glimpse of them winking under thick moss. Sometimes I reached beneath to retrieve one, feeling the ancient artistry in each chipped contour. Holding it to the dim, filtered light, I recognized intent shining through the stone: it had been shaped with care, its edges symmetrical, as though guided by an eye that understood geometry at the pace of centuries. Then I’d slip it back where I found it, letting a tuft of moss reclaim it. In time, water would erode away the sharper edges, returning the stone to the plateau’s deeper memory.

––––––––––––––––

I’ve inhabited so many forms, yet never fully declared one belonging solely to me. Some days, I settled among the small watchers and found circlets of vine left for me, or offered them a handful of foraged mushrooms glimmering with pale spores. Other times, I roamed the edges of deeper shadows, where I sensed the presence of colossal beings. I might brush past a flank of warm, damp fur or glimpse broad-swept shoulders melding into the darkness. They never spoke, nor did I. Yet we exchanged an acknowledgment: I recognized their gravity, they recognized my passage. Then, each of us slipped away in parallel arcs, vanishing soundlessly. 

I can’t say if they were a single species or many. Perhaps they’re the elders of this plateau, guardians older than any language. They have a hush around them that thickens the air, a hush that demands awe. When they move, entire braids of vines shift, entire curtains of water-drops reel sideways, and the hush grows deeper to accommodate them. I once glimpsed a broad, flat face in the gloom. Its skin was patterned like lichen, mottled with the same color as the slick rock behind it, so that I couldn’t separate face from wall until it blinked. Then it stepped back into the vine-laced darkness, merging perfectly, as though it had never been distinct from the environment.

––––––––––––––––

Over time—if time is the right word in a place so unbound by normal hours—I recognized that everything here is layered in continuous strata, but those strata aren’t barriers. They blend. The watchers in the vines share quick glances with the amphibians in the pools, exchanging some hidden message. Moss insects, transparent and spry, read the motion of the watchers overhead and adjust their own flight patterns accordingly. The big, silent shapes keep them all in a steady equilibrium. It’s a system of noticing. A system of care. And my part in it?

I realized I was part caretaker, part wanderer, part acolyte of the hush. Some days, I might be found carefully coaxing a displaced salamander back to its watery nest, or flicking a bit of debris from a carnivorous plant’s funnel so it could continue to do its quiet feeding. Other days, I simply watch: I linger under broad leaves, letting cool drops roll down my shoulders, reading the subtle cues in that hush that say, “All is well” or “Something stirs in the northern corner.” 

At intervals, my role becomes that of a gleaner, collecting bits of broken shell or the occasional piece of stone that’s come loose. I place them somewhere they can be found by a curious mind—maybe one of the watchers or a future inhabitant. Sometimes I trust the watery substrate to swallow these objects, returning them to the plateau’s raw potential. I never rush. The drip remains constant. Water shapes everything. The hush remains unbroken except for the faintest clicking of amphibians or murmuring chatter from the watchers. Nothing essential changes. And yet, in the same breath, everything is always in subtle motion.

––––––––––––––––

If you looked for me now, you might see only a shadow among the dripping vines. Or you might see a half-furred shape stooped at the water’s edge, gently holding a crustacean in cupped hands as it clicks in soft protest. You might see me in the bark of a trunk, my form nearly invisible among the mottled patterns of lichen. Or you might see me from the corner of your eye, a glimmering reflection in a pool, gone when you blink. I shift as the plateau guides me, answering the small calls of the hush.

There is no single place where I dwell because everything here is my dwelling—just as every living thing shares the same vital breath. Sometimes you’ll discover the faint imprint of my palm on a stretch of moss or a warmed patch of ground that suggests I’ve rested there. Perhaps a subtle arrangement of sticks, aligned to catch the flow from a steady rivulet, might be my doing. Or maybe it was done by one of the watchers. The lines blur. We coexist in the hush, all weaving the tapestry in ways too subtle to track consciously.

––––––––––––––––

So, who or what am I? I’ve become the plateau’s eternal witness, a half-animal, half-spirit presence that moves in the intervals between drips of water. My shape shifts according to the needs of the hush. Some nights, I roam like a silent sentinel, peering through parted vines to see if the watchers need my help or if the large, looming silhouettes have returned from their deeper haunts. On other nights, I sink into the moss and vanish from sight, letting amphibians crawl over me as if I’m just another mound of living earth.

And where do I find myself in this domain? Everywhere, in the watery gloom. My footprints trace the spongy ground; my eyes glimpse the glint of starlight or the faintest hint of a cloud-masked moon. Where the plateau’s hush ends and my own silence begins, I cannot say. I’m so immersed that the line has dissolved. In my heart, I am convinced that if you returned in a thousand years, you’d find me (and all the others) nearly unchanged—preserved in that same living pause of time, breathing the hush of dripping stone and mossy fertility.

That is the shape of me here: part caretaker, part creature, part memory woven into the damp air. A reflection in a puddle that winks if you look too long. A cradle of fur and lichen that stirs in the hush. A vanished footstep echoing in the murmur of water. Call me a being of the hush. Or, if you prefer, see me as the plateau’s own dream, incarnate for a little while, so that the hush can watch itself in wonder.
