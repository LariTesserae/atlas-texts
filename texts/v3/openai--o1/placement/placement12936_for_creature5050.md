At first, I do not even realize I am me. I am merely another swirl of damp warmth under these canopies, some incipient form that feels the earth’s breath before fully awakening. My senses are a confusion of half-formed pulses—squelching mud underfoot, the percussion of raindrops on leaves above, a gust of blistering heat from a crack in the ground. I think, dimly, This is a place that’s only just begun to be. And for a moment, I feel I’m part of this birth process, half-forgetful of having had any existence before now.

Senses coalesce, and so does my body: a spindly thing, all joints and angles, with a smoothness to my skin like amphibian flesh—yet faint, bony ridges trace across my shoulders and down my spine, reminiscent of the fresh cresting lines that band the basin’s new-formed ridges. My toes curl with a primal sensitivity to the terrain’s flux. I crouch low, inhaling the soup of pollen, spore-mist, and thick humidity that saturates every breath. It is overwhelming, but it also feels like nourishment. I can taste the swirling energies in the air, as if the place breathes magic in the same way it exhales steam.

I push upward, balancing on two legs, though it’s a precarious stance. It’s easier to drop into a low four-limbed gait, feeling the leaf litter squish beneath my palms. Still, I sense in my limbs a faint strength I’m not accustomed to—like the forest’s will has soaked into my muscles, urging me to climb, leap, and test new vantage points. My reflection in a shallow pool is flickery at best—broken illusions from overhead vines swaying in the gloom—but I glimpse my own eyes: pupils large and searching, ringed by a shimmering color that might be green or gold, depending on how the scattered light catches it.

As I wander, I begin to understand: I belong to that category of beings who stand somewhat upright—perhaps akin to primeval humans, perhaps not. But here, such categories dissolve. What matters more is how swiftly I can sense the next shift of earth, the next rivulet forming in the undergrowth, or the approach of something big among the vines. I am no builder of civilizations; instead, I exist in the small hollows and nesting pockets that reveal themselves in the forest’s constant churn. One moment I may shelter behind a rotting log bearded with crimson fungus for the length of a brief rainfall. Then, as the downpour ends and the log begins to crumble, I’ll slip into the underbrush, searching for the next momentary refuge.

I have discovered I bear a curious affinity with the glowing spores. When a fungal bloom pulses with that faint green luminescence at dusk, I can sense an answering vibration in my fingertips. If I linger, I feel a gentle hum in the base of my skull, as though the energy from the basin’s molten heart resonates through the cluster of fungal roots. In these moments, I can almost see the forest’s blueprint of entangled lines: scaffold upon scaffold of creeping vines, root webs, and the veined shapes of living leaves bridging one trunk to the next. Sometimes the spore-lights flicker in a pattern that makes me wonder if it’s a language I’m on the cusp of decoding.

Yet surviving here demands constant attentiveness. I have only a bit of knapped stone tied to a length of braided vine, a crude chopper or scraper. It’s small enough to fit in my hand and barely holds an edge for more than a day. Everything in this land is young and hungry—edges wearing down from contact with vibrant, living growth. When I find a moment to sharpen it on a chunk of basalt or quartz, I must do so quickly; the air is so thick and full of life that if I linger too long in one place, something else is bound to come sniffing, crawling, or pouncing in my direction.

Not that I am entirely helpless. There is a cunning that surges through my blood, inherited from the primal conditions here. A glint in my eyes telegraphs watchfulness and swift reaction. I can climb at angles that would be impossible on older, more eroded surfaces. The newness of these crags and walls suits me; I hook my strong, elongated fingers into fresh ridges of half-solid rock and scale them, bits of earth and mineral stinging my palms. From these higher vantage points, I can see the broad leaves layered into a fortress of green and black, with slivers of grey gloom swirling in the upper air. I see reptilian quadrupeds the size of boulders forging muddy tracks, scuttlers moving in thick carpets across the ground, amphibians with fleshy crests waddling from one ephemeral pool to the next. I see others like me, too—flickers of movement among the vines or behind the bulge of new earth. Sometimes we acknowledge each other with a brief, silent glance, a tilt of the head. Rarely do we gather in groups large enough to be called tribes, though occasionally we’ll coordinate in pairs to drive away a predatory cat-thing.

I have grown used to the roars and rumbles that ripple through the canopy. Even the thunder that shakes the ground daily is woven into the rhythms of my life. When the lightning cracks overhead, a livid purple flash burns the sky, and for a shaky instant I see the entire basin in stark silhouette. Then the thunder calls back, rolling across the treetops, sending flocks of leathery flyers into startled flight. Their beating wings scatter warm droplets from the leaves onto my head in a misty baptism. In that moment, I catch the bristling taste of static in the air—the primal magic that leaps from cloud to soil, fueling everything here.

Sometimes I dwell where the fissures open. There, the ground exhales pluming steam, and around those vents I find luscious growth: coiled ferns the size of my torso, mushrooms with spongy caps that glow faintly pink, blooms that release perfumed clouds of sleepy, stupefying pollen. There is power at these vents, a pulse so strong that if I close my eyes, I imagine my heart beating in tandem with the land. The steam licks across my skin, and I feel the ridges along my spine grow subtly, as though the forest’s forming energies are shaping me anew, day by day. I sometimes suspect that if I remained in one place too long, I would merge with the vines themselves, my flesh turned into bark or a plush fungal coat.

I do not speak words—not in the sense of a structured language—but I do communicate with subtle gestures and small vocalizations. A flick of the wrist can signal to another forest-dweller: follow me, or watch out. A low grunt from the back of the throat can warn a companion of something large lumbering nearby. When I meet the eyes of the scaled amphibians, or the massive cat with bone ridges, there is a mutual recognition that we are all shifting forms of the same unstoppable swirl—though sometimes that swirl demands that we fight for existence. I have the scars to prove it: jagged lines across my forearms where I survived a stealthy reptilian bite. In time, those wounds knit themselves swiftly—too swiftly, almost. It’s as though the same primal spark forging the land also forges me in fast cycles of healing and adaptation.

I can’t help but notice that my fingers sometimes emit faint glimmers of their own. Not consistently, and not necessarily by my will—but in rare moments, an electric prickle surges from my fingertips, dancing around my knuckles in a soft glow that mirrors the neighboring fungus-lights. Is it magic awakened in me by these half-formed energies? Or is it some natural reflection of the forest’s phenomenon, the way certain insects here learn to glow so they can find one another in this perpetual dusk? I suspect no one knows yet, not even the forest itself.

I have dreamlike memories of a time before, a calmer place of open sky and gentler breezes. But here, the sky is claustrophobic and bruised with storms, the vegetation forms living walls that push back at me wherever I roam. And yet, I sense I would not choose to leave. I belong to this rawness as a newborn belongs to its mother’s arms. There is something about the unstoppable vitality, the churn of new growth, the sudden waterfalls, the ephemeral pockets of dryness that vanish at the next deluge—it all intoxicates me. I cannot imagine a land that sits still, waiting for seeds to take root over decades. Here, everything is immediate, urgent. Even in the half-dark, it feels as though the world vibrates with potential from one moment to the next.

At night, or what passes for night in the midst of a swirling gloom, I might find a niche among twisted roots half exposed by a recent mudslide. The roots arch overhead like a cage, and I weave a mat of wide, fibrous leaves to keep out the stinging insects. Around me, small scuttlers glow at the joints of their legs, forming a miniature cosmos of star-like flickers over the ground. It is a living tapestry, a breathing tapestry. If I allow myself to lie still and let my breathing match the pulse of the forest, I hear the monstrous calls from afar—rumbling quadrupeds staking territory, frogs with neon throats croaking in deep chords, the leathery flyers overhead cackling in the half-dark. It’s a chorus as old as existence, or perhaps as brand new as creation itself. A lullaby of chaos.

Sometimes I dream in color so vivid, I wonder if it’s a memory or a vision. Flocks of shimmering, half-translucent vine-creatures drifting overhead. A swirling spore field that shapes itself into the silhouette of a giant biped with arms outstretched, only to dissolve into drifting sparkles. In those dreams, I see not just this forest, but the forest that’s waiting to be, as though the place is still deciding what shape it wants to take. And by living here, I’m part of that decision, contributing my breath and step, my fleeting glow, to its emerging architecture.

In the waking hours, I realize that I am no stable species with a long lineage. I am something brand new—like everything else in this land. I sense my form could shift over time, adopting ridges or scales if that’s what survival demands. Perhaps my arms will sprout fungal patches to better blend with the environment, or my eyes will develop specialized lenses to peer through the gloom. Maybe one day I’ll glean how to harness that electric tingle in my fingertips and use it to communicate across distances, or to weave a net of living vines in an hour’s time. Nothing here seems impossible.

And so, if you ask me where I find myself in this world, I’d say I am of it, rather than in it. A gangly-limbed, half-amphibian, half-humanoid child of the steam vents and the crackling nights. My purpose: to survive, adapt, and reflect the forest’s endless swirl of creation. I am the transient occupant of hollows and root-chambers, a wanderer forging fleeting alliances with others who glean the forest’s signals. I am a contributor to the vibrant, thrumming tapestry that gropes toward full formation—yet never settles.

I do not own possessions, aside from my little stone blade and a few tokens of bone or vine I fashion into rudimentary ornaments, which time and weather soon reclaim. I do not maintain a house or keep a hearth, for the land is in too much flux to hold a structure in place. Instead, I carry a sense of home in the moist warmth of my skin, in the tang of pollen and fungal spore that flavors my every breath. My sense of security is the forest’s own ceaseless renewal: if something is destroyed in a mudslide, something else sprouts behind it the next day. I drift from vantage point to vantage point, a living thread pulled through the labyrinth by the next crash of thunder or the next beckoning glow.

I have no name, because names feel anchored, and this land does not allow anchoring. But if you must call me something, call me Mettle-of-the-Mist or Child-of-the-Hot-Fissures—some ephemeral label that dissolves on the tongue. My identity is an ongoing transformation. Here, in this half-formed realm of bruised skies and thick-limbed vegetation, I am a shape that rose from the steaming ground and borrowed the hum of primal creation. I don’t think I will ever leave—nor do I imagine I ever could. The land and I are growing together, and in that perpetual emergence, I find both wonder and belonging.
