I have not always known this place for home. It pulled me here slowly, as water pulls earth, shaping me into something different than I was. Or, perhaps it was always where I belonged—drawn by the steady chorus of dripping corridors and the promise of a damp kind of life. Because I cannot recall a singular arrival, only the gradual discovering of myself in these watery halls.

At first, I must have been among those restless watchers, a traveler stepping softly between the arches, unsure of my footing as I groped for a lantern’s weak glow. That first encounter was a feast for the senses: the sly green of algae winking at me in the torchlight, the amphibian calls reverberating in the crooked geometry, the tang of water and clay thickening in my throat. It was all so foreign that my breathing trembled, my pulse too rapid—as though some part of me was both delighted by and wary of what lived in the shadows.

Yet over time, days or years—there is no simple clock here to measure—I found that my shape had changed. Instead of flinching every time something slick and amphibious darted across my path, I grew to expect the notes of their singing and the sudden bursts of motion when they leapt away. Instead of tensing at the drip that forever punctuates these halls, my ears attuned themselves to the larger harmony: the interplay of croaks, hums, squeaks, and footfalls. I realized that I no longer needed my voice to be loud. I began to speak in murmurs, or not at all.

Eventually, I understood that I was more akin to the tall, half-aquatic folk who carefully shape the clay of these corridors—yet I was not quite one of them. My limbs were longer once; they have grown lean, and my hands have broadened like theirs, though not exactly. My skin has begun to match the sedimentary hue of these walls, in splotches or faint smears, like I’ve been pressing myself into the wet earthen textures day after day. When I run my fingertips over my forearms, I feel the fine layer of moisture clinging there, as though my body yields easily to the damp that saturates this realm.

I inhabit a constant, low-grade chill now, accustomed to the idea that no real warmth will find me. Sometimes, I’ll sit by a shallow pool in one of the courtyard chapels, letting my toes dangle into the water, half-lost in the haze of lamplight that ripples overhead. Sounds come to me from all directions: an older humanoid figure guiding clay and stone into newly shaped channels in a distant hallway, the stooped folk grunting softly as they lift fresh baskets of earth, the frogs resonating in a half-submerged alcove. I can almost close my eyes and hear the entire place breathing.

Yet I’ve found my own small purpose here among them—something neither fully amphibian nor fully humanoid. I’ve become a caretaker of the thresholds. There are doors here, though they aren’t doors as we know them above ground. They’re transitions: shadowy archways, tight squeezes in the corridors, places where water stands deeper or the floor abruptly plunges. These spots can confuse travelers or hamper the flow of the community. I linger near them, not permanently rooted, but always drawn to them, as if I sense the currents of water that want to push in or seep out.

I’ve learned how to place smooth stones in patterns that coax the water into gentler channels, how to brace my shoulder against a sagging clay arch until help arrives with fresh material. I know which salamander-like creatures prefer the vantage of each threshold—those with translucent membranes who cling to the walls overhead. They have small squeaks to greet me in the mornings, or what I think of as morning, though true daylight rarely pierces these halls. My bond with them is a quiet one: an exchange of presence, a shared acceptance of cool air and watery surroundings.

When the bigger, older dwellers glide by with their hammered copper chisels or baskets of newly dug clay, they sometimes pause to murmur a greeting—a phrase that flows from them like water trickling over stones. I have learned a few of those liquid syllables, enough to respond in kind. I notice a faint glint of satisfaction in their eyes when I do; we share this place, after all, and so we share a common language of breath and water. And though I have fewer words than they do, my silent acts—helping with a block that’s about to shift, cupping a flame in my hand to shield it from a stray breeze, guiding amphibians to a safer pool—speak for me.

In time, I have grown able to creep through narrow corridors and slip into half-forgotten chapel areas, crouching by slow-dripping crevices to watch the watery trickle gather and swirl. My hair, bound in ragged braids, sometimes picks up flecks of clay or algae, until the strands themselves show a living mosaic of sediment and filament. My skin, though not as thick as the stooped laborers’, has become more resilient, and I no longer shiver at every cold draft. If I brush up against a streaming trickle, I merely let the water run down my neck and settle into the folds of my woven wrap.

Often I find myself near the deeper channels that connect the labyrinth’s distant corners. Here, the temperature drops further. The clay walls glisten with a nearly black sheen, and the water’s surface is darker yet, as though it holds entire worlds in its slow reflections. In these corridors, the presence of those larger, hidden shapes is almost palpable. Sometimes I hear a rush of movement below the surface, or sense a ripple that hints at something vast sliding through. If the creatures ever surface, they linger at the edges of the flickering lamplight, sleek humps of scale or fin just visible. On my boldest days, I dip a carved wooden ladle into the inky water and sip the cold that seeps through my teeth. I like to think these great ones sense my small gesture of communion—perhaps they can taste that I have become part clay, part water too.

Now and then, a visitor will wander in—someone foreign, a shape of dryness and stiff posture that steps uncertainly into the gloom. I recognize the look in their eyes: the same sense of awe and wariness I once felt. They peer at me and see a being not quite amphibian, not quite the tall keepers of the halls either. They might ask in a timid whisper, “What is this place?” or, “Who lives here?” and I find that I can answer with a soft swirl of vowels, or a gesture that leads them to safety. Often, I’ll guide them to a place where the stooped folk or the tall ones gather, so they can find a seat near a flickering lamp and let the hush of this realm seep into their bones. I always watch to see if they will stay or slip away. Many do not remain; it takes a particular heart to embrace these dank corridors as home. But some do, and we accept them quietly, teaching them how to move in tune with the perpetual drip.

In my dreams, I sometimes wonder if I’ll see the sun again. Perhaps I will—there are hidden vents and open courtyards that catch slices of daylight, though they are never quite bright. I also dream that my footprints in the clay are soon worn away by the gentle flow of new water, replaced by fresh footsteps of future dwellers. And I find I am content with that: my presence here is as fluid as the rest of it. I flow through, just as the water does, shaping and being shaped in turn.

I understand, now, that I am a bridge. Not an architect of grand designs, nor a secret guardian of lost halls, but a gentle caretaker of edges, forever guiding the damp currents that unify this settlement of moss and stone. My breath mingles with the amphibians’ call, my footsteps echo softly in the corridors. I lean over cracked sections of clay, pressing them gently back into place. I take each day in the hush of dripping water, in the flicker of swampy light, in the chorus of scuttling creatures that vanish when the torch beams grow too near. A small, persistent presence in a world that breathes with the hush and ripple of the tide.

And so I remain, neither fully amphibian nor fully human, one of many, quietly part of the labyrinth where water seeps through every pore. Here, the passageways mold me as much as I shape them, each of us discovering an equilibrium that is never final but perpetually, gloriously damp and alive. And in that interplay—of clay under my fingernails and low lanterns brushing the walls—I have found my place, and in it, my being.
