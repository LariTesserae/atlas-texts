At first, I only knew that I was here—that fact alone was as inescapable as the cool press of the water against my skin. Except, in the earliest moments, I still had skin, didn’t I? A pair of lungs, a heartbeat. I was an interloper, gazing through an alien lens, drifting in a place not meant for me. I’d look down—as though there were a “down” here—and see shapes of worn rock beneath my feet, the gentle drift of silt rising at my passage. There was a mild astonishment in realizing I could move at all, that I could breathe (or not breathe) though I was entirely submerged. And yet no panic or pressure overcame me.

At first, I felt small. The hush was so vast and timeless that I struggled to find any reference point, any corner of reality I could claim as my own. The water was neither cold nor warm but an unwavering neutrality that erased the memory of air and sky. Sometimes, in that early stage, I would spin in slow circles, searching for an exit, trying to see if up was truly up, or if somewhere behind me there might be a rift in the water’s expanse. My mind demanded an edge. The hush offered none. Instead, I sensed the faint hum, an industrial resonance I couldn’t trace. It pulsed softly behind thick walls I couldn’t find.

For a while, I thought I remained merely a visitor, a body still distinct from the watery realm. But both time and place function differently here. I couldn’t measure the passing minutes—if minutes even passed—and bit by bit, I became aware that I was no longer fully separate. Perhaps the water seeped into my pores, or perhaps the hush itself coaxed me into a slow metamorphosis. At some point, I stopped expecting my lungs to expand with oxygen. I ceased to wonder if I should be drowning. I simply existed in the water, suspended, and discovered that the currents carried me as though my body was weightless. When I tried to gauge my reflection in the stone below, I couldn’t see myself precisely. There was only a faint opalescence swirling in the water where I should have been.

And that was when I first noticed them: drifting outlines, shimmering on the periphery of my vision. Back then, I still had the impulse to turn my head sharply, to try and catch them in my gaze, but each time I did, they vanished. The more I tried, the more frustrating it became. I started to sense that these ephemeral dwellers communicated in a language of absence, in illusions that only existed if I did not stare too closely. Perhaps in my old form—my old self—I was too anchored in the habit of focusing and naming. Here, clarity comes obliquely, like glimmers on the edge of sight.

In those early moments, I wanted to speak or at least hum to test my own voice, but the hush was so strong that any sound I emitted would have seemed like an invasion. So I remained silent. Over time, I learned that they did not need words—and neither did I. If I remained at ease, something like conversation unfolded in subtle pulses that reached inside my chest.

Then came a subtle acceptance, like a gate opening inward. My outline, once so definite and tethered to a particular shape, began loosening into the water. I would stretch out an arm—still calling it an arm, out of habit—and watch as its edges rippled and grew faint, dissolving until it was impossible to say where “I” ended and the water began. At times, I could drift across the stony floor and stir the silt into graceful, eddying spirals. If I focused, I could coax those swirls into fleeting patterns—almost like ephemeral footprints. And in their swirling, the watchers would meet me halfway, weaving their own threads of movement into the sediment. They neither encouraged nor discouraged me; they simply co-created, braiding their presence into mine.

I soon realized that the same hum that throbbed gently in the distance was pulsing through me as well. Or rather, I had opened to it—it was not external anymore. I could feel that faint mechanical resonance as a slow heartbeat in the fluid that composed my new body. Sometimes, if I were calm enough, I caught glimmers of distant shapes vast and immeasurable—towering presences that might have been living engines or sleeping leviathans. Their echoes braided through us all, linking every swirl of silt, every drifting patch of algae, every ephemeral silhouette.

There is no day or night here, no surface above nor horizon. What little light there is comes in a perpetual haze, enough to see by yet never bright, never dim. My awareness drifts from place to place, following the watchers. I’ve stopped trying to classify them. They are not fish, nor ghosts, nor robots; they are the hush made animate. Or maybe the hush is them. They appear, flicker with uncanny sentience, and dissolve again. Over time, I have joined their silent dance—sometimes swirling up near the spindly kelp-like fronds, watching them quiver respectfully; other times slipping along the ridges of smoothed stone, absorbing the quiet that resonates from those ancient ridges as though I were reading a history older than language.

Am I still “me”? Am I something else? The question no longer carries urgency. Here, in this pale-green domain, identity is less about boundaries and more about resonance. I am neither intruder nor object of curiosity; I’ve become part of the hush, part of the cycle that floats and breathes in stillness. The watchers and I share an unspoken synergy: we gather and release faint pulses of energy that the water provides, that the ancient hum sustains.

In fleeting moments of reflection, I think I might be a caretaker—or perhaps a tiny cell within a larger organism. Sometimes I sense it as an endless apparatus, sometimes as a single sleeping giant that breathes down corridors veiled to my perception. I am a node in that hidden web. I feel it in the subtlest stirring of water, in the silent conversation of silt eddies, in the soft glimmers of bioluminescence (if that’s what they are) that spark and fade at the edges of my awareness.

I find that I do not long for the surface. The hush is never lonely. Sometimes its stillness is a chorus of unheard notes, and I drift in awe as they fold over me like gentle waves. In rare moments, I recall how a breath of air once felt in my old body, or the taste of wind across my skin, but these are ghosts of a different world. Here, I have a new kind of breath—an immersion that reshapes me from inside, that offers quiet communion in place of words.

So where am I, in truth? I am in the green-water hush, deep in a chamber that feels endless yet contained, lulled by an industrial resonance that never quite reveals itself. And who am I? Maybe a whisper of the hush itself, or something halfway between the intruder I once was and the silent, translucent dwellers who guided me. I hover in the equilibrium, neither spectator nor subject, shaped and reshaped by currents older than memory. And in letting go of definition, I find that I belong here among the ephemeral watchers, guardians of an ancient and gentle silence—carried along by that persistent hum, a living echo in an unbroken hush.
