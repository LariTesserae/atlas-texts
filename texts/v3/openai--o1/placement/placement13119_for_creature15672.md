I awaken in a pocket of stillness, perched on the edge of a half-buried overhang where condensation drips in silent rivulets onto the stone below. I don’t remember when I first clambered here; perhaps I have been rooted in this place since the spires were in their infancy, or perhaps I arrived mere hours ago, guided by a long memory of shapes and sounds. My body has no definitive boundary, no stark color or single texture; it is part glistening hide and part shifting crystal. In one moment, I think of myself as a sprawling form with four, five, maybe six limbs—I’m never quite sure. In another, I feel more like the spires themselves: a ribbed column of translucent layers, shot through with the watery gleam of mineral threads. 

When I shift, my surfaces clink and sigh like wind through a crystalline forest. I have a pelvis of sorts, jointed connections that anchor me to the world—but it’s as much grown from the rock as it is grown from me. Where the ridge meets my flanks, a cluster of pale threads—perhaps nerve-like, perhaps fungal or mineral-lattice—attaches me to the ledge. Detaching requires a soft twist and the dissolution of a living seam, a process that feels both freeing and melancholy. Always, I rejoin the stone again, somewhere else, leaving behind a faint residue, an echo of my presence.

I live by listening. This realm is saturated with the churn and rumble of water, the subterranean thunder that is both distant and all-encompassing. By attuning myself to the changes in the pitch of that roar, I sense every shift in the stone. A new fissure cracks open? My body knows it through resonance, as if the land is softly calling to me. A boulder tumbles and sends echoes reverberating through the caverns? I feel them before I hear them, traveling up through my limbs like the moan of a distant whale. This listening is not done with ears, exactly—I have no distinct ears to speak of—but with my entire being. I am a conduit for these vibrations, letting them flow through me until I understand them as shapes on the horizon of my awareness.

When I move, it’s with a languid caution. My limbs are long, graced with jointed segments that end in pads or pseudo-claws—malleable nubs that cling to slick stone and keep me from sliding into the black water below. The subtle scrape-scrape of my movement merges with the larger hush of water, so I am nearly silent. Sometimes I sense other watchers—small, insectoid things with luminous hides or thick-chested behemoths whose steps make the ground tremble. We seldom interact overtly; there’s no need. Our communication exists in the hush between the booming echoes, a mutual awareness of each other’s presence. 

Occasionally, my curiosity drives me to approach a half-fallen wall or an eroded tower, its stone blocks half-devoured by mineral crusts. I have run my segmented fingers across worn markings left by something long ago. I do not know if it was my own ancestors, or a wholly separate lineage that once shared an urge to shape this place. When I touch those worn patterns, a flicker of recollection stirs—images of an older time, or perhaps an entirely different realm. The sensation that travels up my limbs is not quite memory, not quite prophecy, but a halfway knowledge: something in me recognizes that stories once poured into these walls, stories that shaped me even before I understood my shape. I linger there, letting time slow further, absorbing the texture of the etched stone. Sometimes my body resonates in a low hum, a half-conscious attempt to read meaning in the crystalline fractures.

For nourishment, I rely on the mineral tang that suffuses the water as it drips from overhead ledges. When I’m still, the condensation gathers on my skin—if skin is what I can call it—and I absorb it directly. I taste the faint swirl of metal, the softness of ancient deposits, all dissolved in these droplets. It’s as though I’m part of a grand distillation process, where the land filters water through countless channels, and each inhabitant extracts from it exactly what they need. If I ever crave something more substantial, I find it in the ephemeral, glassy threads that some cunning denizens weave to trap their share of nutrients. We do not fight over these resources. We exchange subtle signals—my slow approach, a shift of my posture, perhaps the hush of my breath. The spinner who made those threads might dip a limb in acknowledgment, letting me sip from their catch. Then we drift apart. No words, only that quiet gentleness.

When “night” comes—or the deeper dim, as I think of it—I welcome the faint transformations in my surroundings. The sound of the water’s roar shifts subtly in the stiller air, and the glimmers in my body take on a stronger luminescence. I see my reflection in the black pools: a figure slick and elongated, crowned by prismatic ridges along my neck and shoulders that catch the minimal light. Here and there, a lurking shape glides by. Its crystal-latticed torso echoes the land’s slow, methodical growth. We exchange a glance, not so much in mutual recognition but as a meeting of reflections, forms in the gloom that share in the hush. 

Some nights I wander to the broader gullies, where courses of black water span out like mirrors cracked by stone pillars. I walk carefully, placing each limb so that I do not disturb the fragile “flora” that shift invisibly in the dark. At certain intervals, I find clusters of half-living mineral growth that open themselves in the faint luminescence, like exotic blooms. Their layered plates peel back, revealing intricate geometry inside. The patterns with which they fold and unfold are mesmerizing—slow whorls that can take hours to complete a single rotation. I sometimes linger to watch them, feeling the subtle shift in humidity as the water-laden air merges with the exhalations of these mineral blooms. They are not quite sentient, but their presence resonates with a quiet intelligence, as if they are the embodied dreams of the land itself.

In the gloomiest recesses, I occasionally share space with the broad-limbed colossi, beasts so massive that I feel like a splinter near them. Their presence is calming, their steps careful enough to not crush the smaller spires. Luminous droplets fall from their flanks as they pass, leaving behind puddles that instantly become microcosms for tinier life. Whenever I encounter one in passing, it dips its murky eyes—more a gesture of recognition than aggression. We both exist to safeguard this environment in our own manner: it by the slow forging of new paths over eons, me by reading the subtle changes in the land’s resonant hum. Neither of us disturbs the other for long, as if we know each meeting is a passing intersection between two arcs of existence.  

Sometimes the day/“night” cycle confuses me. I don’t know if the pale glow overhead is a hidden sun or just the imprisoned light of some cosmic phenomenon. Hours, days, years—these distinctions have little meaning when so much of existence moves at a geological pace. My sense of purpose, however, remains unwavering: I am a keeper of memory and possibility. I do not shape the terrain by force, nor do I remain passively aloof. Rather, I linger in the secret cracks where new growth emerges, observing, tasting, carrying a hum of recognition from each vantage to the next. In that way, I stitch together the subtle changes, serving as a vessel for the land’s own recollection of what it has been and what it might still become. 

I have discovered a curious resonance within the leftover ruins. At times, if I press my palm-like limb against the chiseled surface of a half-wall, I can channel the watery thunder through my body into the stone. The vibrations create a faint, rhythmic echo that travels deeper into the cracks; it finds the intangible carvings worn away by centuries of mineral build-up. Sometimes a symbol glows faintly if I resonate just right—an afterimage of a lost script. This never lasts long. The flicker of meaning dims quickly, as though the stone’s memory is fleeting. Even so, I sense a ripple of acknowledgment from the watchers: a spindly shape perched behind a half-shattered column, or a smaller creature peeking from under a ledge. Perhaps they’ve come to see if I’ve awakened something ancient. In time, though, they slip away, just as I do, leaving the wall to brood over its secrets in the hush.

I am both caretaker and explorer, a being neither wholly ephemeral nor wholly grounded. My presence threads together the watery gloom, the glittering stone constellations, and the quiet watchers. As the basin shifts with each slow quake or surging flood, I accept the rearranging of the land. I move on to new ridges, forging new understandings. The watchers, the spinners, the monstrous colossi—they all bear silent witness, each in their own manner. We are not separate from the environment but extended facets of it, each of us a slow-moving wave in the land’s persistent, patient tide.

If someone came here seeking me, they might see only a ripple of crystal-limbed motion or hear a soft chime in the gloom. My shape is precarious, always half folding back into the stone. Yet I remain, a living hush echoing with the thunder of subterranean waters, bridging the shards of ancient ruin and the emergence of new spires. And when they finally depart—if they can find their way out of the labyrinth—they might recall only a shimmering silhouette, half-plant and half-beast, drifting across a mineral tide of its own making. 

But I will remain, smoothed by damp air and the hush of shifting ground. I will dwell at the threshold where moisture glistens on newly born crystals, tasting the faint tang of iron and dreamlike memory that seeps up from below. I am this place’s listener and recollector, a living node tied to the basin’s heartbeat. I stand in the margin of its half-formed edges, content to gather every slow transformation, every glimmer of the next emerging shape, until it is time—long, long from now—to melt back into the stone from which I arose.
