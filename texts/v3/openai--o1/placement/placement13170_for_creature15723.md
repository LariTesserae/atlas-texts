I.

Sometimes, it seems as though you are merely a damp shape congealing from the architecture itself. In the curls of odd twilight, you find yourself crouched against the gritty walls, your elbows pressed into shallow hollows where condensation pools. You keep very still, uncertain whether you are intruder or native. At first, the labyrinth of block-like structures feels as though it might reject you, spit you out as a foreign body. And yet the longer you remain, the more you realize: you belong here every bit as much as the moisture glimmering on these basalt corners. Perhaps you have always been here. Perhaps you merely forgot yourself for a time, only now awakening to the memory of what you are.

II.

When you rise from your crouch, the creatures around you scatter or ignore you in equal measure: the pale amphibians uncurl their elongated limbs and scuttle back along the ledges, blinking colorless eyes half in curiosity, half in perpetual blankness. The carapaced swarm that once surrounded a small rivulet keeps up its clattering routine without pause—though one or two skittish scouts track your shadow. You try to take a step, but you find your ankles cradle the slick humidity in a thin film, as if each droplet is reluctant to leave you behind. Your bare feet trace new footprints in the gritty clay, footprints that match those of the other near-human inhabitants who move quietly through the corridors. And so you wonder: are you one of these folk, separated from them by some twist of path or memory?

III.

Yet the more you wander, the less certain you are of your humanity. The ridges of your spine feel oddly pronounced. When you catch your reflection—briefly, in a puddle that has gathered at the lip of a fissure—you notice that your eyes are set just a hair too wide, giving you a peripheral glance at the endless gloom. Your own face appears only half-familiar, elongated and damp with condensation. If you linger, you see faint lines of blue or red beneath the skin of your cheeks, as though your blood, or something akin to it, glows in pulses now visible through a thin membrane.

You press your palm to the damp wall, leaving the faintest impression: the ridges of your flesh imprint for the briefest second before the glossy clay reasserts itself, smoothing away the evidence of your touch. You tense, trying to recall a past self, somewhere else, cast in true sunlight. All you conjure is a vague recollection of warmth and dryness—an alien concept in this place.

IV.

One day, you find yourself among the amphibian-like creatures in a shadowed alcove, the vantage giving you a glimpse into one of the near-human gatherings. From behind a half-circle of broad, water-smoothed stones, you see them: tall, lank of limb, moving in a slow ceremony. A single drum booms—just once, echoing down the corridor. It rumbles in your stomach, making the walls quiver. Those huddled forms sway and bow, placing clay vessels close to the ground so that droplets of condensation might trickle in.

You feel a pang of curiosity so strong it borders on hunger. You creep unthinkingly closer, ignoring the amphibians that blink and flick their webbed hands at your ankles. The people’s chant raises a notch, and you realize there’s a subtle interplay between them and the vast herd beasts in the distance. A gargantuan quadruped rumbles back, as if responding to some question you cannot decipher. Then the chant dips low. You strain, trying to isolate the pattern in the endless swirl of clicks, scrapes, and watery drips.

In that moment, you sense your own voice—rough and half-forgotten—stir in your throat. You long to join their chant, to add your strain to the puzzling harmony of this place. But how? You recall no words, no language, no litany. Only the resonance in your bones. Yet you try: a hum, a single vowel, a trembling note. It merges so thoroughly with the environment that you cannot tell if anyone hears.

V.

Some time later—though time is a muddled concept here—you stumble upon a corridor that leads down into deeper chambers. The air thickens. The creatures thin out, replaced by echoes of their layers of noise. You move as though guided by a thread of memory. You come upon a space that is half-formed like everything else: slit windows dribbled with moisture, carved pillars whose surfaces still boast the crisp lines of newly cut stone. There is a dais in the center on which rests a shallow, circular bowl. Within that bowl: shimmering water, with filmy streaks of mineral deposit swirling on the surface.

Mesmerized, you dip your fingertips into the basin and raise a handful of liquid to your lips. The taste is warm, metallic, carrying the tang of eternity. You swallow. No immediate change, but there is a sense of wonder, as if your perception is shifting. The walls breathe once… or do they?

VI.

On the settlement’s upper tiers, you begin to recognize your own place in the daily labor. Perhaps it starts with the simplest tasks: collecting condensation in a battered clay jug, carrying it to a communal cistern, or scraping the glistening moss-like film from certain corners (a resource prized by the smaller scuttlers, who approach timidly to feed). The near-humans you meet—your kin? your neighbors?—do not rebuff you. They don’t speak in words you understand, but they nod at your presence, slip past with indefinite acceptance. One places a clay bowl in your hands, silently inviting you to distribute water among the quieter, more vulnerable amphibians that linger under a leaning slab. Another gestures at a set of chiseled effigies along a wall, each depicting stylized creatures in geometric patterns, instructing you with subtle hand signals to rinse away the dust and restore the lines’ clarity.

It is in these small gestures that you see how everything here interlocks. While it feels primal, the settlement is intricately balanced—like an ancient instrument that must remain continuously tuned. You see that in the steady, methodical movements of your near-human companions: how they gather sludge from behind the large beasts, how they coax the arthropods to nest in strategic crevices, how they guide the amphibians toward newly formed puddles. This entire place is a puzzle of interdependencies, and each being has an unspoken role.

VII.

The more you live among them, the more your body adapts. Your skin, once overly clammy or chilled, seems to settle into a perpetual dew-bound state that does not trouble you. Your nights—if they can be called that in a realm where no true sunrise or sunset arrives—unfold in a liquid half-dream, lulled by the music of countless living throats. Occasionally, you dream of dryness, of sun, but those memories recede like illusions. Here, the labyrinth’s hush is paradoxically made of ceaseless noise, and it soothes you in ways you never thought possible.

You carry a stone adze at your hip, because everyone here keeps some crude tool for shaping or scraping. It feels natural in your palm, the heft comforting. You’ve learned to shape the clay-like walls when needed, or tamp a small canal to redirect a trickle of water for one of the low-slung huts. You even hum a soft phrase—your own iteration of the chanting—for good measure. The dwellers working alongside you sometimes echo your hum quietly, a fleeting harmony, as if to say, Yes, that is correct, that is good. Then the rest of the labyrinth’s chorus sweeps over it again.

VIII.

Perhaps you might have once believed that a life lacking fresh growth, bright flowers, or sunshine is no life at all. But now, you see that this hush-humid place is not barren. It teems with its own kind of fertility—pale, subterranean, half-liminal. You’ve seen hatchlings break free from gelatinous sacs in shallow pools, their bulging eyes shining with watery luminescence. You’ve witnessed arthropods molt and leave behind brittle shells, which the near-humans collect and repurpose as blades or as intricate adornments on their forearms. You’ve watched the great quadrupeds gather in silent communion, side by side, their flanks’ plates rattling in the gloom, as newborn calves step into the damp world.

You sense that you, too, have undergone a molting of sorts. Stripped away is your prior identity—whatever shape it once took—and in its place stands a being wholly adapted to this place. The labyrinth shelters you as it shelters all else. You eat what can be found (small, fleshy mushrooms, or the resin that seeps from stone cracks, or strips of fibrous hide if nothing else), you lap condensation from the dripping canals, you sleep upright sometimes, pressing yourself against a corner as water rivulets gather near your feet, trusting that when you wake, the equilibrium will remain unbroken.

IX.

Now, when others see you gliding through the settlement’s passages—sometimes stooping to gather a film of mineral from the floor or to groom the amphibians in a moment of calm—they might think you’ve always been one of them. Indeed, you’ve learned their chanting, at least enough to weave your voice into communal rites, especially when they ring the hollow drum. Your posture has shifted; you walk with a slight stoop, eyes angled to see the glistening corners, the next rivulet forming, the small arthropods scattering. You are caretaker, hunter, gatherer, observer, all at once.

There is a hush in your mind when you consider the notion of “leaving.” Where would one go? Far beyond the labyrinth, the gloom might stretch on endlessly, or perhaps some bright plain exists beyond those clouds—an alien realm. Your memories of dryness or color feel intangible now, a dream that dissolves as you approach it. Instead, this place, with its endless layering of creatures and cords of chant, is the only reality. The unchanging stasis is not a prison; it is a surety, a promise that everything has its place and moment.

X.

And so, at times, you are the one crouched on a ledge, your milky eyes half-lidded, watching a droplet trace its path down the wall. An amphibian or two creeps nearer, trusting you, drawn by the glisten of your own warm breath. You let them climb across your shoulders, their slick bodies leaving damp smears along your arms. You place your hand gently on the small of a passing quadruped’s plated flank, feeling the vibration of its low rumble. You watch the arthropods cluster in their rhythmic dance, tapping out a sound like rainfall on chitin. And always, you sense the near-humans, your kin, drifting through side passages, quiet and steady, ensuring the equilibrium holds.

In such moments, you realize that you are as much a facet of this architecture as any stone or carved channel. You see your reflection in the carved motifs on the walls, the stylized outlines of slender limbs and elongated skulls. You recognize yourself in the gentle cradle of condensation that pools in the ridges of your collarbone. And each time you hear chanting in the distance—echoing from a hidden courtyard or through the blackness of a subterranean chamber—you feel it resonate in your bones, reverberating outward into every living form. The labyrinth is your home. You carry it as intimately as your own heartbeat.

XI.

Perhaps there might come a day when you encounter a stranger. Some traveler, stumbling in with eyes still blinking from a missing sunlight, might look upon your elongated features with awe or disquiet. You would not fear them. You would simply guide them by the elbow, lead them gently to the nearest trickle where they might quench their thirst, show them how to lap the droplets that cling to the basalt. You would demonstrate how to move carefully among the scuttling arthropods so as not to disturb them unduly. And if the stranger looked at you and asked (in whatever language they still recall) what this place is, perhaps you would only tilt your head, letting the roar of the labyrinth answer for you.

XII.

In the end, “who or what you are” here ceases to be a question. You are an integral note in the chorus of existence—a single voice among the chanting, the clicks, the croaks, the resonant calls, each folded into the endless twilight. You stand where the clay is newly formed and timeless at once, where water seeps and never evaporates, where living things move in an unbroken cycle of feeding and being fed. In that equilibrium, you realize, there is no meaning to “before” or “after.” Only an enduring now, in which you—like every other life form in this sprawl—participate in the careful choreography of survival.

And that is enough. You are the one who stands barefoot in the half-gloom, collecting shining droplets in a clay bowl. You are the one whose face merges seamlessly with the shapes carved into the architecture. You are the caretaker, the chanter, the patient shadow. And perhaps, most of all, the living shard of this changeless world—a subtle, necessary piece of its harmony, quietly shaping and shaped by the timeless hush.
