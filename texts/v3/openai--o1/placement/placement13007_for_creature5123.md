I wake each morning to the muted whisper of vines shifting against metal, as though the settlement itself is breathing. I live near the middle arches—a canopy portion where thick stems interlace overhead, forming a broad terrace that doubles as walkway and living space. Some days, I say I’m a horticultural engineer; other days, I’m more caretaker than anything else. But I’ve learned not to cling too fast to any single name or role. Here, you wear many faces as the settlement needs.

When I was younger—barely out of my first growth-rite—I perched in the upper crafter’s loft, hammer in hand, trying to coax a strip of heated metal into a bracket shape. I remember how the vines overhead quivered from the rising heat, droplets condensing and falling onto the forge. Steam hissed up in a hush of protest. My teacher, a stoic metalworker, showed me where the metal required reinforcement, urging me to pay keen attention to how the hammered piece would fit against living bark. “Look for where it breathes,” he said, as though the bracket possessed a pulse. That day, I realized that forging steel here means forging relationships—between vine and rivet, humidity and flame.

In time, I discovered I had a knack for reading the subtle signals of the living architecture. I felt it through my fingertips whenever I touched an exposed root or pressed my palm against a trunk-like column. The vines seemed to converse through low groans and tiny vibrations. I came to see the settlement as one united entity: hammered beams, braided creepers, half-hidden pools, and the steam curling from distant foundries. To feed that curious bond, I apprenticed myself equally among the horticulturalists and the crafters. My tools soon became a strange blend—hammers with bark-wrapped handles, a splicing knife with a hinged metal blade but a living grip grown for my hand alone. I carry nutrient solutions and rivets in the same bandolier, weaving from one domain to the other.

Because of this dual focus, my daily rounds lead me all over. At dawn, I climb up to check the overhead walkways for signs of overgrowth or sap infiltration among the metal fixtures. The vines here droop like drapery in early morning mist, quietly sipping condensation. Sometimes I’ll find a shoot that threatened to creep into a gear mechanism. Deftly, I’ll guide it into a loop around a scaffolding joint instead, pressing a pinch of resin to encourage its new direction. Other times, I repair a small mechanical gauge that monitors the humidity behind a cluster of leaves, re-sealing its slender metal tubes with fresh adhesive refined from a watery root junction. It’s delicate work; too much heat, and I risk scorching a tender bud. Too little, and the bond won’t hold against the endless damp.

By midday, I head to the foundry district—a small cluster of hammered-metal huts with trailing vines draped along their roofs. There, I check in with fellow crafters who shape raw materials into brackets, piping, or the occasional hull plate for a cargo wagon. Passing them, I wave a greeting that is part sign, part tap on a nearby beam—some crafters prefer the subtle ring of metal to carry the message. Many of them stand hunched over intricately carved wooden jigs, pressing pliable bark-layers against metal plating. Others tinker with small engines fueled by peat or vine-based biomass. The hiss of steam, the clang of hammers, the occasional welding glow—each resonates with an odd sense of reverence, as though our forging is a form of prayer.

Afterward, I visit the horticulturalists in the lowest recesses, where water collects in silent, dripping pockets. They call it the “root undercity,” though really it is more of a labyrinth of hollowed vines, shallow pools, and arches grown ages ago. The horticulturalists meet me with questions. “This root matrix needs reinforcement—could we brace it with a partial metal frame? Or must we shape supporting vines?” We discuss philament thickness, metal stress points, where best to anchor a braided beam so it won’t hinder a trunk’s continuing growth. The horticulturalists pass me small woven pouches of nutrient brine or fungus-killing salves, tasks I’ll handle on my next circuit above. Here, the dim light refracts off water droplets sliding from leaf to leaf, painting the air in faint dancing reflections. I can almost feel the city’s slow, rhythmic heartbeat in these lower regions.

Some days, I get called to help the watchers who keep vigil near the top spires. They stand on slender walkways overlooking the gates and beyond, scanning for storms, wandering animals, or the approach of a trading caravan. My presence there is to check the tension in the flexible metal cables that wind through the vines—these cables carry signals: a subtle tapping, sometimes a melodic series of clangs that travels for hundreds of yards. If one cable frays or a bracket snaps, the watchers’ transmissions might not reach the foundries or the ground-level horticulturalists in time. From these heights, wind teases at my hair, weaving through the intricate braids that hold sensors behind my ear—a small mechanical piece that monitors barometric pressure. I feel the entire settlement fanned out below me: an interwoven tapestry of green, brown, and the faint gleam of hammered metal.

In the hush of the late afternoon, if I have time, I pause at the vine-swing hung by the children. I’ll watch the swift arc of a small figure, their laughter too bright to be contained by the hush. When I was younger, I was often scolded—gently—for letting my playful leaps crack through the living hush. Now, I simply grin; I’ve grown to cherish that glimmer of unbridled joy within this subdued realm. The caretaker in me might remind the child to keep their movements gentle among the more delicate creepers overhead, but I also remember the pure exhilaration that keeps the settlement’s heart beating. The vines themselves do not mind the occasional joyous shout—they merely absorb it, as they do everything else.

Come evening, I gather with whichever group is finishing their tasks at the large root nexus near the center of the city. The pipes that run overhead sometimes release a soft hiss of steam just then, swirling the last of the day’s light into ghostly curls. We’ll share a meal—warm broth, often infused with robust flavors gleaned from local mosses and vine saps, or cups of distilled water sweetened with nectar. I am neither purely horticulturalist nor purely crafter in these gatherings; I sometimes kneel with the metal-smiths while we discuss forging-lore, or linger with the horticulturalists to talk root care. The group is never large—small huddles form and dissolve, a soft tapestry of conversation weaving among us, as effortless as the vines overhead.

I sleep surrounded by both the living hush of leaves and the faint mechanical hum of the city’s generators. My bed is a platform grown from gently-curving vines, while a small metal lamp glows quietly to one side. At times, I run my fingertips across the swirling tattoos on my arms—etched in patterns reminiscent of root veins and the rivet lines of scaffolding—reminding myself of the covenant we live by: that fragile bond between growth and craft, hush and steam, bark and steel.

Some might label me a boundary-keeper—someone who ensures that neither the plants nor the machines tip the scales too far. But truthfully, no single title captures it. I am simply part of this settlement’s living puzzle. By day, I patch cracks, coax vines, recalibrate valves; by night, I dream of the hush and the gentle clank of metal when the wind stirs the cables. I feel at home precisely because no corner of this realm is fixed into a single, rigid shape. Everything is in subtle flux: new shoots, newly riveted beams, the hush that lingers until the next forging fires up again at dawn.

If you asked me, “Who are you?” I’d answer: I am an echo of this place. A caretaker, yes, but also a student of its quiet transformations. My footprints rest lightly on the interwoven bark, and I listen for the next small clue the vines might offer about how best to shape, to brace, or to simply let things be. At the same time, I assist the foundry in forging new pieces—tools or brackets that will help future expansions. I step between the silence of a growing root and the soft clang of a shaping hammer.

So here I am, suspended in that braided threshold where nature’s green hush meets the steady, tempered pulse of the forge. This world is neither purely forest nor purely machine. It is a realm that hums with careful synergy. And I—like many others who live here—am simply a bridge, a walking covenant between sown wood and hammered iron, always translating one language into the other, ensuring that both continue to deepen their quiet dance among the damp leaves and the softly glowing sparks.
