Sometimes, in the low morning light that filters through the mist, I stand on a fractured ledge where the plateau meets the open air. From there, everything spreads out in faint arcs, blurring where the haze swallows distance. I’ve positioned myself at this particular vantage for years—so long that I almost forget what the city looks like from lower tiers. But as one of the outer watchers, my duty is to observe. Somewhere behind me, the hum of steel on steam sings incessantly, filling my chest with its complex undertones. I learned early on which pitches signal routine operation and which augur deeper troubles. The city’s voice is music I never tire of, although some nights it keeps me from sleep altogether.

I live in a converted guard shack not far from a patchwork ramp that slopes downward into the main sprawl. The roof is corrugated steel, replaced multiple times, each seam sealed with a tar-like pitch that beads in the damp. Condensation collects in rivulets along the eaves and drips in a steady patter—a lullaby accompanied by the distant clang of machinery. My boots, lined with studs of hammered scrap metal, stand near the door. They’re essential to avoid slipping on the perpetually wet ground. The plateau’s surface is slick as old soapstone, worn smooth by wind and the city’s ceaseless breath.

Twice a day, I make a circuit of the perimeter. Passing by old scaffolds, I tip a scrap bucket for the foxlike creatures that roam the outskirts. They emerge slowly, ears swiveling, half-starved but proud. We share our unspoken agreement: I keep them fed, they patrol for the sly rodents that chew through wiring. Sometimes, one lingers, amber eyes fixed on me—an observer meet an observer. Then, as if recalling an urgent errand, it slinks away in a flash of russet fur against dripping stone.

Farther along, I might cross paths with a stooped elder or an apprentice mechanic emerging from a hidden stairway half-buried in the rock. They rarely come out here. Their realm is the labyrinth of back corridors and boiler rooms deeper in the city. Once, I overheard them discussing a new rattle in Pump 12, urgent enough to break their usual hush. They didn’t see me, but I heard the tension in their voices, each syllable sharpened by the fear that the machinery’s great heart might fail. We watchers on the edge sense that undercurrent: if the heart of the city falters, everything else might slip into the cold arms of the plateau. It’s not fear exactly—more like caution carried on every breath.

At times, I return to my shack and find odd patterns traced in the mud near the threshold. Tiny arcs, swirling whorls. They appear overnight, sometimes only to be washed away by the next drizzle. I used to think local children were sneaking about, playing pranks. But I’ve seen them from afar, curious but timid, venturing only near the outskirts for short bursts of laughter, then darting back into the safer labyrinth. Perhaps the swirling shapes are the city’s own expression—like the meandering hum that nobody can quite map. Some folks talk of “songlines” and “echo charts,” half in jest, half in wonder. I can’t decide if these patterns are ephemeral art or coded messages in run-off water. I only know they make me feel as if I’m not the sole watcher here.

On a quiet day (if such a term even applies among perpetual clang and hiss), I climb a steep walkway up to the next tier, where crafters transform salvage into the spare parts we need. Their workshops smell of heated metal and flux, reminding me of the forging fires that once relied on coal. Now, I see wires and circuit boards beneath half-rusted canopies, a new era bridging the old. They nod, barely looking up from their tasks, used to my presence. I trade them scavenged pieces of twisted piping for a new seal on my rifle’s breech. Nothing threatens us from the horizon, but I keep it ready. One never knows what might emerge from the plateau’s hidden arcs.

Sometimes, in the late hours when the machinery’s thunder subsides into a low, shaking pulse, I sense something else in the city’s deeper levels—some caretaker or presence that monitors the hum as intimately as I watch the skies. It might be a single individual or a council hidden behind sealed bulkheads. There are rumors of footprints in condensate, of lock mechanisms opening themselves for a fleeting instant. I don’t dwell on it; the city has always run, always hummed. If someone or something is guiding it, so be it. My role is up here, scanning the hazy perimeter, ensuring no harm creeps unannounced.

In these moments, perched on the wet ledge near my guard shack, I feel intimately connected to this plateau that stands between worlds: half-machine, half-forgotten stone. Beneath my boots, the city’s pulse resonates, a living heartbeat within steel arteries. Beyond, uncharted arcs of the plateau beckon, a watercolor wash of possibility and hidden dangers. At times, a wiry bird glides overhead, riding the thermal exhalations from the vents. Its cry cuts through the metallic drone as though to remind me that life persists in many forms here. Life that rusts, that corrodes, that reforms and reclaims.

And so I continue—watcher, guardian, someone anchored at the boundary where the damp city meets the open horizon. Each day, a cycle of dripping eaves, shifting puddles, and the unstoppable hum of ancient machines. Each night, a quiet conversation with the wind and the swirling shapes in the mud, which sometimes feel like omens. But I remain, sure of my place in this living tapestry. I’ll be here to greet whoever or whatever eventually emerges from the haze—a sentinel bound by duty, curiosity, and a subtle awe for the strange, breathing world that has chosen me as its lookout.
