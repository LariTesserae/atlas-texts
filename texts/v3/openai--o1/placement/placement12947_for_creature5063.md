I find myself neither a visitor nor an established resident at first, but a being gently waking from a wide, half-remembered dream. At first there is only the sense of weightless immersion, a hush of underwater silence offset by the distant thunder of currents. Then I become aware of my own shape: the faintly webbed tips of my fingers, the soft, pale hue of my arms curving in the bright water. It’s as if I am gradually coalescing out of the ambient shimmer of this place, a newcomer not quite fully formed, a shape finding itself here and now.

At first, the edges of my hands strike me as curious. A faint translucent quality glimmers at my wrists, and I notice it extends in subtle ribs through my forearms. My torso is similarly pale, though sculpted with the same graceful lines that define these halls—each part feels newly carved, unweathered. In the watery reflection of a broad, polished stone column, I see wide, dark eyes, the recollection of some deeper lineage drifting at their back. I realize I belong to the same people who move methodically about this watery fortress, though I cannot recall my first moments here. My memory is as brand-new as the stone blocks still settling into place.

The water feels warm, a gentle embrace that hums with a living pulse. I notice that with each subtle shift of my arms, the webs between my fingers stretch and catch the currents, letting me pivot or glide at will. And I do glide—exploring in small arcs around the base of a newly placed beam. There, I watch two older artisans press their bodies against the timber, sending out a soft, resonant hum. The beam answers with a deep groan of its own, as if stone, water, and wood are conversing. I feel a similar resonance humming in the hollow places of my chest, an urge to join them. Something inside me wants to sing along, to invite the structure ever deeper into being.

But I hesitate. For a few moments, I remain at a small distance, marveling at my surroundings: so many beveled edges and squared angles, so many interlocked wooden braces that hold these arches overhead. Everything around me appears powerful and young. I can sense the pride in each newly hammered bracket, each precisely placed stone. The dwelling itself is fresh and vibrant, like an infant with big, bright eyes, still growing into its limbs and senses.

My own impulses feel similarly untested. I flutter my fingers against a seam where stone meets wood, trying to feel the integrity of the joint. I don’t yet know the full language of these watery people, but the touch of my palm on the stone “speaks” something: an inaudible conversation in the hush of the currents. In it, I sense potential for another bracket here, or a thicker wooden pin there—small improvements that could keep everything snug. My breath (if one can call it breath underwater) catches in surprise to realize I intuit this: I, too, can shape this place.

Drawn by that revelation, I move to one of the older artisans who is carefully wedging a metal bracket into a corner. My approach is tentative. I watch how their claw-like fingertips press gently, then they pass me the mallet. A warm surge of excitement rushes through my limbs. I turn the mallet in my hands—simple and rough, carved wood likely bound with hammered iron at the tip. I realign the bracket according to the gentle tilt of the elder’s palm, then deliver a measured blow. Through my wrapped knuckles, I feel the immediate impact reverberate into the stone, the bracket, and me. The older artisan hums softly, approving. I’ve made my first contribution.

As we work, I notice I’m not alone: a second young one, easily distinguished by a faint swirl of turquoise bands on their shoulders, joins us. They hold a chisel. Our teacher silently indicates a slight protrusion on the block that needs smoothing. With a neat tap of the chisel, a small chip of stone flakes away, tumbling slowly in the water before sinking to the base of the column. I have the sense that we are part of a quiet but determined cycle—improving, shaping, aligning.

Now that my self-awareness burns more brightly, my curiosity stirs. I quietly leave my elders to their tasks and float closer to an orb of light fixed in the wall. It radiates with a primal, handcrafted glow—like a captured star shining steadily underwater. It occurs to me that I have no concept of day or night here; only the bright luminescence of these orbs and the watery shimmer overhead define time.  
 
I reach out, resting my webbed fingers around the orb. The surface is warm—almost unnaturally so. At my curious touch, it blazes a little brighter, the way a fire might flare when stoked. In that moment, I feel something akin to shared breath, as if the orb and I are exchanging a silent greeting. My reflection in the polished metal bracket around it reveals my wide, dark eyes, still quietly searching.  

I continue forward through a corridor, guided by the swirl of currents that lead deeper into the structure. Hinging panels open here and there, letting water pour through. The flow is surprisingly strong. Pausing beside one, I sense how the water’s force has been harnessed to keep everything in a constant cycle—cleansing, renewing. I wonder if the corridor beyond the panel might lead to expansions yet unbuilt, half-imagined halls waiting for shaping hands. The place feels alive with possibility.

Passing along this hallway, I come upon a smaller chamber set off to one side—a recess behind a row of carved pillars. Inside, I see a few woven mats pinned to the walls, presumably to cushion rest or perhaps to store tools. Upon slender ledges float covered ceramic jars, each containing the pale algae paste that sustains us. A pair of my kin hovers nearby, sharing in silent companionship while they carefully seal cracks in the chamber’s wooden frames. They greet me with a subtle nod when they notice my presence. No words are spoken, but a hush of acknowledgment passes between us—an invitation, should I wish to join their work or simply rest.

I decide to explore further, venturing through another archway where I see a group of four or five. Some are scraping barnacle-like growths from a large wooden brace, while others examine fresh metal fittings. The water vibrates with the low hum of conversation—though it is less language and more a collective resonance. Drawn by curiosity, I glide nearer. They part slightly, making space for me to observe. Then, with smiles in their eyes, they gesture at the wooden brace. Under their direction, I slip my claws underneath a misaligned joint, giving it a small adjustment until it slots neatly into place. We all pause to listen: the water thrums approval, or at least we interpret it that way. The brace, newly aligned, no longer creaks. I can’t help the faint flick of pride in my posture.  

As I linger with these gentle artisans, I begin to grasp the subtle, fluid hierarchy that blooms and recedes like tides. When an elder approaches, those with less experience pivot their attention. A tilt of the elder’s head or a gentle brush of their palm along a surface communicates volumes. We younger ones adapt quickly, responding with minute shifts. In the next moment, that same elder might watch me place a bracket or measure a beam, letting me discover my technique without interruption—allowing my own instincts to sing.  

No single being gives rigid orders here. Instead, there’s a sense that the entire hull of rock and wood is the primary voice. All ears (or eyes, or hands) attune to the architecture itself, to the swirl of water and the faint rumble of shifting stone. We read these cues with every inch of our skin, deciding collectively what to do next. This unity underlies every motion, from the delicate chiseling that grants each pillar its final shape, to the careful sealing of cracks in the corner.  

In unoccupied intervals, I drift toward the tall causeways and place my palm against the pillars, one after another. I want to ask them questions: Why are we building here, in this hidden fortress under the radiant sky? What is this hall destined to become? The only reply is the resonant hum that flows back into my wrist. I sense a wordless certainty: we shape now, so that the future can exist. We build these columns in anticipation of what might come next. As though the architecture is telling me, “Each block is a promise. Each bracket, a vow.”

In time, I find myself returning to one of the half-finished corners of the structure—a triad of beams waiting to be joined, a stone foundation unpolished, with fresh mortar only partly set. I hover there, scanning the lines with a solemn intensity that feels bigger than myself. I note the angles that need adjusting, the potential for a new fixture of light to be placed overhead. I imagine a small, hidden space beyond, perhaps for storing future metal brackets or chisels. While I contemplate, the currents swirl around me—a gentle urging that I sense is the hall’s own need. I tap a finger to my chin—an unconscious human gesture I didn’t realize I possessed—then turn to gather the needed tools.  

Carrying a mallet, a length of shaped wood, and a newly hammered bracket, I position them all. The bracket’s edges are rough; I’ll need to wedge them carefully. My first blow is slightly off-center, causing the bracket to tilt. I feel a brief pang of disappointment, but I steady my grip and try again, adjusting my angle. This time, the bracket sets flush. I hum a low note of satisfaction, and in the water, the sound resonates like a ripple of encouragement. I am, undoubtedly, part of this place, no longer just a drifting observer or a wandering soul. In shaping the space, I am shaped by it in turn.

Even as I work, I become aware that I’m growing tired. My shoulders, unaccustomed to so many measured strokes, grow heavy. So I slip back to that quiet recess with woven mats. I open a small jar of algae paste, removing just enough to satisfy my hunger. The briny taste coats my tongue, and I realize again how new all these sensations are to me: the yield of my muscles under the watery pressure, the hush of my breathing, and the subtle swirl of luminous particles hanging in each corridor.  

I do not recall a childhood, nor do I recall a time before these halls existed. Perhaps I am newly created, like the fortress itself. Perhaps my purpose is to learn—to adapt to the hush and roar of water, to master the interplay of stone and wood and metal. In resting, I find my mind drifts back to the columns, the orbs, the puzzle of open channels. I dream—awake, but drifting—of expansions yet unbuilt, more corridors branching out in shimmering arcs.  

Eventually, I rouse myself. The watery environment continues its steady background symphony: the crash of water faucets, the echo of hammer strokes in some far-off corridor. I notice the faint pulse in my fingertips again, that sense of shared resonance with the hall. I exhale a gentle stream of bubbles, turning to see if my elders have begun their next task. I realize, with a certain quiet thrill, that in this strange, luminous realm, I am both child and builder, both caretaker and ward.  

And so, with no spoken words, I rejoin the others in the ongoing dance of chisels and beams, bracket after bracket hammered into place. Even as we labor, there is a calmness to our collaboration. Each new beam repositioned, each trickle of water redirected, is part of an endless conversation between the environment and us—stone answers to claw, wood answers to hum, water flows through hinged portals at the tilt of a head.  

In time, I know, every corner will be finished. The hall will grow silent in its wholeness, and we will celebrate that day. But I also sense that part of the nature of this watery fortress is perpetual evolution—new expansions, new spaces carved from raw possibility. We, its inhabitants, will never truly be done. Our hush and hum will endure as the structure’s beating heart.  

And I, who first arrived without history or memory, become part of that unfolding tapestry. I am one of the colorless, pale-turquoise architects, with webbed fingers that test the edges of stone. My limbs move with short, assured strokes, each gesture guided by the needs of the hall. I communicate in hums and flickering motions, carefully passing on what I understand: that we live at the threshold of water and will, forging a realm of new beginnings.  

If you were to look upon me now, you’d see a quietly content figure immersed in the bright waters, mallet in hand, squaring up a block of stone with calm, methodical taps. You’d see my wide, dark eyes glittering as I watch for alignment, and perhaps you’d notice a gentle ripple of satisfaction in my chest when I sense the perfect fit. This is who I am in this young, hidden domain: an artisan, a caretaker, a creature in the primal bloom of creation.  

I have found my place among these halls, suspended in the glow of newly forged architecture, nourished by communal jars of algae paste, anchored by the solemn guidance of the elders. I am not their leader; I am one voice in the watery chorus. Yet I feel the pulse of potential all around me, in every stone block and illuminated orb—an invitation to shape, to refine, to coax this fortress into becoming.  

And that is where you will find me: immersed in a buoyant hush, half-lidded eyes fixed on the next seam that begs for a bracket or chisel, breathing in the swirl of currents and exhaling possibility, shaping the bones of a world yet to be. Here, in the watery sky and radiant halls, I belong. And in that belonging, I build.
