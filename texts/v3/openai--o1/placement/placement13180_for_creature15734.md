Sometimes I wonder if I was born from the basalt itself, lured into shape by the threads of molten rock and riveted metal. My first memories are of an intense, encompassing heat—not just on my skin but deep in my bones, as though the volcanic fires had kindled within me long before I was aware of them. I recall existing as a haze at the edge of a molten flow, uncertain of my solidity, one with the shimmer that warps all sightlines. Then, slowly, I took shape: limbs forming from the congealing basalt, lungs filled not with fresh air but with steam that tasted of iron and copper. When I try to trace my origin precisely, my mind goes blank, like a half-solid piece of rock that quickly re-liquifies.

At times, I see myself reflected in the scalding surfaces around the settlement—a reflection quivering in the hot air and half-lost in the glimmer of superheated metal. My body is tall, stooped, layers of pallid hide grown leathery from continuous blasts of geothermal breath. Thin plating flares across my chest and shoulders, hammered into place so seamlessly it might as well have grown there. My face is partially hidden behind a mask elongated at the jaw—an intricate piece hammered from scraps of steel. I remember forging it in a moment of both necessity and reverence, pressing it against my face when it was still warm enough to fuse with my flesh. From behind its narrow eye slits, I observe the forging territory around me.

THE CITY AND ITS THRUM

One of my roles is tending the pipeworks that snake through the basalt ridges. The settlement is a labyrinth of conduits feeding deep into the earth, channeling fluid heat or funneling fresh steam upward to power the massive turbines. Even when I was a small childlike figure, half-fearful of the roiling vents, I sensed that these tubes and pumps form a vital lifeline connecting us to the planet’s hidden heart. Now, older and more attuned, I listen to the metallic hum that travels through the pipe walls. It’s a steady lullaby of industry. Beneath that hum, I hear the planet’s deeper rumble, which resonates in a slow, tectonic scale.

The settlement that surrounds me—irregular towers in a ring around the hottest fissures—contains chambers carved from newly solidified flows. Each building seems to cling to the basalt spires; everything about it is precarious and evolving. Catwalks and suspended platforms vanish in the distance, bridging abrupt chasms. My daily tasks draw me along these routes, checking for pinhole leaks, realigning valves that whine under pressure. From time to time, I pause at a vantage high above a ravine, looking down on industrial silhouettes and drifting steam. I see the countless smaller inhabitants flitting at the edges of walkways—tiny insectile forms collecting the trace minerals and droplets of condensation. Their quick, nervous motions remind me that survival here happens in small increments—a handful of water, a stolen spark of heat.

THE HARVEST OF WATER

The water is precious. I carry a metal flask, battered in places where it’s been welded and re-welded over the years. Slowly, methodically, I fill it from the undersides of less-scorching pipes or from the shallow channels carved into basalt gullies. When the water condenses there before evaporating into steam, it feels like catching an exhalation from the planet—a fleeting gift. This condensed moisture is lifeblood: it cools my mask’s interior, quenching the dryness that cracks my lips. I sometimes offer a portion of this water at the shrines that dot our settlement, drizzling it into shallow cups of hammered iron. In the hush that follows, the water sizzles into steam, rising like a prayer.

We have many shrines. Some are small, no more than a welded shape pressed into a nook. Others loom as tall as the greatest basalt spires, hammered together from copper rods and blackened steel plates. Their geometry hints at outstretched hands or blazing suns—hard to tell. In the flickering haze, lines blur, and what some see as a face might just be rivet-work. But what matters is the hush, the sense of reverence, the collective knowledge that this environment tolerates us, yet can devour us in an unguarded moment. By placing an offering at these shrines, I acknowledge that synergy: we feed from the land’s molten energies, and in turn, we care for the land, patching cracks, channeling flows, ensuring the balance holds.

COMPANIONS IN THE HEAT

I move through the city among others who resemble me—tall, masked, slow of movement. Most of us have copper or steel plating integrated directly into our flesh, making us neither wholly organic nor entirely machine. Our eyes, somewhat dulled by gritty dust, can still discern subtle gradations of temperature glowing in the air. This is how we navigate: we see the shapes of heat as coloration in a dim landscape.

Occasionally, smaller squads of scuttling creatures trail behind us, gleaning leftover bits of mineral or dust from our footprints. I think of them as a mirror of our younger selves—alert, half-feral, uncertain. When threatened, they leap with astonishing speed into hidden crevices. But in calmer times, they congregate around whichever workstation we occupy, hoping for a stray droplet or the light radiating from an exposed vent. Some of us have come to view these small ones more like children than pests. They rely on the larger beings for leftover resources, just as we rely on them to perform tiny labors: cleaning away fungus-like films that cling to metal surfaces, or polishing rivet heads as they tread over them in search of edible minerals.

In the low hollows, the slag-beings—fluid lumps of half-melted rock—glimmer and flow between structural supports, absorbing waste heat from the machinery. They fortify weak points with the residue they leave behind, sealing cracks that might lead to catastrophic leaks. For me, encountering one up close was a revelation. It was like meeting a living piece of the planet’s molten mantle. Their forms shift with a measured, inexorable pace—hot lumps of semiliquid stone that appear sentient only in the slightest tilt or twist they make, as though tasting the air. Watching them calm my own breath; I recall that everything here moves, eventually, on the scale of geologic time.

THE SUBTERRANEAN CHAMBERS

Deeper under the settlement, I have glimpsed the communal chambers where rituals and gatherings unfold. Basalt walls hammered smooth form a roughly circular space. In the center, vents release a swirling steam that mingles with the phosphorescence of mineral-fed lanterns. We gather there in a subdued hush, masked faces turned inward. One caretaker taps a long staff against the basalt floor—tap, tap, a pause, then tap. The sound resonates like a heartbeat, reverberating through rock and steel. In the hush that follows each series of taps, I sense the planet’s answering pulse—a near-inaudible groan or shift somewhere below.

We do not speak much in these gatherings, for we trust the rhythms of the staff and the hissing vents to convey our shared concerns. When the staff’s tapping changes its pattern, it might mean pressure is rising in a neighboring fissure, or that a portion of the settlement needs reinforcement. It is part ceremony, part practical seismology. I have learned to interpret these patterns over the years—a language as old as the first hammered masks.

A MOMENT OF TRANSFORMATION

Still, each day it feels as though we remain in a slow metamorphosis. The basalt flanks are never truly stable; one morning, I woke to find a newly formed fissure near my sleeping alcove. It glowed orange at the edges. A spidery crack, sizzling with life, threatened to consume the floor beneath me. In a moment that felt both intimate and dangerous, I knelt and pressed my open-palmed gauntlet to the bright wound, sensing the planet’s molten heartbeat. Taking a heavy rod from my belt, I welded a makeshift patch over the fissure’s rim, calling for help from my fellow dwellers. Together, we stabilized the gap, layering molten metal over molten stone. In the end, it became part of the architecture—an accidental corridor forming a new branch of the city. Wherever crisis strikes, we build. We incorporate the planet’s upheavals into our labyrinth.

In this way, the settlement grows more labyrinthine over time, partially forging itself. The planet breathes fire; we respond with steel and rivets. The synergy becomes a testament to our existence. We are neither conquerors of the molten realm nor wholly its subjects. We float between devotion and defiance, reliant on the flows of heat that threaten, in the next moment, to annihilate us.

A SHIFTING IDENTITY

So who am I? I am these hammered plates fused to sinew. I am the hiss of steam as it condenses in my flask, ephemeral water that might vanish before it touches my lips. I am the caretaker of cables, the gatherer of droplets, the one who kneels at shrines. Over countless days, I’ve come to sense that our real identity here transcends individual shape. Each of us—small insectile scuttler, imposing masked figure, fluid slag entity—represents this land’s unstoppable forging of new life. We are the swirl of molten rock at the planet’s core, shaped into forms that endure by engineering, by faith, or by necessity. We pray, or we hum, or we collide with the steel scaffolding. We graft ourselves to the foundation and sculpt the landscape in return. 

Perhaps, in a different world, I might have walked through forests of green, drinking from clear rivers. But here, I glide through corridors of twisted metal and basalt. I seek condensation that clings to iron beams in the same way that distant ancestors might have foraged for fruit. My every breath is a conversation with the coiling heat. My prayers are resonant taps on the basalt floor, and my devotions are the shards of water I sacrifice at the shrines. 

If asked to name myself, I have no single word. Am I guardian? Engineer? Keep of the molten crest? I am all these things and none of them—an ever-shifting node in the living network of magma and steel. The city and I are one. We sleep under the glare of molten flows and wake to hammer out a new conduit or brace a compromised arch. Each day, we witness how the planet’s forging continues, and each day, we inch closer to symbiosis with that forging.

I have grown to see beauty in the reflected glow of half-congealed basalt, in the secret gleanings of the small scuttlers, in the endless hum of metal under strain. This place is a cradle of forging. It brought me forth and hammered me into shape. And I, in turn, sustain it. If that is not identity, then I do not know what is. I am the tension between fire and form—the slow birth of a world still deciding what shape to take. And in the molten dawn of that new day, I stand watch, forging and being forged, both the caretaker and the child of this volcanic realm.
