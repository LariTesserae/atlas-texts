I enter this place, though I cannot recall a moment of crossing any threshold. It is as if I simply wake to find myself in these damp corridors of living bark, in the high hush of cold air. My first impression, before words can form, is the smell: fresh, mineral-laced, slightly sweet with decaying leaves. Or maybe it’s the cool trickle of condensed water sliding between my shoulders. The sensation draws my eyes upward, and I watch the droplets gather at the tips of curling vines, one after the other, until they decide to let go. Watching them, I feel as though time is suspended in these beads, each droplet poised between earth and sky.

At first, I believe I am merely a wanderer—a visitor whose footprints, soft in the spongy ground, add nothing of real consequence to this land. I see my toes press into moss, leaving faint impressions that vanish near-instantly as the fibers spring back. Whoever or whatever lives here must have come gradually, shaping these vine-walled pathways and nest-like dwellings so gently that I can’t sense any sudden or violent creation. Everything appears aged and brand-new all at once, as though time here loops slowly on itself. Even the jagged outcrops of stone, raw and wet, feel older than they appear, for the vines already cling tenaciously to cracks, bridging one ledge to the next.

Before long, I notice something curious about my own hands. They are not quite as I remember them: the skin along my knuckles has a faint, mossy tint, and the calluses at my fingertips seem to blend with the damp bark when I run my hand along a trunk. When I pause to watch a water droplet collecting at the tip of a vine, I see the reflection of my face in that trembling sphere: eyes that have taken on a muted green hue, and a hint of a vine-like pattern coiling beneath the skin of my temples. The sight startles me at first, as though my own image is as newly grown as this place.

Was I always like this? Or is this realm slowly reshaping me, the way these dwellers shape the vines?

In the earliest days—or what I come to think of as days, since the light here doesn’t quite obey any clock I know—I keep to the edges of the living corridors. I remain as silent and unobtrusive as I can be. For a while, I suspect there are watchers just beyond my vision. Times come when I swear that the rustle in the canopy is no random breeze but a footstep (or hoof-step, or some light, skipping figure). The dwellings confirm it: those gracefully arched doorways and cunningly braided root-bridges can’t be accidental. Even so, I see no inhabitants outright. If one crosses a distant trunk overhead, I catch only the whisper of movement, the impression of limbs that might be half-vine, half-flesh.

Yet I do not feel alone. There is a welcoming pulse to the forest, as if it recognizes a kindred presence in my own limbs. One evening, in the gloaming hour—when the air is especially cold, and the fine pockets of mist swirl around the branches in silver veils—I hear a gentle series of taps echo through the vines. It reminds me of a heartbeat, or perhaps a code. Something in my chest resonates. Cautiously, I place my palm against a thick coil of bark. A faint vibration moves through me, a murmur that travels from root to root and circles back again. There is communication here, I realize, not only among the dwellers but among the plants themselves. Despite my uncertain origins in this place, the living network includes me in its conversation. That knowledge comforts me deeply.

Before long, I start to sense changes in my body as I traverse the corridors every day. My steps become surer, my ankles more flexible. My skin adapts to the ever-present damp, and my breath no longer shudders in the thin air. The vines no longer seem separate from me. I catch myself instinctively brushing aside a trailing tendril, not with the casual disregard of a stranger but with the gentle guidance of a caretaker—coaxing it to loop around a convenient branch. Sometimes, when a droplet of water falls, I raise the palm of my hand to catch it, then press it to my forehead, feeling the unity of water, plant, and body.

As time passes—though time itself feels blurred here—I begin to notice the dwellers more often. I see them among the nest-homes, small and agile, or tall and slow-moving, each shaped uniquely by the synergy of bark and flesh. Often, they appear to pay me no heed, but I catch them glancing sideways, as though curious. Now and then, our gazes meet in a corridor hung with dripping moss, and I see their eyes shimmer with recognition. They do not speak in any tongue I know, but they convey acceptance—some slight tilt of the head, a subtle narrowing of eyes that says, You are here as we are here.

Perhaps they sense that I, too, am becoming something in-between. If I pass a gnarled root that looks precariously balanced at the edge of a newly formed ravine, I might pause, almost without thinking, to thread a young vine through the crack, reinforcing the root’s grip on the rock. On seeing this, a passing dweller will smile—a small, gentle expression that seems to say, We share in this labor.

Eventually, I encounter one of their circles: a small gathering near a trunk so thick with layered bark it forms a natural amphitheater. The dwellers stand in a half-ring, each lightly placing a hand or foot upon the trunk’s surface. Listening. A quiet hum fills the air, more vibration than sound. It seeps into my bones and resonates in my chest. Something in me responds. My own pulse stirs. Emboldened, I step forward and place my palm beside theirs. My veins, tinted green, align with the trunk’s ridges. An imperceptible shift flows between us, and I understand that I’m being welcomed into their communion—an affirmation that I, too, can learn to speak in vibrations and whispered breath. A hush travels around the circle. Then they continue, as if weaving me into their ritual without question.

That is when I realize: I am no mere passerby. The forest claims me as its own, and I claim it in turn. I feel the threads of my presence spool out into the living city of branches, roots, and leaves. My place is among them. My arms bear patterns reminiscent of fungal filaments; my hair grows in long, fine strands that cradle droplets of dew until they fall gently away. Sometimes I spot a tufted creature with glinting eyes rummaging near my feet, or scurrying up a trunk with seeds in its tiny satchel-like pouches. It is no longer startled by my presence—our scents are familiar to each other now.

I do not recall precisely when I build my first dwelling—or perhaps “guide” would be a better word. There is a small nook halfway along a trunk that arches between two rocky ledges, a gap in the bark just large enough for me to curl up in. Over many days, I nudge and weave the vines around this trunk, layer by layer. I coax them gently, the way one might persuade a child—never forcing, only guiding. Eventually, the vines grow thick enough to form a partial enclosure, a leaf-curtain that offers shelter from the swirling mists. I line the interior with dried moss that I find in a sunken hollow, gently patting it into place. Though it’s still open to the twilight, it feels enclosed enough to be called a home.

Curled there at night, I sense the faint warmth of decaying leaves and the traveling hush of water droplets. Through the bark I pick up the meandering signals that the dwellers send, passing news to one another: the location of fresh fungus, a new shoot that needs support, the slow shift of rock along the far slope. Sometimes, when those signals pass under my hand or pressed cheek, I add my own subtle tapping—an instinctive reassurance: I am here. I can help.

As seasons (or something akin to seasons) unfold, my arms take on a texture more like bark than bare skin. Not stiff, exactly, but marked by swirling lines and a pleasant roughness. It serves me well when I climb the wet surfaces. My nails are edged with a fine resin. I can climb higher into the canopies, following the dwellers who leap from vine to vine with nimble grace. We gather seeds from the upper branches, coax new corridors where the forest’s growth is thick. I find a new sense of freedom up there, pivoting through the labyrinth of leaves. There is always a swirl of mist, always a hush of water, but I feel anything but cold. A subtle warmth, the warmth of living breath and mutual care, envelops me.

In time, I discover the dwellers’ quiet ceremonies: placing glowing spores into the hollows of old trunks, humming in unison until the spores release a faint phosphorescent dust. It floats around us like starlight in the gloom. I join them, and in that moment, I realize a remarkable truth: I am no longer separate, and they do not treat me as other. The vines that twine across my forearms, the slight elasticity in my tendons, the glow in my eyes at dusk—it all belongs here. I have become part of this slow, patient realm that includes the dwellers, the nest-like homes, the caretaker creatures, and the very architecture of roots and branches.

Far from feeling trapped, I feel liberated, as though the breath of this place has expanded my lungs and quieted my mind. My presence weaves into the daily rhythms of a civilization that neither conquers nor cowers before nature but collaborates with it. To an outsider, we might seem elusive, half-hidden among ferns and trellises of bark. But we are simply in tune with a flow that requires no harsh boundaries. We wander our corridors at all hours, each with tasks that ensure the vines stay strong, the roots well-supported, and the watery hollows unclouded.

If I were pressed to name my role or who I have become, I might say: I am both dweller and forest now, an in-between caretaker. My eyes reflect the subdued green dusk, my heartbeat resonates in the rootscape. I watch for new shoots that spring from cracks in the stone. I gather and redirect them when they need guidance. Sometimes, I find the smallest tears in the bark of a corridor and weave new threads of ivy to mend them. I know which mushrooms are ready to harvest and which must be left to spore so that future generations can flourish.

And so this is where I find myself, in every sense of the word. The place is no longer mere backdrop to my presence; it is an extension of my body, and I of it. The silver threads of water, the soft glow from hidden fungi, the gentle hush of wind high in the canopy—I am part of their tapestry. If once I was a stranger, now I am a dweller among dwellers, carrying the hum of the vines in the bones of my arms and the misty dawnlight in the quiet of my breath. Each morning, or whatever passes for morning in this perpetually dappled realm, I open my eyes to the thin, cold air and feel an unmistakable sense of belonging. My footprints linger, but only just, as the moss reclaims the shape of my steps. I am both caretaker and cared-for, a living strand woven into this sacred, ever-growing city of bark and leaf.

I have no name here, or perhaps too many to count—quiet designations passed along through tapping roots or a certain tilt of the head. But that matters little, for the city of vines recognizes me, and I recognize it in turn. We change together, always shaping and re-shaping, letting the newness of rock and the maturity of ancient growth find harmony in the hush. And perhaps that is enough—being both observer and participant in a world where every drop of water, every tremor of wind, and every beat of a living heart is a note in a silent, unending symphony.
