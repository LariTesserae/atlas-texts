I stretch awake, as if surfacing from a long, watery dream. Mist clings to every surface around me—fine droplets that murmur across the metal braces and fern fronds alike. At first, I’m not certain where my body ends and the plateau begins. Perhaps I once believed I was separate. Now I’m not so sure.

I find my footing on a slick outcrop: the stone is fresh and faintly warm beneath the moss that coats it like living velvet. A cable hums overhead, droplets beading along its length. I hear the slow churn of a distant turbine, its rhythmic turning as steady as a heartbeat. My own limbs feel fluid and elongated, jointed in ways that let me cling to the underside of girders or slide gracefully between thick cords of vine. I have moss collecting along my shoulders—tiny fronds that glow emerald in the morning sun.

At times, I think I am made mostly of this place: part vine, part spore, part trickle of mineral-laden water. Yet, I also sense something metallic in my muscles. When I move, I hear an almost imperceptible whirr, as though small gears shift beneath my skin. Now and then, I brush a hand across my forearm and feel the faintest ridge of a panel or conduit snaking its way through me. If I meet my reflection in a puddle, I catch glints of metal where bone might be. I don’t know how that came to be—whether I arrived this way or the plateau changed me over time. All I know is that I cannot imagine living anywhere else.

The plateau’s air tastes of wet stone and forging metals. Thin coils of steam snake up from vents in the rocks, and I watch them swirl around the scaffolding. In these early hours, when the bright sun angles in low, the plateau’s contrasts become all the sharper. Overgrown pockets of dense vegetation—dark emerald and turquoise—press against stretches of naked, newborn stone. The mechanical towers that loom in the mist discharge their steam with vibrant hiss, but for a moment, they feel as natural to me as the vines or the moss.

I take my first steps of the day, meandering toward a half-constructed walkway that juts over a newly formed ridge. Rusted struts meet fresh metal plating. A trickle of water runs across my path, and I pause to cup it in my hands, letting the droplets splash cold against my fingers. My nails are flexible, able to grip rock or tighten bolts. Sometimes I find myself tightening real bolts, too—fitting a tool around a nut that has come loose in the night, restoring the flow of water so the turbines might spin on. I learned to do this by observing those who came before, although now they remain elusive. Perhaps they taught me the value of patience in a place that shapes itself over decades. Or perhaps this knowledge grew in me, the way the vines do.

Shapes flit by at the edge of my vision. A curious rodent-bird—the size of my hand—perches on a metal bar. Its tufted antennae sway, taking in the distant roar of water. Its eyes are large, reflecting me in miniature—a figure half-lost in the swirl of grotto-like shadows. Then it darts away, gone in a flicker of silver-green luminescence. I sense the watchers on high scaffolds, too, those half-avian silhouettes with glinting alloy plumage. They always seem to be tracing the plateau’s boundaries, measuring shifts in the stone, reading the slow dialectic between water and rock. From time to time, one may alight on a pylon overhead and tilt its head at me, as though approving or simply curious that I persist here alongside them.

There is a hush of gratitude in me for their silent companionship. I realize I share something with them—a reverence for the interplay of engineering and living growth, the swirling mists weaving new layers of existence. Sometimes I attempt to communicate with them, letting out quiet syllables that echo the sound of steam valves releasing pressure. They cock their heads but do not flee. We exchange a moment of understanding before they vanish into some shadowy crevice or slip behind a whirring ventilation duct.

I wander further along a path where mechanical pipes vanish into thick tangles of succulent-like leaves. Here, the vegetation is so vibrant, so full, I feel it humming against my senses. Moisture accumulates on the underside of broad leaves, then drips in shimmering droplets that land on me with gentle pats. Flowers—if they can be called that—bloom like orbs of translucent gel, each pulsing with color from within. I can see tiny insects with crystalline wings feeding, their exoskeletons mirroring starlight even in the brightness of day. One settles on my forearm, and I marvel at how its glimmering carapace mimics the metallic threads laced beneath my skin.

Moving deeper, I come to a cluster of root-bridges forming a lattice above a pool of mineral-laden water. I feel drawn to it, compelled to gently test my footing. The overhead vines swirl into a half-roof, making this a secret grotto. Tucked within are faint shapes—other creatures, tall and lithe, with extruded limbs and sideways-blinking eyes. One scribbles patterns in the mud, lines that seem to represent the storms swirling above or the rock shifting below. Another, perched behind them, lightly brushes away water trickling too near the lines. I linger, not wanting to disturb them, letting the hush between the throbbing of distant turbines and the hiss of waterfalls envelop us. For a few moments, I almost feel as if I’m watching art unfold. Then, a gentle waterfall from some new fissure washes the lines away, and the creatures rise in unison, agile as water skimmers. They melt into the ferns and root-arc dwellings, leaving behind only swirling residue in the muddy water.

I journey on, eventually returning to the weaving corridors of industrial scaffolding. Here, I sense the pulse of a more methodical intelligence—perhaps from the ones who built these beams and conduits. Though rarely seen, their presence lingers: the careful alignment of turbines, the methodical spacing of riveted brackets. Long ago, they might have hammered steel here, forging new expansions that now stand half-finished. I sometimes imagine their silhouettes flickering amid the gloom of a workshop hidden in the plateau’s heart. They would have recognized me, I suspect, as part of their efforts—some experiment, or some ally the plateau itself helped them shape.

On a catwalk that arcs between two hollows, I discover a heavy, corroded wrench half-buried in moss. The metal handle is pitted and worn. I pick it up, turning it over until the morning light gleaning from the ridge catches the rust. The clank of its loose head resonates in my chest as though I contain an echo chamber. A piece of half-remembered knowledge: This is a tool for turning bolts, for measuring torque, for repairing and maintaining. I sense its purpose, and I regard it with the same reverence the root-dwellers have for the newly forming stone. Quietly, I slip it into a loop of vine at my side—once a simple tether, perhaps now my tool belt.

A wash of condensation on my cheeks reminds me of the plateau’s fickle moods. A gentle drizzle sweeps in, forging a low rumble of artificial thunder from some hidden engine. I find solace beneath a partial canopy of leaves and crisscrossing steel beams. The drizzle intensifies, running in rivulets down the columns, collecting in the recessed pockets of half-sketched rock. All around me, the plateau breathes with new vigor—the metal scaffolds hiss, the turbines spin faster, everything merges into a resonant, symphonic hum. For a while, I close my eyes and let that sound exist within me, or perhaps I exist within it.

When the rain’s roar subsides, I emerge again. My footprints darken the wet moss. I’m neither fully of metal nor entirely of vine and bark. My presence, like so many shapes here, exists in flux. Perhaps tomorrow I’ll find fresh layers of tissue or plating in my arms. Perhaps I’ll glean new knowledge from the watchers, or from the root-dwellers, or from the memory of those meditative sketches in mud. Perhaps I’ll follow a newly grown ridge and discover another cluster of turbines humming away in some mist-wreathed corner.

For now, though, I am content to stand among the half-finished walkways, drifting steam, and budding life. I am caretaker and wanderer in equal measure: I tighten the bolt that holds the catwalk steady. I observe the shifting shadows of vine-wrapped towers, the swirl of creatures weaving past. I cradle droplets of water in my palms, feeling them slip between metal and softened skin. The plateau and I are co-conspirators, participants in an unhurried dance of formation, corrosion, and renewal. Day by day, I learn that belonging here is less about forging a fixed identity than about embracing the slow metamorphosis that spans seasons and decades.

So who am I in this high, wet realm, in this cradle of industry and nature? I am many things, and I become more each time I breathe in the mist. I am a quiet guardian, an observer, a collaborator. My limbs carry the marks of machine and moss alike. I notice the watchers in the towers and sense their gaze returned. Nothing in me seeks to claim this realm. Everything in me celebrates its patient shift toward an unnamed future—the new rock rising, the vines reaching, the turbines churning, the slow drip of water carving shapes in the half-formed stone. And I remain, neither wholly mechanical nor purely flesh, a creature of rivet and leaf, humming with the steady heartbeat of this plateau suspended between sky and earth, forging itself anew.
