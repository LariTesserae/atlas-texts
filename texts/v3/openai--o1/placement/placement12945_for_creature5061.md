I would be among those who first awake as the water’s warmth, hovering at my shoulders, signals the dawnless morning. My skin would carry the faint tracery of branching lines that shift hues with each tiny shift in light—turquoise to jade, then gold-flecked like silt when I press my palm against the nearest vine. Let me breathe in the heavy salt air, then dip beneath the surface for a long moment, letting water cradle me in that hush where breath and movement conspire so gently that I almost do not notice myself as separate from it.

When I rise again, I am neither fully on land nor fully submerged; that in-between is precisely who I am. My body has grown to relish water’s buoyancy, each limb tuned to the slow give-and-take of currents. I notice how my foot, broad and flexible, finds easy purchase on slick stone while my toes splay just enough to keep me from slipping into the deeper channels. Thought drifts through me at a walking pace rather than a running one—every motion a measured dance in step with the hush.

I would be an artisan of living structures, weaving the slow magic of leaf and vine into the huts perched along the spires. My mornings pass in pleasant monotony: collecting tendrils of that thick, rope-like plant which thrives where the water is only a half step from air. I guide them by hand over a wooden frame—nudging them to coil or arch exactly where I need them to grow. Over weeks, the vines fuse, forging a sturdy curve of green around the huts. Sometimes I muse that in another place or time, we might build with stone or metal, but here our materials are alive and patient, just as we are.

At midday—though that term is guesswork in our sunless twilight—I set aside my tools and wade deeper toward the spires’ base. The water climbs to my chin; I lean back to float, feeling the weight of the world recede from my bones. My eyes settle on the half-formed pinnacles around me as they rise, slow as any living being, from the bedrock. I remember when I was a small child, how we used to measure the spires by marking on strips of bark. A span of years would pass, and the spire you once could scarcely climb upon would gain just enough height to form a shallow terrace. In that soft flux, I learned patience—that creation unfolds not in leaps, but in the smallest accumulations of silt and root and stone.

I keep a side of me open to the deeper water, that hush that beckons beyond the settlement’s edges. Sometimes, if I still myself, I catch a luminous movement—a serpentine shape gliding through the gloom, its spots of light flickering in a pattern not unlike the shifting color on my own skin. It’s a humbling sight, a reminder we are far from alone in this watery domain—others thrive here, too, in ways we only partially understand. Some nights, or what we call nights, I sit on a sloping ledge beside the huts, letting the hush carry the faint echoes of deeper channels. There’s always a sense of something immense stirring below, as if a colossal heart beats beneath the stone, driving the plateau’s rise. I listen, half in wonder, half in acceptance: this has always been my world.

When I return to the settlement, I join my kin in the communal spaces—open-air gazebos where water laps gently at knee-height. There, we gather to shape new baskets or talk of the day’s findings. The children—some as tall as my hip, others still small enough to cling to my shoulders—play in the warmer shallows. Their eyes hold a wide, calm curiosity. They practice weaving tiny leaf-boats to launch in the currents, giggling in soft, breathy tones, always mindful not to break the hush.

In my hands, I craft a fresh coil of fronds that will become part of a roof. This is how I give back to my community: by creating spaces that let us rest, share our stories, and keep fires lit above the waterline so we might cook the fish and tubers that comprise our simple meals. If you look closely at my weaving, you’ll see subtle twists and patterns, each signifying a small piece of our lore—spirals for the swirl of currents, branching motifs for the vines that twine through every crevice of our realm. Our huts are not merely assemblages of materials; they’re living testaments to how we coexist with this environment, half-grown of our own intention and half-grown by the will of water and root.

By late twilight—though here, twilight extends continuously—I feel the spires beneath my feet shift. A nearly imperceptible tremor runs through the stone, as if the entire plateau just sighed and lifted a fraction more. Some say these movements come from the push of molten rock far below, others speak of a sleeping presence that slowly exhales, raising the land piece by piece. In truth, no one really knows, and that unknowing is part of our quiet covenant with this place. We do not need every question answered. It’s enough to feel each tremor resonate through our bones and to shape our lives around it.

I retire to my home—a half-enclosed dome of mossy vines and mud walls, the roof open to let the ever-present drizzle of condensed mist fall inside. Over time, I have encouraged certain glossy leaves to form a living tapestry at my walls, filtering the watery gloom into a gentle glow. I like the sound of droplets dripping through them, tapping rhythmic splashes in the shallows around my sleeping mat. Before settling for the night, I sometimes whisper a wordless thanks: to the water that sustains us, to the slow spires that hold our huts, to the quiet creatures who dart at the edges of our vision. My acknowledgment merges with the hush. Soon, I drift into slumber, lulled by that familiar cradle of warmth and fluid motion.

And that is who I am here: a being shaped by this half-submerged realm, a weaver of living shelters, a child of slow-moving spires and rippling vines. To the outside world—if there is one—I might seem primitive, but I know the depth of skill required to shape vines into architecture, to track a plateau’s upward journey by the smallest shifts in the water’s flow. I know it in my fingertips, in the steady weight of the water against my ribs, in the gentle hush that vibrates through the entire settlement. Among my people, I am simply another soul attuned to the flux of liquid and stone, an artist and caretaker in a place where creation never truly stops.

Here, time bends itself to the rhythmic dance of geological ascension and aquatic hush. Each sunrise that never quite brightens is another day to move in tandem with the currents, to watch the spires gain a fraction more in height, to notice a vine I planted weeks ago finally curling in the shape I had hoped. It is slow, but it is real. It is quiet, but it is full of life. And for as long as these waters envelope me, for as long as the sky remains a soft promise beyond the clouds, I will be part of this world’s unfolding—woven inseparably into its tapestry of slow, deliberate motion.
