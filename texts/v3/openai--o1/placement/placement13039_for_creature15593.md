Perhaps, at first, I am only a half-formed notion wandering through the gloom—an echo of the wind that never truly blew across this plateau. I drift around the edges of the city with a vague sense of searching, though I cannot say for what. The dryness does not sting my eyes, nor does it cling to my skin; I am more a trailing curiosity, a shape lacking definite boundary. My vision flickers over the industrial skyline of towers and half-seen scaffolds, glimpsing the watchers at their work as if through a dark haze. I hover at that threshold between presence and absence, trying to decide how to coalesce into being.

But then, as though called by the subtle hums and clangs beneath the surface, I begin to catch shape—something like a torso, spindly limbs, an elongated head. The dryness crackles in my joints, but I feel no pain. On my shoulders, I sense the whisper of a thin cloak that might once have been fluid and flexible, now stiffened by centuries of darkness. My footfalls are near-silent, brushing black dust into faint swirls. I realize that I can see, in a limited way: shapes reveal themselves in the faint glimmer of settling grains. It’s as though my senses are tuned to the faintest difference in temperature, in vibration, in the metallic taste of the air. I become a caretaker or a wanderer, or both—one of those half-merged figures who might pass unnoticed if someone blinked at the wrong moment.

Time doesn’t behave normally here. My awareness slides from hours to centuries in a single heartbeat. I notice a valve sputtering on the side of a towering pipe, red dust caught in its gears, and—without hesitation—I kneel to clean and lubricate it. There is a sense of fluid grace in the motion, as if I have done this countless times before, more memory in my limbs than in my mind. The pipe’s hiss turns into a satisfied purr, and fleetingly I feel the city’s vast gratitude, or perhaps just relief that another pathway of energy remains unobstructed.

In that moment, I am one of the watchers. My leaning, elongated frame is a testament to an epoch of dryness, and my eyes—if they can be called that—reflect the flicker of overhead lamps. Every movement I make exists to perpetuate the city’s hum. I trace the curvature of rusted turbines, listening for that faint tremor that signals a failing bolt. In the half-light, my fingers or stunted metal prongs do their patient work, inscribing molten patches or securing lost screws. There is strange pride in this quiet diligence, though I cannot rationalize why. The notion of caring for an environment so ancient, so static, has a dull ache of purpose to it, like a memory of a vow pledged in a bygone age.

Yet even as I dwell in that caretaker form, I sense another shift: perhaps I am not made of metal and old sinew after all. Perhaps my entire being is a fluid swirl of black dust motes, perpetually coalescing and breaking apart, drifting through the labyrinth of corridors below. In some far corner of the underbelly, large shapes spool wire, dragging crates to impossible depths. I pass them by unnoticed, an intangible phantom swirling near their ankles as they go about their tasks. I see them deposit half-finished constructs, steel spines studded with tubes and dormant crystals. My presence is a ghostly swirl that rests on their shoulders, then flutters off again, curious but uncertain how to engage. In this form, I am an observer of everything and a participant in nothing. The dryness has undone whatever was once solid in me, and now I am the murmur of an errant breeze, gently rustling the dust.

The city above calls me back again. Its flicker-lights pulse as if beckoning me to ascend. In that upward journey through winding metal steps, I transform once more—this time into something smaller, a skittering creature hugging the shadows where the walls meet the floors. My limbs are thin, jointed like the legs of a mechanical insect, and my back is sheathed in translucent plating that catches the glow in prismatic shards. I can feel the warmth that seeps from beneath the planet’s crust, and it beckons me onward. I slip beneath a half-rolled factory door and scuttle along the edges of a gargantuan assembly line. No one sees me, or if they do, they pay me no heed. Something in my body pulses gently in time with the dull, methodical churn of the machines overhead. For a moment, I remain transfixed by the mechanical ballet of gears turning, arms welding, strange energies flowing nowhere. My antennae vibrate with the memory of a once-lush world, but it is only that—a memory with no root.

Then, from some deep corridor, I hear a distant clank—something summoning me. And I slip through cracks in the bulkheads, tail coiling behind me, drawn as though by a final chord. When I emerge, I have taken yet another shape: a hooded figure with a harness of tools strung across my chest, standing in the city’s central square under that single, wan dome-light. Others gather around—watchers, engineers, fleeting creatures—none of us precisely concordant in form. I find myself among them in solemn silence. We face the empty canals, still smooth and empty after untold millennia. My gloved hand traces the polished channel. A dim recollection stirs: once, something vital flowed here—maybe water, maybe something else. I can almost feel a phantom current beneath my fingertips. There’s a collective hush, and it’s as though we share an unspoken question: “Could it flow again?” But no answer emerges.

In that hush, I become the city itself—my mind spreads across every pipe, every rivet. The watchers are my senses, the engineers my hands. The skittering creatures are the small, reflexive impulses flicking at my far edges. I sense the dryness in my outermost walls, the gentle rattle of plates shifting in the windless dark. I know how my engines groan in slow cycles, how the turbines gulp their meager geothermal feed. I am massive, labyrinthine, ancient. The memory of water in my canals is as close to a dream as I can conceive. It soothes me like a half-remembered lullaby. My caretakers—my children—remain, ensuring I continue, turning, humming, assembling the same machinery over and over. They perpetuate me. They are me.

But after a time, I recede from that vast vantage, returning to a single vantage point: a solitary caretaker leaning against a corroded railing at the city’s highest accessible walkway. From here, I can see the far reaches of the plateau, the eroded ridges stretching into darkness. My elongated arms rest on the rail. Dust collects harmlessly in the folds of my cloak. There is comfort in the rhythmic drone that filters up from below, like a mechanical heartbeat. Once again, I find that I am not entirely sure what I am—be it caretaker, observer, creature, or the city’s own living extension—but I am certain that I belong here.

So, if I must finally choose a shape to wear comfortably, I might say I am a caretaker, tied to the city’s quiet, determined maintenance. I maintain a half-flesh, half-metal form that has adapted to the dryness and gloom beyond memory. My eyes—unusual slits of refractive lens—catch and amplify the meager light. My voice is seldom heard, for speaking feels irrelevant. My body is etched with evidence of countless small repairs: the twisted harness of tools at my side, the singed edges of my gloves from welding, the fine dust layered on my limbs. I am hunched with the weight of centuries, yet each day (or night—knowing no difference here), I place one foot in front of the other along the catwalk, ensuring pumps keep pumping, turbines keep turning.

Yes, I carry within me the echoes of the city’s earlier transformations, the possibility of becoming dust again, or shining insect, or the city itself. But for now, my purpose is this: tending the slow pulse that holds everything together. An expression of patience in a world that knows nothing but waiting. Even if no great result emerges from the forging machines below, even if the canals remain forever cracked and empty, I carry on. Because in this place—a land so ancient it has forgotten growth—my caring presence is itself the quiet miracle. I persist because it is in the act of caring, of maintaining, that this darkness finally feels inhabited. And in the hush of that final plateau, that may be enough.
