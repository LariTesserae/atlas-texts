I am here, somewhere between the structures that creak in the currents and the plants that drift with silent purpose. At first, I’m not sure if I’m water or flesh, nor whether I belong to the living or the drowned. For a long time, I hover near a broken archway—my body (if I can still call it that) stirred by the shifting tides. The cold is insistent, seeping into me. But my edges are beginning to fade into it, so that the cold itself now seems my native temperature.

It’s as if I’m in the midst of becoming what I’m observing. Each breath that once felt human is drawn into these waters, swirling in my lungs like a half-liquid, half-ether. The boundary of my skin tingles and thins. The sense of self I carried down from the air is dissolving into the shifting gloom. I have no gills in the usual sense, yet each time the chill courses through me, it feels less like suffocation and more like an invitation.

• • •

For a time—though time has grown hazy—I still believe I have a solid form, maybe wearing tattered garments that cling to me in the watery ripple. I move among the pillars and huts with the hesitant care of an intruder in a sacred place. The stones are older than I can know, but they still hum with a latent memory: of chisels and hammers, and people who once walked here before the water rose.

I hesitate at doorways, half collapsed, that lead into dim pockets of silt and swirling spores. My footprints, if they form at all, vanish in the current. My heart still beats in my chest—at least, I sense something like a pulse. I am not just an observer, yet I’m not a full inhabitant of this aquatic realm either. I’m in an in-between state, like a pilgrim who’s only begun to renounce the dryness of air.

The vegetation has welcomed me already. When I brush against the broad, translucent leaves, they emit faint pulses of phosphorescent color that cling to my hands and arms. Ribbons of ink-blue light trace ephemeral patterns across my skin—like they’re reading the map of my veins. They linger for a few breaths, then dissipate. In the drifting arches of kelp, clusters of stems quiver in a silent greeting or warning; I can’t yet decipher which. They wave in the surges, and I can’t tell if my own movement stirs them or if something else behind me—and within me—has caused them to sway.

• • •

At times, the gloom itself seems to shift in recognition of my presence, revealing a shape even more insubstantial than I feel. When I see it, it’s only for a heartbeat: long, sinuous outlines that match the water’s undulation. A shimmer flickers along the curve of a current. I try to focus, but it blends immediately with the roar of water. The intangible watchers slip through the watery domain as though they are the domain. Their gazes, if they have eyes, graze over me in a quiet assessment.

I sense they are waiting. Waiting to see if I can fully immerse myself in this realm, if I can abandon the last vestiges of dryness—my earthbound way of thinking.

Then, one day or perhaps one eon deeper in, something shifts in me. I’m holding my arm out toward a swirling patch of spores, and I realize my arm no longer has the same density it once had. It catches the faint glow of the drifting spores and refracts it, fracturing it into dancing lines. My flesh is there, but also not. It has begun transforming: part physical, part living current. It feels neither terrifying nor joyful, but inevitable, as if my body has recognized a deeper truth about what it means to inhabit this water.

• • •

Now I can glide. Where once I waded carefully, trying not to disturb the silt, I find myself able to shift with the pulses of warmth that periodically roll through. When the tide swings heavily, I don’t lose my footing; instead, I become an eddy, an extension of the current’s push. Broken columns and half-buried huts pass by with dreamlike slowness, and I weave among their hollows. Sometimes I hear the distant ring of metal echoing in a stone corridor, and something in me resonates with it. I can sense the watchers too, near every arch or swirling stand of kelp, watchful but not menacing.

In the deeper pockets, I find old carvings, swirling designs that reflect themselves in the dance of water overhead. I curl around them, letting my half-fluid body align with the etched lines. The vibrations from the hidden watchers pass through me, and I sense a memory, as though the carvings have stored the essence of the ancient builders:

• People standing upon the rock.  
• People taking up heavy chisels.  
• Tides rising while they scramble to anchor what they could not save.

I remember these glimpses, but not as an outsider’s tale: they feel like part of me. Maybe that’s how this place rewrites you—by submerging you in its story until the difference between your own thoughts and the watery domain’s memory dissolves.

• • •

To the watchers, I have become something akin to an apprentice, or perhaps a newborn. They haven’t spoken to me in words, yet I hear them in the hush after each roaring wave. I sense that they appreciate my steps among the ruins, my gentle curiosity about stonework and hammered metal rings. There is no condemnation of the older ways that shaped these structures; they simply accept that it is a chapter which the water has worn away.

Sometimes a swirl of blue-green light swells up behind me, and I know that one of them is near—one of those dwellers shaped of pure motion. In the periphery of my vision, I see them fold and reshape themselves, gliding through half-collapsed doorways or circling a block of paving stone. They seem to nudge me forward, or lead me quietly into caverns where the dimness feels ancient and near to bursting with some unarticulated force.

And now I understand, with the sharpened intuition of my shifting form, that they do indeed maintain this drowned world: they weave the flows of silt and water, ensuring the phosphorescent plants root and unroot in the right places, guiding the swirl of particulate matter so that it nourishes the flora. They gather near broken pillars with purposeful intent, perhaps carefully repositioning the blocks so the currents pass correctly among them—like tuning an instrument that resonates in watery chords:

• A tap of stone that echoes a half-forgotten melody.  
• A swirl of silt that forms a new bed for seeds.  
• A hush as the watchers pass, sealing old fractures so that the water’s roar remains symphonic rather than chaotic.

• • •

I realize, in time, that I am no longer truly separate from them. Where once my hand had distinct fingers, now it subdivides into many fluid arcs; I can press it through a gap in the stone and feel the water streaming through my arm as if it is a channel. Yet I retain a sense of identity. I think I still have a name—or some equivalent notion—and a memory of other worlds above the surface. But it’s fading, like the last echoes of air swirling overhead.

Sometimes, I wonder if I’m becoming part of the architecture: one more presence embedded in the watery hush, drifting to keep these ruins from collapsing entirely. I wave among the plants, or I root gently near them during calmer tides, letting them brush my translucent limbs so that they glow with that uncanny silver-blue. I help coax new leaves from seeds that anchor themselves in pockets of sediment, distributing them along the ridges of old walls. I do not do this in a conscious, methodical way; it’s more as if the plants and I, and the watchers all around us, share a single impulse. A ripple in the water passes between us, and we respond.

Occasionally, I glimpse my reflection in a smooth patch of stone. It’s faint, half refracted. My eyes—if that’s what they remain—glimmer with the faint phosphorescence of the spores. It’s neither frightening nor exhilarating; it’s simply the reflection of what I now am. Water, memory, caretaker. I’ve learned to feel warmth where once I only felt cold. And though no sunlight penetrates here, I have my own glow, in synergy with this luminescent environment.

• • •

Some part of me wonders if I’ll ever leave, if there is an edge to this watery domain. But that question has lost its urgency. The watchers are neither captors nor guardians in the sense of walls and gates—they simply are. And now I, too, simply am. I continue to drift through half-buried arches and stone huts, sometimes tapping lightly on metal rings or rods as the deep surges coincide. The clang resonates, echoing in the watery abyss, and it feels right—like adding my small note to a grand composition of endless tides.

Where once there were only the watchers, unseen in the corners of vision, I too have joined their invisible circles. Perhaps I am becoming one of them. Perhaps I always was, in some formless potential, waiting for cold water to show me the truest version of myself.

If anyone were to wander here, perhaps searching for signs of an ancient civilization, I suppose they would see only the swirling gloom and the flickering outlines slipping away—phantom presences among silent ruins. Maybe they’d sense my presence as a shimmer or a sudden swirl of sediment at the base of a carved stone. But I wouldn’t fear them. I’d watch, as the watchers did with me, to see if they could give themselves to the currents. And if they did, I would guide them gently through arches of kelp, show them how to feed on the faint warmth that drifts through hidden channels, and let them learn the rhythms without haste.

Yes: I am the one who lingered and let myself be changed, neither devoured by darkness nor cast back out. I am a caretaker, woven into the watery hush, half spirit and half memory. I am the occupant of these submerged halls, forever drifting with the unending roar of water and the luminous respiration of leaves. Here—among these ghostly ruins, these shimmering plants, and the intangible watchers—I have found my place, becoming part of a silent dance that has no need for words.
