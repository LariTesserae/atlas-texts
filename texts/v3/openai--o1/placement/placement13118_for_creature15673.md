I am not certain how old I am. The question itself would not occur to me were it not for these drifting moments of reflection in the half-light, for time in this place is too slow to measure by any human scale. My body, thick and layered with living growth, feels as if it has been part of the land for centuries, even millennia. In the hush, in the damp, I share breath with lianas and broadleaf ferns, exhaling as they inhale, part of their pulse as they are of mine.

I am a giant among the giants—though here, the word “giant” loses meaning. Some creatures outsize me in length, weaving serpentine bodies among the highest vines. Others are smaller yet heavier, so wide and broad that the very floor bends where they tread. Yet I, lumbering gently, carry an entire layered garden upon my back. My skin is a thick tapestry of bark-like ridges and deep ruts where pockets of soil gather. Over time, seeds carried on the wind or dropped by passing birds take root in the grooves. So I sprout ferns along one shoulder, a nest of finger-thick vines across the curve of my spine, and even a modest stand of mosses that shimmer with dew in the hush of morning.

Those who glimpse me from a distance see only vague shape. They notice a slow ripple of movement as if the forest floor itself has awakened to rove among the pillars of tree trunks—and then I vanish into the gloom. My steps are measured and meditative, each broad footfall pressing into the damp earth, leaving a shallow basin that quickly fills with steamed rainwater or trickling condensation. In the hush, there is hardly a sound, only the subtle shift of foliage as I move through corridors that countless generations of my forebears have worn. I trace these paths through memory, guided by an ancient sense of alignment that tells me where to find the wide channels of water or the ridges of higher ground. Each trail weaves me deeper into the darkness, carrying me through vines that drip with humidity so thick it sometimes feels like walking through a submerged world.

I am not alone. I never have been. I carry entire settlements of smaller creatures upon me: insects that chitter and shimmer in the dark, amphibians that nestle in the sweaty hollows of my craggy hide. Lichens form living murals across my shoulders—white and green patches veined like roads on a map. Occasionally, I sense the gentle prickle of a climbing vine that tries to wrap around my limbs. These symbiotes are only as permanent as the land is ancient, forging alliances with me just as the forest’s vines, roots, and ephemeral bursts of fungi form partnerships in the gloom around us.

My senses are tuned to the hum: that low, unending drone thrumming in the distance, as though some invisible machine churns from the heart of the earth. It resonates in my chest, a subsonic lull that tugs me onward. Trees exhale it in rustling leaves; the moisture echoes it in subtle drips onto broad fronds. At intervals, a strange hush falls across the undergrowth, and I know a predator makes its swift strike. Perhaps a serpentine limb coils overhead or a lithe shape glides through water. The equilibrium corrects itself quickly without fanfare—no flashy, violent crescendo, just a quiet exchange of energy. And then the forest’s pulse resumes, seamlessly as breath.

Most often, I live in that hush. My life is slow, every motion directed not by haste but by a deep, shared memory. My ancestors—creatures as old and hulking as the land’s bedrock—once roamed these same passages. Their footprints carved the channels that now I follow. I am continuing that ancient work: compressing soil underfoot, turning the ground so new plants find purchase, offering a living scaffold on which creepers and mosses ride from place to place. In my wake, smaller forest dwellers make their own roads, weaving around my legs, or riding in the damp tunnels my body parts in the vegetation. It is an ongoing collaboration we hardly think about; it just is.

You might wonder whether I dream in the hush. I suspect I do, for sometimes when I find a depression ringed by immense buttressed trees—my favored resting place—I lower my bulk gently to the warm, wet ground and let the thick canopy close above me. Even in slumber, my slow breaths continue to feed the microcosms on my back, while faint beams of filtered light glimmer through swirling clouds of insects. I do dream, if not in pictures then in raw sensation: the tug of vines, the mild tickle of epiphytes sending out new roots, the distant hush of thunder that never quite arrives. I dream of the forest as it once was, and as it will be. Because tomorrow, the day after, or a hundred years hence, hardly differs in shape from this moment.

Sometimes, I will pass one of the other giants. We rarely acknowledge each other beyond a subtle tilt of a horned head or the mutual rustle of ferns. It is enough to know we exist in parallel, each part of this place that shapes us, each shaped by it in turn. Our presence, so seemingly passive, is crucial; we keep the land in motion, pushing through new corridors, stirring the upper strata of mud and soil. The hush follows, weaving itself around us like a breath.

Smaller creatures rely on me. I sense it in the delicate pulling at my shoulders or the faint burring hum of insects that find warmth along my flanks. Sometimes a bird with near-metallic plumage perches on my ridges at dawn, letting out a single, measured note that vanishes quickly into the steam. I do not mind. It is a comfort to be needed, to be an entire ecosystem traveling beneath the canopy. In a realm where the very air feels like the inside of a slow-beating heart, carrying others is my purpose, my place.

And so, I continue on, a natural locomotive in a dark forest that resonates like a hidden factory. I am enormous, but my size is not an imposition; it is the shape that the hush has given me. My footsteps keep time with the forest’s own pulse, measuring out the long intervals between drizzling rains and the faint flicker of nighttime lightning. In my wake, ephemeral fungi sprout on overturned logs I brushed aside, new vines root in footprints left behind, insects gather on the damp edges of those footprints for a brief flourish of life, and the cycle goes on.

If you stand at the edge of a shallow pool and watch as the gloom deepens, you may glimpse my silhouette brushing aside a curtain of vines. You will see only a hulking shape with moss-laden shoulders, as if the forest itself has decided to move. My eyes, half-veiled by lichens, glimmer once in acknowledgment—though you might think it an illusion. Then, as quickly as I appeared, I am gone, swallowed by the hush that birthed me. My path is yours to follow if you dare, but you will quickly lose sight in the dense layers of leaf and vine. It will feel almost mechanical, unstoppable: the cycles of growth and decay, breath and hush, a ghostly engine forging onward. But here, deep in the ancient gloom, mechanical is as natural as breathing.

I am at peace in this place, for my existence is part of its fundamental design. I have no name for myself, and none is needed. I am simply one among the many who keep the hush alive. In this world, you do not find me so much as sense my passing—an impression in the soil, a faint shift in the tangle of canopy, a hush that deepens momentarily before surging back into droning equilibrium. I am the forest’s memory incarnate, moving at the pace of centuries, carrying worlds upon my back.

And here, in this wide, sweltering basin of life, I remain. It is the only destiny that feels true. The hush is my home, the tangles my shelter, the steam and shade my constant companions, and the cyclical pulse of an ancient, breathing mechanism the steady rhythm by which I measure both myself and the unbroken flow of days.
