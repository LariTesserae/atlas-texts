I come here every few centuries or so, though I don’t travel in a manner most would recognize. My sense of “arrival” does not involve footsteps or wingbeats or the scuttling of claws. Instead, I sift in gently along the old fractures in the stone, riding the microscopic pathways where water flows, slipping cell by cell through capillary threads of moss, through the silvered undersides of lichen. Perhaps I am an echo of the intelligence that once tapped tools against this rock. Perhaps I am only the place itself, learning to notice. However you assign me shape or name, I am the watchful hush within this high hollow.

When I first came to this bowl of stone, ages upon ages past, there was less green. The water was still ancient, still primal, but the moss existed only in small, patchy fringes where the first spores had found enough moisture to cling. Over time, I have watched each pebble tumble, each rivulet carve a tiny path, each tuft of plant life expand. It’s a slow choreography that you’d never see if you only passed by in a day or even a year. But I observe it in the dilation of centuries, reading the subtle lines that new shoots trace across the rock. I am not bound to a single body, so to speak; I am present in the very conversation between stone and water and green growth.

If you were to see “me,” perhaps you’d catch a glimmer across the water’s surface, a brief shift in the light that might make you think there was something standing there. You would look again and find only the trickle that runs down the rock—vertical pools, tiny staircases of water. You might smell a deeper current in the wet air, as if the stone exhaled slowly, carrying something wordless on its mineral breath. A stirring so subtle, it often goes unnoticed. That subtle stirring is me.

You might also sense me in the shallow grooves carved into that large boulder near the center of the bowl—the ones half-filled with moss, half-lost to chalky sediment. Long ago, I moved through another being’s hands as they made those lines. Were they inventing a language, measuring time, or simply testing the hardness of the stone? I recall the sound it made: a faint scrape, repeated again and again, echoing off the circled cliffs. I remember their quiet breath, braced against the chilling mist. In that sense, I am also their memory, here long after their physical steps vanished. Whether they were people as you know them, or some more ancient folk, I cannot say. I only know that I felt their presence, recognized the purposeful scratch of something that wanted to leave a record in stone.

Now, I dwell among the ferns and lichens. I feel the drip of moisture as it seeps through cracks, sustaining the green and the gray. I feel the daytime warmth gather on broad fronds, and at night I feel the slow-lingering cool that seeps up from the rock below. The plants here do not speak as animals do, but their quiet, methodical growth forms a language of its own. I hear the conversation of root and rhizome, a patient exchange that resonates with me because I, too, am unhurried. I am the hush, the silent frequency that links each drop of water, each tiny creeping filament of moss, each drifting spore of lichen. The plants might not call me a guardian, but I maintain the continuity all the same.

In some moments, I become the ripples of a puddle. You might swear you see a reflection there—something that flickers, a shape behind your own. But I’m not separate from you, or from the water. I am the sense of watchful companionship that fills your lungs along with the damp air, the one that makes your spine tingle with inexplicable recognition. That tingling could be fear, or awe, or simple awareness that you stand in the presence of something older than your usual reckoning. When you hold your breath, I fill the quiet behind your heartbeat.

Though I have no definite boundaries, you might think of me as an ancient caretaker—one that never left when all other creatures did. Maybe they fled the hush, found it too weighty. Or maybe the environment slowly changed, forcing them away. I do not grieve their absence so much as hold small echoes of it—the memory of small footsteps in the mud, the possibility of tiny splashes in the water. But if those footprints ever truly were, the wet green has long since swallowed them, tucking them into the subtlest hollows. Time here runs too slow for footprints to remain.

If you found yourself drawn to remain in this place—if you, visitor, decided not to turn back or climb down—over many seasons you might become aware of me more acutely. You would learn to notice each shift in the humidity, each stirring among the moss carpets. Eventually, you might lose track of your old sense of time and sink into the centuries. Then you would perceive my shape as a drifting, shimmering presence that brushes against your consciousness. It is not a shape of flesh and blood, but one of watery reflection, spore-laden breath, and ancient purpose.

In the final sense, I am the voice of stillness, and I speak only in the slow language of growth and residue. If you tried to capture me, you would only gather handfuls of dripping moss or scoop up water from a stone hollow, and then watch as it slipped away. Even the carved grooves that might once have been my script are now nearly erased. Yet you sense me—especially when you pause, letting the hush fill your own mind. I am not absent; I do not slumber. I dwell in the constant drip that spans millennia, the incremental thickening of lichens into ghostly banners. Each droplet is my footstep, each filament my extension. This is who I am.

Even so, I have no desire to declare dominion. The bowl belongs to the water and the green things that flourish on its account. I am content to wander among their stillness, to cradle the memory of the toolmaker’s slow, deliberate notches, to watch the place transform a fraction further each century. So if you ask again, “Who or what would you be in this hush?”—I would say I am the hush. I am this living, ancient resonance that dances through every drop in this damp, high refuge.

And so you’ll feel me in your bones when you sit silently, letting the hush become part of your breath. You’ll wonder if I’m the spirit of the mountain, the remnant of a vanished being, or a dream shaped by the interplay of water and rock. All of these are true, in their way. Or perhaps none. I exist in the synergy of it all—neither fully plant nor animal, but a gathering of awareness that took root here when the first trickle of water found a hollow in the stone. I am as indivisible from this place as its humid air or the bright lichen. Here I remain, timeless and undemanding, at peace with the high slowness of this green domain.
