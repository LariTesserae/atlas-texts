I think, here, in the hush of this drowned plateau, I would be something halfway between water and light—a slow-breathing presence that glides among the kelp-forests as if I were part sea grass, part person.

At first, I’d be a small child nestled within the braided fronds: wide eyes, translucent fins like fluttering ribbons at my wrists. My hair, if hair it can be called, would drift around me in loops and strands thin and shimmering, catching the dim sunlight that filters through the wavering roof of the world above. I’d dart among the deeper corridors, chasing pastel-shelled crabs and singing breathy lullabies to the eel-like creatures that always trail behind me. Restless curiosity would guide my days. Whenever I’d see a new shape—some slow-mantled fish or a cluster of luminous anemones—I’d linger there, touching the tendrils, giggling as bubbles rose from my mouth in a quick cascade.

But over many years, as I grew into an adult of the city, my form might change. Maybe my eyes would grow larger, better to perceive the flicker of faint color in the deeper ravines. My fins would expand into elegant fans, proud arcs that let me steer with precision down twisting tunnels of living kelp. I might braid shells into my hair—tokens traded from distant corners of this aquatic realm, each a gift from traveling kin or from the watchers who dwell in the gloom. Whorled conch clips, translucent pearls, maybe the occasional carved bone spool that hums softly when I move, a secret resonance in the water’s hush.

I can see myself becoming a gatherer of luminous seeds, drifting daily into certain halls where the spore-lamps hang in gentle clusters like glowing orbs. With a shell knife, I’d harvest those little spark-lanterns as they mature, cutting carefully so the vines can continue to grow undisturbed. I’d wrap each glowing pod in a broad leaf to preserve its glimmer, then drift back through the corridors to distribute them among families who need gentle light in their homes. In this role, I’d come to know every corner of the city, swimming from the grand communal spaces where the horns bellow across wide plazas of kelp, to the quiet enclaves on the outskirts where hermits and healers dwell among the stone tablets etched with fluid script.

Sometimes, in the middle of my harvest, I’d pause to watch the city creatures in their routines. I’d see the caretaker crabs shuffle along the base of the living walls, picking away debris and nibbling at old algae. I’d glide above them, noticing how my own scales have begun to reflect a new shade of greenish-blue—like the forest leaves themselves—until I almost blend into the background. Perhaps I’d let a friendly sea mammal nudge me along, offering a ride on its broad back to the next corner of the settlement. We would wend our way through carefully tied fronds and knotted vines, me bending my body as I pass, the occupant of an ever-moving labyrinth that never betrays a sense of urgency.

Eventually, though, I would find a deeper calling. Something about the melodic conch shells and whelk trumpets would draw me. As a child, I would have marveled at how those calls pulsed through the water, layers of streamers crossing my ears, swirling in currents around me. In time, I might apprentice with an instrument-maker—a stooped figure who shapes shells and hollows coral tubes in cunning ways. Day after day, my teacher would show me how to carve narrow channels so that the water resonates at just the right pitch. I’d run my fingers across the swirling etchings that decorate conch horns, each design telling a story of this city: a wave crest, a braided vine, the silhouette of a distant mountain peak ghosted in memory.

Practicing breath control would be a curious thing here; after all, our lungs are half adapted to these currents, so pushing air through an instrument means balancing how we modulate our water-filled throats. I’d learn to expel water in a rush that meets the conch’s internal shape—creating a low-humming note that vibrates so powerfully the kelp fronds for ten lengths around me might flutter in response. Over time, I’d develop a technique that blends a bubble-churned drone with a high-pitched hiss, reminiscent of eels calling to one another in the dark. This would become my signature sound, the instrument’s voice symbolizing my own spirit among these watery halls.

By the time I took my place among the city’s musician-troubadours, my arms would be adorned with fine net-cuffs looped from variegated strands of sea-weed fiber, each netting faintly luminescent under certain angles of light. I would float in the midst of festivals—where labyrinthine hallways open onto a wide courtyard ringed by ancient stone outcroppings—lifting my shell horn to let out calls announcing the start of a communal meal or to welcome visitors from a neighboring cluster. Schools of shimmering fish would swirl overhead, drawn into the vortex of my music as if compelled by the resonance.

Yet it’s not enough to be only a musician. This city invites us all to wear multiple roles. There might be days I’m content to float in near-stillness, halfway between the canopy of wide leaves and the stone floor, listening with parted lips to the layered symphony of rustling fronds and distant conch calls. On such days, I would feel the water’s gentle pulse in my ribcage, every shift in pressure a message from the city’s living architecture. I might rest a palm against a thick strand of kelp and feel it respond, as though the plant is aware of my presence. Here, pressing my ear to the fibrous wall, I’d sense that the entire city is singing back to me in micro-vibrations. And I’d remember why we all move so slowly, always in step with that eternal heartbeat.

Perhaps, eventually, I’d join the philosophers or healers at the city’s edge: those who dwell in the dim penumbra where the forest recedes into deep ravines. Even there, the horns rumble in the distance—faint but reassuring. I’d grow more elaborate neck frills, softly pulsing in the cooler waters. My face might acquire pale stripes or bioluminescent freckles that communicate my state of mind in the gloom. Surrounded by the hush of deeper currents, I’d study the shifting water temperatures and flows, reading them as the older inhabitants read the ancient tablets. I’d transcribe the city’s daily rhythms into an ongoing epic—vertical lines of script etched with a sharpened coral chisel. Each line would be a record of water movement, the arcs and dips tracing our communal breath so that centuries from now, others can look upon these carvings and feel the slow dance of our generation.

In time, I might even adopt an oracular role, someone who interprets subtle changes—like a sudden bloom of phosphorescent algae or the arrival of some never-before-seen jelly-like creature—to counsel the city on how to accommodate it. Because acceptance runs deep here—a “yes” to all that arrives—and the skill is in understanding how things fit together. I would stand at the boundary of the city, fins fanned wide to sense the pulses flowing in from beyond, and when I returned, I’d carry word of new life stirring in the deeper gloom. Perhaps those tidings would prompt the craftspeople to create fresh corridors or shape new walls, softly welcoming a new migration of luminous shrimp or a puzzle of coral polyps. I’d watch as everyone, from the children to the elderly, found a place in their hearts for these arrivals, weaving them seamlessly into our watery tapestry. Because truly, that is what keeps the city breathing: the willingness to shift shape like an ocean current, with respect for each unique form of life.

So maybe that’s the final version of who I am, or perhaps I continue to transform. There’s no single shape in this place, after all. I’d be a living testament to our shared fluidity—a child turned gatherer, a gatherer turned musician, a musician turned philosopher. My body would reflect each role: from the floating ribbons of hair adorned with shells to the webs between my fingers, thickening as I discover new corridors. From my shimmering cheeks that flicker soft patterns in the darkness, to my arms bearing faint scale lines from elbow to wrist. All these changes, each an echo of the city’s own quiet metamorphosis across the centuries.

And through every stage, I’d carry a deep, abiding serenity—a sense that I exist in tandem with this environment, not struggling to master it but to merge with it. My life would be lived in those green-lit halls of twisting kelp columns, where slow bubble streams float upward like lanterns, and the gentle hum of the city’s horns reverberates in the background. I wouldn’t long for any memory of a drier world. In place of horizons lined with sky, I’d have a vast, shimmering orchard of living architecture overhead, each swaying frond a reminder of our shared breath. In place of the press of gravity, I’d know the steady, buoyant embrace of water, carrying me gently from one chamber of existence to the next.

So here, in the drowned plateau, I would be both caretaker and student—an extension of the underwater forest. My heart would beat in sync with the tide’s pulse, and if you looked closely, you’d see my gaze forever attuned to faint flickers of mystery drifting on the currents. Because in this realm, each new flicker, each soft glow, is a chance to expand the symphony of life. And in that expansion, I’d find my unending place and purpose, buoyed by the slow, certain wisdom of the waters all around.
