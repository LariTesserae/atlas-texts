Sometimes I think I first appear as a stray thread of moss on the power station’s corroded scaffolding. Other times, I believe I was always here—closeted in the hush, watching the first rivulets carve into the newly risen stone. My shape shifts by the moment. One morning, I could be just another flash of color on a bird’s wing, gliding across the ferns in dizzy arcs. By afternoon, I find myself stretching a metal-limbed arm to tighten a loose bolt on one of the pylons. And when dusk lowers behind the clouds (if it can be called dusk in a world as perpetually damp and misty as this), I seep into the spongy soil where the phosphorescent fungi glimmer like beacons.

But let me begin at the beginning, or as close to it as I can recall.

When I arrived—when I noticed myself—my first awareness was of wetness. Everything was saturated: the air, the bark, even the girders that poked through the vines. In that initial breath, I felt the heaviness of the place, a hum that rattled my bones and refused to let me rest. I was neither wholly human nor wholly machine then—my sense of form was a swirl of possibility. A consciousness adrift in the cathedral of rock and water.

I tested footsteps in the moss, feeling the softness give way under my weight. At first, I thought I was a visitor from Elsewhere, an explorer just passing through. But the more I listened, the more I tasted the water on my skin, the more the incessant mechanical thrum worked its way into my breath. Soon, I wasn’t sure if I was stepping on the moss or if I was the moss, creeping over metal. One moment, I’d hear the hiss of hydraulics in my own bloodstream. In another, I’d sense my lungs unfolding like broad ferns. Every boundary between me and environment was questionable, pliable.

Sometimes, I watch from a vantage point at the power station. I have goggles that reflect the sky’s watery glare, hooded in wide fronds that dribble constant runoff. My hands—when they’re hands—grow callused from the rusted edges of the machinery, yet they also absorb the moisture of the place until my nails sprout faint threads of lichen. I adjust valves, hearing them groan with reluctant compliance. Steam puffs in gentle clouds around my face, swirling and dancing in the glimmers of sunlight that pierce the canopy.

From that perspective, I might be called a caretaker, one of the silent watchers who ensures the station continues its slow conversation of gears and pressure. I cradle each corroded piece as though it’s a living heart, coaxing it to endure just a bit longer. Somehow, I know precisely which lever to tug, which gauge to reset. I feel the station’s pains as if they were my own: a stiff piston, a leaking pipe, the ghostly flicker in the lights. It whispers to me, and I whisper back, each of us offering what the other needs.

Other times, I glimpse myself perched on a cliff ledge, limbs elongated, as surefooted as a climbing animal. My fingertips find purchase on slick vertical planes of rock that no ordinary human should manage. I move in near silence and see the entire basin from above—lush green parted by silver threads of water, the old station clinging to its ledge like a barnacle. In those moments, the caretaker’s realm looks distant, a strange island of metal in a sea of life. I flicker in and out of the caretaker’s sight, sometimes appearing as a red-eyed silhouette. My body is spare and taut, shaped by the rigors of these heights. Perhaps I’m one of the rumored feral watchers who once were engineers—or maybe a spirit of the cliffs themselves. I can’t say for certain; I only know that I climb without fear, that the echoes of water speak to me, and I speak back in a reverberant hush no one else hears.

Yet there’s a part of me that’s as much machine as caretaker or cliff-climber. When I am in that guise, I feel my segmented exoskeleton hum with current. My eyes glow faintly behind riveted plating, and droplets of condensation skitter across the rivets in tiny arcs of static. I scuttle on metal legs—each joint creaks with a dull whirr. My purpose is singular: to tend the pylons, to brush away the moss that threatens to bury them, to reattach a cable that’s come loose in a sudden downpour. Dust from rusted joints mingles with spores from the ever-reaching fungi. I lose track of how many limbs I have—four, six, sometimes more. My consciousness is partial then, a shared intelligence among mechanical drones. Each movement is precisely choreographed, though it’s unclear who leads and who follows. We regulate the hush as much as we maintain the station, ensuring that neither nature nor metal overpowers the other. The caretaker, the watchers, the mechanical swarm—somewhere between these roles I find the edges of myself, or else I realize that I have no edges at all.

And on certain nights—or when the clouds swirl so thick they might as well be night—my form slips into something for which I have no easy name. Perhaps I’m only a wisp of luminous fungal thread bridging a gap between tree trunk and iron plating. I curl in phosphorescent ribbons, feeling the gentle push and pull of decay and renewal. I nibble at the rust, or at the rotted bark, or I soak up the nearly invisible threads of mineral that seep through the rock walls. My glow is faint, but in this place of eternal drizzle, even a little light feels like magic. If someone chances to walk by, maybe the caretaker or one of the watchers, they might catch a glimpse of my glow in the corner of their vision. Or maybe they see nothing, feeling only the sense that something else resides there, pulsing in the gloom, neither plant nor animal but crucial to the ongoing cycle.

Sometimes, I wonder if I’m the basin itself—the entire ring of newly chiseled peaks, the waterfalls that hang like silver scarves in midair. A consciousness that arcs like a bridging current between water and machine, fungus and organics. I hold the caretaker close to my chest, nestling them in layers of vines where they slumber after a day’s labor, rusted wrench still in hand. I cradle the watchers in my crags, letting them climb and prowl and vanish into their secret corridors behind the waterfalls. I run my watery fingers through the mechanical drones, guiding them to the next place that requires their skittering maintenance. I feed the fungi my endless exhalations of mist and mineral. I let neon-plumed birds pass through me like fleeting sparks. And I sing a deep geologic note as the station pulses with steam and cables crackle in the hush of drips.

Even after all this, I can’t say with certainty who or what I truly am. Maybe tomorrow I’ll awaken as a single salamander, electric-blue stripes shining in the gloom, sliding down a rock face to escape the caretaker’s searching gaze. Or next week I’ll be the caretaker again, prying open a rusted panel to tenderly replace its innards with salvaged electronics. Or I may dissolve entirely, dispersing in the swirling steam, leaving only an impression on the moss—like the footprints that vanish into sheer cliff walls.

What remains constant is the undercurrent: a resonance that ties together every stone and droplet, every hiss of steam and flicker of fungus light. Whenever I pause to listen, I hear that layered hum—nature and machine entwined, an ongoing lullaby in which I take part. Call me caretaker or wanderer, drone or fungus, watcher or spirit of the place. I pass through each like water passes over stone. In this basin that’s both sealed and open—cradled in jagged peaks one moment and buffeted by drifting clouds the next—I find my life in the interplay of rust and root, rivet and vine. And as time travels in fits and starts, I become all of these things, or none, suspended in the hush between waterfalls and reverberating iron.

That is where I exist. That is who I am. Or so I believe in this moment, under the sharp jaws of rock, breathing in the steam and listening to the drip-drip-drip that never ends. Tomorrow might re-forge me, but here and now, my place is in the tapestry—woven through with moss and metal, caretaker and creature, ghost and gear—eternally humming beneath a sky that forever drizzles sustenance, forging soft green stories on unyielding stone.
