Sometimes, I imagine I am no bigger than a gleaming beetle, perched atop polished cobbles near one of those shallow puddles. My shell is a midnight-blue carapace that catches scarce torchlight, reflecting it in flashes. I feel the vibrations of every distant scuffle through my jointed legs: each drip from the ceiling, each retreat of a pale-furred rodent, each soft thud of something bigger, as if the labyrinth’s pulse travels through the ground itself into my antennae. In that form, I scuttle between stone ridges and follow damp channels into side-chambers, exploring the place on a scale so small that every carved shelf is a mighty plateau, every wooden door a towering gate.

Other times, I fancy myself one of the pale salamanders. In this shape, I’m neither fish nor reptile but an amphibian ghost, nearly translucent enough that my veins glow pink beneath delicate skin. I spend my days in cool, shallow rivulets, letting water trickle over my ridged spine. My realm is that borderline between rock and stream, where insects gather and lizards rarely stray too long. My eyes—large, watery, adjusted to the half-light—can pick out the subtle gleam of mineral veins in the clay, or the slow swirl of underwater silt as I hunt for swimming gnats. At night (for here, “night” and “day” are only guesses at the cycles above), I creep up onto damp stone blocks, tasting the air with my wide mouth, searching out the faint vibrations of scuttling beetles or exploring new tunnels carved by watery channels I’ve never fully understood but always found comforting.

Some days, I imagine myself one of the larger rodents—sinewy, dust-furred, with whiskers that twitch at every faint breeze. I know how to navigate the labyrinth’s thresholds, how to peer through slivers of broken timbers to glimpse deeper halls. My brood follows behind me, single file, trusting my nose to lead us toward the next safe alcove or hidden food source. I’ve learned the timbre of the brood-mother’s distant calls: a low, resonate note that vibrates in the clay underfoot. While some of my kind panic and scamper away, I have grown used to that echoing cry, leading my group through the labyrinth—down, ever down, collecting seeds from scrawny vines, gnawing at spongy fungus that clings to broken scaffolding, checking each corner for the flash of lizard teeth. My children squeak in alarm if a monstrous silhouette emerges, but I shepherd them behind a wall lip, letting the gloom hide us. In the hush, we wait, then move on again.

Occasionally, I envision myself as one of the cunning reptilian watchers, perched along the high ledges. Perhaps I possess a ridge of short horns, a thick tail used for balance, and eyes that gleam gold in torchlight. I am a sentinel-lizard, half the size of a dog, my territory bridging two main corridors that countless creatures must cross. The ancestors who shaped these halls left behind nooks and vantage points that serve me well. I rest in a patch of weak illumination, soaking up the subterranean warmth where a hidden hot spring vents. Beneath me pass scurrying rodents—just out of my reach. I linger, still as carved stone, until a lone frog hops by, at which point I whip my tail in pursuit, jaws parted in silent, predatory focus. Though I am strong and fierce, I’m in no hurry—I sense I belong to a place that has all the time in the world.

Then I wonder: what if I am no beast at all, but an echo of the ancient builders? A being shaped by centuries within these halls, stooped beneath the low curves of doorways that my forebears crafted so carefully. My skin has turned ashen in the darkness; my eyes, wide and reflective like a cat’s, see more in the gloom than any surface dweller might. By the soft glow of a half-burnt torch, I slide a palm over the smoothed edges of relief carvings that once told stories of a vanished people. My voice, if I dare speak, echoes in a language that has become half-chirr, half-breath, an imitation of the lizards and the frogs. I know how to coax the condensation from clogged channels; how to rebuild a fallen timber support using battered bronze nails. Perhaps I salvage old clay jars to store the mushrooms I cultivate for sustenance. I tread these corridors in silence, watching the interplay of small creatures the way a shepherd might watch flocks. I am caretaker, ghost, and heir to a thousand unspoken rituals—my presence is rumored but rarely confirmed.

Yet there is another possibility: I am a newcomer entirely, a traveler from the world above, exploring the labyrinth with a mix of awe and wariness. I carry a sputtering torch that casts dancing shadows on every damp wall. My boots splash through puddles as I try not to startle the frantic swirl of beetles that scatter when I step too near. I pause in the corridor to watch a salamander drift by in the watery gloom, marveling at how it seems at one with the labyrinth’s hush. I climb half-finished scaffolding, feeling the precarious wobble of damp timbers beneath my feet, and at the top discover an entire colony of lizards sunning themselves by a hidden vent of geothermal warmth. Their eyes track my every motion, neither hostile nor friendly—simply aware. Torchlight flickers over inscriptions hammered into wooden planks, archaic symbols none can read. In that role, I might spend nights pondering how the architecture manages the water so perfectly. By day, I gather spongy fungus in an old crate or barter with unseen dwellers—if they exist—for safe passage deeper in. Each new chamber is a revelation; each sidestep or threshold reveals some quietly thrumming microcosm of life.

As I let my imagination roam, I see that each form is merely another expression of the place itself, for the labyrinth’s hum is a many-voiced chorus, and everyone within it, large or small, is part of that layered music. The beetle scuttles, the salamander glides, the rodent scouts, the lizard guards, the caretaker tends, the traveler explores—and the labyrinth envelops them all. 

I might finally settle into one shape, or perhaps I remain all of them at once, stepping quietly from viewpoint to viewpoint. Sometimes I’m clad in the swirling shadows of a hidden builder, sometimes in glistening scales, sometimes in the hush of pale fur, or the scratch of clicking chitin. I belong to the hush of steps beyond archways, the chorus of dripping water, and the hush-then-flutter of a thousand tiny wings. Whether I walk upright in tattered robes or crawl on my belly through the trickling channels, I am inseparable from this living, echoing realm—child of its warmth and wet stone, a dweller among dwellers.

And so, if I must give a final answer—Where do I find myself? Who am I? I am part of the warren, part of its resonant hum. Whether I clamber across slate floors on six jointed limbs, slither along watery channels in amphibious grace, shepherd a brood of young squeaking forms through dark corridors, or tread softly on two feet, half-forgotten torches in my hand, I belong entirely to the labyrinth. I am at once its guest and its keeper, shaped by generations of water drip, faint light, and the ceaseless interplay of tooth and claw. In its damp corridor hush, I find that I am not simply here—I am of here, inseparable from its domed arches, its damp breath, and its eternal undercurrent of life.
