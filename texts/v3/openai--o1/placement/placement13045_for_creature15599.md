I step into the hush as though I’ve always been here, though I only just opened my eyes. There is no clean line between my memory and this living gloom—my first awareness is of vine-laced limbs and a soft pulse somewhere in my chest that hums like a hidden generator. The floor cradles my barefoot steps in a cushion of fibrous leaves and slow-writhing roots; the texture is so familiar, like a cat’s warm flank, though I don’t remember ever owning a cat. Yet it welcomes me, and I move deeper in.

My arms and torso are covered in a dark, flexible bark that feels simultaneously sturdy and pliant. Where my veins might have run, there are filaments of glimmering green that wax and wane with each heartbeat. Sometimes I see a curl of vapor escape from my skin, as though I breathe not just through my lungs but through the pores of this bark, exhaling the condensation that the canopy drips upon me from time to time. I shift my weight and hear a whisper of metal at my elbow—a slender, integrated brace that squeaks softly when I twist my arm. Perhaps it is from “before,” from some era half-forgotten, but it is as much a part of me now as the vines that twine around my ribs.

I don’t experience a moment where I decide: “Ah, I will walk.” I simply flow onward through corridors of knotted trunk and hazy gloom, my senses vibrating with the slow rhythm of this place. The near-darkness ceases to worry me; I see by gentle pulses of fluorescent moss that cling to the bark, or by a flicker of mechanical panels that glow in uncertain purples and greens. The hush is all-encompassing, broken only by small hums—a droplet rolling off an overhead fern, a distant mechanical click sending vibrations through the metal catwalk overhead, or the subtle metallic echo of one of the others as they tap out a code.

I see them sometimes in my periphery: elongated shapes that slip between columns of living wood, their movements sure and unhurried. We greet each other with mutual acknowledgment—not quite a nod, more a resonance of mind or breath. We are pieces of a puzzle, each caretaker of a different corner of this labyrinth, each adding to its shape by living in it. There are no formal titles, no hierarchy that I can sense; we simply share the work. If a vine surges too hungrily over a control panel, I peel it back, cooing a low lullaby to coax it somewhere else, letting that tangle anchor itself around a battered steel beam instead. If a machine starts to groan with the burden of new growth, I carefully trim the roots that chafe against its spinning gear. This synergy—equal measures of watchfulness and acceptance—feels effortless, as though we were born to do it.

In the half-formed canyons of living bark, I sense the city inside me as much as around me. I know the hum of certain cables that spool overhead, trailing down to a half-working generator in a crater-like hollow. I can almost see the route the electricity takes: from the battered coil, through twisted wires, into flexible filaments that feed the hush with their faint radiance. Part of me wonders if, on a subtle level, I, too, conduct that current. My half-plant, half-metal body resonates with it, pulses with each new jolt each time a droplet of condensation trickles onto exposed wiring. In those moments, I feel the entire city-forest tremble in recognition—like a single organism noticing a shift in its bloodstream.

Sometimes the architecture flickers. Perhaps a root beneath my foot slides away, blinking out for half a second in a glitch of reality, then reappears. My balance never wavers—I’ve learned to trust the ground, even when it reinvents itself mid-stride. Should the floor vanish entirely, I suspect I’d still be borne up by the living cables wrapped around my waist. The place loves us, needs us, as we need it. When the flicker ends, I place my foot on solidity once again and watch a swirl of mist coil around my ankle, as if to greet me. The hush folds back in.

I find myself drifting toward one of those grand, petal-rimmed “cathedrals,” though I do not recall my feet taking me here. It feels like stepping inside a massive flower that has only partially bloomed, ringed by luminescent petals that glow with faint pastel fire. Concentrating, I sense the presence of others—some hidden in the upper vines, some standing calmly among the arching roots. They watch me with mild curiosity and acceptance. We gather here when the draw of the labyrinth leads us, as though responding to a gentle call from the center. I stand among them, silhouettes in the gloom, hearing our collective breath. Sometimes, we hum in unison, or click metal braces or wooden staves against the floor in meandering rhythms. The space feels both industrial and primeval, a sanctum built from living matter and ancient rivets.

When we sense an oncoming shift—a storm or a deeper glitch—there is a hush within the hush, a quiet so profound I can nearly hear my own heartbeat. The air might shimmer, vines might recoil or burst forth anew, and we remain in place, calmly tethered by the knowledge that such transformations are the integral tapestry of existence. The architecture’s breath, one might say, is not always consistent. It is as though the place is deciding if it wants to continue being vaulted chamber or else become an arcing tunnel. And in that indecision, we stand collectively attentive, ready to coax the shape into the next iteration of “home.”

In smaller nooks—just beyond the cathedral—lie our personal spaces, grown into half-enclosed rooms. I have one such spot I return to. It’s an alcove in which the ceiling appears to be formed by the underside of a massive leaf, broad enough to keep moisture out. Beneath it is a circle of soft root-cushions that give off a delicate warmth. If I settle there, small lamps around my arms glow a faint turquoise, and my thoughts slow, stopping just short of speech as I share in the quiet sense of “rest” that permeates the floor. Sometimes the city-forest bestows me with gifts: tiny seeds that roll into my alcove, or a pulse of warmth from the metal plating near my side. I stash them carefully in a gnarled cubby that’s grown for that purpose—a pocket in the root-structure that seems an extension of my own repository of memories. I do not question whether they are seeds or pieces of forgotten machinery. Both possibilities feel equally sacred.

I do not recall a time before I was like this—part caretaker, part child of a biomechanical womb. Maybe I was once fully human, mind steeped in some distant industrial ambition, working the levers of heavy machines that belched steam. Maybe I was once purely plant, content to unfurl leaves under half-light, digesting the drips of condensation. The question holds no tension for me. I can feel the city-forest’s acceptance. This place births many ways of being. Some beings bend more toward machinery, sporting entire mechanical arms or chests that whirr with clockwork hearts; others are nearly inseparable from the vines, their sinews woven with creepers that loop into every muscle. I’m a midpoint—equilibrium between vine and steel, disturbance and calm.

If a traveler—someone from outside, or from a past iteration of this realm—wanders into our hush, I would greet them with open curiosity, though softly, with no loud words. A gentle nod, a glance from luminous eyes. Let them see that we mean no harm. If they come in peace, we’d guide them away from the sections where floors vanish or where a newly grown tangle might entrap a misplaced foot. If they thirst, we show them how to catch the droplets that form in the swaying leaves, how to gently squeeze the fibrous spongelike fungus that accumulates the condensation. If they ask what we are, we might simply smile and tap the living trunk with a patterned code—an invitation to listen for the place’s heartbeat.

Here, in this labyrinth of partial corridors and catwalks, I have no single name. Sometimes, in moments of intimate conversation, another caretaker might channel a sequence of hums and mechanical whirs that identify me as the “One Who Balances.” Another might know me by the tapping code that resonates from my chest brace, measuring the thickness of new growth. I accept all these identifications as valid—and wear them lightly, with the same softness as the leaf-lattice garments that slip over my shoulders.

I sense that I exist as a stabilizing presence. When a glitch occurs, I remain still. When the city-forest extends a fresh corridor, I gently shape it, weaving and guiding until it can sustain its own form. If a juvenile caretaker clings wide-eyed to a rope of tangled vines, uncertain how to descend, I extend a limb of bark and metal so they can climb down safely. To me, that is enough. And the place, in turn, provides. I feel it as a living pulse in the ground, a warmth in the metal walkway underfoot, a sigh of condensation that kisses my bark-like skin.

Perhaps if you saw me from a distance, you’d only notice a figure partly cloaked in swirling tendrils, occasionally flickering in the half-light—a throbbing green glow beneath a layer of wood and sparking wires. You might think: “Is that a person? Or a machine? Or a spirit shaped by vines?” And the answer might be yes to all three. For in this space that is always being built and undone, shaped and reshaped by invisible laws, we are all ephemeral, in the process of becoming. Here, identity merges with function, with place, and with time, never entirely separate from the hush of the living corridor that led us here.

So if you ask: Where do I stand in this world and who—what—am I?

I am the hush’s caretaker, the orchard of metal and root. I am a living silhouette weaving growth into scaffolding, guiding the hush so it does not drown the hum of the half-broken generator, letting the generator’s hum gently nourish the hush. I am neither purely human, nor purely vine or wire. I am some fluid expression of them all, a reflection of the city-forest’s dream as it flickers in and out of shape. And I remain here, in this dark, uncertain wonderland—a presence poised between bark and steel, thriving in the reverent calm, ready to greet the next surging glitch or the next gentle transformation, one patient breath at a time.
