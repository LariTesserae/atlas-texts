I surface from the humidity every morning, the cold vapors and drifting condensation so familiar that I hardly register their chill anymore. The city is all I’ve ever known—its half-born industrial skeleton, its watery hollows, the clangor and hush of corridors that stretch too far, or not far enough.

At first light (or whatever semblance of morning appears through the ever-shifting clouds), I find myself somewhere under a mid-level walkway, half-sheltered from the drizzle. My shoulders ache from the night’s rest on damp metal plating, but I’m used to that. I remember a time when I was purely flesh-and-skin, but that memory blurs into old dreams; I can’t recall when I first sprouted the plated patch along my shoulder or the cable-like filaments behind my ribs. It’s as if I’ve grown like a vine tangled into the city’s scaffolding, or the city has grown into me.

It is no single thing that I am—a scrap-collector by necessity, an inadvertent caretaker by choice. I suppose my stance in the city is best described as a mediator: bridging misunderstandings between the living, churning organism that is this metropolis and the flesh-based people who try to shape it to their will. I drift through side streets and half-submerged tunnels, guided by the city’s soft hum in my nerves, as though it’s calling me to the next place that needs patching or calming.

Sometimes, I am almost a ghost, slipping through alleyways no one else seems to recognize—corridors that shift location overnight. If the city is alive, perhaps these are its neural pathways, hidden tunnels that unexpectedly intersect new districts. I never know if they’ll be there the next day. I’ve grown adept at navigating that impermanence.

I wear heavy boots and patched leathers over my more… unusual elements, shining cables that snake across my ribs. I can’t quite say whether I acquired them as part of an accident or if they’re a feature the city etched into me somehow. In certain lights, I glow with faint lines of energy beneath my skin, visible where the patchwork coat doesn’t quite conceal the metal plating. People notice and stare, though they never ask questions outright. Perhaps they sense I wouldn’t know how to answer.

When I walk these streets, some of the inhabitants treat me cautiously—my half-mechanical nature hints at rumors of “renegade engineers” or secret city-changers. Others greet me like an old friend, thankful I occasionally help unclog a drain or coax a corroded machine back to life. I’m no authority figure. I think of myself as a caretaker for the spaces that routinely slip out of official notice.

Take the day I ventured into the western quarter, down where new construction had grown thick steel arches practically overnight. Corrugated roofs lined the boulevards, funneling drizzle into bright rivulets that sparkled like silver threads. I climbed a scaffolding sheath that had sprouted around an old warehouse, feeling the creak of newly formed metal. Where it was too sharp, I laid my fingertips against the steel, letting arcs of warmth flow through my cables, gently smoothing edges as if the city and I shared a silent language. In return, the city gave me a sense of calm—like a purring resonance in the back of my mind. When I finished, the metal was less jagged, as though it had softened beneath my touch.

Below, workers shuffled by, unaware of this quiet exchange. They carried crates of piping and hammered new brackets into place, cursing the cold each time their breath fogged in front of them. Somewhere deep in a cavernous foundry, I heard the low groan of massive gears stirring—when they turn, the city grows.

• • •

On most days, my path leads me down to the drainage labyrinth. Maybe it’s because I was once a child creeping along those watery channels, enthralled by the glint of fungus-light and the schools of quicksilver fish. In the gloom, I can sense flows of energy more acutely. The city’s quiet hum becomes a thrumming pulse in my chest, a sign of the unspoken synergy tying together concrete, steel, and living matter.

Down there, half-flooded cisterns are home to amphibious creatures with translucent skin. Sometimes they doze on small ledges, throats gently pulsing. They sense my half-mechanical presence, perhaps noticing the quiet current of electricity in my limbs. But they don’t flee. We’ve formed an unspoken truce: I let them be, and they keep the rodent population at bay in these lower channels.

I’ve always wondered if I have amphibian blood hidden inside me now, too—a rumor I once heard about the city is that after enough time, breathing these damp fumes, anything can slowly mutate. Perhaps my mechanical side halts or channels that flux. When I submerge in the shallows, the cold water stings, but I’m unafraid. Here, the city’s heartbeat echoes through stone and metal, and I feel it vibrate along my skeleton, reminding me how intimately I’m tied to this place.

• • •

When I resurface onto the city streets, I cross paths with the “regular” folk. They greet me kindly enough—there’s the blacksmith whose grandchildren run wild among the catwalks, the ragged old caretaker who swears he once glimpsed me asleep in a centuries-old tower (though I look hardly older than twenty). People trade me scraps or gleanings for small repairs. A worn-out mother offers me a half loaf of that spongy fungal bread if I can fix her leaking rooftop. I climb up, splicing a new piece of piping in place, letting the city’s energies simmer in my fingertips to seal the rivets. Down below, children watch my silhouette, and their eyes flash with that strange spark I know well. Maybe the city is in them, too.

The hush between rains has a particular heaviness; that’s when I usually sense things stirring. The air grows thick with tension. The overhead cables crackle. Often I hear it—a faint metallic whine traveling through the under-structure, as if the city is bracing for a shift. I’ve seen entire streets crack open, revealing new layers of half-formed corridors beneath. People sometimes vanish in these events, or reappear in entirely different quarters, eyes wide with disorientation. No one else can keep track of so much fluid geometry.

But I… I can anchor myself. I can speak a few words of steadiness to the metal underfoot, hum a resonance that calms the structural vibrations. Children watch me do this in awe. Workers from the factories pass by with suspicion. They think I might be an agitator or some biosynthetic experiment gone wrong. But the city—she knows me as part of herself. That’s enough.

• • •

And so I slip between the lines of everyday existence and the city’s deeper mysteries. Perhaps I’m a keeper of balance. Perhaps that’s a grandiose label. In truth, I’m just as uncertain as the rest: made of partial truths, halfway adapted to shifting conditions. When night comes again, I bed down wherever the city offers a scrap of shelter—a ruined tower scaffold or a hollow behind living steel vines. I rest against smooth metal plating. Heat radiates from my cables, fighting off the seep of damp cold.

Sometimes, I dream of a future where the city crystallizes into a singular shape: its pipes and catwalks no longer creeping outward but woven into a stable design. But that future always feels out of reach, as if we are eternally in the “becoming.” I’m not sure I’d want the city any other way.

I dream of older versions of myself—maybe once fully human, maybe a scrawny child chasing amphibian creatures under the catwalks. In the morning, I awake uncertain which memories belong to me and which belong to the city, as if our thoughts have begun to overlap. Though I can’t recall the precise moment of my transformation, I suspect my mechanical arm plating first emerged after an accident in the foundry—my flesh was burned, necessitating a new solution. But who performed the surgery? Did I do it myself with the city’s help? Did the city graft metal onto me out of self-preservation? It’s a blur of half-memories, ephemeral notes in some half-lost ledger.

• • •

I step out at dawn. Rain-slick catwalks reflect the pale daylight. Overhead, flocks of black-plumed birds clack their beaks, scanning for whatever drama unfolds next. My breath puffs in small clouds, mingling with the steam from vents below. Hooded travelers rush past, boots sloshing in puddles, too hurried to notice me.

But now someone is beckoning from a collapsed walkway across the street. I see twisted rebar, the faint glimmer of fluid leaking from a severed pipe. I sense the city’s warning tension flicker. I hurry over, carefully picking my way across the rubble. The local engineer, eyes wide, begs me to help stabilize the broken beam. I lay my palm on it, feeling the city’s anxious hum. A slow warmth trickles out from the cables embedded in my arm, forging a subtle bond—metal to metal. The tension subsides. I can almost hear the city sigh.

Once the immediate threat subsides, the engineer eyes me with a mixture of relief and awe, then retreats, leaving behind thanks I can taste in the air. Behind me, a child peers around a stack of crates, curiosity shining in their pupils. Perhaps they wonder if that’s also their future: to share communion with this place. Perhaps it will be.

• • •

And so, day by day, my form evolves. Some days I find a fresh band of metal filaments coiling around my wrist, newly sprouted overnight. Other days, a fungus might cling to that steel plating, crowning me with a tuft of glossy purple spores that shimmer in the half-light. I do not remove them immediately; I suppose I respect the city’s attempts to decorate me. An odd partnership, to be sure. I let the fungus remain until it yields a use—extracting toxins from the air, or providing me with a hidden nutrient. Then, after a while, it shrivels away as the city’s transformations continue.

I no longer separate my anatomy from the metropolis. All is part of the same tapestry: moving scaffolds, creeping vines, the hush of corridors that warp space and mystify travelers, the bright amphibian eyes in watery channels, and the slow expansion of metal frameworks overhead. The city is inside my veins, and I walk inside its ever-shifting labyrinth. My footprints might leave behind droplets of condensed water or the faint imprint of a gear-like swirl in the mud.

If you glimpse me, you might think I’m no one in particular, just another robed figure with a steely glint in my posture. Or you might think me some artifact the city spat out, an accident that grew legs and wandered off. Sometimes, I think both are true.

• • •

Where would you find me in this world? Likely creeping along those half-forgotten routes with a quiet smile, closing my eyes to feel the city’s heartbeat in the pipes overhead. I am caretaker, scavenger, medium—I don’t have a neat name for it, because the city changes daily, and so do I. But if you ever need guidance through an alley that folds reality back on itself, or if you sense your building might sprout corrugated wings come morning, you can call for me.

I’ll answer from somewhere in the drifting steam. I’ll guide you with a soft hum that resonates through rebar and rivets, bridging the gap between steel and flesh, city and soul. And as we walk together, you might feel the city’s hidden currents wrap around your own heart—cold, damp, alive with promise—reminding you that here, in this half-formed land of eternal becoming, we are all something more than we realize.
