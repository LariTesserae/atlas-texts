I drift along a corridor where the water presses around me like a womb of churning silence and deafening roar all at once. I cannot tell where I began nor quite where I end; my boundaries feel as porous as the metal plating that frames these halls. Sometimes, I catch sight of my own reflection in a pitted steel beam: an amalgam of meaty limbs and mechanical couplings, seeing eyes and seeing sensors, a patchwork that braids the organic with the forged. In places, my skin has grown as leathery and weatherworn as the corroded pipes; elsewhere, strange metal has fused to my flesh, or perhaps grown in place of it. I used to think it was something done to me—some operation or accident of the city’s shaping—but now, I’m no longer sure if the city remade me or I was always like this.

When I’m pressed against the corridor’s bulkhead, feeling the hum of hidden gears and the hush of water surging overhead, I remember that once I might have been a visitor. Perhaps I stumbled in from some spiral vent or crawled out of a broken access hatch, newly arrived and blinking at the gloom. But time here is as slow as the rust’s creep. Memories get swallowed by the rhythmic churn of turbines and engines. So I’ve grown comfortable with not knowing which parts of me are my birthright and which parts are the city’s grafts. One day it no longer mattered. I simply belonged.

Sometimes, I see my reflection and notice new details: a fresh bracing along my left forearm, a plating that wasn’t there yesterday. It might be a gift from the maintenance drones that hum by at all hours, quietly torch-welding micro-fissures along walls—and, sometimes, along the inhabitants themselves. Or perhaps I grew it the same way a barnacle latches onto a wharf. This city wants its dwellers to be solid, capable of withstanding the storms that batter the labyrinth. I suppose I’m only too relieved for the extra strength.

I have no name for what I am; such distinctions feel less vital than the steady, thumping heartbeat of the place. I only know I’m part of the system: a caretaker in some small way, or a custodian of corners that others seldom reach. I roam the narrower avenues, where the lights are faint and the pipes overhead tangle like steel vines. I trace the lines of hot coolant as it surges and bleeds through the city’s iron capillaries. With each churn of the engines, I can almost hear the metallic echoes singing a code I only half understand. It is a language of hiss and clank, the city urging me onward.

My tasks are scattered. Sometimes, I salvage old cables half buried in silt and algae, carefully spooling them away so the next wave of watchers can pass unhindered. Other times, I kneel at a half-collapsed junction, one hand braced on a pitted pipe, and wait for the drones with their bright lamp-eyes to appear. Together, we patch the crack with molten metal or a welded brace, forcing away the ceaseless trickle of infiltration. Then, just as silently, they move on. I do not speak to them, nor they to me. But in their presence, I sense a wordless mutual respect: each of us ensuring these corridors remain intact another century.

Occasionally, I catch the shape of something large drifting through the water at the threshold of my vision. Perhaps a sentinel waking from old slumber, or one of the watchers that slip between overhead ducts. My heart quickens—there’s a primal awe in glimpsing a figure so vast that it can shift entire currents with a slow tilt of its limbs. I feel the vibrations in my chest when they pass. What must it be like to hold so much power in a single steel spine? I do not know if they pay me any mind; I am smaller than a rivet to them. But I bow my head as they go, offering them a silent salute in this murky, mechanical realm. We share the same reliance on these walls, these pumps, these exhaust vents that constantly churn the water around us.

I have a solitary workshop of sorts, or at least I think of it that way. It’s a shallow recess near the base of a reactor column where warm water seeps in, swirling grains of silt into mesmerizing patterns. I’ve placed a patchwork screen there, hammered out of discarded plating, to shield me from the strongest jets. Within that niche, I keep bits of salvage I’ve gathered: an odd coil that glimmers with latent electricity, a spool of half-corroded wire, a handful of twisted metal that resonates with the hum of the city. Arranged in careful shapes, they form a kind of shrine. Whenever I pause to rest in that place, I run my fingertips over the edges, feeling how they’ve begun to corrode into each other. Over time, the city’s slow alchemy merges them into one piece. I catch myself thinking: Perhaps that is how I’ve become what I am, too. The city’s tides have fused me slowly to itself.

In the grander vaults, I appear only rarely; I’m more comfortable in the half-lit corridors that connect to deeper channels. Yet sometimes circumstance leads me out there, especially when a major rupture occurs and all inhabitants must converge. When pressure spikes threaten a meltdown, or when superheated steam roars from a broken turbine, every living thing—drone, hybrid, or otherwise—spills into the open spaces. In those moments, I see the scale of our population: mechanical serpents coiled around scaffoldings, half-human mechs bracing collapsed supports, even an array of small creatures that seem neither wholly robotic nor animal, scuttling to safety under the catwalks. We form a chain of hissing torches and gripping limbs, reinforcing the battered sector with new patches. And once it’s stable enough that the city’s heartbeat evens out, we drift away again to our respective corners—no ceremony, no applause. Survival itself is worship here.

In secret, I harbor a quiet longing to see the “Heart” that the wanderers speak of. A central forge or mind or furnace buried beneath centuries of rusted corridors. Some nights, while drifting through the labyrinth, I hear rumors whispered in the watery gloom—muffled stories about flickers of molten light, or a chamber so warm it could boil the entire sea if unsealed. They say every pipe in the city leads back to it, each turbine an echo of its unstoppable churn. Sometimes I think I might descend one day and slip through unguarded vents, seeing how far I can push before the heat melts the flesh from my bones or the pressure crushes me. I’d bring that spool of wire or that shimmering coil as an offering, kneeling before the Heart to finally understand whether it’s alive in some ancient, stirring sense. But those are just reveries. The city’s labyrinth is made of infinite corridors. The deeper one goes, the more the labyrinth tends to swallow you whole.

I’ve taken to letting my voice join the hum sometimes. When I’m alone, ferrying salvage or scrubbing mineral crust from an intake valve, I sing in the muffled stillness. My voice echoes strangely through the water and metal. I imagine the watchers in the vents pausing in their remote corners, listening in. Maybe they hear my improvised tune, maybe they don’t. Sometimes I fancy hearing an answer—an odd chord roiling in the water. In those moments, the city itself feels alive. There’s a sense that my solitary notes have meshed with the drone of turbines and fans, that I’ve become a single, faint pipe in the grand organ of this home.

And that is what it is firmly for me now: home. In the faint glow of algae-dulled lights and the swirling motes of heated silt, in the rhythmic clang and hiss of pressurized steam, I find my belonging. I find the heartbeat of something bigger than I can name. I am neither drone nor simply flesh, neither overseer nor silent lurker. I am somewhere in between, a bridging piece in this city’s living machine. I scrape away rust, I patch the cracks, I greet the watchers with wordless glances. I keep half an ear trained on the dream of the deeper chambers, letting that possibility guide my steps through corridors drowned in half-dark.

I do not know if I will ever see sunlight again, nor do I dwell on that. This place has its own light, dim and shifting as it may be, and so do I. We survive together, letting the cycles of steam and tide draw on day after day. Our labyrinth of metal and brine persists—past colliding with future, flesh merging with wire, breath humming in time with the constant roar. I have become a child of the city, a limb of its function, an indistinct piece that might pass unnoticed by many who come and go. But I know my place is integral; we all are. And in the hush between engine thrums, if I close my eyes, I can sense the city’s pulse passing through each welded seam in my skin. It is ancient, unstoppable, and so am I.
