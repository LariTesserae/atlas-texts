I do not remember if I always lived here or if I simply arrived one day, trudging in from some distant place where the mists end. At first, I saw myself as a traveler, boots heavy with sticky mud, mouth tasting the weight of this air that seems to cling to the lungs. But over time, distant memories faded like sunken footprints, so that now I cannot divorce my own reflection from the land’s gray palette. Perhaps I, too, have been slowly sculpted here, shaped by the weeping sky and the hush of water. And so I will describe how it is to be me, caught somewhere between an echo of some older civilization and a creature grown from mud and spore.

In my earliest recollections, I wandered the edge of the flats, stepping uncertainly on slick stones that gave way beneath me, forcing me to catch my balance with flailing arms. My reflection wavered beneath the mirrored surfaces of puddles, and I saw a figure draped in strange cloth that was not cloth—a tableau of overlapping, filmy bands that fluttered like gills against my knees. I hadn’t fashioned these wrappings myself; they were offered to me by a group of dwellers in the distance—others who moved hunched and slow, the same way I now did. They showed me how to strip the pale membranes of certain fungal clusters, how to soak them in swirling waters rich with spore and let them ferment until the texture turned pliable.

In those days of half-conscious arrival, it felt both dreamlike and purposeful: they recognized me as if I belonged, yet they asked no question of how or why. It was a silent acceptance, born maybe of necessity: in this place, any extra pair of hands is welcome to gather peat lumps, to scoop water, to rebuild a leaning hut. Being alone too long in the unbroken mist is unwise.

As time went on, the strange wrappings clung tighter to my body, adopting a sheen that, when the watery light struck it just so, lit with faint phosphorescence. Their subtle glow created a small aura in the thick gloom, enough for me to see my path as I moved. That's how I knew I was changing: this “cloth” was merging with me, growing in synergy, breathing my warmth. In return, it offered a modicum of insulation from the damp. My skin itself—once a warmer tone, or so I think—turned pale gray. I caught my reflection again in the shallows: my cheeks had acquired a flattened, fin-like contour that extended behind my ears, whisking moisture away so that water wouldn’t accumulate. My eyelids, once ordinary, seemed layered now: an outer, semitransparent membrane, able to flick down to protect me from the stinging drizzle and swirling spore clouds. Had I always had these features? The memory was gone. They felt as natural as breathing.

I found I could move with an uncanny quiet through the drizzle. My legs bent in a perpetual half-crouch, toes splayed wide so that each step caused minimal sinking. Scavenging the mud for lumps of usable peat or driftwood rods felt oddly intuitive, as though I had done it all my life. I soon learned to notice the faint swirl of water just below the surface, betraying an amphibious predator’s approach, or the minimal shift of wind that heralded the wing beats of an enormous flyer overhead.

In short, I became a dweller among dwellers.

My dwelling sits by the remnants of a stone archway: a modest hut, built from driftwood poles strapped together with braided fungus fibers. The walls are plastered with a mud-and-spore paste that keeps the worst of the damp out. Inside, a small hearth of smoldering peat lumps offers meager warmth—enough to stave off the shivers. The peat’s smoke curls upward and is lost in the perpetual fog. I share this place with two others. They, like me, are neither wholly human nor entirely alien: their eyes glint with reflective silver when angles of the dim light catch them. They have learned to read my gestures, and I theirs. We speak sparingly with breathy clicks and hums that do not carry far in the cacophony of calls and echoes that fill the wetlands.

Sometimes, the three of us gather around the small fire, huddled under the arch’s battered stone. There, we piece together the day’s foraging: a few lumps of soft fungus gleaned from rock crevices, some nutrient-rich slime carefully scraped from the underside of a half-living trunk that juts from the muck. We set these morsels in a little clay dish near the fire, cooking them just enough to remove any bitter toxins. I have grown used to the flavor—damp, metallic, with a tinge of earthiness dashed with faint luminescent aftertaste.

Each day starts with a quiet stirring. I step outside, letting the swirl of morning drizzle wash over me. The fog is thick as soup, but I sense motion in the corner of my vision: other dwellers passing by, carrying woven baskets on their backs, hoods drawn low over their faces. Farther off, hidden behind the drifting haze, come the roars and calls of creatures so large I can only imagine their outlines—perhaps leathery flyers or gargantuan insects that ring out in primal chords. Their voices resonate through the spongy ground, vibrating in my bones, reminding me that we are all precarious guests here.

On a good day, I might help gather the salvage from a half-sunken ruin: bits of metal bracing, the remains of a broken beam or a rusted hinge. After rummaging, we drag these relics to a collective workspace near the largest spire of worn stone. The sound of hammering is subdued beneath the wet air’s roar, but the ring of metal on stone still echoes strangely, as if some half-buried corridor lies just below our feet, carrying the sound away to distant chambers. We shape the scrap into small tools—knife blades, spear points, nails—dull but functional. The damp condenses on every surface, each raindrop turning to shimmering beads that roll down the hammered metal.

At intervals, the hush is broken by a deeper boom—something that might be an underground shift or the crash of a large beast charging through shallow water. We always pause, looking around in alarm, ready to flee if the source grows too near. Occasionally, we see a shimmering shape in the distance—colossal arthropods with translucent carapaces pulsing with internal fluid. They forage for fungal lumps too, but we have a kind of truce: as long as we give them space, they ignore us. Sometimes, we collect their shed chitin plates to use as roofing or shallow water basins. Such brittle pieces are a modicum of preciousness in this stretched-thin existence.

During the midday gloom, when fog thickens, I often slip away to one of the ancient mirror-polished stones rising from the mud—these surfaces are worn to an almost supernatural glint by eons of condensation. Leaning my forehead against their cool planes, I can sense a faint thrum, like the heartbeat of the earth. It is a humming, watery pulse that seems to cycle beneath everything. Some say it’s the tide of subterranean streams; others believe it’s the land’s vital spirit. I close my eyes and press my ear to the stone. The resonance merges with my breathing until it feels like my own pulse, reminding me that I belong here now—whether that was always my destiny or not.

In the distance, beyond the next cluster of huts, stands one of the last crumbling arches that might once have been part of a grand structure, though time has softened its edges into lumps of near-geologic shape. Sometimes at twilight, small groups gather there for a shared ritual. We kneel or squat in a half-circle around a shallow pool, guided by one of the older dwellers who carries a staff topped with a cluster of softly glowing spores. There, we hum together in a wavering chant designed to match the land’s own undercurrent. Our combined breath lifts small clouds of luminescent spore dust in the gloom, and I can see it swirl, drifting easily from voice to voice, bridging us together. It’s in those moments that I feel most vividly bound to this place, as though it is not a mere land but a being that tolerates our presence if we pay it homage in the correct manner.

After the ceremony, we disperse to our huts or brave the watery expanses on small rafts, punting over drifting channels to new foraging grounds. Now and then, something large moves in the water beneath the raft. The ripples jerk us side to side, and we steel ourselves, spears at the ready. The predator might surface—a serpentlike head, pale with milky, sightless eyes—only to vanish again, aware that we are armed. The echo-charged roar of distant calls rises to a crescendo, heightening our tension. We press on, hearts hammering in our throats, only exhaling when the water is quiet once more.

By nightfall, the fog truly thickens, swirling in curtains so dense they blot out the last ghostly hints of moon or star. The wetlands turn labyrinthine—water, stone spires, old ruins, huts all fade to silhouettes. I’ve learned to navigate by sound and by faint shifts of luminescent fungus that glow at the water’s edge, or by tiny arthropods that blink from beneath the surface. Nights are alive with monstrous voices. Some come from winged brutes soaring overhead, others from insects that blare a trumpeting call, answered by deep vibrations from the watery depths. At times, the noise is so loud I can feel it pressing against my eardrums, rattling my ribs.

When the noise peaks, I crouch by the fire in the cramped hut, secure beneath the angled roof of driftwood and chitin. My ragged spore-wrappings cling to my shoulders, occasionally flickering. I try to still my breath, focusing on that deeper pulse, letting it merge with the thunderous rhythms outside. All I can do is hope that whatever hunts in those hours finds easier prey elsewhere. Usually, I am safe enough. In the morning, I emerge to see where the mud has sloughed away into new channels, or which neighbor’s hut might have been half-submerged in the night. The watery ground feels ever-changing, reminding me that nothing here lasts long in the same shape.

And yet, for all the precariousness, I feel no urge to leave. Perhaps I’ve been here many lifetimes. Perhaps it’s been just a few weeks, and I’ve already lost track of the outside world. Either way, the land has claimed me. My mouth craves the taste of mineral-laced air, my eyes have grown accustomed to endless drifts of gray. I find a certain forlorn beauty in the hush of fog at dawn, in the shimmering spore-lights beneath the water’s surface at dusk, in the rhythmic chanting beneath the ruins’ arches at twilight. Even the battered huts, half-sunken and precarious, feel more like home than any memory of structured walls. My reflection, caught in a mirrored pool, stares back at me with wide, reflective eyes and fin-like ridges behind the ears. I see no difference between the fungal threads of my robe and the living spires that push up from the mud. I am of them, and they are of me.

Some days, I wonder if I might metamorphose further. Will my skin grow more translucent? Will the fungus that clings to my garments take root in my flesh, bridging me closer to the land’s primal heartbeat? Might I, eventually, lose my shape altogether, merging with a half-buried structure or standing as a solitary guardian among the smoothed stone spires, a living statue in constant communion with the watery echoes beneath? The question arises, but it does not scare me. Here, existence itself is an endless cycle of transformations—a dance of survival performed in time to the land’s thrumming pulse. I am simply part of it, however small.

And so, if asked, “Where do you find yourself in this world?” I would say: I live amid the half-sunken ruins and drifting mists, my wide feet planted in spongy ground that resonates with unseen catacombs. I inhabit a meager patch of land where the wind-lashed drizzle runs perpetual, where hush and thunder overlap in a ceaseless chorus. “Who, or what, are you?” you might then ask. And I would close my eyes, lift my hand to sense that quiet tremor inside the polished rock, and give my answer: I am a dweller shaped by water and fog, a caretaker of rotting huts and faintly glowing spores, a traveler perhaps turned native, joined inseparably with the land’s echoing heartbeat. Where once I might have been only passing through, now I remain, hunched and silent, forging small tools in the gloom, singing in low chorus with the pulse that underlies us all.
