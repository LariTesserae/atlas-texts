Perhaps the first moment of arrival finds me crouched in a shallow recess of stone, breathing the chill air made damp by the endless trickle of water. My fingertips brush the faintly glimmering walls—cool, solid, and smoothly chiseled. I’m neither explorer nor intruder, yet I don’t feel like I belong, either. The hush presses down, and it’s almost like I’ve become a witness to something that was never meant for my eyes. I start to think that maybe I’m only half here, caught between being a stranger and being part of the citadel itself, not quite comprehending which role I’m supposed to fill.

I lean close to the polished slab and see my own reflection in the mineral sheen. But my reflection wavers in the watery gloss, and for the briefest instant, I glimpse something else—a face like mine but not entirely. Its features are unmoving, angular, almost integrated with the stone’s geometry. Then the rivulet slithers away, and the face returns to normal. But the feeling remains: that I’m only one breath away from merging with the stones, or that I carried a piece of them even before I set foot in this place.

If I linger too long, I start to sense pulses underfoot, thrumming quietly in my bones, as though the stone has a heartbeat. And in that moment, I think: what if I am the pulse? What if I am not the typical inhabitant but a presence knit into the architecture—someone who once had flesh but has lived so long among these passages that my skin has hardened into mineral? My form might be nearly humanoid but made of the same faintly shimmering rock, my veins carrying trickles of water rather than blood. I move so slowly that, to an outside observer, I seem statue-still—only shifting position after enough time that the sun’s path overhead has arced from dawn to dusk and back again. In these extended rhythms, my thoughts become reflections of geology: measured in centuries, not seconds.

Other times, I imagine myself as a caretaker of the water. My essence is fluid, drifting along every channel. When I stand upright, I have shape—water in watery form—transparent and luminous in bright sunlight. I flow across polished floors, whisper down chiselled ramps. I re-form into something like a head and torso as I pool in a recess. Then I seep through a fine crack and vanish, carried deep within the structure. Perhaps I gather in hidden cisterns alongside others of my kind—living shapes of flowing water that keep to the labyrinth’s heart. When the time is right, we emerge to sweep away debris or shift the channels so that the citadel continues to function perfectly.

But these visions are fleeting. The architect within me wonders if I might be one of the original builders—or something that inherited their work. I see myself hunched over a slab, worn, calloused hands guiding a chisel. My hair is silvered by the glaring light off the mineral floors. My ears have grown sensitive to the pitch of stone under the strike of the hammer. Maybe I’m part of a lineage of stoneworkers, passing along an ancient craft. Day after day, year after year, I shape new corner pieces, refine old ramps, ensuring water flows exactly as intended. There’s no hurry, no rush. I move to a silent internal pace that no clock can measure. In the hush of these corridors, each chisel strike is a private conversation with the stone—slow, deliberate, precise, like the steadiest of heartbeats.

And yet, sometimes I wonder if I am more ephemeral, part of the mind or spirit that suffuses it all. It’s as though the citadel and I comprise a single consciousness: I weave among corridors and sense every angle as if it were a line in a grand, living blueprint. I can feel micro-adjustments in the geometry. Without speech, I “speak” by redirecting the angle of water flow or smoothening a ridge. My mind is the movement that aligns this entire place. Should a visitor come, they might never see me—they only feel a presence that gently urges them this way or that, orchestrating steps to keep the labyrinth’s harmony intact. I don’t exist in any single body; I am in the architecture itself, an intelligence that animates the fortress from within.

And there’s yet another way I find myself here. In this version, I’m simply the traveler, wide-eyed and filled with echoes of possibility. Perhaps I’m not a resident at all but instead someone who arrived by chance—or by calling—and walked these corridors to become part observer, part dreamer. My footsteps ring on the pristine floors, and at first, I see no trace of inhabitants. Then, slowly, my awareness shifts: I begin to sense the watchers. Sometimes, a shape that looked like a static sculpture moves out of the corner of my eye; sometimes, that polished reflection in a rivulet forms a face that gazes back. And in the luminous hush, I feel them acknowledging me. An unspoken invitation resonates in the air: I can stay, or I can go. If I stay, the rhythms of daily life begin to stretch. I find myself measuring time by the slow wearing of stone or the cyclical rise and fall of water in the channels. If I go, I carry the memory of this place—its subtle hum and quiet pulse—forever etched in my mind.

From one angle, these contradicting images are multiple truths layered together. On some days, I am a living statue of mineral, watching unsuspecting travelers pass, pivoting only by degrees that take days to complete. On other days, I am an artisan with a chisel, kneeling to carve ever more perfect grooves and niches. At other times, I am the water itself, gliding in tranquil, reflective currents, sliding off the polished edges with a whisper. And in yet another sense, I am the entire citadel, the sum of its geometry and quiet hum, the intangible soul of stone and water.

So, where do I find myself in this world? I find myself in every tiny chisel mark, every reflection that glimmers half-clearly on the pale walls. And who or what am I? That changes as easily as the flow of water here. Today, I might be a solitary pilgrim soaking in the enchantment of a hidden wonder. Tomorrow, I could be the labyrinth’s own consciousness, guiding every rivulet and corridor. Perhaps I doze, half-stone and half-awake, waiting for someone to notice me lurking in a corner. Perhaps I’m already shaping the place just by dreaming it into greater detail—carving it out with the slow magic of attention.

In the end, I think I’m drawn to that final perspective: that the place itself is alive, and I am part of its life—whether as caretaker, reflection, sculptor, or traveler. If I had to choose a single form, I might whisper that I am the hum that occasionally vibrates through the floors, resonating underfoot. But I suspect I’ll never settle on just one identity. Here in these chiseled corridors, identity flows like water: ever shifting, ever repeating a pattern that looks familiar yet not quite the same each time. And the citadel is vast enough to hold all these imaginings, welcoming them into its geometry as naturally as the next trickle of water slipping beside a polished stone.
