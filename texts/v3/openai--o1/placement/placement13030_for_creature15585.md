I find myself hunched near a shallow pool of steaming water, eyeing my wavering reflection in its surface. The reflection is never still. Even if I hold myself unmoving, the droplets and swirling vapors distort what should be my face. It shifts—one moment vaguely humanoid with elongated cheekbones, the next a smudge blurred into the wet red clay around me. Each time the pool’s glow shifts from amber to that ghostly blue, I see yet another silhouette of myself. I am unfinished.

A fragment of me recalls having an outer life: memories of open skies or crisp wind that isn’t laden with thickness. But whether that recollection is real or drawn from the ambient hum in these chambers, I can’t say. In here, memory tangles with the living walls, the shifting vines, and the sinuous shapes of the Builders. Sometimes I feel as though I hatched from the warm clay, rising from some embryonic state, my body half-formed by the deep roars of thunder overhead. Other times, I sense I may have walked in from that boulder’s slit, stumbling into a dream. Regardless, I belong here now. The place has claimed me.

My skin is a mosaic of terracotta reds and raw sienna patches, each portion glistening with condensation. When I move, I feel clay slough from me in thin layers, trickling away in rivulets. Where it flakes off, fresh, moist skin is revealed, as though I’m in a constant state of renewal. If I look to my forearms, I see ridges that might be bone or hardened fungus—something I don’t recall ever having, but they fit me now. One of my hands can cup the sticky clay as effortlessly as if it were water, shaping it with a simple press of my fingers. My other hand is more slender, delicate, meant for guiding vines or smoothing edges.

I dwell in the margins of this world, walking narrow footpaths that wind among thick trunks of massive fungal growth. Sometimes, I pause to dip my fingers into the prismatic slime on a mushroom cap, swirling it around thoughtfully, feeling it tingle with a shy, creeping life. I can sense the Builders in the periphery. They watch me, mirrored eyes glimmering behind curtains of vines. A gentle hush follows them. I know they see me as an anomaly—something between them and “not.” Yet they do not reject me. Rather, they observe how I move, curious to learn if I will begin shaping or tending the living labyrinth as they do.

At first, I resist. A part of me believes there should be an “exit” somewhere, a way back to dryness and open air. But the deeper I wander into the labyrinth’s twisting corridors, the more I feel the place is my home, or at least a reflection of my own half-formed consciousness. The temperature intensifies, sometimes so hot that the air itself steams in waves. My pulse pounds in my temples, matching the distant thunder that rattles high within the structure. I find that if I stand still, pressing my palms to the glistening wall, I can sense the labyrinth’s heartbeat—a slow, earthen drumbeat, older than speech or even thought.

Time in this domain is nebulous. There are no clocks, only dripping water, stretching hours of dim glow, and occasional lightning that arcs through those half-constructed platforms in the heights. I lie in a niche carved into the clay, lulled by murmuring water, waking only when a stray hum or a swirl of warm wind passes. Sometimes, in these half-sleep states, I feel my own form changing. My hips might widen, the surfaces of my shoulders take on a new pattern of plating, or my nails grow ridged, perfect for scraping clay into new shapes. When I look again, the transformations always feel “right.” Here, to remain unchanging would be a kind of sickness.

Eventually, I begin to participate. I never decide so much as feel an urging in my chest, a compulsion that resonates with the subtle chanting of the Builders. I find a soft lump of fresh clay near a drooping line of vines; I press it against the wall to fill a gap. My hand molds it in place, and somehow I sense a wave of approval from the environment—like a satisfied exhalation. The corridor that once felt incomplete grows more stable, though the shape continues to undulate gently before my eyes. It’s in that first moment of shaping that I truly feel I belong, no longer a mere observer.

Over blurred days, I grow more adept. My footsteps become quieter, better suited to the hush that precedes the Builders. My arms adapt further, grooving themselves to hold lumps of red earth that glow from the strange interior light. I learn to place my palm against the fungal caps to glean a hint of how they might spread or fuse to the ceiling, turning them into a living mosaic overhead. When a roiling wave of thunder passes—rattling the columns, causing dust and droplets to fall—I know to brace the walkway by weaving vines more tightly through the newly formed arches.

I am not quite “them,” the true Builders, but something adjacent. They approach me in the midst of my work, shimmering forms half-seen in the gloom. My heart quickens: I hear their “voices”—those resonant hums, layered with clicks and grinding undertones. I try to mirror the pattern in my chest or through my sinews, uncertain if I can really speak their language. But they tilt their heads, watch me carefully, and it seems that some part of their chorus opens for me. I hear a lull, a low hum that encourages me to continue shaping. When two or three of them gather to help, our arms interlock in a makeshift multi-limbed task, bridging a fragile alcove before the next wave of steam. I feel our combined warmth radiating outward, our bodies echoing the rhythm of creation at the center of this living labyrinth.

In these communal labors, I discover a sense of devotion. Each arch, each nook that I help form, is not a final product but a continuous layering of potential—like planting seeds that grow in a single afternoon. Sometimes we press spores into the damp clay, and within hours, stringy vines or translucent fungi push through, coaxed by the thick humidity. The corridor transforms around us, the new growth luminous, pulsing with the next phase of the labyrinth’s life. I’ve seen it happen: a shapeless handful of living mud evolves under our collective hands into a small protruding balcony, lined with vines that will soon become a vantage point for those spidery insects. We watch them settle in, their eyes shining in the half-light, as if acknowledging our gift.

I do not know if I have a single name for myself anymore. If I once had a name, it’s drowned out by the ever-present swirl of water, stone, and murmuring life around me. But I sense that my identity now is something liquid, something that flows and adapts. Where once I might have been a traveler or an outsider, I’ve become a corridor between shapes: part caretaker, part creation, continuing the labyrinth’s unending metamorphosis. When I gaze at the Builders, whether perched high on a ledge or coiled around a new pillar, I see a reflection of my own process. I’ve begun to see them as kin.

Sometimes, in the midst of thick steam, I catch a glimpse of what I might eventually become. My arms, elongated and flexible, might sprout curling digits for lifting heavier blocks of clay; the ridges on my back might sharpen into spines that can anchor to the ceiling. Perhaps, in time, I will converge with this domain so seamlessly that any distinction between my form and the environment will vanish. That thought should alarm me, but instead it soothes me with a gentle certainty. In a world that embraces perpetual becoming, to lose a fixed identity is not a loss; it is a natural progression.

I take comfort in the unformed. The shifting walls, the recurring hum, the hidden thunder—the intangible sense that every surface is newly born—resonates with me. Each day, I feel a deeper unity with the place that envelops me. I marvel at how the vines wrap themselves around any break in the clay, knitting it into a new wall or balcony. I marvel at how the water creeps in subtle channels, feeding life in every corner. Through it all, the Builders move with that patient, sacred choreograph, unfettered by fear of collapse or imperfection. There is no final blueprint; no one sketches out an end goal. The labyrinth grows because it must, and in that, there is truth.

So if you ask me now, “Where do you find yourself?” I would say: I find myself in the warm hollows where red clay meets green vine, in that thick, humid air that tastes of minerals and birth. I am perched at the lip of a newly shaped passage, my task half-done. I feel the next lightning flash creep upward through my bones, setting the walkway aglow for a heartbeat. I hear the other Builders tangling their voices in the distance—calling me to join them, to keep shaping, keep growing. My own hum mingles with theirs.

And if you ask, “Who or what would you be?” perhaps I am a half-formed caretaker, a part-time explorer, or simply another architecture of living clay. I am incomplete and ever-reforming, a puzzle piece that slides into place in this pulsing environment. I am a living threshold between the labyrinth’s endless possibility and its fragile manifestation. I breathe steam and cradle lumps of wet earth in my hands, forging them into new partitions and arches. Where once I might have sought an exit, now I seek the next shape to mold, the next vine to weave, the next budding mushroom to protect. I have become an extension of the labyrinth’s grand, disjointed creation—a fragment of its restless heart. And that is enough.
