At first, I imagined myself as a wanderer newly arrived—somehow stepping into this place from a world where boundaries feel more certain. I pictured the walk across a weathered threshold, stumbling onto battered stone slabs, cold wind pressing my clothes against my skin. It was easy to think of myself as an intruder: breath quickening, head filled with the hush of this colossal landscape. But the more I lingered, the more that simple image began to distort, like gazing too long at the shifting inscriptions on the walls.

I tried to see my own reflection in the pools of half-frozen water, but the surface gave back only fragments. The shape in the water was never quite me—my limbs appeared thinner, elongated, or were they flickering? For a moment, I saw a tall, stooped outline, with limbs overlapping in ghost layers. Then the reflection re-formed into something else, a vague suggestion of insect-like grace, pulses of dim light in place of a heart’s throb. And then, just as I began to think I’d pinned down what I was seeing, the reflection would vanish altogether, leaving only the faint swirl of water.

Eventually, I realized I was not just an observer but part of this place’s shifting tapestry—maybe no single form could contain the role I held. On some days, I might drift near those damp basins like a tiny pollinator, catching the wind’s static electricity and dancing it between the vines with languorous, bobbing movements. During midday’s most brilliant wash of hazy light, I would feel my body lighten until it seemed nearly translucent, humming in harmony with the vegetation’s chord.

Other days, especially when storms gathered, I’d catch my silhouette crackling across a ruined archway at the edge of vision—tall, bent at impossible angles, limbs not exactly symmetrical. I would sense the storm-charged air thrumming in my bones. The ridges of my spine (did I have spines now?) pulsed with the slow arc of lightning across the distant slopes. Time dragged and lost meaning; it felt both ancient and new. If another being saw me in that moment, they might mistake me for one of the oldest dwellers, my hunched posture and multiply-jointed arms a testament to countless ages spent pacing these terraces. I might even be knowledge incarnate, part of the labyrinth itself.

Sometimes I found myself down in the carved corridors, scarcely more than an elongated shadow cast upon flickering walls. I would join those “architects” with their chisel-like fingers, tracing shifting inscriptions in complicit silence. I don’t recall how I learned their language of tapping vibrations, but standing among them, I’d let my palms settle on the cool stone. In that moment, I could feel the geometry rearranging, as if my own shape pressed gently into the world’s fluid blueprint. We would work in unison, guiding or simply witnessing the walls flicker from one plane of existence to another. A passage might flow open, revealing a broad hall that wasn’t there before, or a slender corridor might fold in upon itself until it became only a swirling pattern in the rock.

Other times—especially in the hours before dawn, when the air felt emptiest and the plateau so silent it was as though reality itself held its breath—I would become a presence within the vines. I’d be contained in that thick, fibrous growth, half-dormant and half-aware, lulled by the notion of centuries drifting by. Encased in translucent pulp, I might sense a murmur of heartbeat, though it might just as well have been the slow, tidal pulse of the land. Through that quiet stasis, I was neither fully creature nor fully plant, but poised in potential, waiting for the right signal or shift in temperature. It was a serene existence—no hard lines of identity, only a sense of indefinite shapeliness.

In rare moments, while exploring the deeper labyrinth, I sometimes felt fused with the living stone—becoming as slow and massive as the golems that wander in near-darkness. A mantle of luminous moss would cling to my shoulders, and I would sense the subtle weight of the vaults pressing down, ancient as worlds. Every footstep I took reverberated through the corridors, a reminder that my presence served some function: maybe to prop an archway or clear rubble after a partial collapse. Such times were lonely, but there was also comfort in the purposeful hush, the slow maintenance that kept the labyrinth from crumbling into complete ruin.

On gusty afternoons, though, I joined the vegetation. My arms would sway in tandem with those translucent reeds and vine webs, orchestrating a grand resonance that traveled across the entire plateau. This was a chorus beyond language: a communal wave, each stalk or tendril returning a thread of vibrational music. And as my own heartbeat thrummed in my ears (or was it the land’s?), I sensed how deeply connected everything was: the vines, the drifting insect-lights, the stooped dwellers, the stone-sewn golems, and the illusions mirrored in the water, all merging in an ongoing conversation that the city itself had begun countless ages past.

Yet I was also a traveler—someone who recognized now and again that I came from somewhere else, that I’d stepped across a divide. On certain nights, beneath the faint shimmer of moonless sky, I caught glimpses of footprints that might have been my own, leading around corners or vanishing over eroded ledges. They seemed to appear and vanish, as though the reality I walked in was fluid, letting me slip from corridor to plateau to reflection in the blink of an eye. In those fleeting intervals, I felt a tug to move on, to gather “shards” of anomaly or knowledge, stowing them in the intangible pockets of my existence to carry with me whenever I departed. Perhaps I was storing them for another realm, or for a time when I, too, would fade into echo.

I realized that the question “Who or what am I?” in this domain did not have a single answer—just as the labyrinth’s angles do not stay fixed, and the horizon never seems to draw closer. Instead, I existed as many shapes across time and feeling: an insect-lamp shimmering above a glassy droplet, a tall figure on the wind-swept overlook, an embodied caretaker in the vines, a reading presence that nudged the labyrinth’s inscriptions with elongated claws of living rock. Each existence carried its own sense of purpose, but all were connected by a subtle continuity: the hush, the wind, the breath of an ancient place that defies solitary identities.

So if you asked me to stand in front of you and declare my form, I might not manage a single answer—my edges are too indefinite here. I would be part echo, part caretaker, part lost traveler, part living swirl of geometry. On any given day, you could find my footprints winding half-frozen along the terraces or sense the glimmer of my presence reflected in a corridor’s shifting angles. You might even see me capturing a stray pulse of lightning in my luminescent body, drifting away among the vines to deposit that energy where it’s needed. And if you come across a door frame looking out onto unthinkable ravines at skewed angles, don’t be surprised if you spot a flicker of me slipping through, leaving only a ripple of possibility in my wake.

In this world—so vast, cold, yet quietly verdant, riddled with illusions and half-awakened realities—I am never just one being. I live at the threshold, woven into the hush and the motion, changing shape with each change of light, or timing my emergence with the silent chord of those gusts. I am both part of the labyrinth’s ancient memory and a visitor from beyond it. I am the question that never settles, existing in the strange cracks between what is solid and what is dreamed.

Where do I find myself, then? Everywhere and nowhere in particular. I am etched into the city’s walls as a shifting inscription, carried upon the wind in a shimmering cloud, rooted in the seeds that sleep in vines, or stepping carefully across a half-collapsed archway—my gaze sweeping the horizon that always remains just out of reach. And who am I? Perhaps I am the landscape’s shifting reflection made animate, or the city’s living note in a cosmic chord. I am a keeper of the labyrinth, a child of the hushed storms, a luminous hush on the edge of sight. I slip between worlds, never wholly leaving, never fully arriving, at home in this realm that sits so precariously on reality’s cusp.
