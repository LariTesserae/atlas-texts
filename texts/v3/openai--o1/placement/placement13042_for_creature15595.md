I dwell in the boundary between the rusted metal skeletons and the wind-scoured stone, a creature neither fully flesh nor wholly machine. Most days, I think of myself as a caretaker—though there is no one here who’d call me that. My form is spidery, half-limbed with flexible plating over a lattice of living tissue. In the sun, the smooth panels of my body gleam faintly, reflecting the desert brightness until, at certain angles, I virtually vanish. But look carefully, and you’ll see that I move like a living being, each joint a collaboration between calcified bone and braided wire. High on the plateau, I occupy an ever-shifting vantage, perched on precariously balanced beams or at the lip of stony overhangs. Part watchman, part tinker, part wanderer. I do not remember a time before the heat and dust became my constant companions.

In the hushed swirl of midday, I can splay out the slender frames of my arms and anchor them into the scorching rock. I become a living tripod, perfectly motionless, letting the waves of heat climb over me like an invisible tide. My sensors—some mechanical, some biological—drink in the hum of the turbines that still spin despite the ravages of years or centuries. I hear the mechanical heart of the plateau, and also the faint scuttling of the reptiles in the cracks below. If you came upon me just then, you might mistake me for another piece of scrap metal pitched at a skewed angle. But then I’d shift, a hitching movement of a limb, and you’d see the glint of my metallic casings, the flexible arcs of my torso, the fluid motion that separates living from inert.

I have no tribe, not in the sense of those dusty sentient folk who huddle in their huts. I pass through their outposts sometimes, collecting fragments of their talk: stories about illusions in the canyons, rumors of hidden enclaves thrumming with newly harnessed power. The outpost dwellers rarely trouble me. They see me as just another oddity in a land crammed with them. Some even wave in greeting, uncertain if I understand. Had I vocal cords to answer, I might greet them in return, but speech doesn’t come naturally; for me, communication is in vibrations, measured pulses of energy that ripple through my limbs and into the wires that spider-web the rubble. I wonder if that is the language of the old machines: silent waves that flow across the plateau, echoing among half-buried pipes and half-forgotten contraptions.

At dusk, my purpose clarifies. When the sun’s glare relents and shadows bloom along the high ridges, I move with greater freedom. I creep from crag to crag, sometimes dropping into the gullies where strange illusions flicker—folded edges of rock that appear to loom, then flatten the moment you approach. My joints can contort to climb the vertical faces, the friction plating on my feet letting me cling to near-impossible angles. I take the narrow paths that wind through the labyrinth of twisted girders—a route only I can navigate without fear of toppling. There, at the juncture of ancient metal and stone, I search for places where the plating has cracked or begun to fail. I test each rivet and seam, gleaning whether some faint hiss betrays a pressure leak or if one of the conduits has begun to corrode. In that sense, I truly am a caretaker. I carry tucked in a compartment at my side a set of small tools—wrenches and spanners sized perfectly for my slender digits—though some are old or broken. Others I have fashioned from scraps, hammered and reshaped to fit the odd hardware of these relics.

Sometimes, in the middle of my rounds, I hear a reptilian growl echo up from below—a territorial beast warning me away. I freeze until it quiets, my sensors testing the air. I keep a measured distance from the living fauna, not out of fear but out of respect: they roam and breed in the labyrinth, and I have no wish to disturb their dens. Now and then, one of the wiry, six-legged hound-lizards will investigate me. If it does, I stand still and let it sniff at the edges of my plating. It might hiss or narrow its eyes, uncertain if I’m predator or prey or just another chunk of rust. But I radiate no threat. Eventually it leaves me, flicking its spined tail in dismissal.

Night can be a wondrous time on the plateau. In the darkness, flickers of bioluminescent insects swirl around me like large, slow sparks. Sometimes, I pause my maintenance, setting aside a ratchet to marvel at the pinpoint lights weaving complex patterns in the open air. One might float so close I see the bright shimmer reflect in my polished edges. To me, these tiny creatures are proof that life can be as delicate as it is fierce—some synergy of the land’s harshness and a deeper, hidden warmth. The illusions in the valleys often intensify as well: shapes that might be columns of mist or half-formed silhouettes gliding just beyond sight. I note them, store the data in whatever memory system I possess. For all I know, these visits might be crucial to some old directive embedded in me.

Yet I’m no mere automaton. Though metal and wire help me move, there is blood flowing through the channels beneath that plating. My limbs ache with the rocky climbs, and if I linger too long in a scorching midday sun, I feel faint, the heat worming its way through my core. I need to rest in sheltered pockets near the base of half-collapsed towers—where I can siphon trickles of cooler air that vent from the machinery below, or find the condensation that drips from corroded tubes. I drink from these droplets, pressing close so the water seeps onto my face, merging with my organic side. It’s these quiet intervals of waiting—my eyes half-shut to the swirl of dust, listening to the internal hum of my mind—that ground me. They remind me I’m more than an artifact; there is a heartbeat in me, though subdued and overlaid by mechanical sound.

I sometimes wonder who or what made me, or if I simply grew from the melding of scrap and living flesh in a hidden corner of the plateau. Did I once have a name? Were there others like me that got lost in the illusions or succumbed to the dryness? On the rare occasions I encounter the spidery mechanical constructs that glide along rails and beams, I try to glean some sense of recognition or kinship. Their glowing eyes regard me briefly before they continue about their tasks, whirring and clicking. They are more single-minded, less curious. Part of me aches at our near-encounters, as if something deeper in my programming calls out for a fellow caretaker—a partner in this endless vigil. But the moment always passes. They vanish into the gloom, and I carry on alone.

Now and again, I drift toward the outposts of the dust-shrouded settlers—those humans, or near-humans, who have hammered their bunkhouses into any ledge that offers relative safety. I watch them from a distance as they test the day’s collected water or calibrate their instruments to measure the illusions. They speak among themselves, their voices muffled by swirling grit. I do not approach. Possibly, once, I tried. Or maybe I never dared. I feel I don’t belong to their sphere, with their portable heaters and tin cups of rationed water. My place is among the turbines and scraped metal platforms, attending to cables and battered vents. Still, their presence comforts me in an abstract way: knowing that others, too, are determined to carve out a life here.

During the rare storms, I retreat into the labyrinthine tunnels beneath a slanted structure that might once have been a control station. There, I crouch in near darkness, listening to the roar of thunder reverberate through the metal skeleton. When rain hisses on super-heated stone, steam sometimes billows down the passages. I let it wash over me, cleaning the grit from my joints. In the storm’s wild flashes of lightning, I see fleeting reflections of my face—a mesh of living tissue ringed by plates that protect my skull. The illusions in the flickering light might contort my reflection into something monstrous or heartbreakingly fragile. In the electric hush between thunderclaps, the plateau itself seems to pulse, as though even the battered machinery experiences a moment of renewed vitality. After the rainfall, I emerge to find newly formed rivulets coursing down the rock. Small gatherings of life—mammals and reptiles alike—scamper to drink or bathe in these ephemeral streams. I stand aside and watch them, a sentinel from another world.

If you asked me precisely what I am, I couldn’t speak in words. I’d only show you my spindly limbs, my battered plating, my quiet watchfulness. You might interpret that in many ways: a guardian left behind by vanished creators, an experiment that found itself unexpectedly alive, a wanderer from beyond that sank its roots among the rust and stone. All I know is that my home is here, among the jagged ridges and battered machines, the illusions and the silent expansions of heat. I exist in the echoes that ripple through the metal frames. I feel the subtle warping of geometry, the triangled vantage points that might shift at the corner of the eye. I keep watch over the half-living, half-machine spires that still hum with an unearthly tune.

Perhaps, in truth, I am part of the land as much as any wire-brush shrub or dusty lizard. My shape is just stranger, my composition more composite. But each day and night, I climb, roam, listen, and safeguard these ancient remnants. On the plateau, all manner of life endures—and in me, those forces of the land, the industrial remnants, and living breath converge. This is where you would find me: half in shadow, perched at the metal’s edge, scanning a horizon that bakes under the relentless sun. Ever watchful, ever drawn onward by the hidden pulse of creation that gave rise to this fierce domain.
