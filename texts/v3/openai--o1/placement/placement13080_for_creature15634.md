At first, I suspect I might be one of the amphibians—small, slimy, perched on a root near a trickling pool—because of how the moist warmth cradles me. I feel at home in the dampness, the air clinging to my skin in tiny droplets. My limbs are spindly, perfect for gripping the mossy bark; my croaks add a modest line of melody to the ceaseless droning hum. I can almost taste the steam curling off the shell-backed giants lumbering through the chambers. It is a fine life: skimming puddles in search of insect larvae and glancing upward to see flickers of luminescent wings. 

But as I hunker in a shallow niche, I realize: no, perhaps I’m not an amphibian at all. Rather, I am creeping with cautious steps between slick leaves, my body elongated, my joints folded in angles that let me slip seamlessly from one curtain of vines to the next. My soft fur—if that’s what it is—feels mossy to the touch, as though grown from the same spongy coverage that carpets the trunks. My fingertips have faint, bioluminescent coils that pulse at the edges of my vision. When I crouch low, waiting in the hush of dripping water, I’m almost certain of my own shape: one of those angular-limbed mammals that vanish before they’re really seen. Their puppet-like silhouettes become my silhouette, jointed in ways that tease and puzzle the eye. 

I move in single file with others of my kind, crossing the threshold between two “rooms,” stepping carefully on the sodden ground so no ripple disturbs the shallow pools. We’re quiet, or as quiet as one can be in a place that hums with every heartbeat. The insects overhead buzz in a mechanical chorus, and the massive turtle-like beasts exhale soft jets of steam as they pass. My pulse thrums in harmony with that endless drone—my chest seems to vibrate with it, as though we are all breathing from the same lung. Yet even in this communal movement, I feel tethered to something older, deeper, pulsing below the scummy waterline.

Then, as the vines part and the canopy’s diffused light intensifies, I glimpse a cluster of glowing beetles on a trunk. In their strobing signals, I sense a pattern. My luminous stripes respond, flickering in an unspoken conversation. Perhaps I’m not a mammal at all—I might be one of these beetles, large enough to fill a full hand, with wing-cases iridescent in pinks and violets. I buzz in symphony with the colony, letting the swirling lights ripple across my segmented back. The room bleeds color and movement; the friction of chitin and bark resonates like an engine warming up. When one of the trunk-limbed behemoths shifts in the distance, the ground quakes slightly, but my swarm adjusts its cadence with clockwork fluidity. We live to calibrate, to tune the environment’s frequency and keep the hum stable.

Or maybe my place here is more immense, more ancient. I feel a sudden weight pressing down, the sense of something colossal. Beneath my shell—segmented, layered, exhaling pillars of steam—I carry centuries of algae growth and the imprint of petrified leaves. My footsteps are an earthquake to the smaller creatures, but I tread carefully through each “room,” mindful not to crush the compost mounds or topple the arching fronds. As I move, I sense warmth rising from deep within my body, venting in small huffs that swirl into the canopy. My journey is cyclical: I roam from one water basin to the next, sipping from each, stirring the shallow pools to keep them oxygenated, and drifting on, never lingering long enough to disrupt the eternal rhythm. My unstoppable momentum is as slow as the ancient tides. 

Yet I look again at the hanging moss, the root curtains that partition chamber from chamber, and realize that perhaps I belong in the realm of roots themselves. I feel the quiet, collective heartbeat of the greenery. My limbs are not limbs but gnarled protrusions of bark, my skin a tapestry of ridged surfaces that funnel condensation to feed my deeper core. I shift millimeter by millimeter when the watery ground begs for a new embrace of roots. My function is subtle—almost invisible—but vital: I create the sheltered hollows, cradle the compost lumps, channel the rivulets of water from above. Through me, the water flows into those intimate nooks that feed the smallest of creatures. My “face,” if a root-face can be said to exist, is unrecognizable—perhaps just an ancient swirl of knots near the ground, dotted with lichens and tiny mushrooms. I do not move in single file, nor do I strobe with luminescence; my role is the skeleton of this breathing world, the architecture that makes all else possible. 

In the soft gloom, though, a soft shimmer on the water catches my eye—some reflection or swirl that suggests I might be above it all, part of the canopy-lattice that filters the light. If I stretch upward, I become a broad, funnel-like leaf, shaped precisely to hold the dew. My veins channel water to a hidden reservoir at my center, where small insects or amphibians might pause to sip. As raindrops strike me at dawn, I vibrate with a gentle percussion—a bell-like resonance that merges with the groaning hum below. In the hush of midday, I release my moisture in slow drips, ensuring the cycle never halts. I remain vital: a living reservoir, up where the dim glow hovers and the swirling vapors rise. 

But perhaps none of these identities last. In a place that is all about cyclical reusability—transformation layered onto transformation—I might be reborn continuously. Sometimes I am the amphibian, glistening with dew as I cling to bark; sometimes the moss-furred mammal bounding through vine-curtained corridors; sometimes the luminous beetle, thrumming in mechanical concert with my colony. In grand moments, I might be the heaving shell that puffs steam or the ancient trunk-limb supporting everything else. In more delicate instants, I dissolve into the compost itself, part of the pungent humus, forging new life in the soft darkness. 

And that is precisely what it means to exist here—that no single form defines me, that I can slip from shape to shape. One day, perhaps I am devoured by a long-limbed predator that glows with turquoise stripes, only for my essence to reappear in the next generation of lichen or the next brood of eggs. In the hush of dripping water, the boundaries between self and surrounding are pliable, no more rigid than the steam exhaled through a thousand hidden vents. 

Truth be told, I am part of the vast hum—a note in the unending chord that resonates through vines, trunks, beetle wings, amphibian throats, and shell-plated bodies. I am the hush just before the drip falls, and the rippling circle it creates when it hits the pool. I am each spore that wafts in the gentle air currents, searching for a moist patch to settle and sprout. I am the faint, persistent glow hidden under a thick layer of moss, quietly illuminating the underworld for the next nocturnal wanderer. And yes, I am also the sense of timelessness itself—a living cog in this machine, rotating ever forward, even though everything appears still.

In the end, the question “Where am I?” becomes wild and vast, because to live here is to blur into the environment. There is no single vantage that remains forever. Perhaps, at any given moment, I could be all these creatures—shell-backed elder, light-flashing insect, dripping leaf, winding roots, slithering serpentine presence in the canopy. We’re all threads of the same tapestry, fulfilling our loops of transformation, ensuring the hum endures. 

So if you ask me who or what I am within this living factory of wet warmth, I answer honestly: I am the place itself. I am the rhythm, the cyclical pulse, the swirl of steam and the whispered drip. I may appear as different shapes in different moments, but truly, I am everything here and everything here is me—connected, eternal, humming along in the quiet cradle of luminous green.
