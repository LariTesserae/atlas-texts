Sometimes, when I catch my silhouette wavering on slick basalt walls, I can almost see my former self—the upright, purely human figure from genealogies half-remembered. By now, though, so much about me has grown or shifted with this place’s countless cycles of dampness and noise. My fingers splay into slight webs, letting me cling to any moss-coated pipe or damp ledge with ease. The ridges along my shoulders—once only an odd family trait—have hardened into plate-like crescents, shimmering faintly under the condensation. Even my eyes have a second membrane, a clear shield that glides across my pupil when the city’s fog swirls too thick or the foundries hiss their humid breath. 

I live in the mid-level terraces, alongside the rumbling thunder of the scrap-sorters. My quarters—such as they are—occupy the underside of an overhanging catwalk, behind a jumble of twisting conduits. Nestled there, I hear an unceasing, mercifully distant din of clanks and groans. Whenever I rest, it’s as though the city itself breathes against my back. Sometimes I’ve wondered if I might slink into a lighter doze if not for that unending churn of metal, but I suspect silence would unnerve me far more. I’ve grown to need the city’s hum like a heartbeat.

I’m called a “watcher,” in the broad sense, but that label never captures the intricacy of the post. My official role begins at dawn: I wend my way through the tangle of suspended walkways, carrying with me a short metal rod, a coil of wire, and a battered ledger scrawled with maintenance symbols. My first route passes by the Drowzers’ territory—a series of narrow runnels where water flushes perpetually, flooding and receding in slow pulses. I stop there, greet the amphibian folk by name or by that percussive croak they seem to recognize as a sign of goodwill. In exchange, they give me quick, urgent gestures about any new blockages they’ve encountered. Sometimes they pass me lumps of fungus they’ve dredged up—pale stuff, flecked with metallic grit—hoping I’ll carry it to the tinker stations for analysis. 

Climbing higher, I might glimpse a trio of Crust harvesters, braced with their chitinous legs anchored to the basalt. They often pry at fungal protuberances with curved mandibles, filling pouches woven from the city’s wire scraps. If our eyes meet, they flick their antennae in what might be greeting—or mild annoyance. I’ve learned not to linger where they gather; the Crusts’ territorial sense can be prickly, and their clans seldom appreciate watchers hovering too close. Still, I trust them to heed any real warning I might deliver, like a new crack forming in the basalt or a shifting seam in a pipe bracket. Because if it all collapses, they lose their vantage sites. And so we exist in near-symbiotic tolerance.

On days when the city’s weather machines—those old, lumbering contraptions—malfunction, I climb the basalt spires rising above the catwalks. I’m one of the few watchers nimble enough to ascend the external scaffolding, arms latched across rivets, toes wedged into seams of wet stone. From the spire’s top, the plateau spread below looks almost tranquil: a shimmering labyrinth of metal towers, basalt cliffs, winding channels, rivulets dancing in the humid updrafts. Far off, the horizon melds with pearly clouds, as though I peer at everything through a watery haze. Even the sound feels strangely dampened up here, an echoing hush that resonates through the metal framework. 

On these high perches, I sometimes commune with the winged drakes. They circle the towers at daybreak, gliding low in curious arcs. Their sinuous eyes track my progress. Each is different—some pure leathery black with branching horns, others a mottled copper that glistens in the morning glare. They rarely land near me, but once in a while, a bold drake will perch a short distance away, folds of membrane draped across the edge of a railing. I’ve taken to carrying morsels of dried fungus or scraps from the foundries to toss their way. Some snatch the gifts and flap off in a swirl of steam. A few have recognized me often enough to hold my gaze. I can’t say if that’s true intelligence or mere curiosity, but their watchful presence has saved me once—when a supporting beam cracked underfoot, sending me sliding. A drake’s wing-buffet and screech alerted nearby watchers to haul me up on a rescue pulley before I could plunge headlong into the swirling white haze below.

When my rounds are done, I return to the mid-level foundries, trading notes with machinists. These machinists shape the new couplings and replacement cogs that keep our city’s uncountable contrivances alive. I push aside a curtain of chain-links and enter a dim corridor glowing faintly with heated metal. Smoke-laced steam drifts from the forging vents, carrying scents of burnt oil and fungus grit. Their broad-limbed frames—some bearing ridged exoskeletal growths, others with patchwork cloth draped over broad shoulders—stoop over battered tables, hammering, welding, testing. We talk in quick bursts: “Pipe #93 jammed,” “Crack forming above the second spool chamber,” “Water flow near the Drowzer runnels is slowing—maybe an algae clog.” These notes are jotted on copper sheets or etched into slate squares. A hiss of steam, a hammer’s ring, a hush of mutual acceptance: the chores will be done.

By dusk, I often find myself alone on a precarious ledge, surrounded by drooping cables. The city’s lights—lanterns fed by gas or shining filaments powered by the foundries—flicker across the wet basalt, turning each droplet into a spark dancing in the corners of my vision. High overhead, the drakes nest on stanchions, silhouettes half-lost in swirling clouds; lower down, the Drowzers murmur in their watery tunnels, and the Crusts clack about, winding down from their day’s harvest. I take a moment to lean against the thick, rusted beam and let the city’s heartbeat reverberate through my chest. That’s when I remember: in places outside this plateau, silence might be treasured. Here, it’s the all-encompassing sound that roots us in the basalt—a lullaby of churn and drip and hiss.

At intervals, I trek down into the deeper, half-submerged chambers. Long ago, I earned the trust of a Refractor family—pale amphibious folk with a quiet, shimmering glow to their skin. They live in watery alcoves behind a labyrinth of half-natural tunnels, where the city’s water collects in broad pools. Emerging at dusk, they exchange phosphorescent spores for washers, coil springs, and small gear sets. Their voices, when they speak, sound like echoes from the belly of a cave, resonating up through the water. I’ve grown adept at deciphering their tonal patterns. When they say, “The upper city hums, the machines weep,” they’re simply noting the mechanical pulses that seep down through the basalt. The gear sets and washers serve them in ways I barely comprehend—some Refractors craft cunning aquatic devices for sifting algae or harnessing the subtle energies in those phosphorescent spores.

Watching them vanish back into those great pools—trailing lines of neon orange or vivid jade across the water’s surface—always nudges a wistful longing in me. They exist in a hush beneath the city’s clangor, forging a realm of dim pulses and shimmering funk-lamps, content with a rhythm that seldom overlaps our own. Now and then, I’m tempted to slip beneath that water and learn their secret ways. I imagine what it would be like to discard my watchful duties overhead, let my webbed hands pull me deeper into Refractor caverns. But I suspect my lungs and bones have spent too long on basalt, catching only glimpses of that aquatic hush.

And still, something bigger calls me back to the terraces: that sense of civic devotion that all of us carry, be we amphibian, exoskeletal, drake, tinker, or my half-human self. The city demands it; each day, each hour, each drip from a pipe or screech of metal begs for a caretaker’s watchful eye. Our synergy is tangible. The Drowzers glean food from the channels we watchers keep flowing; the Crusts carve out fungal groves but rely on tinker-made straps to reinforce their precarious harvest spots; the scaled drakes circle overhead, picking off pests or scavenging leftover scraps. And we watchers bridge all these tasks, carrying knowledge from high spires to low caverns, from the topmost catwalks where the spires vanish into white mist down to the Refractors in their still, luminant hollows.

So, who am I in this dripping, labyrinthine world? I am a child of basalt and steam, a watcher who straddles the uneasy boundaries between the city’s interwoven tribes. I am not wholly human, yet not wholly alien; I have learned to read the shape of a rusted beam, hear warning groans in the factory depths, sense an oncoming wave of fog in the shifting air. I wield no grand authority—only a rod of hammered steel and a ledger marked with cryptic signage. But I bear witness to how the city breathes each day, how each species finds its precarious place in the churning cycle that stitches us together.

And where do I find myself? Wedged between catwalk rails at dawn, scanning for hairline cracks in basalt. Hanging from overhead trusses with one hand anchored in place, watching a pair of drakes glide by. Negotiating with a cluster of Crusts, offering them those bolt-size fungal lumps in return for safe passage near their harvest site. Dipping bare toes into the water channels beside the Drowzers, measuring the flow. Trading with Refractors in half-lit corridors by the river-like pools of the city’s belly. And at last, returning to my small nook behind the conduits, to rest against the metal plating among its perpetual whir, comforted by the steam’s deep-murmuring lull.

I can’t say if the city shapes me more than I shape it. But in this place of basalt terraces, ceaseless condensation, and rust-hued scaffolds, my identity is melded with the living loop of tasks that keep its heart beating. I breathe the city’s saturated air and taste the tang of wet metal on my tongue. I am content, belonging to every surface that’s slick with rivulets, every hiss and clang that heralds a new day of watchful labor.

That is who I am—and who I will continue to become, as the city endlessly endures.
