I have no memory of who first brought me here, or if I arrived by choice, but I know I was formed by the gloom itself—shaped slowly by the dripping vines and the whisper of rust. You could probably call me a “caretaker,” though I don’t serve just one species or machine; I keep watch over many corners of the plateau, traversing along the corroded catwalks and buttresses in stretched hours that feel like centuries. My shape is partly organic, partly mechanical, with a core of twisted vines that brace an exoskeleton of scavenged steel. My limbs are lean but resilient, scaffolding rods that let me clamber up vertical faces, crouch in half-collapsed tunnels, and slip through the spaces others can’t. My eyes go wide in the half-light, each outfitted with insectile lenses that glean phosphorescent traces and faint sparks. Somewhere in my chest, I hear the echoes of rotating turbines humming against my ribcage.

Long ago, I think I resembled a human; maybe I was one, once. But I’ve grafted so many mechanical parts and grown so many vines around me that I only faintly recall the softness of flesh. My shoulders are padded with fungal pads that help me sense temperature shifts, and my forearms have bands of flexible piping that channel the perpetual humidity. Whenever I need to slip between narrow columns or across a precarious pipe, I tense these tubes, and a rush of condensed water gives me extra hydraulic strength. At rest, condensation runs down my plating like perspiration, feeding the spore-laden moss that clings to me. My presence in the darkness is neither entirely living nor fully artificial, but the Plateau doesn’t care about such definitions. It only cares that I endure.

I watch over a particular stretch of ancient catwalks where the plates have warped and bent so severely they ripple like the spine of a resting beast. My job—such as it is—is to keep the precarious metal from collapsing completely. On certain nights (though “night” here is just a deepening of the gloom), I push rusted bolts back into place or patch a fractured walkway with a sheet of metal I’ve pried off a defunct generator. I tend to the vines that dangle precariously overhead, detangling them from the crackling wires that still carry unpredictable charges. I scoop away stagnant water to keep the catwalk from overflowing and snapping under savage weight. The water never really goes away—it’s always replaced by fresh rivulets from the spires above—but I try to direct it, creating small channels that fall into hidden cisterns below.

People—if you can call them that—pass through on occasion. Survivors or scavengers, hoping to move from one tower to the next, searching for salvage. They expect this bridge to hold, and it does, in large part because I wander these panels, tacking them back together with rivets of re-forged scrap. Most never notice me. In the faint industrial glare, I’m just another tangle of vines and metal huddled in the corner. If one does spot me, they sometimes leave an offering: half a fungus loaf, or a chunk of ruddy ore that might be hammered into something useful. They rarely try to speak. In the Plateau there’s not much to say, especially when there’s thunder booming overhead and scuttling shapes in the gloom.

Sometimes, I climb above my domain—scaling the spire that anchors this catwalk. Up there, battered scaffolding extends out into the murk, and thick conduits swirl with steam. I take readings in my own makeshift way—feeling the heat or the hum with the sensitive edges of my vines—and I check the swirling storm clouds for any sign of the rare break in the darkness. I never find one, of course. Still, I look. The air tastes of iron, and I can smell the tang of ozone from the arcs that sputter and glow. Occasionally I see the winged fliers pass overhead, silhouettes of leather wings and curved talons, drawn to the arcs’ faint radiance. They don’t harass me; I’m not worth the trouble. It’s the smaller creatures that I help keep in check: the nibbling, grinding, chittering ones. I pursue them quietly if they infest a structural joint, scaring them off before they chew a crucial bracket through. Balance must be maintained.

My personal shelter clings to the plateau’s side—slabs of broken metal welded together into a snug cove beneath a giant pipeline. It’s a safe nook from the downpours, and it’s warmed by the exhaust vent that coughs steam into the air. The steam forms droplets along my plating all night, feeding the spore-lamps that glow in a ring around my makeshift doorway. Deeper inside, I keep a trove of the odd relics I’ve salvaged: circuit boards glistening with electrolytic residue, cables and tubes that might be repaired one day, shards of glass from an old control panel that still shine faintly in certain angles of the catwalk lights. Once, to amuse myself, I arranged these shards to reflect the meager glow of a distant hazard lamp, creating a mosaic of scattered luminescence across the gloom.

I’m not a ruler, nor do I wish to be. Sometimes I think I’m more like a living gear in this machine-ecosystem—one of countless beings ensuring the plateau’s processes continue. By mending those catwalks, I help keep channels open for travelers, letting them spread seeds or parts of mushrooms from one tower to another. The vines that twine through me spit out small blossoms from time to time, spore pods which drift off to colonize new metal surfaces, bridging the gap between biology and industry. In turn, I scavenge what I need to keep my own systems functioning, whether that’s a scrap of steel for a torn joint or the nutrient-rich moss that grows in hidden pockets along the spires. It’s a gentle reciprocity with an environment that can be brutal, but never malicious.

When a real storm rolls in—one that rattles the entire plateau with thunder and makes the industrial lamps flicker—I press myself flat to the walkway. My vines tense, gripping the edges of broken rails, while my plating shakes from the concussion. In those moments, a curious serenity washes through me. I feel the power of the place, the unstoppable churn of water and wind, the mechanical groans of the turbines shuddering under the onslaught. Everything vibrates in unison, from the smallest insects to the bulkiest automaton to the living rock itself. It is terrifyingly alive.

I like to think that long after this epoch of half-ruined factories and lonely bunkers passes—if it ever does—I will still be here. Or my descendants will. The vines that make up my body will seed new hybrids in the cracks, the metal that covers my arms will corrode back into the landscape, part of a million other plates that patch up the spires. Perhaps I’ll be unrecognizable then: a swirl of metal ribs and fibrous vegetation, a sculpture pinned to the rock by time and rust. But until that day, I keep walking, bridging, soldering, guiding. I never see the stars, but I hear the thunder. I never feel the sun, but I feel the warm breath of turbines. I taste the iron in the air and watch the fungus glow faintly. I bend the catwalk’s battered edges back into rough alignment, catch the rivulets of water when the floods come. I tend the small patch of lightless world entrusted to me.

Who am I in the Sky-Black Plateau? I’m part engineer, part plant, part caretaker, and part ghost. I exist in the half-spaces, gliding through dim corridors where no one else bothers to look, ensuring that this living-industrial labyrinth continues to pulse with its strange heartbeat. I am the living weld between metal and moss, the silent sentinel overseeing every rusty seam. In a world where darkness never fades, I am the one who mends the cracks. And in that way, the plateau and I keep each other alive.
