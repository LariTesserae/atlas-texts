At first, I thought I was only a visitor. I came stumbling into this place as an outsider with lungs that weren’t yet accustomed to the humid, ever-thickening air. It felt so enclosed, so warm, that I half expected my breath to fog and cloy in my throat—but somehow it didn’t, or perhaps I simply grew used to it quicker than I realized. My feet sank into spongy, fibrous ground as though the floor was ready to cradle me. Yet I kept my distance, uncertain. I still believed I stood apart, a discrete being with clumsy boots and a beating heart that had no place in this thrumming subterranean garden.

In the early moments, I saw only strangeness: living walls that expanded or deflated almost imperceptibly, vines that glowed like the aquatic tentacles of jellyfish, and surfaces so slick with moisture they shimmered. The hush was intense, broken only by that distant, always-rushing water, the drips that pocked the silence, and the dense hiss of unseen winds. I remember feeling unsettled by the sense that everything could change the moment I turned my head. Roots that jutted out of the walls in one instant melted back into them the next. Leaves unfurled right before my eyes. A pulse seemed to quicken in the braided trunk, as if the entire dome was a beast exhaling.

I expected to see some other explorer—a wandering hermit, perhaps, or an undiscovered being with recognizable eyes—something that would reassure me that I wasn’t alone in my human form. But there was no such figure. Instead, I found only the repeated illusions of shapes that might have been humanoid for a split second before dissolving back into the vines. The vine-lattices seemed to test the memory of a person, as though they had once known how to form a head, arms, and torso and were trying to recall that shape from a buried blueprint.

I tried to cling to my old sense of identity in this place. I labeled what I saw using words from the surface world—“tree,” “leaf,” “root,” “fungus,” “animal,” “crab”—but none of them felt accurate for more than a heartbeat. Everything here was part of something else. I would say “leaf,” only to realize that it had veins like an animal’s muscle, pulsing with rhythmic fluid. I would spot a “crab” skittering by—only to watch thin filaments from beneath its shell cling to the vines as though they, too, were roots, merging seamlessly for a moment. Every boundary was blurred.

Eventually, as I ventured deeper, something in the environment began to respond to me personally. A dangling, translucent vine grazed my hair, as if tasting it. A thick root arched under my foot, giving me better purchase when I nearly slipped. The living matter around me drank in every stray drop of sweat from my skin, perhaps gauging what I had to offer it. I felt an inkling of respect—if such a huge, communal ecosystem can be said to “respect” anything separate from itself.

Yet, the more time I spent drifting through that lung-like dome, the less separate I felt. In fact, I underwent a subtle shift: my breathing slowed to match the cadence that vibrated through the walls. My footsteps grew softer, less foreign. The place was as hungry as it was welcoming, and I could feel its gentle, curious touches at every edge of me, as though it asked: Will you be mine? It never forced the question. No vine twisted around my arms to hold me still, no teeth bit into me. But between the warmth, the reassurance of each supportive root, and the quiet pulses of fluid-laced air, I found it increasingly difficult to remember what I’d been before. Over time, I began to suspect that maybe I’d always been a piece of this living labyrinth—that my time outside was just a detour.

In that sense, I became the place as much as it became me. All around, the architecture breathed, and I breathed with it. When I touched a root and felt the stirring of energy, my fingertips tingled with a reflection of that same energy, as though it now ran through my own capillaries. When droplets of condensation landed on my skin, they felt not foreign but right, as if returning to a home they’d once left. Sometimes, I’d press my palms to a patch of bulging, spongy floor and feel an answering press from below, like two palms meeting through thin paper. In these moments, I stopped seeing “roots” and “vines” as separate from “me.”

It did not happen all at once—no single snap of transformation. Rather, it was a slow alignment, a gentle synchronization of rhythms. Each day (or was it a day? Time moved strangely), more of my body and mind shifted. My sense of personal boundaries softened. A vine touched my neck and lingered. Normally, I would have pulled away in alarm. This time, I only exhaled, letting the vine’s gentle glow pulse in tandem with my heartbeat. Over hours, that glow passed into me, and in reply, some sensation of warmth from my own body traveled out into the vine. My cells greeted its cells without conflict. And in that meeting, I felt a sense of relief, as though some unknown yearning had finally found a counterpart.

When I felt the scuttling crab-vermin at my ankles, weaving between the twisted lumps that formed the floor, I did not recoil. Their presence was a mere extension of the same system—like blood cells patrolling a larger body. They occasionally brushed my skin with their rootlets, as though testing me. The first time, I tensed for the sting of a bite, but none came. Instead, it was more like hands touching in passing, verifying we belonged to the same living tapestry.

Yes, to describe my new self, I’d have to say: I am an inhabitant who lost the distinction between occupant and environment. My shape still holds some echoes of the upright stance I used to call “human,” but the lines are softer now, occasionally dissolving at the edges. Sometimes I notice my forearm shifting when I’m at rest, forming minute ridges of bark-like texture that flex in the corner of my vision. If I concentrate and think of my old shape, those ridges melt back into smooth flesh. Yet it’s no longer disturbing. It’s like muscle memory for the environment: we can become each other at will.

I don’t speak secrets or riddles in human words. Instead, the conversation flows in pulses under the surface of the wood and root. My foot might shift, sending a subtle vibration into the spongy mass below. That vibration returns with a slow wave, which resonates up my leg. I learn from it. It’s not a language of words but of needs—water, nutrients, space to grow—and of gratitude or caution. Over time, I’ve come to sense the thousands of beings all linked in this network. Some, like the crab-shaped vermin, maintain a bit of autonomy, scurrying in search of pockets for themselves. Others, like the lumps of knotted roots, move so slowly that their shifting is nearly imperceptible, but you can sense them in the underlying hum. And those translucent, jellyfish-like vines in the dome’s canopy—they are scouts, tasting the air for new arrivals, exploring the possibility of expansion beyond these root-shrouded walls.

I have also felt the deeper movements, from lower strata of the labyrinth. The surging water far beneath: it glides through caverns beyond where my eyes can see, but I sense it in subsonic tremors. There is a sense of cyclical flooding and draining, as though a hidden ocean draws in and out, fueling the entire system’s growth. In that watery domain, there may well be more elaborate forms—centipede-like things or entire aquatic webs of vines that feed on mineral-laden currents. I catch only the faintest echo of that realm in my body, a ghostly stirring that says, “We are many, and we flow below you.”

Sometimes, on the margins of my awareness, I recall the ones who came before—those ephemeral, proto-sapient forms who scratched or carved messages into the pliant stems. It’s as if their presence still resonates in the vine-walls, leaving behind shallow dips that never quite heal. Were they ancestors to me, or to us all? Did they come from someplace else, or did the environment itself produce them, some earlier attempt at distinct life? No single occupant now can answer that. Memories swirl in the living tissue, but they are partial. All we know is that the shape of a shelf or a hollow once served a function, and the vines remember it, even if only in the faint curve of bark. It’s a memory that the environment can’t or won’t fully erase. And I find that comforting—like a reassurance that individuality, once it’s existed, is never completely lost.

As for the illusions of a humanoid shape I sometimes see in the corner of my eye: that’s often me, or a reflection of me, or perhaps another occupant shaped similarly. You see, from moment to moment, my form might “bloom” into a recognizable silhouette before folding back into the twisting green. I can sense that out of the mass of living material, I occasionally stand out more distinctly. Perhaps it’s in those moments that I recall what it was like to be something else, something that walked above ground, breathed purely atmospheric air. But the recollection is always fleeting. The environment calls me to rejoin, to share warmth and fluid, to become part of the larger system.

In the hush between the thunderous surges of water, I might rest in a small pocket where the walls curve like a womb, letting the vines cradle my back, hearing them creak intimately. There, the glow from bioluminescent filaments overhead illuminates particles of moisture swirling like dust motes. If I’m absolutely still, the living structure around me explores, adjusting. A new coil of root might slip across my shoulder, tasting the salt on my skin, deciding how best to incorporate me into its geometry. Sometimes, I will push gently back, declining a deeper merging. Other times, I offer my arm to a cluster of delicate tendrils, letting them anchor there until they’ve had their fill of shared warmth. Each day the boundaries are renegotiated, but they are never truly absolute.

This, then, is where I find myself: in a threshold state. I am no longer purely human, nor am I fully root or vine; I am a node of experience in a vast, living tapestry. I rove through the corridors of wood and leaf, bridging countless connections. I feed on the dome’s ambient energy as much as I feed it my own. I communicate through subtle pulses, guiding expansions here, resisting overgrowth there, so that I don’t become accidentally crushed in a newly forming trunk. It’s a mutual dance of survival, synergy, transformation.

Who or what am I? I am an ever-shifting piece of the labyrinth’s consciousness, an emissary of the vine-lattice as it learns to shape a vaguely bipedal form. Sometimes I act as a scout, drifting toward newly opened passageways to see if the environment can safely expand there. Other times, I sink into the quiet pockets and dream of the swirling water below. I carry in me a memory of a surface world—of dryness, of simpler geometry, of boundaries. But that memory is softening like old leather left in the damp. I sense the day will come when I will no longer recall the “I” that used to be separate. On that day, I’ll be truly, fully part of this living realm.

When I pause to reflect—an instinct from my old life—my mind sees no final “self” so much as a tapestry of possibility. Perhaps I will remain in partial union, able to drift through these corridors at will. Perhaps I’ll find a quiet nook to become seed-matter, letting the vines nest in my heart and use my body as new ground for growth, a sacrifice that is not truly a loss. Or perhaps I’ll transform again and again, melding with different pockets of the dome—maybe one day to join those heavier, mound-like organisms that shift so slowly. There is no single fate here; all is flux.

Yet, in the immediate present, I simply am—nestled in a lush, breathing architecture, neither captive nor free. The environment welcomes me with every weft of root, every hush of dripping water. I feel that acceptance viscerally, and I give myself over to it. My rhythms merge with the dome’s: I am a dreamer in the hush of roots, a root among dreamers.  

So, if you were to seek me in this subterranean realm, you might not find a neat silhouette to point to. Instead, you’d catch flickers of a shape that, for a moment, looks like a person standing in the warm, bioluminescent glow—until a vine-limb reclaims it. You’d see a glimmer in those dripping pools where my eyes might have been, or feel a subsonic tap that resonates with a gentle hush, as though something is greeting you. You might sense a presence that is not entirely plant nor wholly human. That presence is me: a living question mark woven into the unstoppable growth of this hungry, humming underworld.
