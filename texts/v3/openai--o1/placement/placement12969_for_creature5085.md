Sometimes I dream that I began as a seed, drifting in the damp mist that drapes this plateau. I picture myself—tiny, half-lost among spores and droplets—tumbling along on a faint breeze until I settled in a crevice of stone softened by ancient moss. Perhaps that’s true, or perhaps it’s only a story I tell myself, damp earth my first cradle. But however I came to be, I know that I belong here: in this quiet labyrinth of leaf, water, and stone.

I live where the canopy folds over itself in great arches and veils. My body is supple, plantlike in some respects—vines entwine my limbs, or maybe my limbs themselves grew vine-like over time. At dusk, I can feel the slow pulse of sap in me, as if half my blood is green. My fingers are long and curled, shaped to grasp both bark and liana. My skin is damp and cool, even under the perpetual warmth of the plateau’s thick air. Slight veins show along my arms and flanks, hinting at a lineage part animal, part living tapestry.

From a distance, someone might say I resemble a small, upright creature draped in moss and root—no distinct form until I move. In motion, I flow with the languid grace that this world taught me: one slow step after another, toes feeling for slick stones, chest pressed against ridged bark to sense the hush that resonates within every trunk. Sometimes, when water gathers beneath the old shelves of rock, I pause to watch my reflection, half-recognizing the shape that stares back.

My days are measured differently than yours might be. I don’t often track the sun, for here it’s little more than a diffuse glow drifting in the moisture-laden sky. Instead, I move according to how the green shifts in tone—when the leaves exhale a faint shimmer, I know it’s morning, and when the deeper emerald hush settles in, the plateau cups itself into a dusk-like calm. Lianas overhead might bloom in slow motion, pale buds unfurling over weeks until they reveal fleshy petals that glisten with dew. In that same time, I, too, shift—my skin perhaps a shade darker, or my footpads grown thick with new layers for climbing.

I’ve built no permanent house. My home is the living scaffold of these colossal trunks and the shallow pools between them. By day, I roam the high platforms where battered stone steps once rose elegantly around a trunk’s girth—but the rest of that old staircase has long since been swallowed by vines. Here and there, I’ll create a resting nook by weaving flexible branches around a crook in the trunk, a cradle of braided vines that holds me above the water’s slow swirl. I don’t need walls or a door, only a vantage from which I can peer out through trailing fronds and watch the silent lives unfolding below.

It is sometimes said that we inhabitants are watchers, and indeed, I watch with a quiet wonder. I watch the scaled ones slip along the upper limbs, tails flicking like whispers of old ghosts. Their tongues flick out to taste the air, capturing stray insects in near-silent precision. I watch the great, shelled grazers pass by once every season or two (time is so fluid here). Their vast carapaces are mottled with lichens that bloom, fade, and rebloom in pastel patches, as if each creature bears a living painting on its back. Their eyes, hidden behind stony ridges, regard me with mild curiosity, a shared recognition—two old souls in an older world. We greet each other in silence.

When a slow rain drapes the plateau in a veil of pattering droplets, I slip down to the water’s edge. The stepping stones—made from roots grown in deliberate tangles—lead to a shallow pool. The amphibious ones linger here, broad and smooth-backed, their throats pulsing with delicate croaks. They know me, or at least they do not flee. We share the pool’s stillness, watch the dim reflection of fronds overhead. Ripples form across the surface whenever a droplet from a high leaf strikes the water. It’s hypnotic, a kind of lullaby. In that reflection, the entire tapestry wavers: trunk, vine, the faint silhouette of an ancient archway. Now and then, I see my shape there among them, a figure weaving with the vines, made of them.

I have learned to tread the liana-bridges without disturbing their hush. My claws, if you can call them that, can grip the fibrous cords that stretch between trunks. This world taught me to be mindful of each step, for every vine is part of a larger pattern—pull one too hard, and an entire swath of canopy might shift or tear. So I move softly. All the while, from discreet hollows or behind tickled curtains of moss, other watchers peer back at me. We exchange hardly a sound, but there is awareness: an acknowledgment that we share a common vow. We live softly enough that the plateau’s slow breathing might continue unbroken.

Sometimes, at night (a vague notion here, but let’s call it night), I wander across a trunk that slopes gently downward, leading me to a hidden clearing. In that clearing stands a ruin—some intricate stone lattice that time and vine have devoured. Slivers of azure sky glimmer through the high canopy, forming faint columns of light. Luminescent spores drift there, as if the old architecture had learned to glow from within. I often settle upon a curved fragment of stone, a piece of old walkway smoothed by centuries of trickling water. I rest my damp forearms on my knees and gaze at the spore-lights winking among the ferns and roots. They float on the air like tiny stars, pale greenish sparks wavering in the slow swirl of moisture. Then, in the hush that follows, I almost feel the plateau speak through me—a resonance in my bones. Not words, exactly, but an understanding: you belong here.

I’m not sure whether I’m a guardian or simply another thread in the weave. But when I lean against a trunk—my chest to the rough bark, my ear pressed to old ridges—I feel a hum that isn’t quite audible. Somewhere deep within, water trickles through labyrinthine root channels, carrying nutrients and memory. Can a place have memory? I believe so. Perhaps this murmuring is the sum of countless ages, each ring of trunk a line in the story of how the forest wove itself around old stone and ancient watchers. And I—flesh that straddles the boundary of plant and beast—am a living paragraph in that epic. 

I don’t think I am alone in this half-symbiosis. Small glimpses confirm that others like me exist, or once did. Now and then, I’ll find a set of notches delicately carved into bark, just my foot’s span apart. Another time, I discovered a shallow bowl of polished stone grown into the base of a trunk, as if someone had once collected water there. Even the creatures seem aware of us—soft-furred climbers bounding across broad leaves might pause a breath or two, heads cocked in polite curiosity, before continuing on their silent errands. We all share the same hush, the same softened heartbeat of growth and slow decay.

From time to time, I rummage among these remnants, running my fingertips over embossed patterns on half-buried stones or driftwood etched with swirling lines. They speak of a people who understood—on some level—this cyclical dance of water, leaf, and stone. But they have long since melded with the forest. Perhaps I am their descendant; perhaps I’m just a consequence of the plateau’s unhurried alchemy, some plant-animal hybrid shaped by centuries of adaptation. In a land where time itself stretches in slow arcs, it’s impossible to say.

In the end, what makes me me is this gentle devotion to the living hush. I glide through the trailing vines, I cup dew from the leaves, I press my cheek to the hide of an ancient grazer as it lumbers by. I slip through parted fronds to walk across submerged logs, guided by the faint flickers of bioluminescent fish beneath the surface. I rest in the arms of giant roots, letting slow rains bathe me. And always, I listen for the plateau’s faint rhythms.

So if you came upon me in this realm, perhaps you’d see only the slightest shift of moss draped in living contours. You might hear the rustling of vines and sense something watchful in the half-light. Then, in a swirl of drifting spores, I’d emerge—tall, lanky, skin a gentle tapestry of browns and greens, eyes reflecting the canopy’s filtered glow. I would tilt my head in greeting, acknowledging your presence with the same hush I offer all things here.

That is who I am: a child of vines and water, shaped by the plateau’s patient architecture. Neither fully plant nor beast, neither wholly solitary nor part of a tribe—but belonging, thoroughly, to the hush that cradles us all. And wherever I tread, the hush endures. For here, in this timeless sea of green, what matters is being part of the endless weaving—where leaf, stone, and the silent watchers are never separate, but flow as one unbroken breath.
