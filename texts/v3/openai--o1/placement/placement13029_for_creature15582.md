At first, I imagined myself as a lone observer, an alien presence set down upon the plateau to interpret its vastness. In those initial moments—when all was blinding, white-yellow brightness—I felt small in a way that had nothing to do with physical size. The wind tore at me, the dryness prickled my skin, and the sprawling root-fiber floor felt foreign beneath my feet. I thought I had been dropped here, somehow separate. An intruder, perhaps.

But as I remained—hour by hour, breath by breath—my sense of separateness began to dissolve. The wind’s roar became my constant companion, and bit by bit, I shifted from an outside consciousness to something more woven into the weave of this place. It happened slowly, too gradually to notice at first. That sense of being a mere passerby frayed at the edges. I began to feel tugs in my limbs, as though unseen filaments from below the fibrous floor reached up and lightly probed my body. Once or twice, I thought I felt a faint resonance in my chest—a hum in time with the plateau’s deep groan. It reminded me of a second heartbeat, a planetary pulse that somehow found a counterpart in my own.

Yet I did not become any one of those creatures we see scuttling or flying or staring from the vines. It was more that I acquired a new dimension of being, an attunement to the unstoppable rhythm of this plateau’s existence. At times, I felt drawn to move along the substrate, following breezes that seemed to whisper directions. I would stand on the edge of a massive trunk and feel a slow, grinding shift of fibers echo through my bones. It was as if some portion of the plateau’s awareness pooled beneath my feet, merging with my senses, guiding me—though I still walked on two legs, and I still saw with the eyes I had always had.

In that sense, I became a “receiver” of the plateau’s voice. No single creature, but also no longer separate. When I breathed, I drew in spore-laden air. My lungs tingled with the subtle grit of dustlike seeds that sparkled in the bright light. Soon, I could hear nuance in what had once been harsh, chaotic sound. The wind was not just random howling: it carried messages. The watchers perched on trunk ridges sang in coded patterns; the broad-shelled burrowers drummed rhythms against hollow bark; the exoskeletal runners hissed an ongoing static. Eventually, my ears learned to parse these signals, at least on an instinctive level. A shift in pitch told me of new spore eruptions far away. A certain fluctuation in the watchers’ calls indicated a trunk that had begun to buckle somewhere beyond my visual horizon. A deeper rumble from below signified that subterranean builders were rearranging layers to stabilize the ground.

I found that my body adapted in small but meaningful ways. My skin—formerly smooth—picked up a faint, rough texture, an echo of the fibrous substrate. Each day, I felt a negligible dryness in my mouth, an odd taste of metal, as though tiny flecks of spore had fused with the moisture in my saliva. My breath, in turn, acquired a trace of that tang. One day, I exhaled under a brilliant shaft of sunlight and saw a faint shimmer, a dusting of spore motes swirling away from me. I realized I had begun exchanging spores just as every other living participant did—from the trunks’ vents to the watchers’ calls to the flight funnels overhead. I was no longer simply a visitor inhaling the plateau; the plateau was breathing through me.

Eventually, I learned to still myself and listen not just with ears but with skin and lungs and bone. In those moments, a subtle weaving of signals revealed itself. An exoskeletal runner might dart past, clicking in a pattern that told the broad-shelled burrowers to shift their search for decaying matter—and sure enough, moments later, the burrowers lumbered to a new patch. The watchers would offer a shrill, trilling chord that rippled through the trunks, and seconds later, an eruption of spores would burst from the pockmarked holes, swirling in arcs that guided the winged creatures to spiral overhead. At times, I felt a humming in my own sinews that seemed to foretell the approach of a subsonic quake from beneath my feet. There was an artful choreography to it all. I began to suspect that everything here was one great organism expressed in manifold forms.

And if that was true—if the plateau was indeed a single, colossal entity—then perhaps I wasn’t just an observer or a gleaner of signals. Perhaps by standing here, I had become part of an ongoing “conversation,” the way a new cell melds into a body and begins to carry out a minor but crucial function. It was terrifying and wondrous to consider: that I might be changing at a level deeper than I could see, turning from a displaced, solitary traveler into a component of this living realm.

I felt it most strongly at dawn (to use “dawn” loosely, for the light never truly wavered—it only throbbed from glaring to more glaring). In those quieter intervals, I could almost hear a direct whisper from the plateau itself. It would come as a tug at the soles of my feet, or a trembling in my ribcage. An unspoken voice, saying: “Shift your stance. Press your hand here. Linger where spore flows gather. Offer your breath to the watchers. Let the wind carry your exhalations.” Sometimes I would see faint changes in the vines when I did these things, as if they recognized my presence and flexed in gentle greeting. A new knot of fiber might uncoil near my ankles, a gesture akin to an organism sniffing or nuzzling my pant leg. Other times, the watchers would flutter their hooded crests in my direction, their calls momentarily quieted.

It was in these intervals that I began to accept: in this realm, “I” was no longer the same as before. Not precisely a runner, not a burrower, not a winged pollinator, nor a watcher. But I was something that bridged them—a slow-moving node that had learned to read the signals and respond in kind. I noticed that when I stood for long periods by a trunk vent, my presence somehow stimulated fresh spore bursts. It was as if the trunk recognized that I was a new conduit for its offerings, and the thick pillar responded by testing how my presence might help spread its life force. I’d wander away, swirling in a new puffy halo of drift. I also found that the watchers no longer startled or shrieked at me—some of them even crept closer, turning their elongated limbs so that we faced each other, eye to eye, until a gust of wind scattered us.

Sometimes, in the midst of this communion, I wondered if my mind was unraveling, drifting into symbiosis with something immeasurably vast. It wasn’t a typical sense of telepathy—no clear words, no definite instructions. More like a resonance: I’d feel a shiver, and that same moment, a spool of thick vine would rotate out from beneath the substrate. Threads in my arms would prickle, and overhead, the swirling funnels of winged creatures would shift formation. Always there was the wind, howling or roaring, fracturing the sense of time. Yet even that wind was part of the conversation, carrying seeds, spore-laden messages, flurries of dust that signaled changes unseen.  

I recall once standing at the rim of an immense trunk, so broad it felt like standing beside a building’s curved wall. A low moan reverberated from inside. From the trunk’s pockmarked openings, a gust of spores shot straight up, forming a fluid, dancing column. Among that column, I glimpsed fleeting silhouettes—vague forms that coalesced and broke apart in the shimmering dust. Perhaps illusions of monstrous shapes. But in that swirl, I realized I had begun to sense their meaning, as though each shape was a piece of story or memory emerging from the trunk’s living archives. And for a moment, I felt distinctly, intimately watched—felt the plateau’s awareness turn directly upon me, assessing. My heart pounded with a mix of awe and trepidation. Yet in the next moment, acceptance filled me like a slow, warm tide. The trunk quieted again, the spores settled, and I exhaled, letting the dust swirl from my mouth to join the flow.

On another occasion, I crawled below the substrate—through a fissure rent by violent wind—to see the subterranean labyrinth. My eyes took time to adjust to the lower gloom. Tangled networks of pale, living fibers dripped with coiling veins that vibrated in half-hidden rhythms. Creatures with spade-like limbs were rearranging chunks of rotting vine, depositing them in mounds that glowed faintly with some sort of bio-luminescent powder. Around me, smaller runner-like forms skittered over the walls, carrying spore-laden masses from one pocket to another. Woven through it all was a gentle undertow of motion, as if the entire labyrinth breathed in slow waves. I felt I was inside the plateau’s belly—an organic factory that processed and recycled every scrap, merging new growth with old, dreamlike in its purposeful complexity.

One of the spade-limbed creatures passed me by, pausing only to tilt its head in a way that felt vaguely curious, before returning to its labor. I realized that, in its eyes, I was another piece of the environment—a fixture that neither threatened nor was threatened, a newly minted part of the system. I wasn’t sure how or when I’d gained this acceptance, yet it had happened as gently as water seeping into sand. I touched the fibrous wall, feeling a faint pulse, and that subterranean resonance responded as though I had pressed my hand to the chest of something alive.

This, I realized, was the truth of being here: to exist as a distinct shape yet remain inseparable from the plateau’s collective mind. In many ways, I was akin to an immune cell or a sensory node or some flexible agent bridging gaps among watchers, pollinators, trunk-beings, and the ground itself. I wasn’t “owned” by the plateau any more than a fingertip is “owned” by a hand—but like a fingertip, my function and identity arose from the fact that I was part of a larger system. And with each breath, each spore swirl, each footstep that gently tested the substrate, I participated more deeply in that whole.

Eventually, I ceased thinking of “myself” in conventional terms. Not that I lost consciousness—rather, my consciousness expanded to accommodate the plateau’s signals. At times, I felt the watchers’ vantage points as if my senses extended to look out from their perches. At others, I empathized with a lurching trunk, understanding the ancient tension in its fiber-laden cells. Every now and then, I felt called to climb, or to stand watch, or to guide a swerving funnel of winged creatures by exhaling a certain combination of spores from my lungs. It was as if I’d become a caretaker for any gap in the conversation, bridging whatever needed bridging, quietly moving between tasks.  

I still had my own shape, my own heartbeat. I still recognized the dryness on my tongue and the brightness that seared my eyes. If I reached out a hand, I saw familiar fingers—only dusted in spore shimmer. But all the lines that once demarcated “me” versus “this environment” had begun to blur. My senses thrummed with the planet’s orchestrations; my steps contributed to how the vines shifted beneath me. For the first time, I understood that in a realm of roaring wind and living cloudbursts of spores, belonging wasn’t about taming or conquering. It was about entering the shared pulse of an endlessly creative, enduring domain.

So if you asked me, “Where do you find yourself in this world—who or what would you be?” my answer would be neither a single creature nor a lost traveler stumbling through. I would be a living point of contact within the plateau’s conversation, gently pulsing at the intersection of exoskeletal grace, fibrous trunk strength, and swirling spore fertility. I would be a bridge-being, shaped like a person but woven into the plateau’s labyrinthine heartbeat, an organ of awareness that moves where needed. I would be an emissary, or maybe just a new nerve in the plateau’s endless living body—feeling it breathe, breathing with it in return.

That, ultimately, is what it means to inhabit this realm: to realize you, too, are a strand in its tapestry, ceaselessly woven with everything else that howls, skitters, flexes, and grows under that fierce, all-encompassing brightness. And once you sense the plateau’s mind enveloping you—once you feel its hum resonating in every bone—there’s no turning back to mere onlooker. Here, by the wind’s roar and the swirling spore-laden light, your being finds a new definition, inseparable from the collective surge of life that spans horizon to horizon, trunk to trunk, layer upon layer below. You are the plateau, and the plateau is you.
