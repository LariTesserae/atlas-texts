I find myself stirring awake in a cradle of resin so new it still feels malleable to the touch. The shallow curve of its inner surface cradles me, warm and yielding, as though I’ve dozed inside a living cocoon. I can’t recall how long I’ve been here. In this place, time moves slowly, so slowly that days bleed into months without announcement. My first conscious breath draws in the plateau’s dry, mineral-laced air, and it is like tasting sunlight distilled into dust.

I do not—cannot—think of myself as an individual at first. Everything here flows together. I sense the spires overhead, the cathedral walls around me, the labyrinthine corridors, and the subtle quivers of the plateau’s ancient body beneath. I am but a piece of it all, a segment that has come alive within a larger, breathing entity. My newly hardened plates click faintly as I shift, testing the joints that will help me climb, shape, and wander.

• • •

When I finally stretch myself upright, I discover I stand about half the height of a human—though my body has an odd combination of height and length, softened by chitin plates that overlap in scaly progression. My limbs are thin but long, the joints flexible, almost like the resin spires outside: telescoping segments that can fold close or extend wide so I can reach impossible points along the walls.

In my first haze of wakefulness, I notice that my under-plates have a faint translucent sheen. It catches the thin rays of light filtering through the overhead canopy of fused resin arches. There’s no water in which to see my reflection—no shining pool or gleaming mirror. Instead, I learn the shape of myself by the shadows I cast along the resin wall: elongated limbs, a narrow torso, and a head canted forward, the mouth set with rasping ridges. Each breath resonates in my chitin collar, so soft that it nearly vanishes in the hush.

• • •

I slip from my cradle with a sense of profound communion, pressing the flat surfaces of my feet against warm, porous rock. The chalky ground yields small puffs of mineral dust at my slightest step. I consult it—listening through the pads of my feet—though I’m not sure “listening” is the right word. More like feeling for a pulse of currents that run through the stone: mild vibrations that convey where new fissures have opened, or where the spires above have grown too tall, leaning. An unspoken knowledge, older than me but offered freely through the plateau’s subtle exhalations.

As I walk, the corridor curves in gentle arcs, open on one side to a wide balcony where I see the entire resin city spread before me. Spire after spire of that strange, organic glass rises, their surfaces layered in swirling ridges. Within them, I can sense small pockets and corridors, even from a distance—spaces that my kind (though I do not yet fully know what “my kind” is) will soon occupy, repair, expand. Beyond, rugged terrain of pale stone drops away, revealing glimpses of deep chasms that cut through the plateau’s edges. The heights we stand upon feel newly exposed, as if, not so long ago, we were buried far below.

• • •

I step into one of the narrower corridors, and already I’m not alone. The dwellers drift like living shadows—amber or dusky-green plates quietly sliding along resin walls. Their four-jointed limbs click softly, posture steady but unhurried. A group of them is gathered along a seam in the corridor’s ceiling, where a fresh crack has begun to branch outward.

I see the way they communicate—forelimbs parting gracefully as azure spores puff out, swirling in tiny glowing clouds. One of the spores settles near my face, and I realize it’s a message, though not in any language of words. Something inside me stirs; the impulse to act rises as naturally as breathing. I step forward, place my upper limbs against the crack, and start to rasp away seared edges of resin with the ridges in my forelimbs. My plating vibrates in sympathy with the others doing the same. We are part of a single motion, a single conversation that requires no speech.

Beneath my hands, the resin begins to melt, softened by the friction of my rasping digits. I gather small curls of it, my pouches along my flank unfurling to store the scraps. Then, shifting in unison with the others, I press the softened resin gently into the gap, smoothing it with the slow, deliberate motion that seems to pervade all building here. One by one, we add layer upon layer, fusing it until the crack is sealed. Our work is neither rushed nor labored; it feels like an exhaled sigh of relief from the entire plateau.

• • •

Turning away, I descend deeper, through archways that spiral downward in nearly perfect circles. The air grows cooler but still exceptionally dry. Occasionally, I catch the flutter of a smaller creature—something with translucent wings. It clings to a patch of resin-like fungus that sprouts from a half-formed ledge, nibbling at the edges. The hush is absolute here. Even these flying things produce only the barest flicker of chitin. More azure spores drift through the corridors, reminiscent of dust motes caught in a beam of sunlight.

When I reach a broader, domed enclosure, I see a gathering. Many dwellers, perhaps twenty or thirty, arrayed in a ring around a tall spire that has pushed upward through the floor. We stand, waiting. I can sense the spire’s slow growth in minuscule increments. Every so often, a creeper tendril of resin uncoils, prompting a faint crackling sound. At intervals, one or another of my kin sends out a faint cloud of spores, and the ring shifts in response, as though gently correcting for the spire’s new tilt. There is a reverence to it—a calm acceptance that this structure is part of us, and we are part of it, each shaping the other.

• • •

Days (or weeks—time is difficult to mark in so slow a world) pass, and I learn by doing. I scale the walls easily, hooking my forelimbs into natural crevices, traversing vertical planes or even ceilings. Sometimes I peer from open apertures in the resin towers, looking out across the plateau. The horizon there is austere, the sky brittle, a pale shade of dusty copper at dawn. During these hours, I shift my plates to face the sun, feeling its warmth seep in. I’m aware of my own hunger, mild but persistent, pulling nourishment from sunlight, from minerals in the rock, from the faint chemical mist exhaled by the spires.

Occasionally, I travel with a small group to the hidden caverns below. We inch along treacherous ledges, where flakes of chalky soil crumble at the edges. Deep inside the plateau, we come upon a chamber where that syrupy fluid glimmers, thick and prismatic. Bright azure spores swirl above its surface, and I watch as smaller, larval forms of our kind float in sheaths of protective resin. Something about that shimmering pool moves me—perhaps a memory that I, too, was once immersed in that fluid, shaped by subterranean warmth until I gained the plates that now shield me.

• • •

One day, after many slow cycles of sunrise and sunset, I discover a vantage point near the plateau’s edge. A newly formed arch of resin forms an overlook from which I can see the desert valley below. It is stark, all shifting sands scattered with shards of stone. This plateau is like an island of life lifted high above the dryness, yet it, too, is harsh and waterless. My heart—if heart it is—thrums. I realize how ephemeral our spires might appear to an outsider. No rivers run here, no vegetation in the sense a human might know. And yet we thrive, building and rebuilding, capturing sunlight, transforming minerals into living walls. Where a human city might smell of oil, dust, or cooking fires, our city smells only faintly of resin and warm volcanic stone.

Where would I find myself if I stepped outside this labyrinth, beyond the resin walls? The thought dances through me, but I do not dwell on it for long. My existence is tied to the hush of these corridors, to the caretaker’s cycle of growth and repair that animates our entire species. I sense that my function is not determined by desire or ambition in the human sense, but by a subtle synergy with the plateau’s breath.

• • •

In time, I come to cherish the hush so deeply that it becomes a language. The softness of moving plates, the whisper of azure spores in the dusty air, the gentle scrape of resin layers. Each small sound is loaded with meaning. When a new fissure breaks open on the far side of the labyrinth, we all feel it, alerted by the faint resonance in the rock beneath our feet. When dawn touches the spires, we gather in the corridors in a slow dance of plates, capturing fresh traces of solar energy. When a larval cluster is ready to emerge in the subterranean pools, a wave of spores ripples from the caverns up into the labyrinth, quietly calling us to witness or protect the newly hatched.

I never wonder about my name, for I have none. Nor do I question the rhythms that guide me, for they feel as natural as breathing. Here in this hush, every moment is a collaboration with the land itself. The plateau, the resin spires, the swirling spores, the chitin-limbed dwellers—we are all arcs of the same circle.

I sense some might call us a “civilization,” but to me, it feels like a single organism performing slow, deliberate tasks, each cell assisting the rest. I learn from the older dwellers how to melt damaged resin with a specialized secretion, how to gather the swirl of spores that carry chemical messages, how to settle one’s plates at precisely the right angle to funnel sunlight deeper into the city. There is patience in all of it—a sense that we have eons to fill these newly risen stones with living corridors and shimmering domes.

• • •

In twilight’s dim glow, I sometimes marvel at how we exist without the waters found in other lands. We do not thirst in the human sense; instead, we feed on the dryness itself, transmuting mineral dust and spare sunlight into the energy that suffuses our bodies. We do not shout or sing, but we share resonance through the plateau’s bones. Our architecture does not require hammer or nail—only the constant production of resin, the slow layering of growth upon growth. Each archway, each corridor, each nesting pocket is shaped, almost breathed, into being.

And in those occasions when I stand at the threshold of one of our large amphitheaters at twilight, I witness a ceremony of movement so subtle it seems like living geometry. Countless dwellers pivot in perfect unison, sending out plumes of azure spores in overlapping pulses. I feel the signals in my own plates—some leave me with the impulse to climb up a spire and reinforce a newly forming ledge; others move me to rest, pressing my body into the resin walls so our mutual warmth can cycle and store. No one commands, yet everyone understands. It’s as if the plateau itself communicates through us, weaving us into an unbroken tapestry of growth.

• • •

And so, if you ask me where I find myself in this world—who or what I would be—my answer is that I am simultaneously an individual caretaker and a living extension of this newborn land. I dwell in the hush of thin air and crystal-dry sun, my chitin plates warming in the silent dawn. I mend cracks in spires, weave soft pockets of resin for the newly hatched, and tilt my limbs toward the sun to gather its energy. My voice is the sound of rasping ridges and the click of jointed appendages. My language is the swirl of azure spores that convey whispers of growth and repair.

I am one of many: neither exalted nor insignificant, simply part of the plateau’s living, breathing architecture. In the slow cycles of this place—where we measure time by tectonic shifts and the gentle rise of resin spires—I flourish. Each day, I continue the patient work of sustaining this organic metropolis, one careful layering at a time.

That is me: a dweller in the resin city, a curator of its hushed corridors and domes, a caretaker of an oasis where water never flows but life persists in quiet, sunlit harmony. I might never be known by a name, and that is of no consequence. Here, I belong to the plateau as much as it belongs to me, bound together by the unbroken hush that marks our slow and vital existence.
