I come to this place neither as conqueror nor visitor, but as one who has forgotten my origins. In my hazy memories, I see shapes of a prior world—where walls were static stone and air was silent unless stirred by passing wind. Yet those recollections wither each day, replaced by the pulsing rhythms of this living city that enfolds me. I suspect I have been here longer than I remember, as though I woke in a dream that is now more real than any other truth I have known.

At first, I believed I was a mere traveler with boots pressing into mossy bark, wide-eyed at the chaos of voices. I wandered, searching for the next water source, half-deafened by the floods of sound echoing through hollow trunks. My eyes were always lifted upward, trying to make sense of branching corridors that shifted day to day. I felt like a child in a labyrinth, enthralled by each new revelation—a hidden pool, a twisting vine that opened into a cathedral-like vault, luminescent insects that burst from the walls in coordinated flights.

But slowly, a different understanding took hold. My senses began to meld with the place. I found that if I leaned against certain trunks and closed my eyes, I perceived movements impossible to see in the surface world: the expansions and contractions of plant fibers, the gentle electricity of roots sharing nourishment, the throbbing heartbeat of the city’s daily cycle. Listening beetles thrummed against my clothes, then leapt away as if satisfied by the reverberations within me. The toads and newts seemed less skittish around my presence, sometimes letting me glimpse the glowing patterns on their skins more closely. They would stare with bright, ancient-looking eyes and exhale in slow huffs, as though beckoning me deeper.

Now, I no longer carry the same thirst for novelty or fear of becoming lost. My footsteps trace pathways that feel somehow preordained; the vines move around me as much as I walk around them. The dryness that once cracked my lips no longer stings. I’ve learned to gather nectar from fresh ruptures in the translucent bulbs, sipping it in small amounts so the creatures that swarm in for the feast do not regard me as an interloper. My cloak—once unremarkable cloth—has been taken over by a creeping lace of moss and minute blooms, so that I appear part traveler, part vine. I run my fingers across the coarse texture and feel that it’s truly become an extension of my own skin.

Some days, I sense I am turning into a caretaker of sorts. I find myself compelled to tend the city’s subtle changes, aiding sprouts and collecting seeds. When a corridor collapses under the tension of new growth, I clear away the broken matter, letting fresh vines knit themselves across the gap. I’ve shaped small basins in the bark where water can pool—be it from condensation or trickling rains—so that amphibians discover new safe havens. Occasionally, the “leaf-feathered” avians circle overhead, eyeing my nonspeaking stewardship with placid acceptance. It’s unclear if they regard me as an equal or an anomaly, but they no longer flee as I pass beneath their perches.

In the heart of this place, I discovered a massive trunk that spiraled upward into an impossible tangle of branches disappearing into an amber haze. Around its base, the living architecture seemed more elaborate, with archways that glowed from within, as if the bark had captured the sunrise itself. Vines the thickness of my own limbs wove together to form a vast, web-like dome. Within that space, the noise of the city rose to a staggering pitch, layered and resonant like crashing waves of sound. Yet at the center of that dome, I found a hush—a pocket of stillness where every echo drew inward, merging into a single note that hummed in my bones.

I sat there for what felt like hours, remaining motionless. The slight quiver of leaves, the intermittent croakings, the thunderous rustles—all coalesced into an ongoing wave that lifted and carried me somewhere beyond thought, beyond my small sense of self. When I finally stood and stepped out of that hush, I realized something in me had changed: I felt a deep connection to the plant-flesh of the city. It was not a matter of ownership or dominion but a resonance. I was aware, in some ineffable way, of the city’s entire shape in time—where new offshoots would emerge, where hollows would open, how the vines planned to reweave themselves next. I could almost feel the presence of those half-plant, half-animal guardians people sometimes whisper about. If I turned quickly enough, I glimpsed a shifting silhouette at the edge of my vision, vines quivering where something had just passed. And though I sensed their watchfulness, I felt neither threatened nor truly alone.

In the odd intervals when silence falls, my awareness grows even sharper. My heart thuds in time with a massive pulse, as if my blood has returned to the roots that birthed me. My limbs tingle, and I grow certain that I, too, have begun sprouting. Not in the literal sense of leaves from my skin—yet I feel an expansion, a branching in my mind that comes alive whenever I touch bark or dip my hand into a nectar pool. With each cycle of hush and roar, I absorb more of the city’s subtle signals: the glimmering dust spores that pass through the slanted light; the ephemeral flickers that might be pockets of another dimension or illusions cast by the geometry of the vines.

So who or what have I become in this place? Perhaps I am a living bridge. I shuttle seeds and spores to new nooks of soil. I brush aside the smaller creatures that would be trampled by the lumbering giants, giving them safe passage. I listen to the city’s countless voices and echo them back—not in perfect mimicry, but in a weaving dance that helps bind the tapestry of sound. My footprints themselves become anchor points where new moss can cling, my sweat feeding microfungi that will help break down fallen stalks. 

Sometimes I wonder if I’ve crossed a threshold into becoming one of those ethereal “in-between” beings—the ones rumored to step through trunk walls in airy whispers. Perhaps if I live here long enough, I will no longer be wholly human. Already my shadow seems more pliant, prone to bending in ways the light alone doesn’t explain. My reflection in the surface of a quiet nectar pool shows swirling patterns creeping across my skin like the labyrinthine grooves of bark. Yet I do not fear this transformation. The entire city is transformation incarnate: each corridor changes shape, each bloom eventually bursts to shower life upon the ground. 

On any given day, you might find me perched on a gently arching root that forms a natural walkway, quietly observing the swirl of living color. Metallic insects flutter, rattling like small coins. Avian forms glide overhead, each creaking wingbeat telling a story of flight. Amphibians chirp from water pockets, their calls dancing among hollow echoes that multiply through trunk chambers. Sometimes the hush returns, and in that fleeting moment, I sense a single, collective inhalation—a readiness for the next great exhalation of growth. I feel it surge through me, and I open my mouth to exhale with the city. 

I do not know how I arrived or what destiny awaits me. But in this realm, I find myself woven into the living tapestry, no longer a mere observer. The city’s heartbeat is in my chest, and in my footprints, new life sprouts. I am caretaker, symbiont, and student all at once. If you were to come upon me, you might call me a wanderer. Yet to me—and perhaps to the vines, the towering beasts, the shimmering insects—I am simply another voice in the complex harmony. I am a leaf on a winding stem, a rung in a living ladder, a note in the endless roar that, when stillness finally comes, merges seamlessly into the hush.

This is where I find myself: in the heart of the labyrinthine, ever-shifting city grown from bark and leaf. And what I am is an evolving expression of it—a being not separate but continuous, an organism bridging plant and animal in the shared breath of this living architecture.
