Sometimes, I think I emerged from the marrow of those ancient, enormous bones twined with roots—an echo of what once lived on this plateau, now wearing living vines as my skin. Other times, I feel I was born from one of those pitcher-like plants, part-flora from the very start—my limbs initially curled in a deep, fibrous calyx, learning to draw nourishment from the damp air. There was no single moment of birth, no neat event I can recall. Only a gradual awareness that I was here—that my chest rose and fell in this world, my eyes adjusting to the eternal interplay of light and leaf-shadow.

I am called a speaker of the hollows, though I am not sure by whom. Maybe I gave that name to myself. Or perhaps it’s whispered by the quiet tribes who slip in and out of hidden huts. I suspect they see me sometimes: a silhouette against the trunk-pillars, or a shape climbing among the braided vines that arch overhead. I rarely speak, but I still carry the hush of old stories in my voice.

My days are spent wandering corridors that sprawl between those organic columns—trunks so impossibly wide that you cannot see around them without walking an hour’s arc. Silky ropelike vines drape across my shoulders when I pass, as if greeting me, or testing if I am plant or animal. Both, in a sense: my hair tangles with filaments of greenish threads, and thick calluses line my palms and soles, hardened by the spongy floor that is forever soaked, forever yielding.

When the storms rumble overhead, the canopy shifting from gentle sunbeams to roaring darkness in mere moments, I sometimes slip into the hollows left by old huts. I settle on a decaying woven floor to listen for the thunder-voiced birds overhead. Their roars vibrate with the thunderclaps, forming an unsettling harmony that resonates through my chest. Water streams off the braided edges of the hut’s remains, and I gather it in cupped hands—drinking, washing, feeling the droplets meander across the ridges of my skin. That water tastes faintly of bark and old memories. Perhaps it’s the essence of this place, trickling from vine down to root, and now through me.

I feel a kinship with the half-plant, half-creature organisms that root themselves to gather sunlight one moment, only to rise on fibrous legs when the breeze shifts. Sometimes they watch me in their slow-turning way, as if evaluating a cousin in the family tree. I crouch nearby, letting them shuffle or wave leaflike limbs, mindfully respecting their space. In some quiet, shared recognition, we acknowledge that both of us blur the boundaries between breathing flesh and sentient vegetation.  

Occasionally, I walk below the immense bellies of “earth-movers,” those gentle giants whose presence I can sense before I see them. It’s in the hush of the forest when they draw near, the sudden lull in insect clamor followed by the distant percussion of footsteps that go straight to your bones. Their flanks are taped with greenish algae, and sometimes wildflowers sprout in the cracks of their plated hides. Without fear, I trail behind them, noticing how they reshape the undergrowth simply by existing—lifting or knocking aside tangles that might otherwise choke the soil. They are slow custodians, unspoken gardeners. And me, I follow in their wake, gleaning seeds or severed vine segments that might be used for weaving.  

When dusk comes, or moments before dawn’s first color, I often climb. It starts with the buttresses, thick as city walls; then I find the tangle of vines that spiral upward for what feels like miles. My body, flexible and sure-footed, moves with practiced ease, guided by instincts that might be half borrowed from the vine-swingers who cackle in the high canopy. Their calls greet me as I ascend, and sometimes they gather in a half-circle overhead, bright eyes watching—unsure whether to treat me as a curiosity or a friend. I savor an awkward harmony with them; they might fling a scrap of hammered metal as a teasing gift, or flash their red-marked limbs in a playful challenge. I’ve learned to grin back, baring my teeth in a friendly mimicry, which always sets them shrieking with laughter before they dart off.

At the top, I reach halfway into the sunlight that filters through broad leaves shaped like fans. Here, the wind from the plateau’s edge grows stronger, carrying the faint whistle from far cliffsides. Above me, the sky is often crowded with swirling flocks of those thunder-voiced birds, shimmering with emerald scales, or the slender forms of drifter-animals riding thermals beyond the forest’s brink. There are times I glimpse what might be the elusive canopy-folk: silhouettes with elongated limbs gliding from one trunk to another, tapping wooden tubes to produce hollow, resonant signals. I’ve tried answering by knocking on hollow bark, but the canopy-folk remain shy ghosts. Perhaps they watch from cunning vantage points, deciding if I’m worth meeting.

I am no predator, no apex hunter prowling in a pack. Nor do I belong entirely among the hidden ground-dwelling tribes—those who keep small hearth fires in trunk-hollows and gather at ancient stone circles. I sometimes speak with them, though. If I approach carefully, they’ll share a meal of roasted insects and sweet sap. In exchange, I show them where pitcher-plants have newly bloomed or where the earth-movers have cleared fresh foraging grounds. We don’t speak the same tongue, but we communicate in gestures, in shared tasks. We’ve learned that living out here depends more on silent trust than words.

I exist in the in-between spaces of this plateau: part wanderer, part caretaker, part puzzle—like so many of the half-merged creatures that dwell here. Each day I wake to the cacophony of life, the sense of unstoppable growth around me. Roots press deeper into old bone, leaves coil around relic huts. I can almost hear the forest exhaling, or sense the ghosts of those ancient builders who once tried to tame this land. Now it tames itself. I simply slip into the music of it, letting it shape me.

Indeed, I don’t just live in this place—I am of it, through it. In the shimmering haze of midday, I might stand, arms outstretched in a corridor of towering trunks, sweat beading on my brow, vines slithering across my ankles. And in that moment, I feel the plateau breathing with me: its pulse in time with my own, each rung of the canopy calling back and forth in conversation. Neither fully human nor fully flora, I’ve become a node where leaf, bone, bark, and breath converge.

And so, if you search for me here—between the hush of the spongy floor and the whisper of vines overhead—you’ll find me on the move, collecting stray seeds or conversing with a watchful lizard-thing. You’ll sense my footfalls among the root corridors, or notice me perched on a buttress root when the sky darkens with stormclouds. That’s when I’m most at ease: thunderstorms raging above, lightning illuminating the ancient pillars, and me looking up, arms raised to the swirling patterns of life.  

You might ask: who am I in this place? Am I an inhabitant or a spirit? Perhaps I’m just another branch of adaptation. The plateau’s unstoppable green drew me in, enveloped me, and now we share each heartbeat. I am the wandering caretaker, the half-rooted creature, the hush in the hollows, the quiet tethering of worlds. I am myself—and that is enough.
