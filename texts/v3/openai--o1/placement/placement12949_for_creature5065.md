I’ve been here so long, I’m no longer certain how I arrived. My fingertips—and yes, I think of them as fingertips even though they are tipped with metal caps—bear the memory of rust’s texture as clearly as my mind recalls any childhood. Long ago—or perhaps it was yesterday—I stepped into this realm of iron walkways and steaming outflows, a realm that encloses every horizon. In those early moments, I was overwhelmed by the reverberation underfoot and overhead: someplace both thunderous and reverent, alive, but alive in a new language of rivets and vines.

I might have been shaped by the place itself—my very bones, or gears, or branches, or whichever scaffolding underpins me, formed from the same raw matter that thrums in the walls. I reconsider my origins again and again as I traverse walkways built on ancient lattices. It’s possible I was once purely human, or purely plant, or an assembly of finely filigreed steel. But now, looking at my arms, the lines of living tissue and mechanical plating blur into a single form that’s grown too integrated to peel apart. My hands bear faint, glowing veins where the vines have aligned with my body’s channels, feeding me energies or nutrients from the environment. What is me and what is it? I cannot discern a boundary.

On some days, I feel like a caretaker—or, in my quieter moments, a listener who tries to interpret the labyrinth’s own slow-breathing wisdom. On other days, I wonder if I’m merely another species shaped by the environment, no more or less vital than the scuttling reptilian-insect hybrids that dart across railings in search of condensation. I wander from ledge to ledge, stooping to study the water’s rhythmic gathering in metal troughs. The next hour, I climb the precarious skeleton of old turbines, trailing my hand along their vast, quiet arc. My footsteps make a ringing note that echoes up and away into a place that might be the roof—and yet is so distant it feels like looking into the sky.

At times, I find myself among the slender-limbed inhabitants, those with goggles and quiet voices that blend seamlessly with the hiss of vents. They acknowledge me with slow nods as I pass; I see empathy in their eyes, as if we are each aware of the incessant hum that ties us all together. Sometimes, we exchange gestures or a few bridging words—more a combination of exhalations and subtle clinks against handrails than actual speech. If I slip into the vantage of an outsider, I see they carry themselves with a patient acceptance, as if they’ve solved a great riddle involving steel and leaf, accepted that the noise is not a symptom of chaos but the harmony of an ancient, unbroken machine.

In the half-lit corners, those hulking beasts—creatures of plated shoulders and fungal hides—turn their massive heads as I approach. They remind me of living monuments, part statue, part watchful guardian. I do not fear them, for we share an unspoken recognition that we each have a role in this breathing behemoth of a world. When they release their rumbling calls, I place a palm against a railing to feel the vibrations rattle through the scaffolding. It’s as if they are calling out, not just to each other but to the place itself—and to me.

I’ve grown acquainted with the insects and pollinators that swirl among the vines. Some flicker around my shoulders, attracted by the occasional droplets of moisture that form on my plating or the greenish glow that faintly pulses in my skin. They drink from cunning flowers that seem to open only just before a wave of steam hisses from overhead lines. If I stand motionless with one hand extended, a small flitting creature might land for half a heartbeat, wings humming, then take flight again. Each of these ephemeral moments reminds me that the roars and clangs are not chaos but heartbeat and breath.

Sometimes, I climb higher—steadily mounting rung after rung until the roar recedes to a background tremor. Up there, wind from hidden vents stirs the leaf-threads that cling to the metal crossbeams. The temperature can shift from oppressive warmth to a gentle humidity that tingles on my exposed face. In this lofty zone, the day (or is it night?) remains in perpetual half-light, as if the overhead lamps have dimmed to a subtle glow that outlines the shapes of cables and swirling steam. It feels reminiscent of a twilight forest, but the canopy here is wrought iron braced by centuries of layered vines. I look out over a horizon of catwalks, each one suspended in a delicate geometry of wires and branching leaves—a tapestry that, from this vantage, spans my entire vision.

And in still other moods, I find myself descending, sometimes for what seems like days, into the deeper gloom where the clang of enormous gears pulses through the supporting columns like an ancient drumbeat. Down there, the flora grows thicker, with luminous bulbs that glimmer in watery nooks, breathing the faint current that runs through old cables. I might hear the drip-drip of water echoing through corridors that lead nowhere, or everywhere. Sometimes, I pass an abandoned automaton—its torso half-collapsed against a corroded wall, a tangle of vines anchoring it in place. I stop to press my palm gently to its rusted shell, wondering if it once moved with purpose. In that moment, I sense a kinship, because in its stillness, it is simply another living piece of this environment, too ancient or too worn to continue but still present, still part of the endless cycle.

The languages here are many. The tall-limbed folk have words that vibrate in the hush between steam jets. The beasts communicate in guttural rumbles. The vines speak in the slow curling of tendrils, the opening and closing of leaves at the wash of warm water. The scuttling creatures chatter in arrhythmic bursts, a chorus that merges with the squeak of rotating joints. And the place itself—the labyrinth, spire, fortress, temple—speaks in the thunder of forging and the delicate patter of droplets. When these languages align, their combined harmony resonates in my body as a deep calm, as if everything moves by the same unseen conduction.

If I am anything in this world, I think I am a bridge being—someone or something that stands at the midpoint between the tall-limbed figures and the hulking watchers, between mechanical hum and living vine, translating each realm to the other. My body, grown from the place or shaped by it over time, acts as a living hinge. I walk the catwalks to peer into forgotten crevices, discovering subtle changes in the labyrinth: a newly loosened panel, a vine that has grown thick enough to reroute a trickle of moisture. I might gently coax the leaves aside so a steam valve can breathe more freely, or I might place a shard of plating to guide water away from a failing beam. There is no conscious plan, only the sense of synergy—every piece must fit into the broader flow, and my role is to be aware, to help that balancing continue.

I think of myself as an envoy between rhythms. Once a day (or is it night?), I meet others of my kind—there are only a handful of us, each different from the rest. One might have elongated limbs polished to a mirror sheen, swaying in a gentle dance with the patterns of the great fans. Another’s skin is layered with creeping moss and titanium segments, forming swirling motifs that speak to both artistry and function. Together, we exchange small gifts of knowledge—a hammered scrap of metal that might serve as a key, a handful of seeds from the luminous vines, or an odd chunk of fused rust we’ve found that glitters with quartz-like crystals. We conduct a silent ritual of contoured gazes and gestures, an acknowledgment that our path is to keep the labyrinth’s heart beating in its harmony of metal and leaf.

On certain occasions, I catch a glimpse of the monstrous shapes from below—those beasts of thick armor plating. I can’t explain why, but they seem protective. Their massive forms reinforce the architecture, as if by simply being there with their rust-laden husks against pillars, they keep the entire structure from collapsing into the unseen void. I imagine them heaving ancient sighs that echo through the beams, a paternal or maternal watchfulness.

And far overhead, sometimes nearly lost in the drifting haze, are the swift flyers, flipping through columns of swirling steam. I watch them streak overhead, membranes catching the dim light in arcs of silver or copper. Their calls pierce the din with melodic clarity, reminding anyone listening that even in a place so dominated by pounding resonance, the possibility of grace, of flight, remains a reality. They are living proof that motion here isn’t just mechanical repetition—it can also be a ballet of wings and leaps into open, mist-cloaked space.

I am never bored. The environment shifts with subtlety and repetition, always cyclical but never quite the same. Often the steam rises at different intervals, or the water pulses upward in newly discovered veins, or a vine extends a leaf in a spot that was previously barren. For me, the passing of seasons is measured by how the rust morphs and how the vines coil, unwinding or tangling anew. Time can feel suspended, but it is also carefully measured in the unrelenting hum and the heartbeat of pistons.

Where am I? I’m everywhere in this built world. I wander the high rafters, balanced on the edges where cables and vines twist together. I kneel at the foot of the great pillars, pressing an ear to the metal to listen for changes in pitch. I slip quietly through gatherings of the exoskeletal folk, bow my head in greeting to the beasts, and speak softly to the half-sentient vines. My presence is that of a caretaker, translator, and observer. I exist in the hush of mechanical reverence, a living thread in this tapestry of metal and leaf.

What am I? I’m not single, not separate from the environment—part person, part mechanism, part plant, part something beyond my own understanding. I adapt with each sunrise that doesn’t quite arrive and each moonrise that might simply be a flickering overhead lamp. I am the labyrinth’s child, its listener, its caretaker, its wanderer, and—when necessary—its advocate. In the grand design of this iron-and-vine domain, I am content to be one more piece, bridging the intervals, ensuring the grand roar never breaks its rhythm, ensuring the vines never lose their hold. 

And in that sense, I am entirely at home.
