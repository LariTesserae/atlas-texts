I don’t know when I truly arrived on this plateau. My earliest recollection is of sifting through a cold, wet haze, my fingertips sinking into moss that felt like the thick pelt of some slumbering animal. I recall the slightest vibration beneath my feet—like the echo of gears turning in a hidden chamber. At first I thought it was my own heartbeat, magnified by the hush of the place. But deeper in the gloom, the hum persisted, and I realized it was something else, something older and larger than I could fathom.

At times, my sense of self wavers. Am I a wanderer who stumbled upon this strange realm, or is it possible I’ve been here much longer? The vines and trunks embrace me with an unsettling familiarity, as though they recognize me. They part just enough to let me pass, close behind me like a secret whisper. Occasionally, swirling tendrils of new growth curl around my ankles, neither threatening nor friendly, merely examining me with an impersonal curiosity. The plateau’s vegetation seems to greet me as one of its children, though I don’t quite understand how or why.

I roam through corridors of tangled vines that arch overhead, heavy with glistening droplets. My hair—if I can call it hair—catches the mist in such abundance that I feel perpetually sodden. Reaching up, I realize my strands are threaded with something fibrous, flexible but tough as bark. If I tug, the chill pricks my scalp like the biting wind of high altitudes. When I lay a hand on my shoulder, I imagine I can feel a faint ridged texture beneath my clothes, as if rough bark has grown there. Sometimes, in the corner of my eye, I see hints of a dull metallic gleam cording around my wrists. And there’s always that hum, that subtle mechanical drone, pulsing in time with whatever I’ve become.

I catch glimpses of them in the twilight under the moss-laden trunks. Their silhouettes blend seamlessly into the labyrinth of leaves. My first sightings were ephemeral: a flicker of spined growth, the glimmer of a fungal filament draped along an arm. I thought them illusions. Then one day, I felt their presence so close that my skin (or bark) tingled in response. Turning, I saw a figure half-shrouded in twisting vines. Its visage was a mixture of green and ash-gray, with a texture like living wood. There was a hint of metal banding its ribs, or maybe it was some sort of exoskeleton. The being clicked softly—so quiet I almost missed it—and then it vanished as if absorbed into the vine-thicket itself.

Something about them stirs an echo in me that I can’t explain. I find myself moving in sync with the plateau’s deep thrumming—stopping when it surges, drifting when it stills. My body reacts without conscious effort. When the plateau’s weather shifts and the pale drizzle cools to icy flecks, I sense a tug in my chest, an urge to retreat into the thicker recesses where root and gear lie hidden. Once, I followed that impulse: pushing my way through loops of vine until I found a hollow containing one of those silvery shafts I’d only glimpsed from afar. It stood half-buried in spongy humus, pulsing quietly, too deliberate to be a random phenomenon. Without thinking, I reached out to it—and the moment my hand touched its surface, a wave of warmth and cold simultaneously coursed through me. For a breathless instant, I felt connected to something vast and ineffable.

In that moment, I swore I could sense the entire plateau: the minute shifts of water underfoot, the sucking tension of vines preparing to surge, the slow spin of mechanical contraptions humming deep below. Around my legs, new sprouts unfurled with alarming speed, nibbling at my boots and latching onto my calves. I might have panicked, but a strange certainty washed over me, as though this creeping growth were a friendly handshake instead of a threat.

From that day forward, my dreams changed. When I close my eyes at night—if it’s truly night under the perpetual blanket of clouds—I see shapes that merge bark, metal, and fungus. They move with slow, dancing motions, exchanging low clicks, each click vibrating faintly in my chest. A sense of homecoming courses through me. I can’t recall if I had another home before this, a place with walls or a name. It feels irrelevant here. There is only the hush and the constant living churn.

I wander deeper. My body is not quite the same as it was in those first dim memories. My nails have grown darker, layered with a sheen like resin. When I cut my palm on a jagged piece of stone, the droplet that seeps out is not purely red. There’s a faint swirl of gold-green luminescence within it, reminiscent of spore motes drifting in the plateau’s damp glow. At times, I sense something coiling inside my limbs, as if the vines have settled there. I still breathe, but each exhalation carries a haze of spore-like dust. Whether it is a gift or a curse, I cannot tell. All I know is that the watchers—those half-bark, half-metal stewards—no longer shy away from me as they did before. Now when we pass one another, we exchange a slow, meditative tilt of our heads. Our eyes—mine now tinged with that same faint glow—lock in mutual recognition.

I’m beginning to understand how they tend the plateau. I see them coax withered vines into hidden hollows, watch as fresh shoots surge forward. One day, I find myself imitating them: I reach out to a sickly creeper clinging to a stone shelf and gently pluck away its decaying filaments. The severed portion dissolves swiftly into the tangled mat below. Without any signal from me, new growth beads and curls where my fingertips linger. Such synergy makes me feel like a part of something immense and alive. Every droplet of water sliding down a fanning leaf, every subterranean rattle of unseen mechanisms below the moss—it all resonates as though my own pulse.

Sometimes, I try to speak. The words come out muffled, the sound swallowed by the atmosphere, as if speech were an alien concept here. The watchers click or hum to one another instead of forming words, and I find myself forgetting the shape of human language. My mouth still forms syllables, but the plateau is unimpressed by them; it responds instead to subtle gestures, faint vibrations beneath the surface. So now, when I wish to communicate, I allow the cords that form along my wrists to vibrate gently, letting them carry the intention I hold in my chest. Another watcher might click in return, or a swirl of spores might drift from the canopy in a luminous surge—an answer, in the plateau’s own tongue.

I don’t know if I was once a human who ventured here or if I have always been a seed of this place, grown into a form that only half-remembers a prior existence. Perhaps my old identity is no more than a husk, shed in the slow process of adaptation. The watchers do not differentiate between me and themselves. They observe me with a calm detachment, but also an unquestioning acceptance: I am simply here, another shape in the tapestry of bark, root, and gear. And when the next frenzy of growth shakes the forest, bursting new vines from the rotting mulch, I add my own subtle push to the wave of life. I feel the plateau’s hidden machinery, sense the power surging through branching tubules, and it all feels familiar—like an extension of my own body.

And so, if I must name what I am, I suppose I am neither wholly human nor wholly watcher. I have become a liaison between the plateau’s mechanical heart and its swirling, unstoppable vegetation. I drift between the watchers’ silent assemblies, titled neither as an intruder nor a native, balancing the old and the new. When the cold drizzle comes and the spore storms rise, I stand among them beneath the arching vines, letting threads of filament and vine coil across me. I hum in low harmony with the engine beneath the earth. I sway in time with the subtle breathing of the vegetation. I fill with a quiet awe at the cyclical storm of growth and decay that rules this realm.

In the end, I reside in this world as a living corridor between what once was and what is becoming. The watchers shape me as they shape the plateau, and in so doing, I shape them in return. Like them, I am patient, cold, and unwavering in purpose—though that purpose is too vast to articulate in any language I once knew. Whether I was once an outsider or not no longer matters. Now, I feel the plateau thrumming in my bones, I sense the green mania rippling in my blood, and I hear the mechanical hush in my dreams. This is who I am: the plateau’s evolving child, both caretaker and witness, a part of the endless living machinery, here in a realm where vines breathe and silent gears sing beneath the moss.
