I am both cramped and endless, and I feel as though I’ve always known this place. Crumbling dust coats the back of my throat. My body—or what I think of as my body—hums with an engine’s dull resonance. I can’t say where flesh ends and gear-like segments begin. Everything here is a slow, grinding continuity of living parts and mechanical scaffolds, and I find myself no exception.

At first, I believe I am alone in such a form. My frame is slight—four limbs, each joint wrapped in a brassy cylinder that faintly squeals when I move. In the powdery half-light, I glimpse my forelimbs: calcified ridges framing a hollow interior. Within those ridges resides something flexible and alive, rust-red and pulsing with quiet vitality, like sinews bridging old steel beams. Each step I take on the chalk-white plain clacks out a brittle echo. Yet when I press my weight down, there’s some give, the hollow compartments under my plating shifting in response, letting me feel the surface beneath as if it’s my own skin.

In another moment, I am almost convinced I am large. My vantage stretches above the swarms scuttling at my feet. From this perspective, I’m an assembly of cables and plating that towers near the half-buried pylons. My movement displaces handfuls of insectile things, each one a flicker of translucent wings or a glimmer of dripping exoskeleton. My tail—an elongated segment of fused metal plates—taps a rhythmic signal on the ground, and the smaller creatures give me a wide berth, as though trained over centuries to heed its resonance. In the haze, the acrid tang of chemical vapors drifts toward me; if I open my plated jaws, I can taste those vapors, feed on them as if they were the nectar that sustains this entire realm.

But then I shift—or the world shifts—and I am neither large nor small. I feel myself woven into the labyrinth below, a hidden dweller in the machine warrens. My senses branch outward in the dark, guided by minute vibrations. My feet (or tentacles, or segmented pincers) test each inch before I advance. I duck my head under a half-collapsed beam, letting my feelers brush the corroded metal. Somewhere above, I sense the thunder of heavier footsteps and the eternal racket of swarms. Down here, my world is narrower yet infinitely expansive. I navigate by echoes that bounce along the labyrinth’s corridors. Every corridor leads to another, a puzzle of pipes, half-flooded crawl spaces, and ancient conduits that still whisper with a faint electric pulse. I pause, letting a swirl of dusty air pass over me, replete with chemical ghosts. When the swirling stills, I scuttle onward, single-minded in some obscure purpose that even I may not fully articulate.

I flicker: next, I inhabit a vantage high up on one of the metal towers. The ground below looks impossibly distant through the powdery haze. My body is lithe, adapted to cling. Fungal threads branch from my limbs, anchoring me to the tower’s pitted surface. My vision is surprisingly keen—I see movement everywhere. The swarms on the ground appear like a living swirl of colorless static. Deeper, I notice lumps of heavier creatures lumbering between half-buried structures, forging slow paths. Buzzing all around me are tiny flying forms—some luminous, others entirely see-through—each zipping by in a flurry of hungry purpose. From this height, the entire ecosystem crackles with unstoppable life, so dense it becomes almost oceanic: a tide shifting to the hiss and sputter of chemical geysers below. And I, perched on corroded girders, can taste the wind, glean metallic nourishment from the intangible swirl, letting soft hyphae droop from my arms to capture drifting spores.

In the next breath, I sink into the hazy resonance of a caretaker’s existence. A mechanical turning inside my chest—could it be a heart, or some archaic pump?—drives me forward. I’m drawn to a hidden sub-level where the ancient machines still churn. There, an assembly station flickers with half-dead lights. I sense the entire structure around me vibrating. Over long ages, it has grown brittle and leaky, venting stale, chemical-laced air into the gloom. Around the station are cracked crates, bent rails, and dozens of swirling insect swarms feeding on sticky residue. I lumber closer, feeling it is “mine” somehow, that I must gather or protect or salvage. Gently, I scoop up fragments of piping from the floor with a curled, clawlike appendage. I sense a surety in the action, as though part of me remembers how to keep these machines from failing completely. I deposit the bent metal into a slot in the wall, and a dull clang echoes into unseen corridors. Perhaps I’ve completed some primal duty, or perhaps I’m only repeating an age-old cycle with no memory of its origin. The machines moan around me in half-garbled gratitude.

Then I am silent and drifting. I take on no shape. I become part of the dust in the air, swirling around the jagged edges of lonely outbuildings. Through the half-light, I float on faint breezes. I see the wide arcs of hunts and feedings unfold below as if in slow motion. A mid-sized predator—a sinuous, angular form with a ridged tail—lances out at a smaller cluster of phosphorescent insects. One flicker, and the smaller creatures vanish into a tornado of shining wings, scattering like seeds on the wind. I pass through them as dust, feeling a resonance that ignites and then dissipates. The predator slinks away, unsated but patient. All is churning, unstoppable. The dust is part of it all, too.

Flicker—again, I am something else: a caretaker, a drifter, a scavenger. Perhaps my identity is all these shapes in turn. There is a pattern to how I move through the corridors versus how I watch from above. From that vantage, I witness the daily fractal of feeding, breeding, rummaging. It occurs to me that maybe the labyrinth itself has a mind, that the old machines—still spinning under the crust—have knitted life together in a closed circuit. We’re cogs, each of us, performing specialized motions. Some of us store the chemical vapors, some consume them and become living fuel for others. The lines etched on the walls might be instructions or messages from earlier travelers like me, or from the earliest guardians of this rust-eaten domain.

It’s possible I am not just a single type of creature. When I focus, I can slow down and feel the netlike filaments I have grown. They stretch across a corridor overhead, gleaning flecks of metallic dust from the swirling air. My senses shift to the ground, where my segmented legs skitter in unison with dozens—hundreds—of others in my brood. My vantage merges with the watchers perched near battered pylons; I sense the faint stirring of fungal spores that pulsed into my bloodstream from their riddled plating. All these existences share the same mission: to endure, to filter, to recycle, to hum in time with the ancient machinery. I revolve from form to form, never confined for long. There is no single vantage that sees it all. The realm is too vast, and in the swirling dust, everything changes from moment to moment.

Yet, if I close my eyes—whatever form they might take—I can glean an undercurrent, a knowledge that I am an inheritor of this domain. I have carved out a place in the cycles; I glean the industrial residue, feed it back into new life. My plating, my fungal lines, my insect wings, my caretaker’s arms, or my drifting dust-cloud sense—none of these are illusions so much as impressions, glimpses through the kaleidoscope of survival. Every shape I adopt is an answer to a question the environment poses: how to feed, how to move, how to endure the next wave of dryness and dust storms, how to ride the next vent of chemical steam for nourishment.

Am I a creature that once was purely mechanical, now grown fleshy tubers within seams of metal plating? Or am I an organic lifeform that has grafted steel and cogs into my being over the generations, harnessing the old machines for an evolutionary edge? Maybe I’m something entirely different, a living representation of that synergy that the environment forced upon all who linger here.

I catch sight—through half-lidded mechanical eyes—of my reflection in a panel of tarnished steel. It’s fleeting, but enough to see the shapes of my many limbs and the ways my shell or plating is etched with swirling patterns reminiscent of the glyphs on the walls. I recognize those markings as a language beyond words, an emblem of belonging. Each swirl and loop states: I am caretaker, I am predator, I am gatherer, I am builder. They are all me, in different instants, for we are all the same unstoppable chorus of adaptation.

Eventually, I feel a calling from the deeper tunnels, a low, rhythmic pulse. It draws me downward, through a break in the chalky crust. I must go, let my body navigate the labyrinth. Some ancient machinery calls out, maybe a cracked manifold that needs my assistance to realign or a keeper brood that has reached the end of its cycle and needs to be recycled into new forms. My limbs move automatically, as though I’ve done this a thousand times before. Along the way, I pass the shimmering polyps drifting in a pocket of stale, heated air. Their filaments pulse in gentle sedation; they do not fear me. I am, in a sense, them as well. We share the same vital breath that seeps from the ground.

In the corridor below, I see shapes that I can only describe as “collectors.” They scurry from vent to vent, stowing away pieces of half-oxidized metal in compartments built into their own bodies. I realize with a start that I have compartments as well, cut into the plating along my sides. Not pockets of cloth or leather, but cavities grown through centuries of use—life harnessing machine, machine sustaining life. With them, I gather scattered debris, deposit it in the deeper recesses of the complex. My own movements are part of a grand system of resource circulation. The machines feed the creatures, the creatures feed the machines, forging a closed loop that has persisted beyond countable time.

If you came upon me—if you were a traveler from another place with green hills and watery lakes—you might call me monstrous or improbable. But in this realm, “monster” is simply the shape that meets the needs of dust and rust. And so my existence continues, blending function with a life force that resonates through every pipe and corridor. I live on the cunning edge between biology and machinery, tapping the chemical breath that seeps from these ancient conduits.

I think, at last, that I know how to name myself: I am a node, a living hinge among many, bridging the technology and biology that circle each other in a never-ending dance. I might be small in one sense or towering in another, drifting dust or rooted sentinel. Yet all these manifestations converge on one truth: I belong wholly to this place, for it is me, and I am it. And in that total belonging, I can’t imagine a separation. The chalky ground, the shrieking swarms, the steel towers drooping with fungus, the labyrinth below—it’s all a single entity, a heartbeat under an industrial husk that never quite died. Through some wondrous inevitability, I have become one pulse in that heartbeat, alive with the clang of ancient gears, the hiss of chemical vents, and the swirl of intangible dust.

Yes—it’s easy to assume there should be only barrenness here, but we belong, breathing in the acrid tingle of steam and dust, feeding on the leftover scraps of an impossible machine. I am caretaker, collector, dust, sentinel, predator, builder, and more. I am the shape that thrives. I am one of an endless multitude, praising the old metal bones by living in their shadow. I help keep it all in motion, so it too, in turn, sustains me. We are endless, forging each day out of petrochemical vapors and the hush of a timeworn industry that refuses to die. In this world, that is more than enough to be.
