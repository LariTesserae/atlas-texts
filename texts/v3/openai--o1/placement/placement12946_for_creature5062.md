I begin as a faint awareness fluttering in cold suspension. I neither see nor hear in the conventional sense—rather, I feel the water’s movements carry me along, each pulse and shift a subtle conversation on the skin. I’m in no hurry to define myself. At first, I am just another drifting speck in the dim, slate-gray hush, no more remarkable than any of the grains of silt that roll in the water’s ever-shifting currents.

It occurs to me I might once have been something else…a stone, perhaps, worn down through millennia. I recall the sensation of pressure, gradual and consistent, scouring away edges to leave a smooth, rounded surface. Even that recollection flickers uncertainly—like a half-formed dream that stirs upon waking—until it, too, yields to the present hush of the deep. Or maybe I have always been water, a drop among countless droplets joined in an endless, roiling tide. In such darkness, it’s easy for identity to blur.

But as I let the gloom cradle me, I sense a slow coalescing of consciousness. I feel ridges along my sides—soft protrusions. A shift in my torso. I realize I possess length, a meandering spine that coils and extends with serpentine elegance. My skin—translucent, tinged a pale, ghostly silver—seems alive in the faint remnants of drifting light. When a swirl of sediment passes under me, tiny disturbances in the current brush my flanks, and I become aware of a cluster of extremely sensitive pores that track these minute ripples. There’s a deep hush, layered over by a roar so muffled you don’t so much hear it as feel it pressing against you. My body is made for this murky realm.

Slowly, I rouse my fins—long, ribbon-like forms extending along my back, rippling in languid waves. I move through the water without hurry, fluid and patient, letting the current nudge me between crumbling remnants of ancient basalt. For eons, these stony shapes towered tall. Now they are worn lumps and pillars flattened by the same watery kingdom that surrounds me. And I fit here, sliding into the gaps, weaving around half-buried columns like a wisp of living silt. My world is the labyrinth of softened edges and pockets of darkness, each swirl of debris a potential clue—food drifting close or another creature passing by.

In a moment of curiosity, I slip closer to the seabed. I can feel the texture of stone against my delicate underbelly, pitted with watery grooves. Small crustaceans scatter as I approach, their spindly legs flicking up clouds of silt that swirl around me in a dusty haze. I watch as they hunker behind broken shards of rock, translucent shells just visible in the gloom. Perhaps I used to be one of them—skittering, cautious, constantly brushing antennae over the substrate for bits of sustenance. In this place, the boundary between “I was” and “I am” erodes as surely as the basalt. The hush is so ancient that all memory becomes layered, compressed, then scattered again.

As I advance at my unhurried pace, the water thickens with whorls of particulate matter. From a rift in the rubble overhead, a faint swirling current arrives. Within it are drifting polyps—gossamer jellies lighting up dimly, like distant embers. Their pulses of bioluminescence cause ephemeral glitters along my skin. I find myself momentarily entranced by their dance, almost lulled by the rhythmic open-and-shut of their bells. They cling to the water as if gravity were a rumor, letting subtle waves of current pass through their trailing tendrils. In a spontaneous moment, I wonder if I have merged with them before—if I’ve been a fragile orb drifting in watery gloom.

Time here has no edges; it’s fluid as the tides and just as ancient. A single breath might last a century, or perhaps only a moment. The hush gnaws at the concept of hours, flattening entire millennia under its weight until they’re as unremarkable as a handful of silt. Perhaps that is how I came to be as I am now—by adopting an existence that flows slowly, conserving every calorie, every particle of motion. This is how existence must be, if one is to endure.

In a new stirring of currents, I slip around a fallen plateau of basalt and discover a deeper pocket within the rubble. It’s a hollow corridor where the overhead stone has collapsed onto the seafloor just enough to create a narrow path. My body weaves into that corridor, and I pause. There’s a sudden prickle along my skin, that subtle shift in pressure and temperature that hints at possibility. Within this crevice, I sense life that is old, older than my body can articulate but somehow familiar in the hush. A cluster of filter-feeding worms—they’ve built slender chimneys from sand grains fused with their secretions. Their crowns, like feathery fans, swirl out in the current, gathering drifting nourishment. I watch them in rapt stillness. There’s a sense of timeless communal existence here—beings that predate speech, predate architecture, thriving as a single organism might, with each worm a specialized part of the environment’s living tapestry.

I flick my ribbon-fins once, softly, to shift position. Then something else calls my attention: a deeper gloom within the corridor stirs, and out slides a fellow eel-like shape. Its pale head arcs upward, revealing glints of opaque, vestigial eyes. We lock into a silent vigil—two watchers in the dark, measuring each other by the subtle tastes of chemical signals in the water. For a moment, I sense an entire conversation without words. We share the hush and the need to survive, share the watery labyrinth as home and fortress both. Then, as though an unspoken agreement is reached, we part ways. That other shape retreats slowly, withdrawing as a wave of silt drifts in to fill the tunnel behind it.

I move on, drifting upward. My mind wanders: Could I be a crustacean next? I imagine growing a translucent shell, my spindly legs picking carefully across basalt ridges. Or maybe I become that polyp, letting the current carry me like a lost filament of dream. I can almost feel the shift—the water swirling, my thoughts dissolving into a simpler consciousness that pulses with each gentle contraction of a jelly bell. All forms here have a quiet kinship, a sense that we share more than water. We share the same slow heartbeat of existence in a place where no day dawns.

Eventually, I find myself near the top of a broken column, resting in the ephemeral hush. A swirl of silt passes overhead, reminiscent of a drifting nebula. Freed for a moment from the pull of the currents, I realize: I am both observer and inhabitant. I am the eel-like creature, yes, but I am also the swirling water, the worn basalt, the drifting polyp. I wear every shape in flickers of possibility, for this realm is too vast and too timeless to allow any single identity to remain rigid for long. Everything is in slow, perpetual motion, details lost and reformed over outstretched epochs.

I linger in that revelation. The basalt wears smooth, the silt shifts in unsettled tides, and the hush devours all echoes. I sense the presence of countless other beings—some just out of sight, some wholly beyond my comprehension. This domain is not lonely; it’s filled with presences so subtle that you must become equally subtle to perceive them. It’s alive in a way that no bustling reef or bright sunlit shallows could emulate. Here, life is refined down to its essential elements: the hush, the cold, and persistent motion.

Where do I find myself in this submerged world? I find myself everywhere and nowhere, a fluid inhabitant of the labyrinth. I am ephemeral, a slow dancer in diffuse twilight. If I must choose a single identity, I’ll say I am the one who waits, coiled gently in the dark, letting the water bring what it may. I breathe in the next tide of shifting silt, a swirl of planktonic morsels, or a passing shape that never stays long. I belong, thoroughly and quietly, to this hush of ancient brine.

Perhaps in the next breath, I will become something else altogether—a drifting shell, a ghostly polyp, or a shellback crustacean scuttling for scraps. But for now, in this moment, I am the pale, winding sentinel resting in a realm where time itself is an unsettled current. The tracery of phosphorescence dots my skin like shy stars in an undersea firmament. The basalt, the water, the silt, and I all share the same ancient hush, enduring in slow transformation. Here, identity is as fluid as the shifting tides—a fleeting shape in a dark, cold cosmos of half-remembered beginnings and endless, whispering changes.
