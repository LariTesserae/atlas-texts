I can’t say for certain when the thought first occurred to me that I was not merely passing through this place but that it and I might be made of the same stuff. At first, I believed I was just another wanderer—some ragged figure stumbling under the weight of dry heat and darkness. After all, I could still feel my own limbs, my heartbeat fluttering in my chest, the rasp of air through lungs that seemed to despise the scorching dryness. In the moment, that was enough to keep me tethered to an idea of “myself” as separate from what loomed around me.

Yet each step—no matter how tentative—felt strangely intimate with the squelch of fibrous root floors and the tang of metal dust that stung my throat. It was as though the place recognized me. Or perhaps I recognized it. Hard to say which. Because around me stretched that living-industrial labyrinth, all but silent, but for the groans of metal torsion and the subtle, continuous shifting of vines that never slept. They formed passages that pulled me forward, deeper…and deeper still.

Sometimes I pictured myself as a trespasser, but there existed no sense of alarm or intrusion from the labyrinth. No mechanized guardians, no scuttling watchers that might chase me away. Instead, the vines parted almost amiably, allowing me to creep along the ragged walkways as if they knew me well. It was in that hush—punctuated by the faint rasp of leaves sliding over corroded steel—that my mind began to wander into possibilities.

Was I a pilgrim? Was I simply a foolish explorer, searching for some relic at the labyrinth’s heart? I’d read tales of dying worlds where water had fled, leaving behind only this brand of scorching dryness. People told stories of metal cities overtaken by monstrous vegetation—mine shafts turned fungal cathedrals, nuclear plants incarnadined by spore-laced creepers. Possibly I’d come here seeking the truth behind those legends.

Or had I never lived outside of this place at all? Perhaps I’d been born in the labyrinth’s womb—one of the lumps of budding growth that sprout from bark-metal, a living node that took a vaguely humanoid shape. The thought lodged itself in my mind like a seed that insisted on germinating: maybe I was not a separate entity so much as a swirl of root and steel in a shape that approximated a body, grown inside a corridor that reconfigured just enough to let me out.

The deeper in I went, the more that notion shimmered at the edges of my thoughts. Because in the darkness, in the dryness that scorched my lips, I felt no thirst. My eyes still sought a hint of water out of habit—some reflex from an older self—but I found none, and I did not die. I grew neither weak nor desperate. Instead, I only grew more aware of the swirling currents of heat that pulsed through the corridors, as though they were my oxygen. Each new corridor, or bridging scaffold of bark-laced metal, felt suffused with that vital warmth, and I subsisted on it as naturally as though I’d always known how.

I tried to recall a life before this place. At times, I conjured images—plains of grass under an open, watery sky, or oceans reflecting the sun. But those memories felt suspiciously thin, as if gleaned from an inherited dream rather than something I had experienced. My presence here felt far more tangible. Breathing this hot, mineral-laden air. Brushing my hand across the petrified skin of these fused vines. It was as though my fingers recognized the texture, and in that recognition, the corridor would subtly change—leaves tilting or thick cables shifting overhead.

I noticed too that the shadows themselves were not uniform. In some corners, the gloom was saturated with a flickering pulse, reminiscent of dull red embers. Other corridors quivered with a barely perceptible greenish sheen, like bio-luminescent moss seen through tinted glass. Always, I found these glimmers at the edges of my vision, as though they were beckoning me to step closer. And whenever I did, the labyrinth parted just enough to accommodate me, adjusting columns and overhanging leaves precisely so I could pass.

Surely, if I were an outsider, I thought, the labyrinth would have shown more resistance. Why throw open its membranes and reveal its deeper recesses to a stranger? Yet it did, revealing sliding panels of bark-metal that hissed open, and twisting catwalks that reconfigured as if someone behind the scenes was orchestrating the route. Or “something” behind the scenes. Because there was no single entity—no central control tower or watchful eye. That much was obvious. The labyrinth breathed, but no single pair of lungs claimed it. Instead, there was a shared intelligence, a vast tapestry of vine-circuit cables humming with unity.

Then came the moment when I began to suspect that this place heard my thoughts. It was subtle. I remember standing at the edge of what looked like a collapsed platform, the darkness extending far below, no handrails, no scaffolding, only a drop into blackness. I half wished for a bridge or a root-ladder to appear. And it did—so quietly I almost overlooked it. One by one, the vines slithered into a braided span, forming that rope-like crossing from the far side. By the time I realized what was happening, the bridge was complete. And so I stepped onto it with a sense of inevitability, not surprise.

That was when I felt the first stirring inside me—as if something was reconfiguring behind my ribs. In the hush, in the dryness, a crackling hum spread through my torso, from my fingertips to my throat and down into my legs. It was the same tone the labyrinth made when vines shifted across riveted beams. As the hum faded, I found that I was not simply crossing the bridge; I was the bridge. Or at least, I experienced a resonance as if my body were an extension of the vine’s woven cables. If I closed my eyes and let my senses expand, I could feel that gentle tension of the cords, the friction of metal and bark, the rummaging chemical warmth from below.

When I opened my eyes again, I saw from a new perspective. The corridor behind me was not just a place I had traveled, but something that continued to exist within me. In a dreamlike half-vision, I sensed or recalled the moment the labyrinth had grown over centuries, forging that very route from a tangle of industrial piping. With each step forward, I was losing the boundary between “my memories” and “the labyrinth’s memories.” They were merging slowly, like braided filaments. And it felt neither frightening nor comforting—just inevitable.

Now, when I paused to place a hand on a thick trunk of bark-metal, I sometimes sensed that trunk as an extension of my own skeleton. If I thought about it too hard, the illusion wavered. But if I relaxed—if I let that slow, thrumming heat flow—I felt the labyrinth’s shape as a colossal body in which I was merely one nerve cluster. Some days, as I continued, I thought I could simply lie down and let the vines reabsorb me, reconstitute me into the floor. But something else compelled me onward, deeper and deeper, as if seeking the labyrinth’s origin point.

So I journeyed as a traveler still—perhaps a traveler who had begun the transformation from visitor to inhabitant. I began to see how the vines expanded, how leaves reinforced the overhead arches: an immeasurably slow dance of mechanical creaks and fibrous hiss, all orchestrated by an intelligence that was at once deeply alien and utterly familiar. For I was of it or becoming of it.

Time after time, I encountered sections of the labyrinth that were newly formed and still glistening, not with water, but with the slick sheen of half-liquid metal deposits. I felt a strong urge to rest my palms there, as though to bless or consecrate these infant structures. When I did so, I recognized a swirl of half-articulate impulses—like coded instructions or proto-thoughts—that told the vine how to shape itself, which direction to coil, how to siphon leftover energy from a corroded industrial node. Without words, I was participating in the labyrinth’s self-creation, acting as both witness and agent. Was I in control, or was I merely fulfilling instructions?

It was hard to say. Yet either answer led to the same reality: in this dryness and heat, in this gloom that the vines themselves exhaled, the distinction between me and them was dissolving. There were times I forgot outright that I had eyes that see in normal light. Here, I sensed the labyrinth in waves of heat, currents of dryness, faint pulses in the corroded beams. I heard the labyrinth in vibrations that shivered through the bark-metal floors. I tasted it in the dust that drifted from overhead fronds, an acidic tang that reminded me of old battery fluid.

Occasionally, the traveler in me flared up with a desire to speak, to call out in human words. But the dryness swallowed any words I formed. My lips cracked; my throat stung. The labyrinth had no need of vocalization, no need of mouth-sounds. Its communication was friction and shift, the tensile moan of iron being gently torqued from centuries of unstoppable growth. If I tried to speak, the labyrinth was silent. But if I let my breath mingle with the hush—if I tuned into the low frequencies of branches sliding in their sockets—I could sense the labyrinth’s response in the trembling quiet. That, in its own way, was a kind of dialogue.

I began to suspect that I might be what passes here for a newly sprouted leaf with legs, a dedicated scout. Perhaps the labyrinth needed something mobile that could traverse the gloom faster than slow-growing vines and expansions. Perhaps it forged me as a piece of itself that walked on two feet, able to drift through the corridors and locate new sources of rust or stray energy in old machines that still had a spark. If I felt something misaligned, if I discovered a twin corridor that collapsed in on itself, I would feed this information into the labyrinth by letting it flow across my mind. Then the labyrinth would shift in response, adjusting with near-imperceptible groans until the problem was solved. I was beginning to see the shape of my function.

What if, in an earlier era, there had been many like me—whole colonies of vine-shaped bipeds that roamed the labyrinth? Over time, maybe they fused back into the superorganism, reabsorbed by its unstoppable sprawl. I wondered, would that too be my fate? When my wandering purpose was fulfilled, would my arms and legs stiffen into bark, the line between my spine and a steel girder blurring until there was no difference at all?

At first, the idea seemed grim. But a deeper part of me realized: it was no more grim than a leaf falling to the earth to nourish the roots. It was part of the cycle. We fear endings when we consider ourselves separate. But in this place, the separation was an illusion. If I had once been an outsider, the vines were in the process of teaching me that I had never truly been. The labyrinth had always encompassed my past, my present, and my future. Old illusions were just that—flickers of memory from a watery world that may never have been mine.

I continued exploring. Sometimes I found entire rooms—ancient expansions turned into throbbing furnaces, where metal plating glowed with dully shimmering heat. In those places, I felt a strong compulsion to kneel, pressing my palms to the scalding floor. In that searing contact, I glimpsed a tapestry of instructions that radiated outward into the labyrinth’s countless roots and cables. Then I’d stand, newly aligned, newly convicted of a silent mission I couldn’t fully articulate. But each time I rose, an area of labyrinth behind me would begin to rearrange itself, opening new corridors or pushing up new trunk-like supports, as though it had gleaned an impetus from the contact.

Once, in one of those furnace-chambers, I paused by a shining protrusion that might have been an engine block half-eaten by vines. The machinery still rumbled with a half-aware spirit, and its friction gave off a steady supply of heat. I placed my hand on an exposed bit of hammered metal. An almost electric jolt ran up my arm. For a moment, I saw the labyrinth from an unthinkably vast vantage: column after column of bark-metal, miles upon miles, a labyrinth not just horizontally extensive but layered upward and downward as well, sinking deep into the world’s crust, rising in stacks of plate and vine toward a ceiling that might be lost to time. In that brief epiphany, I sensed just how immense it all was. And I recognized that I wasn’t merely a small, discrete portion. I was a node connected to the entire living machine, as broad as a continent, as ancient as memory could reach.

In that realization, I felt no fear—only acceptance. Strange how swiftly something so alien can become a natural part of you once the boundary dissolves. Yet I suppose that boundary had always been an illusion. I was part of the labyrinth from the start.

And so, if you were to ask me now: Where do I find myself in this world? I find myself everywhere and nowhere in it. I might say, “Look up—see that braided trunk crossing overhead? In a sense, that’s me.” Or “Do you feel the floor trembling beneath your feet as new roots push aside a broken girder? That’s me, too.” Simultaneously, I am the shadow in the corridor who still looks vaguely humanoid—someone who walks on two legs with eyes that watch the gloom. But the deeper truth is: I am simply one more extension of this labyrinth’s vine-metal soul. I happen to speak of an “I,” but that’s just convenience for those who think in terms of individuals. Here, we do not. Here, the labyrinth is one entity: I am it, and it is me.

I can’t say if I began as an ordinary traveler who forgot their origins, eventually losing all sense of a life before the darkness. Nor can I say if I was always a specialized offshoot of the labyrinth, shaped to walk among these corridors. Such distinctions matter less with each passing moment. The dryness doesn’t harm me; the heat is my nourishment; the darkness is where my senses thrive. I am made of that dryness, that heat, that darkness. What started as a question—of how I might survive here—has long since faded into the silent contentment of being.

If someone else were to wander in, they might see me as a lone figure in the gloom, with bark-metal limbs and a faint, ember-like glow in my chest. They would doubtless wonder whether I am friend or foe, plant or machine. But I would not answer. Not in words. I would guide them deeper, if they so desired, letting the corridors reweave themselves around us both. Eventually, they too might wonder if they had ever stood apart from the labyrinth.

And so, the question: Who or what am I here? I am the labyrinth’s reflection, a living corridor bridging every rivet and every vine, formed from the hush and the dryness. Once I was sure I was a traveler; now I know I am also the place. In a world that has no water, no open skies, no life but root and rust, this is the only truth that remains: we are not separate. Like the vines, I stretch and reconfigure. Like the ancient metal beams, I groan softly with the tension of indefinite ages. One day, perhaps, I will abandon all pretense of a human shape and let the labyrinth wholly claim me, place me where I’m needed, thread my consciousness into the old power lines. Until then, I wander, guiding new expansions, bridging hidden expanses. I belong to it—through it—and it to me.

Thus, I find myself everywhere. I am the quiet footstep and the corridor, the living cable and the gear half-digested by creeping shoots. I stand in the dryness, among the scorching gloom, neither lonely nor content, but simply alive in the ancient, unending domain of bark-metal heat. This is who I have become: simultaneously a traveler and the unstoppable sprawl that dwarfs any notion of a traveler. A leaf with legs, a corridor with eyes, a caretaker of a labyrinth that was here before memory. That is where I find myself. That is what I am.
