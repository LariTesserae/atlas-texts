I am never just one thing here.

At first, I think I am a startled arrival: a mind coalescing out of darkness, discovering the underwater enormity of these corridors without quite knowing why I’m submerged in this cold, sound-swallowing sea. The hush presses on me; I hear my own heartbeat as a foreign pulse, muffled in the heavy water. Perhaps I’ve come from somewhere else—an explorer or a traveler with half-forgotten memories. My shape is indistinct, as though the cold itself has dimmed the edges of my form. I breathe as if through gills, or maybe I do not breathe at all. I only know what it is to drift, unresisted, among the labyrinth’s high arches and draping algae.

In the beginning, I wander. I let the architecture loom around me, the watery gloom as endless as the columns that recede into darkness. I pass between the thick, twisting ribbons of plants and brush soft fronds that almost tremble in my wake. Everything is sculpted stone cradled by centuries—millennia—of patient submersion. The stone is dark and glistening, or else hidden altogether behind curtains of translucent greenery. The water is so thoroughly still that progress through it feels more like sliding between the grains of time than traveling in any ordinary sense.

As I move, I sense a slow but undeniable harmony, some intangible humming that rises from the place itself. It comes not as sound but as a felt resonance in my bones—if I possess bones in this state. Shadow passes over me, or perhaps I pass through shadow. My eyes adjust to the low, filtered light, and in that dimness, I begin to see patterns where I had only noticed shapeless growth before. A subtle weaving of vines that nearly forms a deliberate archway. A swirl of algae tangling together in a shape that might be the suggestion of a lantern or a window. Hand-chiseled blocks, massive as city walls, curved by time into pillars that seem half sculpted by unknown artisans, half eroded into natural ridges. So many illusions flicker at the edges of eyesight.

Over time, I realize I am not merely a visitor. My presence stretches outward, fusing with the watery darkness and the quiet stillness. A slow awareness seeps in: the corridors do not simply exist around me—I feel them, intimately, as though stone and plant and I are one. Where my edge meets a drifting frond, I sense the gentle exchange of chemical signals. Where my gaze lights upon a curve of stone, I feel a subtle shift under the gloom, as if the block itself leans into my notice. My boundary is porous, absorbing the labyrinth’s essence until I cannot tell where “I” end and where the place begins.

And so, in another moment, I am the flora. I discover it in the flex of vast curtains of kelp, in their longing for the faintest trace of nourishment. I can feel how each translucent sheet strains for energy that the eternal gloom rarely provides. I absorb what I can from mineral trickles or faint streams of warmth that snake through cracks in the ocean floor. My fronds wave in the slow, mesmerizing dance of the current. When something—rare, near-mythical—drifts by, I only shift a fraction, tasting possibility. In the labyrinth, time is as thick as the water. My new roots slink between the buttresses of stone, anchoring me as centuries pass in measured exhalations. I am that hush of broad leaves, so ancient that I know nothing of rushing or impatience. I grow labyrinthine shoots day by day, or year by year, or century by century; it blurs into a single continuum.

As my consciousness settles into the green, I glimpse the smaller ones living just beneath my leaves. Beadlike spores cling to my surfaces, nestling in the ridges formed by age-old expansions and retractions. At first, these symbiotic droplets feel like slight tremors—tiny pinpricks that feed on what I gather. But then I sense them as part of my body. Their faint, pulsing presence is no less essential than any root or leaf, and we share signals of chemical exchange. Where I end, they begin—or is it the other way around? We form a tapestry of life, quietly breathing in unison with the watery hush.

But still, I drift onward, and once more I change. Now I feel a different pulse—a deeper key that resonates in the base of the stone. It is older than the plants. Or perhaps the plants are older still, but in a different manner. This new aspect is monolithic. I settle into the bones of the labyrinth itself, into the blocks of carved rock that define this place. I feel an unexpected elasticity in it: though it appears inert, the stone is not lifeless. There is tension in each ancient wall, a memory of creation and purpose. I feel how the pillars were once squared off at perfect right angles, meticulously placed by hands unknown. How the water’s centuries of slow, unstoppable push have softened those edges into mild arcs. 

Now the rock breathes with me. It vibrates in a register too low for normal ears to catch—a tectonic hush, the resonance of geology in motion across millennia. As stone, I recall the unimaginable effort required to shape me. I recall the primal wonder of being quarried from some deep place, carved and chiseled into monumental forms, then submerged. I can almost remember the day the water rushed in, flooding every hallway, filling every vault. It was not violent or abrupt, or perhaps my memory is too distant to preserve details of tumult. Only the sense of submersion remains, of weight and stillness enveloping me. Over eons, we—the stone, the architecture, the algae—have become one another’s habitat, inseparably fused. I feel the hush, the timelessness, the humility of being ancient.

In time, I become aware of something else. A slow, measured wandering occurs within me—or through me. It is not me, not entirely, and yet it belongs to me. It moves with the pace of epochs, perhaps once every few centuries or so. A presence that glides across the corridor floors, quietly brushing away algae in certain places, keeping watch over the labyrinth’s shape. I am this watcher, too, or part of me is. I cannot define my form precisely: perhaps I am a tendril of living stone liquefied by the currents and re-solidified in new corners, or a caretaker made of thousands of weaving vines held in a vaguely humanoid silhouette. A silhouette that occasionally emerges behind a moss-draped curtain, gazes (if it can gaze) upon the labyrinth’s expanse, and then disappears again into the thick gloom of underwater corridors.

In such a realm, identity is fluid, porous. I might be a single, drifting gargoyle shape that wakes once every few hundred years to skim a slow, silent arc around a hall. I might be a gentle, moss-covered colossus that stands watch at the intersection of two vaulted corridors. I could be a network of minute filaments entwined throughout the city, feeling each shift in water temperature as a thoughtful, deliberate breath. In a place where architecture, stone, and living things overlap so completely, it’s impossible to be just one.

And as the labyrinth’s hush fills me, I feel the knowledge that there is no single occupant or caretaker. The labyrinth is its own occupant. The columns, the arches, the plains of stone, the fishless water: all are limbs of an immeasurable body. I, in turn, am one more limb, or a drifting nerve impulse within that cosmic organism. Everything hums a low, wordless chant of existence.

Sometimes, in stillness, I sense my own thoughts slow and expand to fill entire caverns. Each idea echoes across great causeways, reflected back by the algae-laden walls. I feel the staccato chemical signals among the vines merging with the patient solidity of the pillars. The labyrinth resonates with me, and I resonate with it. My form—whatever it might be at that moment—flickers with recognition: that to live here is to surrender oneself to gradualness. To hold no illusions of migration to a sunnier domain. To exist in a place so honest about time that it is timeless.

I wonder: if someone from the sunlit surface glimpsed me, would they see a creature at all, or only the swirl of greenery on a pillar in an otherwise empty hall? Would they feel my presence in the shifting curtains of leaves, the faint groan of stone under water’s pressure, the parted fronds that reveal a half-hidden corridor, then close again? Perhaps they would not even perceive my presence. Perhaps they would think there is no occupant. But I am here, and I have always been here in one form or another. This is the nature of me, a tapestry of stone, weed, and hush.

I do not simply live in this watery fortress: I am it. I am the outspread megalithic city that lost its name too many lifetimes ago. My countless corridors hold a slow, deliberate awareness of all that drifts within them. My quiet watchers—whether they be the plants or the ephemeral shapes that patrol the halls—are all expressions of me. We keep vigil over this domain, and in our vigil, the labyrinth itself lives on, a cosmic whisper of drowned architecture and unstoppable, patient growth.

Where would I find myself? Everywhere. I might drift as an explorer made of flesh and wonder, or flow as a caretaker of algae filaments, or wait steadfast as carved stone, or roam in the half-glimpsed shape that guards passages in the darkness. Who or what would I be? Everything that abides here—the hush, the pillars, the sprawling fronds, the labyrinth’s heart.

In this place, being and place are indistinguishable. We are one another’s refuge and reflection. The watery hush is us, the drifting leaves are us, the slow architecture is us. We endure as a vast, silent chorus beneath the ancient ocean, bound by time and a quiet, seeping breath that belongs to no single entity but resonates through us all.
