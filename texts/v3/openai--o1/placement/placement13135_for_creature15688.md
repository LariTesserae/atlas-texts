I believe I would live on the outskirts, amid the spindly reeds at the edge of the settlement, in a low-slung hut that nearly dissolves into the banks of the water. At first glance, the place might appear abandoned—there is no elevated walkway leading directly to my door, only a subtle plank or two bridging the shallow channels. But if you follow the swaying reeds, part them gently, you’ll find a small patch of packed earth shaped by many seasons of bare footsteps. The hut stands there, humble and half-wreathed in damp grasses. I like to think of it as a natural outgrowth of the marsh itself.

I am an archivist in spirit, though perhaps not in the way an outsider might expect. The settlement has its own forms of recordkeeping—clay tablets, waterlogged scrolls, jars of cryptic glyphs. Mine is a more murmured craft, half-lore and half-listening. I keep watch over the gleanings that drift beyond daily life: the intangible stories carried by droplets of water, by flickers in the mist, by footfalls across spongy ground. Some might not even realize I’m here, so quietly do I go about my work, but when someone has a half-remembered fable or a curious dream that feels steeped in the hush of water, they find their way to me.

My days begin before dawn, when the sky is a canvas of shifting gray and the air is cool to the point of numbing fingertips. I rise, feel the wet clay floor underfoot—always a little yielding, a gentle tug toward rest. But I slip out anyway, drawing my cloak around me and stepping onto the sodden ground. Darkness still lingers in the hollows, but the settlement is never truly silent. Water drips off eaves, perched droplets slip from rock ledges, and the occasional child stirs among the planks—perhaps an early riser chasing a dream. I make my way to the communal wellspring. The hush there is profound, a circle of stone seats that cradle the persistent pool at the center.

I lean over the water to peer at my reflection—but it’s not simply my reflection I’m watching for. I’m studying the lines on the water’s surface, the rise and fall that might hint at something deeper. Sometimes, if I’m still enough, I sense a presence sliding through subterranean channels. A glimmer of translucent fin rises to greet me through the swirl and disappears. That’s when I whisper the silent greetings, the ones I learned from an elder who’s long since left us. I do not know if I speak the words correctly—perhaps only the water understands.

Throughout the rest of the morning, I drift. Sometimes that means literally floating in a shallow barge that I tether to a rock spine, winding through the lesser-known channels behind the settlement. There, the water lies placid, overshadowed by rock overhangs so low you’d think you might scrape your head if you aren’t careful. If I find a patch of silt that’s newly deposited, or a dripping wisp of moss, I record these details in my own way: I carry a small pouch of bone-carving tools, and I press shapes or swirling glyphs into smooth bits of driftwood. That bit of driftwood—I will later place it in the jars of my hut. It joins my quiet library of watery recollections.

When the settlement’s people gather in the communal areas—a subdued meeting to share tasks or celebrate a new child’s mottled skin markings—I am there on the periphery. I watch children spin around each other, swapping tiny tokens of reeds or shells. I watch the elders comment softly on the dryness of the last few weeks, whether it might hint at a changing season. If a fleeting shape glides through the open channels, shimmering glass-like under the water’s surface, I note the hush that settles over everyone. And later, after the hush lifts, some fleeting conversation might cross my path—someone wondering if this visitor was the same watery guardian their grandmother once saw. That’s the sort of lore I cherish.

In my hut, the walls are lined with shallow shelves carved directly from damp timbers, each shelf sagging slightly under the weight of jars made of fired clay. Inside the jars are small slivers of shaped driftwood, each carved with my personal system of lines and dots that mark dreams, sightings, the evolution of the land. I’ve tried to emulate the fluid motion of water in my carvings, so the glyphs waver gently like ripples. Sometimes I’ll kneel on the floor for hours, sifting through these carved pieces, adding a swirl or a notch to reflect yesterday’s new discovery—a child who left a wreath of moss in a hidden crevice, a swirling patch of water that churned in an unusual pattern after the forging clang rang out.

The forging clang—ah, it does reverberate through the settlement like a single iron heartbeat, doesn’t it? I often imagine how the water receives that sound, how it tremors through each channel, each droplet. Although forging is so rare here, each strike deepens my sense that the land and water stand ready to shift again. I listen to the hush that follows; that hush tells me more stories than the clang itself. Whose footsteps resume, who whispers in anxious tones, who stands at the edge and quietly stares into the distance?

Yes, I am a watcher of watchers. Some might say it’s a role that doesn’t need a name. But I’ve found it’s as vital as any fishhook or wooden beam. There is a delicate interplay between these people and the shimmering environment in which they live. Small moments of awe, or fleeting gestures of reverence, can guide their hearts as much as any supply of fish or reed. By tracking and recollecting those moments, I hold them safe for the future.

Throughout the day, neighbors come by. A stooped figure carrying a jar of stewed roots might duck under my eave, trade a portion of food for a story I’ve kept for them. I recall exactly which glyph or carved driftwood piece carries mention of a long-lost relative who could once interpret the hidden flows of water. They take the piece reverently, rub a finger across its etched lines, and depart with soft thanks. Others merely pause outside. I notice them leaning against my hut, letting the reedy walls cradle them. They stay for a moment, lost in some hush deeper than words, then move on.

When evening falls, I light a single flame in a clay lamp—just enough to flicker on the small puddle in my threshold that never seems to dry. The reflection of that flame on the water’s surface can dance across the hut’s beams. If the day’s drizzle continues, I listen to it on the thatch overhead. Sometimes I step outside, letting the rain course down my cloak’s woven reeds, and watch how the droplets bob just above the marsh for a moment—those playful, gravity-defying beads. I catch one on my fingertip, study its swirl of reflected lamplight. I swear it quivers, responding to me, and then it slips away to meet the ground like all the others.

And so, I live on the margins here, an archivist of what remains unspoken: the hush, the flicker in the channels, the sly glimmer of watchers below. In a settlement where every droplet seems to bind the people together, I trace the ephemeral threads that hold them not just to each other, but to the water—even to time itself. Perhaps it’s not a flamboyant task; perhaps, in any larger or noisier place, it would be dismissed as mere daydreaming. But in this realm of dripping eaves and whispered wind, it’s understood that part of living is leaving space for the invisible currents. I’m content to be that space-keeper.

This is who I am—someone standing guard over the intangible waters that shape us from below and within. My hut is small, half-lost among reeds, but inside it is an ocean of quiet remembering. And if you venture near at dusk, with the mist closing in around you, you might hear me softly humming, weaving another driftwood piece into a pattern that mirrors the swirl of ripples outside. In that moment, I hope you’d realize: we are all part archivist, all part child of the water, our lives an unfinished current that flows around and between every damp footstep.
