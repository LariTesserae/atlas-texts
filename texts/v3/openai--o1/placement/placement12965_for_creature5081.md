Sometimes, I imagine I come to this land not as a single being but in many guises, each unfolding in slow revelation. At first, I see myself only as a keen observer, drifting in with the morning haze—or maybe I am the haze itself, roving among those spires of rock, gathering in translucence above the slender-limbed dwellers. I slip around the sharp stone outcrops, condensing in the quiet corners of their village. Whenever a mossy plateau sweeps into view, I murmur overhead, blending with the hush that underpins this entire realm.

But then, I shift. My vantage changes, turning me into a lone traveler whose bare feet press on the damp, gripping rock. I clutch a small woven satchel at my side, collecting seeds from pockets of soil. My limbs feel long, my steps careful. The subtle drip of water from a projecting ledge sprinkles my hair with a cool patter. Where do I come from? Perhaps I have always been here. I picture a childhood spent crouched by trickling streams, fingertips trailing across the glassy surface. The hush, the gentle murmur of drops, are as much a lullaby as any cradle-song. My voice, when I speak, is a soft thread that barely stirs the air. Maybe I glean from the shapes of clouds drifting overhead or the new shoots of moss appearing after the seasonal shift. Each detail is knowledge, held quietly in my heart.

For a while longer, I linger behind a sturdy stone structure, arms crossed over a woven tunic that’s damp to the touch. Nearby, an elder shows me how to fit the stone blocks together. I watch his calloused hands position chiseled edges so precisely that the rock fragments lock without mortar, as though magnetized. He taps them lightly with a wooden mallet—tock-tock—and listens for the resonance, nodding with approval. I wait, enthralled, remembering how I once believed all grand structures must come from more advanced means. Yet here, in the hush, in the swirl of light mist, I see how focus and patient craftsmanship forge a different kind of mastery.

At moments, another possibility awakes—a shape crisp as the jagged ledge to the north. I become a watcher of water, a sentinel perched by an earthen cistern carved into the village’s edge. Each morning, it brims with fresh condensation trickling from roof ridges. I stand guard, stirring the water gently, not to purify but to honor it, because water is the lifeblood of our plateau. My fingers swirl, forming eddies in the pool. Some neighbors might pass behind me, offering a smile and that slight upward tilt of the chin, as though acknowledging that the hush remains undisturbed. I return the gesture, reverent in my role.

Sometimes, in a dreamlike turn, I see myself as a direct extension of the plateau’s jagged stone. My limbs are angled planes, my shoulders ridged with the same geometry that the dwellers have grown adept at carving. My skin has the dull sheen of charcoal rock, beaded by perpetual moisture, and I realize I neither hunger nor thirst. In this shape, I am a living earthen outcrop, quietly threading roots into the plateau’s hidden crevices, absorbing the slow pulse of the water that seeps from within. Do I know speech? Perhaps not in the human sense. Yet I am in constant communication with the land: I feel each tiny shift, each micro-fissure, each droplet of moisture traveling through porous channels. When children come near, they rest a hand on me, feeling the faint warmth. Their presence is enough to remind me that I am part of their world. Their hush is my hush.

Other times, my presence is more communal. I am a parent guiding my young in the cautious footsteps that navigate the stone gullies, showing them how to cup their hands around the broad leaves to gather condensed dew each morning. I lead them to an elder’s fireside at dusk, where stories murmur about the days when winged creatures soared in places far below this world of plateaus. By the flickering flame, we inhale the faint steam rising off a simmering stew of moss and tubers. The hush gathers in a ring around us, part warmth, part starry wonder from the swirling clouds above.

In one of my deeper imaginings, I become the hush itself—an almost intangible presence that laps at each stone threshold, coils in the doorways, brushes across the inhabitants’ tunics at dawn. I cradle every spoken word, carry it gently so it resonates only softly between buildings before dissolving in the thin, mist-laced air. The hush is an active thing, not an absence: it has texture and resonance. It’s a presence that fosters introspection in the plateau-people, helps them feel the land’s abiding stability. I dwell in their communal traditions, in the reverent tilt of their heads, in the silence that is never empty but always full of subtle dripping, rustling, breathing.

Eventually, though, I must choose. If I were truly among them in a single shape, who or what would I be?

• I might be a caretaker of the cisterns, ensuring each building’s angled roof collects enough moisture to sustain the village. Rising each day at dawn to check the channels for debris, I’d work quietly with carved wooden tools, speaking in hushed tones if a neighbor helps me clear leaves that have gathered overnight. My satisfaction draws entirely from the sustaining hush—the gentle swirl of water and the relieved smiles from those who fill their clay vessels before heading off to tend their gardens.

• Or I might be a strolling wanderer, carrying a stone-chisel kit in my woven pack. Methodically, I would roam beyond the settlement’s edge, along those ridges that dip and climb like sculpted waves of rock. I’d search for a new outcrop to quarry, hunting the perfect stone that rings with the right pitch under my mallet. If I found a spring or discovered a patch of hidden vegetation, I’d return with seeds or small samples of moss tucked into a braided pouch. The hush would map my journey, stretching out on each side like the promise of more to learn, more to shape.

• Perhaps I’d be an elder figure, quietly guiding the next generation. My skin might have the dulled charcoal patina of decades in mist. My gaze would carry deep calm, something the youth sense when they gather near, eager to learn how to coax the shrubs into producing stronger fibers. I’d be the keeper of old traditions: the memory of how the plateau once changed after a rare storm, or how the community arranged its very first ring of quiet dawn watchers. 

• Last, there is a corner of me that imagines becoming a quiet pilgrim from another land entirely—someone who somehow scaled the steep ridges from below. Arriving breathless, I would discover the hush and be enchanted, deciding never to leave. I’d shed my old habits of loud speech, letting my voice soften in reverence to the ever-present drip of water and the glow of clouds. In time, the villagers would welcome me into their communal circle, teaching me how to carve stone with gentle dedication and harvest tubers with respect for the land.

Ultimately, the shape that fits me best is the caretaker spirit—a gatherer of droplets and quiet footsteps. I see myself wearing a simple tunic, arms muscled but lean from carrying small cisterns and chiseling at rocky outcrops. My hair would carry beads of mist in the morning. My voice, a low murmur that resonates more in the chest than in the air. Each night, I’d kneel by the gently steaming pools that form at the settlement’s edge, stirring them just as I might stir the day’s experiences, until they settle into a smooth reflection of misty sky. The hush would cradle me as I immersed in that reflection—a reminder that in this land, all forms are part of a whole, woven by water and stone.

Yes—if I must name it: I am a steward of stillness. A caretaker who senses life in each droplet, who stands watch with reverence over the plateau’s enduring hush, making a modest but necessary contribution to a gentle, patient world. A world still becoming itself, day by soft day, and I would be there to witness each shift—content, balanced between stone and sky, letting my heart fill with that diffused, tranquil light.
