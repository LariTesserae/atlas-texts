I arrive in this place in a dream, my perspective unmoored—like I’m both here and not here, my senses drifting through the veils of perpetual half-light. First, I think I must be a visitor from beyond the plateau, for everything is so alien, so improbable in its dryness and density of life. Then, as I edge closer, a suspicion arises that I’m no stranger at all. My skin feels rough; there is dust caught in every crease. I look down to find calluses on my palms, and an ache in my spine that suggests I’ve known this daily labor for years. The boundary between “self” and “place” seeps away. In a land without fresh water, perhaps I, too, have learned to find nourishment in the air itself.

At first, I see myself as an Atilk child—barefoot, agile, with my arms streaked by swirling tattoos the color of deep, ashy teal. The lines trace the shape of succulent leaves, looping around my wrists in a repeating pattern. I recall the day the elders applied the designs, tapping carefully with flint needles. My father stood behind me, hands firm on my shoulders, whispering that these marks would connect me to the plateau forever. With each gentle tap, I felt a resonance with the stony ground beneath my feet. Even now, I can shut my eyes and feel the plateau breathing, as though its ancient rock has lungs that slowly rise and fall in the dusty wind.

In that form, I run with my siblings through the labyrinth of half-sunken huts—clay walls that match the color of our arms, roofs that slope and dip like the broad succulent leaves clinging to the ground. We chase each other around storage pits where brackish water glints in the meager light. The spindly, six-limbed lizards watch us from the perches of woven rafters, their tongues flickering out to catch drifting insects. My heart thrums with the excitement of youth: each day feels like a game, even if the scarcities are real. For a moment, I bask in that childlike freedom—dust swirling around me, the landscape both massive and intimate.

But then something shifts. The dream changes, or perhaps I’m simply looking at another layer of my existence. I am no longer a child. I crouch near a loom of rough-hewn wood, weaving fiber into sheets of thick linen. My back aches with the memory of carrying heavy bundles of succulent stalks along the plateau’s edge. The loom’s threads scratch and tug, my hands calloused from repeated labor. My hair—what little I keep tied back—is the same dusty color as the rock. I’ve watched countless dawns slip by without ever seeing the sun break fully over these cliffs. Instead, we have gentle, diffused glimmers that turn the plateau’s surface into a perpetual twilight. Working in that twilight is routine now, each day punctuated by tasks that keep our community alive: weaving, scraping hides, examining dew traps, collecting even the tiniest droplets. Survival is equal parts chore and ritual.

Time ripples again, and I see myself shrouded in a patchwork cloak, standing at the edge of the settlement’s courtyard. I am neither young nor old. My face is heavily lined, my eyes wide and dark, well-adapted to the dimness. Around me, the daily bustle proceeds: I hear the hush of whispered conversations, see the flash of flint striking rock, watch dust devils swirl around the huts. In that second, I sense something deeper than belonging. I feel the plateau’s pulse running through me—an awareness that I’m part of this precarious tapestry, bound to it by the water I’ve gleaned, by the saps I’ve drunk, by the dust on my skin. Perhaps I am the plateau itself—or a living fragment of it. And that realization brings a curiously comforting feeling, as if I’m rooted in the same bedrock that shaped my ancestors.

Yet the dream extends further still. My vantage drifts upward, beyond the clay and woven roofs. From this vantage, I’m compelled to see the swirl of wingless birds, the creeping vines, all those hidden dwellers. I become one of the day-black lizards, skittering along the rock face, my elongated toes gripping the stone as if magnetized. There’s a delicious sense of freedom in moving up sheer walls with six nimble limbs. Rock ridges pass beneath me like old scars on a body—places I know intimately, having hidden within them for generations. The chirruping of insects is a constant lull, and the dryness no longer bothers me; my thick scales hold in enough moisture for me to thrive.

In this form, I watch the Atilk from high vantage points. Sometimes a child will leave a morsel near an entrance beam, beckoning me to scuttle closer. I meet that child’s gaze—recognize a flicker of curiosity that is both respectful and bold. I snap up the treat, feeling my tongue lash out in a whip-fast streak. Is that a grin on the child’s face or a momentary flicker of fear? Hard to say. The bond between lizard and human is tenuous but ancient; we cohabit, sharing the plateau’s limited resources. In my lizard-heart, I sense a pact: we keep the insects in check, and they allow us respite from larger hunters—predators that even a lizard’s nimble claws cannot outrun forever.

Next, I feel a tug—a wordless pull from another direction. This time, I root myself in the silent hush of the plant world. I sense the dryness of the air as a soft rasp along the perimeter of my leaves. My name is lost to human tongues, though they use my fibers daily. My bark is thick, layered over centuries, each ring embedded with grit that the wind whips into me. I have learned to welcome each faint bead of moisture, funneling it through microscopic channels that feed my succulent interior. I sense the hum of the plateau’s life all around me: insects crawling across my branches, rodents digging at my roots, human hands occasionally slicing away bits of me to weave or to feed the community. It does not pain me as it would a creature of flesh. We are all cogs in this miraculous cycle of exchange. My seeds ride the dust-laden wind, drifting across the plateau’s cracks to find new homes. Each new seedling roots in the fractures, quietly swelling with stored water, waiting for just enough vapor to bloom. In my stillness, I witness it all.

Returning to myself as human—perhaps that’s next, or perhaps I remain a wandering perspective, both inside and outside the Atilk’s realm. The plateau’s hush envelops me. I see the entire settlement: huts interlocked like puzzle pieces, each supporting the other. Dew nets anchored on the perimeter ripple in a gentle breeze, leading their collected droplets into carved basins. At midday, families converge in the courtyard with wooden bowls of hearty stew, thickened by the paste of crushed leaves. Children chase rodents that skitter away in search of seeds. Elders hunch in the dappled light, chipping stone tools or weaving mats for the floors of the huts. Overhead, a band of flightless birds squawks and hisses, engaged in their own pecking order.

I feel the hush of their language in my own throat—those breathy clicks and gentle stops that carry over the plateau’s rocky surface, ensuring the calls do not echo too loudly. At the same time, I feel the drumming within the rock, those rumored voices from somewhere in the deep cracks beneath the settlement. Maybe that’s another version of me, living in hidden chambers, practicing rituals no one else remembers. A thousand possibilities swirl.

Who am I? Or what am I? The answer changes with each breath taken in this plateau, each shift of wind that stirs the dust. Maybe I am child, lizard, succulent, elder, or the dusty wind itself—there is no single shape that captures the totality of being here. This place is a living mosaic, and in stepping onto its surface, one must accept the invitation to merge. I am each life that endures in improbable dryness; I am the hush of half-light that never truly brightens, the hush that resonates with all the scuttling and fluttering forms. I am the plateau’s ancient memory, a witness to eons of survival.

In the end, I decide I am all of these things—and none of them. I am the Atilk child with dust between my toes. I am the watchful adult, weaving fiber and gleaning dew. I am the hungry lizard perched on a beam. I am the succulent cradling each precious droplet. I am the rumor of hidden song in the catacombs below. And I am the ragged wind that slips across these sheer cliffs, singing an ageless tune.

This plateau, worn by time yet thrumming with tenacious life, claims me as its own. Its dryness is my dryness, its hush is my hush. If I had to pick a single name—or shape—for myself here, I would fail, because the place does not permit such simplicity. It invites me to inhabit its many roles, to slip between them as easily as dust drifts on the air. And so I do. I remain—fluid, many-souled, forever part of the plateau’s balancing act between thirst and abundance, silence and ceaseless hum.
