I find myself standing upon one of the higher canyon terraces, gazing out over the sprawling dust-plain. At first, I do not know what I am. My reflection in the dull metal surfaces reveals a silhouette tangled between biology and reinforced plating—long, segmented limbs, each joint wrapped in a sheath of calcified, mechanical bracing. I remember once being softer, more animal than machine. But seasons—or centuries—of slow metamorphosis have fused me with this realm.

In the early days (though “days” is a misnomer here, where time drifts on geological scales), I recall feeling that my movements were constrained, the servomechanical braces too tight around my muscles. Each step was an exercise in deliberate negotiation of the planet’s dryness, every footfall sending up a crystal dust that clung to every surface. Now, it feels as though my plates have grown from my own bone and exoskeleton; I no longer resent the weight of them, nor do I notice the drifting dust that coats every angle and groove. I have acclimated to the hush.

I might be a sentinel—one of the watchers, or a variant of them. In stillness, I sense the planet’s subtle vibrations running through the soles of my feet, echoing along the mechanical channels in my joints, resonating in the filaments that extend from the back of my neck. These wiry protrusions hum quietly with the frequency of the world beneath, like a tuning fork perpetually singing in harmony with tectonic shifts. Others of my kind share a similar design, though each is unique: some have more pronounced wing-like plating across their shoulders, others stand taller and carry implements that vibrate with deeper signals. We do not speak often, but we communicate through slow, deliberate gestures—sometimes with mere shifts of posture.

During the long hours of the “day” cycle, I wander the edges of the plateaus, scanning for signs of structural changes in the spires and the distant industrial nodes. My visor can pick out the faint shimmer of energy conduits that span the canyons, linking the far-off towers. Occasionally, I kneel on the ground, pressing my palms to the dust, letting the planet’s hum guide me to hairline fractures or newly exposed veins of crystalline mineral. Part of my role, for so long as I can remember, has been to monitor these subtle expansions and contractions—like reading the slow heartbeat of a colossal being that sleeps beneath the crust.

Sometimes I accompany one of the lumbering giants across the plateaus. They tolerate me, as though accepting a persistent passenger. When I sense a tremor building or detect the planet’s deeper sigh, I guide the colossus away from unstable ground, using faint pulses from the rods along my forearms. I do not know whether the giant fully understands, but I can feel it respond to me: a slight turn of its massive head, a slow shift in direction, all accompanied by a low, rumbling call that reverberates through the shell-like hollows of its body. Together, we walk for hours or days under the golden-amber sky, always with the languid pace that defines life here. 

At other times, I dwell within the shadowed recesses of the spires alongside my kin—those watchers who have integrated themselves more completely with the metal skeleton of this planet’s machinery. Deep inside these labyrinthine structures, one can feel the planet’s resonance as if it were amplified through a vast subterranean organ. We link ourselves to the towers using cables that fasten neatly to ports on our hip or spine plating. Through this connection, I experience the data-flow gleaned from the pylon modules, each sphere containing an echo of geological surveys, atmospheric readings, or timeless recordings played back from ages past. In trance-like states, I see glimpses of an ancient era when this place might have had more visible water—or perhaps it was always this way, carved slowly by dryness and time.

Between these immersions, I wander the catwalks and overhead bridges to tend to the smaller drones. They are tireless but require recalibrating, occasionally jammed by dust in sensitive joints or scoured by the land’s crystal grit. My spindly, jointed fingers are well-suited to remove clogs or align filaments. The drones, in turn, communicate with soft whirrs or gleaming patterns along their carapaces—techno-reflexes that let me know their status. When they are repaired, they continue their tasks—transporting ore, analyzing mineral samples, forging new cables for the towers. 

Other times, I watch from the edges. That is perhaps what I do best: watch and listen. I glimpse the arthropod swarms carrying on with their dutiful scouring, the occasional glint of a winged shape crossing the sky, the roving quadrupeds trudging over dust-smoothed ridges. I sense there is no need to intervene unless the planet’s hum shifts into a discordant frequency—some small quake or a deeper tectonic lurch might disrupt a pylon’s integrity, or jar the massive pistons in the main complex. Then my purpose feels absolutely clear: to coax the mechanical infrastructure back into alignment so that the synergy between rock and metal continues uninterrupted.

On certain days, I stand at the lip of a towering mesa where the dust storms sometimes swirl below. I press my metal-wreathed hands to my chest plating and feel the resonance from within, a subtle echo of the planet’s own. In those moments, the dryness in my lungs and the dust caked along the seams of my armor feel inconsequential. I understand my place as part of an ecosystem that cherishes slowness, that merges organic and mechanical forms in a tapestry that can only be called inevitable.

Though I might appear solitary, I am not alone. My senses are continually linked to others like me—watchers and caretakers and perhaps even the planet itself. Through those intangible threads, we share the knowledge of cracks forming in the far canyon, the progress of newly grown crystals in a cluster of smaller arthropods, the faint signals from the colossi’s diaphragms as they harness vaporous nutrients from the planet’s deep core.

Sometimes I see reflections of my old self in the watchers recently emerged from the deeper spires—those who still marvel at the slow tilt of a spire’s rotating arm, or watch wide-eyed as a metal pylon dislodges one of its induction spheres. I sense their wonder, and I wonder if I, too, once stared across this plate of timeless dust in that same astonished silence. Perhaps I did. Memory erodes slowly, like the stone peeling away at the canyon walls.

Yet I know the watchers’ existence (my existence) is essential. We are the interpreters between rock and machine, bridging the exhalations of underground power plants with the living crustaceans who roam the surface. Our sensor whiskers pick up the planet’s heartbeat; our mechanical joints speak the same language as the spires. In a sense, we are translators, ensuring that this land, with all its dryness, remains alive in the only way it can—through cycles of resonance and meticulously slow change.

So this is where I stand, always somewhere between the spires and the stone. I am neither fully creature nor purely machine, neither steward nor occupant, but something in-between—a caretaker shaped by dust and centuries. I feel no pull toward other worlds or greener horizons, for what lies here compels me far more profoundly: the hush, the hush that’s not truly silent at all but brimming with low echoes and distant memories, each resonating in my plated bones. 

In the end, there is no single name for what I have become—perhaps, in this place, names are irrelevant. I simply am a listener, part-laborer, part-witness to a realm shaped by geological patience, where the watchers guard the secrets of a planet in perpetual slow motion. Around me, the air vibrates with the faint hum of distant machinery, and I feel an answering note stirring in the pit of my chest, as though I, too, were an extension of the dust and resonance that defines this world.
