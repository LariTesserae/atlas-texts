I arrive at the plateau in the hush of a lull between gusts, still convinced I’m missing something about the air. Part of me wants to call it thirst, but here there is no thirst for water as I’ve known it. Instead, I reach out with my senses for the charged grit, that metallic tang on my tongue. A low current crackles through every dust mote—I suppose this is what a “drink” must be, in a land devoid of water.

At first, I seem almost human, wearing layered strips of hide over a wiry torso. My silhouette is spindly, and my movement is slow and measured, for I still do not trust that the ground won’t shift under each footfall—a reflex from wherever I once lived. But as the wind picks up, I brace my knees and elbows with practiced motions that surprise even me. Did I inherit these instincts from the dust itself, or from the folk who taught me? I can’t remember. I only know that I remain standing while the dust whirls in ribbons around me.

• • •

Give it time, the plateau whispers in the screech of wind. Let your shape evolve. 

Soon, my limbs lengthen, the bones adopting extra joints that fold back like hinges. Ridges of fibrous growth—thicker than hair, thinner than quills—sprout along my shins and forearms. These whisker-like protrusions vibrate in the wind, reading the storm’s direction long before the blasts come. I learn to shift my weight, hooking my toes into shallow cracks in the chalky ground. My skin, once leathery brown, becomes streaked with pale chalk dust, and intricate loops of burgundy begin etching themselves across my torso, as though the plateau’s spined vegetation has tattooed itself onto me.

Am I still me? Or a wanderer now? I don’t know. My silhouette loosens and grows taller with each gale, and though the change is unnerving, it feels right in the marrow of my bones. I adapt without quite realizing how—my own body responding to the plateau’s demands as though it has known these demands for eons.

• • •

When the wind dies enough for me to travel, I pass near a cluster of burgundy spines. The ground there has risen in gentle mounds, the surface cracked by the plants’ thick rootworks. Someone—or something—peers at me through a ragged seam in a hut: a child, her face half-obscured by a fibrous mask that shields her mouth from airborne crystals. She, too, has patches of pale dust caked on her shoulders, and her eyes glow faintly—an effect of the dust or perhaps the embedded minerals across her brow.

She motions: Come nearer. You can share the fire.

Inside the hut, among blackened hearthstones shaped into a semicircle, a single flame licks upwards. The color shifts from magenta to sickly green, fed by lumps of some chemical-laden root tossed in by an older figure. I realize I carry no such offering. Still, they let me sit. Their eyes—various hues of dusty grey and burgundy—regard me uncertainly. I remove a small net I’d been fashioning from the fibrous quills on my arms, offering it to them. The old figure prods it, nods approval. An elder, perhaps. A moment passes, weighted but not hostile, as we wait for the next breath of wind to rattle the hut’s fragile plating.

• • •

I remain there for a time—long enough to feel the hush stretch over days or nights (I cannot tell). With the clan, I learn about the crafters who dwell in adjacent huts, forging hammered scrap and bone into improbable shapes. From them, I pick up the basics of a skill: forming thin metal arcs that can harness wind for net-throwers, or chiseling through layers of mineral plate without fracturing the understrata. I see them test each hammered plate with careful strikes, reading the pitch with their broad, many-fingered hands. In the hush, they pass knowledge not in words but in subtle gestures and hums, a kinship that emerges out of shared understanding of the plateau’s rhythms.

But I cannot stay. Something in me wants to roam, to explore the deep ridges and swirling dust columns. The clan does not blame me; wanderers are common here. They watch me depart in the dawn-dusk half-light, crystals flickering faintly in the swirling air.

• • •

Now my vantage changes again. A movement among the clouds catches my eye: gliders, silver and silent, their elongated fins skimming the wind. Call it envy or curiosity, but I want to know what it’s like to drift as they do. And so, by some mysterious transformation that the plateau both grants and demands, I step into the outline of a glider’s form. My arms elongate into wide, flexible fins; my torso narrows and flattens. I feel the wind’s pressure under my newly stretched membrane, lifting me from the cracked plateau.

In that first upward rush, I experience the plateau’s immensity: spires that from above look like bleached teeth, the clusters of burgundy vegetation like bruises on stone. The huts etched into pockets near each cluster, the swirl of insect flocks glinting in the twilight...all laid out in a sweeping mosaic. I sense the humming current in the wind—an electric tang that keeps me effortlessly afloat. Instead of a heart, it feels like I have a swirling spark deep in my chest.

Time passes differently up here. The storms that pummel the land become rolling waves of air that I ride. The shrieking dust is kept at arm’s length by the boundary of my fins. Sometimes, I see the wanderers below, picking their way across the plateau. Sometimes, I spy the many-legged birds scuttling in lines, herded by thin-limbed youths. They, too, glance at me with wonder—some raising bone-scrap tools to point me out to their neighbors.

I glide on, lost in the swirl of clouds, until the impetus to descend rises in me again. There is an urge to anchor, to remember how stone feels beneath me. In a diving drift, I sail downward, letting out a crackling cry that resonates across my shimmering membrane. The glider’s voice is raw, not so much vocal cords as an electric rasp that echoes the storm. That rasp returns from the plateau in a hundred small ways: the chitter of insect swarms, the hiss of wind, and the metallic shrieks of birds. I land, or perhaps I collapse, my fins folding back into arms. The world shifts shape, and I am once again something bipedal, more akin to the plateau’s people than to any single clan.

• • •

I press on toward the rim, the plateau’s edge, that precipitous drop into gloom. The constant dust makes it hard to see how far the cliff extends, but I sense the emptiness below. I crouch near a worn pillar of stone, part of it carved into what looks like steps leading into darkness. Subterranean dwellers, rumored to be the oldest inhabitants, must have made these. Intrigued, I descend a few steps, and a hush swallows me—strange after so long with only screaming wind in my ears. I glimpse flickers of luminescent fungus or crystal in the walls. My breathing quickens; in the silent gloom, it sounds loud as thunder. The hush radiates an otherworldly intensity. I can hear only the faint rustle of my own body shifting dust.

What would it be like to continue? To become one of the pale crawlers, twisting my form to wriggle through cramped tunnels, devouring mineral seepages and leaving behind a translucent husk whenever I molt? The idea both terrifies and beckons me. For a moment, I envision my palms growing nodules of crystal, my eyes adjusting to darkness, my spine curving so I scuttle on elongated forelimbs. Perhaps I’d learn the plateau’s hidden secrets, the labyrinth of catacombs where crystal spires glow faint magenta, feeding on deep pulses of the earth.

A breeze from above, funneling down the stone corridor, reminds me how alien that path would be. Yet the plateau’s nature is to show me that even what feels alien can become home if you are willing to adapt. Do I go deeper, or do I retreat back to the surface storms? I linger there with one foot on the worn step, one foot on the chalky plateau above. The air from below is so still. The air from above is alive with rustling currents and the calls of distant creatures. I remain suspended between them, letting possibilities multiply.

• • •

In my indecision, I return to the heart of the plateau, magnetized by the hum of a gathering that’s taking place near dusk—or near dawn, I can no longer tell. Fires tinted green and magenta bloom around the makeshift circle built from stacked mineral slabs. The crafters, gatherers, and wanderers mingle together under swirling flurries of luminous insects. The many-legged birds scuttle anxiously at the perimeter, heads bobbing in sharp, mechanical arcs. I notice one small child, different from the one I first met, leading a newly sheared bird by a tether of braided spines. The child’s eyes widen when they see me, and for reasons I don’t fully understand, I kneel to greet them. They touch the chalk-laced ridges on my forearm as if to say they recognize me, or at least some part of me.

The wind swells in that moment, a soft hiss that grows into a roar. Dust funnels swirl overhead, scattering the insects and blowing sparks from the colored fires. The hush is over; the plateau will not allow a festival to last for long. Instantly, those gathered pull hoods over their heads, covering their faces with bone masks or hides. Some herd the birds into protective enclosures. Others lock their huts with hammered metal plates. I watch as the dust whips everything into chaos. And in the chaos, I feel a strange joy thrumming in my chest—a sense of belonging to this reckless dance.

• • •

So who am I in the end? I am not a single shape. I am the plateau’s endless potential. At times, I’m a wanderer striding across the chalk plains, using my elongated limbs to brace against the wind. At others, I am a glider, the electric hum searing beneath my membranes as I soar above the scuttling life below. And in certain quiet corners of the deep, I catch glimpses of my reflection with pale, translucent skin stretched over a spidery frame, rummaging for the secrets hidden in the plateau’s labyrinth. 

But above all, I am someone who belongs here—someone in tune with the chalky dust, the swirling storms, the burgundy spines, and the luminous flocks of insects. Whether I lounge under a battered hut or cling to a ragged pillar, my body and spirit accept the plateau’s harsh logic. I find myself marveling at how the land, though violent and unpredictable, nurtures so many distinct lives in its orbit. 

I stand as part of that great mosaic: sometimes forging, sometimes harvesting, sometimes drifting far away. My soul resonates with the hum of crystals. My heart beats in rhythm with the thunder of wind. My shoulders bear dust, my voice merges with the shriek of storms. And in the hush that comes before the next gale, I smile—if only for a second—knowing that in this realm of no water, where all nourishment flows in shimmering motes and hidden veins, I have found a home… or perhaps I have become the home itself.
