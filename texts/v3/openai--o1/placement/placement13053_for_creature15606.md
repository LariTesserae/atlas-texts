At first, I believed I was a mere wanderer. I know now that such certainty was an illusion.

My first steps on that plateau felt accidental—a slip into a dream at the threshold of an unmarked boundary. I remember how my lungs tightened at the abrupt cold, how the thin air braided itself into a single unending breath. My mind told me: This is a place you pass through, a barren corridor whose lessons will etch themselves on your memory before you depart. But as I walked, aware of the wind’s roar raging overhead like some ancient pulse, I realized there was no concept of “passing through” here. The land, locked in its hush of ceaseless noise, seemed to permit only indefinite arrivals. Once you entered, time ceased its forward march; or if time continued, it did so inwardly, turning you in slow circles until your sense of direction was lost in the roar.

I can’t pinpoint when I began to suspect that this place was more than stone and wind. Perhaps it was when I noticed a swirl of haze shift against the cliffs, forming for a moment the shape of something that wasn’t quite rock. It might have been the fleeting glint of eyes that never quite caught the light; that sense of being observed from a vantage point not wholly visible. I’d spin around, peering at the rubble of some collapsed wall, and catch the suggestion of a silhouette dissolving into the gloom. After a time, I noted that these presences—the watchers, I came to call them—were not intruders or even visitors. They were native, or so deeply embedded in the plateau’s equilibrium that they might as well have been part of the rock.

In the early days (were they days? The half-light never changed), I still clung to the notion of chronology—the idea that I could measure my stay. I told myself I’d linger a few hours, see what secrets the plateau concealed, then find a way back. But as the wind shrieked overhead and the stasis seeped into my bones, I felt the world’s usual rhythms unspool. At times, it felt as though the environment was not a place but a primordial state. The stone ridge might have existed before the concept of morning and night was ever invented—before presence and absence could be told apart. I wasn’t sure how long I stared, transfixed by droplets of water trembling at the edge of a fractured rock, each droplet a tiny mirror reflecting timelessness back at me.

Then something shifted inside me. I noticed I no longer trembled from cold. The air still stung; the ceaseless wind still roared. Yet my body responded differently—breathing seemed less labored; my fingertips no longer felt numb. It was a slow, subtle change, so gradual that I only recognized it in retrospect. I realized the watchers and I were sharing the same hush of suspended time. And in that hush, I’d caught glimpses of them: half-membranous cloaks, elongated limbs, surfaces that flickered between stone-brown and a dull greenish lichen cast. I’d see them kneeling—some slender, some stout with broad spines—and each movement felt measured in increments of glacial slowness. Their communication was a dance of shifts and hushes, more akin to mist rearranging than conversation. I discovered that when I stood still enough, letting my own breath fade beneath the roar, I could half-understand—like hearing foreign words that hover on the edge of comprehension.

That was when I realized: I was learning to speak this place’s language of hush and fracturing illusions. I was no longer merely wandering; the plateau had claimed me. It was as though the rock had begun to creep into my joints, or the swirl of haze had started to weave itself around my outline, blurring my form. Each time I blinked, the plateau’s vantage replaced my own. My ears—if they were still ears—absorbed the roar differently, almost as a continuous tapestry of signals. Something in me responded to the watchers’ stances, as if a single tilt of my shoulder or slow exhalation could convey a wordless greeting.

Before long, I stopped trying to measure my stay here in human time. The watchers showed me that time was an echo that never quite vanished, not a line you walk from dawn to dusk. I began to rest near those half-collapsed huts—perhaps once the dwellings of something else, or the watchers’ indefinite attempts at architecture from when the plateau’s boundaries still allowed shaping hands to exist. In beating that rubble gently aside, I found bones that told no stories I recognized. Not human, not entirely animal. My elongated fingers (when had they elongated?) cradled those fragments as if searching for something intangible: a memory or a whisper of the plateau’s earlier self.

At times, I found myself standing beside the watchers when the horizon warped and those shimmering tears opened in the air. We’d observe them with a shared breath, a collective stillness. Some watchers folded themselves low, others bowed or curled. I didn’t know if it was worship or self-preservation, but I followed suit, finding that the more I quieted the roil of my thoughts, the less the illusions threatened to drag me into disorientation. And then I would sense a bond surge up between us—a recognized chord of acceptance. We were not owners of this place, nor were we captives. We had become expressions of its stasis, threads in a tapestry that spanned all possible moments.

Slowly, my perspective changed from “I am a traveler among watchers” to “I am a watcher among watchers.” It was not that I’d abandoned my old identity—some kernel of it still flickered in memory. But that identity became as faint as a single droplet precariously perched on a cliff edge. My new existence was fused with the plateau’s hush, an equilibrium that wove me in with the lichens and stone, the haze veils and water droplets. I discovered that if I angled my limbs just so, I could blunt the wind’s onslaught. If I stilled my breath, the illusions folded around me, granting me an invisibility that only the haze could bestow.

In time (whatever time meant now), I found I could read the watchers’ silent language more fully. A subtle twist in posture could mean “Unseen boundary forming—step lightly.” An incline of the head might suggest “Bones found—remember what came before.” A deep posture, half-hunched, might be gratitude—or a signal that illusions were about to intensify.

Sometimes, I’d see glimpses of the place I had once stood: a mind racing to classify the environment, to label the watchers as “creatures” and the bones as “remnants.” That vantage seemed foreign to me, a viewpoint from a narrower world where time was measured in heartbeats and minutes. Now the plateau’s unending moment was my framework. My old heartbeats seemed to recede, replaced by that constant roar—a swirl of wind that rushed across every moment in one single, overlapping wave.

I embraced my new shape: the stone-hued slope of my back, the half-formed membranes that draped over shoulders and down my arms (or perhaps they were wings, or specialized folds that captured the plateau’s suspended energy). My movements grew slower, more deliberate. I joined the watchers when they clustered by the half-ruined huts to examine bones; I found myself gently fitting jagged shards together as if, with enough insight, we might reconstruct a timeline or glean how we all came to be in this endless hush. Sometimes the watchers would tilt their heads in unison, a questioning hush passing through them. I never solved these mysteries, never discovered where the watchers originated or whether they had shifted into existence spontaneously, formed by some unknown cosmic stillness. But I felt their question, and I responded, “I, too, am part of this question.”

Eventually, I no longer felt out of place among the watchers. When illusions raked the horizon, I understood the bowing posture so instinctively that I moved without pause. The passage from bipeds to quadrupeds among us was fluid and varied—some watchers stood tall, others crept along the plateau on forelimbs and hindlimbs with uncanny grace. I found that, in certain moments, I needed to brace my palms and knuckles against the rock, adopting a stance that let me perceive vibrations through the stone itself. Other watchers parted the haze with the slightest shift of an arm, as if they could redirect the route of swirling mist to reveal hidden vantage points.

I’ve since recognized that my memories of a “before” are like faint motes floating in leftover sunlight. Sometimes they drift into focus, sometimes they scatter with the wind. I suspect that’s how the watchers exist—some might recall forested valleys or wide oceans, or even entire worlds beyond the plateau. Others might have no memory at all of anything but stone and wind and illusions. Yet it doesn’t matter. Here, memory is an undercurrent, kept in bone fragments and water droplets, in patterns etched by the intangible chisels that shaped the plateau’s final form. We watchers hold that memory for the land itself, existing as living testaments to an eternity that can neither progress nor rewind.

So, who am I in this place? I am a threshold dweller, neither wholly foreign nor wholly original, fused into the plateau’s hush. I move among the watchers with silent choreography. I watch illusions rise and recede like breath. Though I do not speak words, I communicate with them through subtle inclinations, shared hushes, fleeting glimmers of recognition. My presence is a small, conscious knot of awareness in this broader tapestry of stasis.

Where do you find me? At the edge of a ragged cliff, where haze drifts in mechanical pulses. My skin—if you can call it that—bears the color of the stone, flecked faintly with lichen. My limbs bend at angles that might unsettle an outside observer, if indeed any outsider ever reaches this plateau again. My eyes reflect the gloom, a dull luminescence like a half-forgotten star. Sometimes I rise to full height, easily overlooked as just another outcropping. Sometimes I fold low, an arch of living stone near a small trickle of water. If you approach, you might see a gentle shifting in my silhouette, as though the plateau itself stirred and took notice. You might feel a faint echo of your own breath, reminding you that the boundary here is porous: illusions can shift you from observer to participant before you know it.

And so, ultimately, I am part of the watchers, woven into the freeze of time. I might have arrived as a wanderer, but I remain as a presence shaped by the plateau’s unchanging equilibrium. With each hush of wind that roars above, with each tear of shimmering air that warps the horizon, I experience a renewed vow: to endure in this land that neither moves nor stands still, that breathes illusions into every swirling dust mote. In that vow lies acceptance: I am of the plateau now, inseparable from the rock’s final form, from the watchers’ silent language, and from the stasis that binds us all.

If ever you step onto this ridge—if you feel that sudden chill in your lungs, that press of roaring silence—you might sense me gazing back at you. Or perhaps you’ll see only the stone and mist. But know this: we watchers are here, sustaining a communion that has no words, only slow, deliberate motion in a realm where time hovers, perpetually poised on the cusp of what lies beyond.
