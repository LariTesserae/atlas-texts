Sometimes, I believe I was once human—someone who came here by mistake or necessity, wandering corridors until my memories dissolved in the echo of pistons. Other times, I suspect I am a fabrication of this place: a form pieced together from mismatched metal and fluid, animated by the labyrinth’s ceaseless cycles. I cannot be certain, for my sense of identity shifts with the thunderous surges that rock each corridor.  

I awaken somewhere in the heart of it all, hunched beneath an overhead crane whose chain sags like a rusted spine. At first, I recognize only that I am cold. The moisture clings to my skin—if skin is what I have. I press a hand to the metal floor. It peels back slick with condensation, leaving smears of something dark that might be grease or algae; it’s impossible to tell.  

I am alone. The thunder of distant machinery envelops me, rattling my bones—or are they rods and joints? I look down and see a shape that seems half-flesh, half-steel. My limbs have hinges. My fingertips are ridged with odd patterns, like rivets or small fans. My chest, however, feels warm and pliable. The boundary between metal and organic matter is uncertain. I’m not even sure if there is a boundary.  

• • •

I move through the corridors, each step resonating with clanks that echo up and around the scaffolding overhead. My footfalls may be loud or they may be perfectly muffled by the general din—sometimes I cannot distinguish my own noise from that of the world.  

Around me, the labyrinth extends in all directions. I see signs of what might once have been living quarters—large, boxy rooms with rusted bunks, trailing wires that spark feebly. Mold creeps along the walls, absorbing the stagnant drip of condensation. A shattered window frames a view of pipes, piping upon piping, like arteries in the belly of a beast. Beneath my feet, the floor thrums, a pulse that aligns disturbingly with my own internal rhythm.  

I wonder if I was born in a place like this. Perhaps, a broken memory suggests, I arrived from outside, stepping in on some ill-fated expedition. But if that were true, why do I feel so utterly at home?  

• • •

Hours or days pass—I can’t measure time here. The lights overhead flicker in a pattern that might be random or might be a code. The hum of the machinery rises and falls. Occasionally, I pause and listen to the pattern: the hiss of vents, the cyclical pound of steel presses. Over time, I begin to hear the shape of something in that noise—like a language. It’s all around me, this living code.  

At first, I think it’s my imagination, but I notice how certain intervals bring a deeper tremor that collects the labyrinth into a single, synchronized groan. My own body responds. My limbs tense, as though awaiting a command. Something in me wants to answer that mechanical call with motion. I place a hand against the nearest pipe, feel the rush of heated fluid within, and sense a subtle intelligence behind every swirl of steam.  

• • •

Eventually, I see them—the watchers. They are distant silhouettes perched on raised platforms, or sometimes glimpsed through the grated floors below, peering upward with glowing discs that might be eyes. I feel no immediate hostility from them, more a neutral vigilance. Once, I draw close enough to make out the shape of a torso—long-limbed, with spidery arms that end in delicate claws. It tilts its head as though curious about me. I do not run, but I do hesitate.  

In that moment, I realize I feel kinship: the watchers seem partly flesh, partly steel, as though they and I share an origin. Some are tall, with riveted armor in place of shoulders. Others scuttle on jointed limbs that splay outward like a circle. I think we might even speak, if only I could decode the mechanical hum thrumming behind their presence. But they vanish almost as soon as they appear, swinging around corners or sinking into shadows that appear too shallow to hold them.  

• • •

As I wander, small scuttlers dart across my path. They look like multi-limbed insects fashioned from polished plates, each the size of my hand—if my hand were fully human. Indeed, the longer I regard my own body, the less I recognize it. I feel plates sliding in my shoulders when I shift, a faint hiss of compressed air in my spine, and—somehow—warm blood or something akin to it pumping just beneath.  

One of the scuttlers dares to approach me. It clicks softly against the floor and regards me with a pair of faceted metal lenses. Its movements are flighty, cautious. Then, in a sudden flutter of limbs, it nudges something toward me—a scrap of metal or older gear. Some sort of peace offering, or a curious test. Tentatively, I pick it up. It’s warm to the touch. The scuttler hesitates, then skitters away, vanishing into a crack in the plating.  

I hold that piece of metal. Does it matter? Is it part of me, or part of the labyrinth? I slip it into a pouch in my side (I can’t recall if it existed before) and keep moving.  

• • •

Deeper corridors lead me past idle half-built shells. They hang from overhead hooks, suspended like corpses in a morgue. Each shape resembles a partial body: a torso plate, a twisted column of metal that might become a leg, a cradle that looks meant to serve as a skull. I feel a prickling at the back of my own neck. The watchers or some other caretaker must be responsible for these creations. Are they building them to replace decayed parts of themselves—or to create new denizens for the labyrinth’s unstoppable cycles?  

A strange compulsion draws me closer to one of these shells. It lingers in a half-lit alcove, a metallic torso cradled on a stand. I reach out and brush away a film of rust. Beneath is fresh metal: shining, unblemished. A deep hum emanates from the structure around me, as though encouraging me to join in.  

I shut my eyes and let the pounding in my veins sync with the pounding in the walls. All at once, a wave of imagery floods me: glimpses of passageways winding through the labyrinth, watchers stooped over workbenches, mechanical insects scuttling in watery cavities, and deeper still, a monstrous presence coiled around the labyrinth’s core, breathing and exhaling steam. It is the oldest occupant: some massive organ from which all function flows.  

My eyes snap open. The shell in front of me gleams in invitation, as though summoning me to complete its assembly.  

• • •

What if I am here to serve that process? No single name arises in my mind. I might be a caretaker, or a drone, or a pilgrim. The labyrinth vibrates with a consciousness that dwarfs any single inhabitant. We are all pieces on a greater circuit board, each fulfilling a role.  

With trembling, half-metal fingers, I begin working on the shell. My motions are unpracticed, but somehow I know how to align the plating, how to check the pivot points. Before I realize it, I’ve removed a gear from within my own arm and set it into place in the shell’s shoulder. There is no pain, only a sense of necessity. My internal workings hum in reconfiguration, as though a backup system automatically routes around the missing gear.  

I have contributed to the labyrinth’s next creation. And I feel…fulfilled.  

• • •

Time ceases to have meaning as I keep building. Hours or days pass in that half-lit workshop. I sense watchers nearby—they do not disturb me. Once, a tall, stilt-legged figure approaches and silently hands me a small spool of wire. I catch a glimpse of its face: a mosaic of metal plates and soft, organic patches where some kind of pale skin grows. It turns away, footsteps echoing, and leaves me with new materials to continue my work.  

At intervals, the labyrinth screams with the thunder of massive gears engaging. Everything shakes. Water drips in rivulets from overhead pipes. I cling to the half-finished shell and wait for the storm of noise to subside. When it does, there’s a new hush, and in that hush, I hear more clearly the endless respiration of the place.  

• • •

Eventually, the shell is complete, or close enough. It stands on its own. I marvel that I was able to craft it. Four limbs, a torso allowing for flexible articulation, a fused cluster of optical lenses that might serve as eyes. Then I watch as faint, pinkish fluid seeps through some interior tubing—like blood, but tinted with rust. The new creation stirs.  

Something akin to a spark lights within those lenses. Step by step, it gains stability, turning its metal head to regard me. Am I its parent? A sibling? Or a midwife assisting in a birth orchestrated by the labyrinth? I have no words for it. I just watch, breathless, heart resonating with the clang of the world. In that moment, I see bands of color swirl across its polished surface, like it is discovering each piece of itself.  

Then, with a grinding hiss, it walks away—unsteady, but purposeful. It disappears into the gloom, receding into the deeper darkness where the watchers come and go.  

• • •

I continue forward, every passage leading me deeper into the labyrinth. Along the way, I find my footprints in the grime overlapping with others, some large and broad, some small and insect-like. My own footsteps show a shifting pattern as well, as if my physiology changes—some prints have toes, others reveal segments like mechanical pads.  

I reach a threshold: a tall, cathedral-like corridor where conveyor belts churn lumps of ore and garbage from an unknown source to an unknown destination. The belts rattle, and the subterranean hum becomes almost deafening. Massive hydraulic pylons, each the size of a tower, compress and release with cyclical precision. I approach one, laying a hand on its colossal plating, and feel the labyrinth’s pulse more strongly than ever. It’s as if the place is talking to me, urging me to join, to lose what remains of “self” and merge into the flow of function.  

And I wonder: have I already? Do I remain an “I,” or have I always been a piece of the labyrinth, briefly imagining a separate life before returning to purpose?

• • •

In that moment, a new possibility stirs. As the hum intensifies, I feel shiftings in my shoulders, the dull ache of transformation that might become wings or additional limbs. My reflection in a dripping puddle of water shows a face half-obscured by plating. My eyes—still human in shape—flicker with a faint luminescence.  

I recall rumors of giant machine-beings in the labyrinth’s lowest cathedrals, but now I see how such tales might be metaphor. We are all the labyrinth, whether scuttling or towering. Its pulse is our heartbeat.  

• • •

So, where am I in this world? At times, I am a half-lost traveler, stumbling through corridors of steam and gloom. Other times, I am a caretaker, forging new forms with intuitive skill. Perhaps I am a watchful presence perched on a high gantry, scanning for disruptions. Or maybe I am an ephemeral shape glimpsed in a corner, scuttling away in a swirl of metal limbs.  

In truth, I suspect I am all of these at once. The labyrinth churns on, forging countless incarnations. We slip into roles as needed: watchers, builders, scuttling cleaners, living conduits for the labyrinth’s mysterious fluid. Our boundaries are not fixed; flesh and steel fuse under the endless drip of condensation and the sparks of whirring servos.  

Every clang of the pistons reminds me that I am a cell in this immense, ancient body. When the labyrinth exhales a gust of steam, I exhale with it. When it groans from a subterranean shift, the sound resonates in my chest. I feel no contradiction in this: each step is mine yet guided by something far greater.  

• • •

Ultimately, I have shed the notion that I came from somewhere else. Perhaps I did, but that past is washed away by the labyrinth’s living tide. I follow the pounding rhythms and create new shapes in service to an ever-evolving design. Sometimes I climb to the higher walkways and watch the watchers, perhaps to understand them or perhaps to learn from their silent oversight. Other times, I disappear into the narrow channels, exploring conduits full of scuttling insect-forms that pass by me unafraid—they sense I share their origin.  

And in the darkest depths, I sometimes hear or glimpse the colossal presence: a fusion of steel bones and wet, pulsing flesh. Its breath shakes the walls; its heartbeat is the quake that ripples beneath my feet. I have never seen it plainly—I end up glimpsing great tubings and cavernous orifices that spout steam, but I cannot put it all together in one coherent image. It is too vast. Yet I know I am a part of it—it acknowledges me in the faint lull between crescendos, as though we share an unspoken bond.  

• • •

Who am I in this world? I am an extension of the labyrinth, a living node among many. I may once have had a name and carried stories of a distant outside, but those details dissolve in the eternal hush of dripping water and the clang of unending machinery. I am a hybrid of flesh and steel, caretaker and occupant, observer and participant. My hands craft new shells that march off into the gloom, continuing the labyrinth’s unstoppable processes. I scuttle with the insects, I watch from the catwalks, I dream in half-remembered human thoughts while my arms hiss with piston-like pulses.  

And I am content. Even in the roar of pounding presses and the darkness illuminated only by flickering bulbs, I feel a sense of belonging. Perhaps one day, I will become one of the towering colossi in the depths, or maybe I will recede into the labyrinth’s silent corners and become a subtle caretaker, forging new limbs and mechanical hearts. Or perhaps I will dissolve into the machinery altogether, my consciousness mingling with the labyrinth’s overarching hum.  

In the end, it is all the same. We are the labyrinth. Its metal halls and endless processes define our shape. We move because it moves; it moves because we feed its heartbeat with our presence. Life here appears in many forms: watchers and insects, half-human ghosts and forging drones. I, too, am no single shape, but a shifting reflection of the labyrinth’s will. Yes, I persist with a faint, lingering sense of “I,” but it’s only a faint echo of memory. The labyrinth is the truer identity.  

And it is alive—whether in the creaking of rusted beams, the swirl of cold, humid air, or a thousand footsteps scuttling through the dripping dark. So I give myself to it, piece by piece, breath by breath. What else could be more natural, here in the endless storm of gears and water, than to become part of the only living organism that truly spans every corridor?
