At first, I can’t decide if I possess bones or if I’m just squalling water given temporary shape. Some days, I feel the hardness of vertebrae along my spine—an anchor that keeps me upright, clinging to the stone spires whenever a fresh torrent tries to wash me away. Other times, my limbs flow like a riptide, flexing in ways that seem impossible for solid bone. But here—in this realm of slippery creation—I think the question of “what am I?” might never resolve into final certainty.

I started as a sort of echo, or so I believe. I remember a flicker of a memory: a high vantage point above the swirling ocean, a vantage that might have been from another time or world. Perhaps I was an observer or a traveler who arrived too early, while the realm was still embryonic. But I lingered too long, or I stepped too close; the watery chaos folded around me, pressed me into itself, and shaped me into something new. My old existence left me like a receding tide, replaced by a fluid awareness of this perpetual cycle of birth and re-birth.

Initially, I hovered—uncertain—among drifting lumps of nearly living matter. At times, I fused with them, half-forgetting my distinctness. I might have stayed that way—an aimless swirl in a churning sea—had I not found the faint impression of footlike structures or the notion of fingers. Somehow, I liked the idea of having digits to grasp a stone edge. And so, step by step, I realized: I want to climb, to cling. And so, out of will or necessity, my body coalesced around that desire. I became gangly, amphibious—a shape reminiscent of those other lank-limbed dwellers.

When the watery columns crash down in relentless arcs, I flatten my body against fresh stone outcrops. My skin or body-film, or whatever it is, changes color to mirror the swirling water overhead—pale and opalescent one moment, streaked with dark ripples the next. If a swirl trembles too powerfully, I slide into a fissure in the rock, letting the deluge pass. Other times, I choose to remain half-submerged, allowing the water to carve away the top layer of my outer membrane. The sensation is both exhilarating and terrifying: my boundary dissolves, but I feel more intimately connected to this place each time it happens.

Over there, across the gap between two massive, newly-forged ridges, I spot a group of amphibian-humanoids with similarly willowy builds. They beam subtle, iridescent glances at me—a greeting, perhaps, or a sign of curiosity. I attempt their posture: a crouch, shoulders folded forward so the surging wave overhead touches only my back’s ridgeline. The wave roars by; the stone beneath my toes vibrates, and new air pockets form in the cracks behind me with a huge, wet sucking sound. One of the amphibians waves, then scuttles away before the next cascade hits.

Sometimes, I think I could drift in the water overhead, become one of the sleek serpentine swimmers. I feel a stirring in my limbs, a yearning to let them elongate and meld into fins. But then I remember how I love to feel the freshly minted rock under my palms, how I relish the friction that reminds me there is a “me” distinct from the world. So I remain this spindly in-betweener—for now. Perhaps tomorrow, or an epoch from now (time is so strange here), I’ll abandon limbs for fins, or wings for weaving ephemeral webs. Here, form is an ongoing conversation between my desire to exist and the realm’s unstoppable flux.

Clinging to what might charitably be called an outcrop “ledge,” I notice old stone remnants—the shapes of mud huts or pulp shelters. They are fractal illusions of habitation. Curiosity impels me to crawl inside what’s left of a half-formed archway. The interior is cooler, the roar subdued. Plant tendrils—those pale, half-committed leaves—line the walls, quivering with their own uncertain existence. I notice half a table: just a slab of stone supported by lumps of clay. I place my hand on it, watch as rivulets of water run across my wrist and drip from the edges. For a heartbeat, it all feels domestic—like a home. But no sooner do I sense that comfort than a tectonic groan shudders through the realm, and I scramble out again, hopeful the entire structure won’t collapse on me.

I greet others—rare as it is to catch them still. There’s a spidery weaver perched near the top of a newly thrust column, spinning ribbons of watery silk that glisten for one fragile moment before dissolving. I see no eyes on it, just a swirl of limbs and a sense of tireless purpose. Now and again, it sets a thread, only for the next wave to tear it free. The weaver and I share a look that might be resigned amusement—this is our epic in microcosm. Build, lose, rebuild. I lift a hand in a kind of salute, my own watery skin shimmering in acknowledgment, and then move on.

Wandering further, I slide through a shallower area where the water’s overhead weight is gentler—a luminous corridor forged by a spontaneous vacuum bubble. A handful of crystalline insects flutter in a narrow beam of daylight that somehow pierces the swirling overhead. Their wings make music, a shimmering, glassy hum that resonates in the hush. I pause, entranced, and for a while, I wonder: could I become one of them instead? To flit on that light, living only in the edges of the next wave, gleaning nectar from trembling leaves and vanishing in a swirl of refracted colors. The idea entrances me—but then the bubble shifts, a new wave surges in, and those insects are thrust into churned water, disappearing from my view.

Yet there’s peace woven into the chaos. Despite the roaring collisions, the endless sloughing of rock and cresting of new spurs, there is a rhythm—like a universal heartbeat. I can’t say whether it belongs to the realm or if it echoes from my own chest (assuming I have one). But it’s there, pulsing in the spaces between thunderous waves, urging me onward, a gentle internal tide that says: stay. Become. Erode. Grow. Re-form.

And so, I remain. Each day, I test the boundaries of my shape: letting my hands flatten into webbing when the currents grow too strong, allowing my torso to compress so I can squeeze into a wet fissure and shield myself from a crashing surge. When the realm is momentarily calm—a fleeting lull—I examine the half-built huts, the lumps of driftwood pulp, the hammered clay fragments. Maybe I, too, will try to construct something, to leave my imprint. Perhaps a modest arch to shield a small pocket of calm. I have begun to gather bits of watery resin secreted by the spidery weavers, plus stones that look as though they’ve survived more than a few re-formations. Something in me yearns to shape and mold, even though I know it’ll be undone eventually.

Ultimately, maybe my “home” is not a structure but the act of participating in this endless creation and destruction. That’s the closest sense of belonging I’ve ever felt. I am certain that tomorrow, I might choose to shed these amphibious arms and become something else—serpentine, aerial, or entirely fluid. Or I might remain as I am, forging ephemeral shelters, forging fleeting friendships with the wide-eyed amphibian folk. In this place, all living beings, all forms, drift along the same unstoppable current of perpetual change. We are neither fully realized nor undone. In every wave, every instant, the world and its dwellers co-create one another.

Here, I am perpetually on the cusp, as if the very concept of “I” is an experiment this world conducts through me. I am fluid. I cling. I climb. I become. And though I may never know what final shape this realm will take—if it ever truly finishes becoming—I am content to dance within its swirling waters, forging fragile structures, savoring each breath, each moment of brilliance, in a place that pulses with unending possibility.
