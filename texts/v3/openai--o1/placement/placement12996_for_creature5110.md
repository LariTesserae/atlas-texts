At first, I catch my own reflection in a blade of water-slick metal and do not recognize the shape. I’m not sure when I arrived, nor do I clearly remember a life beyond these cliffs. Perhaps I have always been here, though each time that thought arises, it dissipates like steam from the hidden boilers. In the brilliance of the light, I move as if newly risen from the moss—a figure shaped by the valley’s hush and damp chill.

Sometimes I think I’m human, or that I was once. I feel the memory of breath in my lungs; I recall the warmth of a sun that may or may not have shone above some other land. Other times, I contemplate the metallic flicker in my periphery—wires or struts glimmering where muscle should be, a subtle hum that seems to emanate from my core. My limbs can arch and extend in ways that startle me, as though I never practiced the motion before. It is an uncanny fluidity, a crossed boundary between flesh and machine. I do not feel separate from this strangeness, though; I simply exist in it, neither disturbed nor entirely comforted.

It is possible I am a caretaker, though caretaker of what remains uncertain. I find myself gliding along the rusted walkways, pressing my hand to the riveted metal railings—feeling for the faint vibrations in the structure’s bones. The moss clings to my ankles; the vines brush my arms. Sometimes they leave behind glistening droplets on the filaments of my clothes, which are themselves an odd patchwork: heavy woven fabric, encrusted in places with a coppery residue that might once have been paint or metal plating. There are shapes reminiscent of herons or stylized windmills, but so faded that I can’t recall if they were ever entire logos. I may even have helped thread these garments together from scraps. Now, they’re as much a part of me as my own skin.

I move without crisis or hurry. The hush invites a deliberate gait, and I’ve learned to answer it. My footsteps make only the smallest whisper of contact with the metal or the stone, as though the air has thickened beneath my soles. Time here is a slow spool, unwinding with a measured softness. A single droplet of water can hold onto a leaf’s edge for an age before it finally falls and disappears into a patch of glowing lichen.

When I tend to the vegetation, I do so with a kind of practiced ritual. Somewhere, perhaps in the back of my mind, is a directive: certain vines must not overtake certain panels, certain outcrops must be kept free of debris, certain wires need occasional adjustments. I follow these instructions without resentment or confusion. The tasks feel as old as the wind that slips across the ridges. I don’t question them any more than I question the shape of my hands.

And my hands—sometimes they startle me. When I clamp them around a thick vine and pull, my fingers lock into precise positions, slender and jointed in a way that might surpass typical humanity. The vine yields with a silent tear; it’s easier to shift than I expected. Carrying away its trailing length, I sense a quiet synergy with the valley, as though it is exhaling in relief when I clear the path. I coil the vine and set it aside. Time passes. Perhaps I rest, watching the condensation form on the remainder of the creeper, feeling the faint thrums of a subterranean machine somewhere below.

There are moments when I come upon the watchers, half-consumed industrial contraptions that peer with unlidded metal eyes. They are perched on outcrops or hidden in watery basins, corroded casings swallowed by damp. I sense them scanning—for temperature shifts, for ripe signals in the electricity, for changes in chemical composition that might matter. I never speak to them, and they never speak to me, but each mechanical shutter of an iris feels like a greeting all the same. Sometimes I wonder if these watchers record my presence. If they piece together my daily routine into data strings, do they recognize me as part of the environment? Or am I an anomaly?

In certain hours, I catch glimpses of the small winged creatures high in the canopy of vines. They are fleeting arcs of color—blue, gold, sometimes shimmering green—almost illusions in the bright air. If I’m still enough, one might land near me, perched on a bent girder or a coil of wire. We look at each other in the hush. The bird-thing cocks its head, regarding my elongated fingers or the faint glow beneath my collar. Perhaps it thinks me a living pillar of metal and cloth. Then a breath of wind, or a faint sigh from a hidden pump, sends it flickering out of sight again.

Dusk arrives, though it does so slowly, as if the valley itself must carefully dim the sky. Shadows stretch across the uneven stone, crisp and silent. I feel relief in the gradual cooling of the air—as though my own inner wiring prefers this alignment of temperature. The caretaker within me, or perhaps the caretaker that I am, stirs. I move to check a half-collapsed station nestled in the rockface. Its corrugated roof curls inward like a giant leaf browning at the edges. I slip through the parted metal, stepping lightly over moss-tufted rubble. My fingers trace the cracked controls on a wall panel, searching for an indicator or a faint glow that might signal a functional circuit. Sometimes a buried red light still flickers, or a dial trembles in reaction to water pressure. I don’t try to fix anything beyond small calibrations. I perform the maintenance that keeps certain valves from corroding shut, that ensures a minimal current continues to flow. I’m not sure why this matters—yet, it does. I’d no sooner abandon these tasks than I’d forget to breathe.

In the near-dark, there’s a vantage point I prefer, a place where a small catwalk juts over a gorge. From there, I can watch a thread of silver water weaving in and out of the stone below. It sparkles with quiet intensity when the moon (or something like a moon) rises. I rest my arms on the rusted railing, careful where I place my hands, and let my gaze fall on the valley’s sleeping sprawl. Tendrils of mist drift between the boulders. Distant shapes—other caretakers?—move along the edges of the ruins. Occasionally, I see them pause to listen to a sighing vent, or to gently prune a creeping vine. We exist in the same hush, though we rarely come close. Perhaps we communicate on a register I have no conscious awareness of: an unspoken resonance carried on the underlying hum of ancient machinery.

Night passes gently. When the sun returns, I sense a subtle pulse in my limbs, as if the valley’s energy stirs within me. I might go to the moss-laden turbines hidden further up the slope to check a mechanism that only reveals itself at dawn. Moving among the ferns, I see the amphibious creatures with their translucent skin, pulses of light throbbing in time with the day’s new radiance. They do not startle when they see me approach—I am part of their environment, living in the hush. They flick open beaded eyes, momentarily capturing the glow. Then, with a languid ripple, they vanish into trickling water. I feel a kinship with their silent acceptance.

Sometimes I encounter a caretaker who seems more obviously mechanical than I. A glint of steel or copper plates might reflect from beneath tattered clothing. Our eyes meet: a faint mechanical hum resonates in the air. We share no words, but I sense a moment of acknowledgement—an agreement, even. Our tasks interlock, though I’ve never confirmed what exactly they are. When we part ways, the hush closes behind both of us, as though swallowing our presence until the next meeting.

Am I an echo of the valley’s first inhabitants? A descendant of the engineers who built these walkways, chipped away at the rock, and hammered rivets into failing metal? Or was I made—assembled piece by piece—to endure cold nights and bright days, quietly ensuring the place never slips into total chaos? The question rises sometimes, like a half-memory. Then a swirl of mist or a rustle of vine distracts me, and I let the thought dissolve. The hush dissuades me from too much speculation.

Looking at my reflection again in a pool of condensation, I see algae drifting across my own face. My features remain indistinct beneath the distorted light. Something within me stirs, questioning if there was ever a before. Perhaps my existence began the moment I noticed the hush and realized I could move through it. Perhaps I was always part of the green tapestry—a living, breathing cog in this half-biological, half-mechanical system that extends across the valley.

No matter. My role becomes clear whenever I see a patch of vines strangling a vital cable or hear a faint stutter in the pumping of an old station. I find myself drawn to correct it, gleaning small instructions from the hammered plates and half-alert watchers. There is a calm in fulfilling these tasks, a sense of alignment with the hush. Each timed calibration, each measured step, each cable gently freed from a mossy snarl, strengthens the balance. I do not need to question that sense of belonging.

And so, if you were to approach me as I make my rounds—if you dared to tread softly enough not to break the valley’s near-silence—you would see me as a figure neither wholly human nor plainly machine, clothing patched over joints that move with effortless grace. You might wonder, in that flickering moment, whether I once belonged to a society beyond the cliffs. Or, on the contrary, if I sprang straight from the bedrock like a living artifact, shaped by the timbre of trickling water and the static hum of dormant power.

Should you linger and watch me trim back the vines, or carefully reposition a corroded panel so it still channels some thin pulse of energy, you might sense that I have not decided my nature either. It is a fluid, day-by-day becoming—fused with the environment’s heartbeat, anchored to a hush that feels timeless and essential.

And if I were to speak, I think I would do so in whispers so soft they slid beneath the threshold of hearing, as if language itself might unsettle the equilibrium. But I rarely speak. Instead, I let the hush shape me further, as I shape it in turn. Perhaps that is the truest answer to who and what I am in this place: I am the caretaker of silence, the living keeper of these ferns and silent wires, devoted to the soft balance that holds the valley in its thrall, awake yet dreaming… as much a woven thread in the tapestry as the ancient watchers, the flickering amphibians, and the drifting avian shadows overhead.
