I dwell at the base of the great stone mound—close enough to feel the warmth seep from the rock, yet far enough to watch the settlement spread its braided arms of rope above. If you asked me where I come from or how I arrived here, I’m not certain I could answer in any coherent sense of time. It feels as though I have always been part of this place, like a shadow clinging to the side of a weathered cliff. I’m inseparable from the dust and the vine, from the dim glow that’s never quite day or night. Here, I simply am.

Sometimes, I feel as old as the rock itself. My skin is cut by time and scoured by wind, callused thick in the corners of my palms where I grip rope or chisel stone. My eyes, narrowed since youth, see everything in soft edges. I’ve learned to detect the faintest shift in the gloom—a new crack forming along a house foundation, or a slight thickening of vine that beckons the next harvest. I feel the hush of a predator’s approach before it ever reveals itself on a cliff ledge. And I know when the creatures in the hollows will fall silent in unison, as if the entire settlement holds its breath.

But then I look at my hands and see they’re not so wrinkled yet. The lines carved into my knuckles tell of just a few decades shaping slabs of rock and learning the ways of splicing vine into rope. In a hundred more years, I will be older and slower, but here that length of time doesn’t seem daunting; we breathe our years in measured increments. We’re shaped by patient generations of stone on stone, just as the land was shaped by lost eras of wind and flood that no one living remembers. Our entire history is an echo, caught in the bones of this canyon.

Sometimes, I climb one of the rope bridges to the higher terraces—there’s an abandoned walkway there where the vines stretch thick. At that altitude, I can gaze back down over the labyrinth of blocky houses, each one snug against the other. The settlement looks tangled, almost chaotic, yet I’ve walked each narrow passage enough to know the paths by heart—how they spiral around communal ovens, wind beneath small overhead courtyards, and then drop abruptly near shrines of stacked stones. From above, you see the slow rhythms: a craftsman chiseling a doorframe, a child dragging a bristly creature on a tether, a group of elders kneading dough in a sunken courtyard that’s never quite dark yet never bright. All these little sequences form a grand tapestry of motion.

To me, the vines are as much guardians as scaffolds. I often pause and rest my cheek on the leathery fronds, feeling their soft heat. They feed on the same radiance we do, pulling nourishment from the bedrock. Whenever I tie a rope or weave a basket, I marvel at how these plants persist without a drop of water, how they cling to the stone with unrelenting strength. In that way, I like to think we humans have learned from them. We, too, cling fiercely to this life, thriving in an equilibrium that would confound anyone who has known rivers or bright, scorching suns.

Most days, I help shore up the precarious structures perched just above the settlement’s main passage. While the older stonemasons chip away at expansions, I slip between the cracks, clearing old debris and lashing new supports into place. My greatest responsibility is ensuring that each family’s home remains secure—no avalanche of rock, no snapping vine, no mislaid slab. I carry a little hammer chiseled from basalt, chipped black and polished smooth from use. People recognize me by its rhythmic ring as I tap along a corridor, testing for hidden fissures in the wall. Rhythm is crucial here—a way to find the voids that lurk beneath stone. The dust that settles on my arms glitters like flecks of mica, turning me momentarily into a living statue as I work.

I’ve seen the luminous insects that light the caverns deeper in the cliffs, and I’ve added their glowing husks to the edges of a tapestry I’m weaving. On nights when the chorus of creatures burrows in my ears—when chirps and hisses collide in a riot of sound—I set that tapestry on my lap, letting the faint luminescence guide my stitches. A hush sometimes falls over the settlement if a large shape darkens the far canyon rim; then, in the stillness, I can hear only the gentle scratch of needle on thread. In those moments, I’m reminded how fragile we are, perched in such a place with no water to sustain us, only the slow well of heat that rises from depths we cannot see.

And yet we endure, not through force but by embracing the deliberate pace of life here. We don’t conquer the rock—we shape ourselves to it, forging scare little expansions from the land’s sloping generosity. If I could peer backward through time, I might imagine a roiling river once carved these chasms, or imagine ephemeral storms lashing these heights. But all that’s left are newborn dust layers salted by memory. We abide in the final remainder, a hush that thrums with subtle life instead of dryness or emptiness.

So who am I in this world? Call me a rope-weaver, a low-tier stonemason, a part-time caretaker of the vines. Call me a caretaker of children’s laughter, one who guides them away from open cliffs. Sometimes I’m a gatherer of dust and mineral shards—small bits of pigment for the potters. Other times, I’m a listener, crouching by the courtyard fires to hear old tales from the elders, storing their words in the back of my mind so they travel forward in story, even after I pass. I have no single title, no single purpose. I fit myself to the settlement’s needs as seamlessly as vines do to the stone.

Were you to visit, you might think we live in monotony, for it’s always the same faintly dim hours, the same near-constant warmth rising from the foundations. But there is a subtlety to existence here—a new crack in the cliff face, a fresh swirl in the dust, a creature’s newly hatched brood skittering out to greet the settlement. Each day, I notice changes so minute they’d escape an untrained eye, but these little changes animate our world. Each nuance feels like a cosmic event in a land where nothing is rushed and everything is purposeful.

And so I suppose I am just one thread in a vast tapestry pinned to the canyon walls. I am stone and dust, vine and breath, a reflection of the settlement and the land that cradles us. You could find me standing with chisel in hand at a half-built wall, or high on a rope bridge, letting the warm dryness fill my lungs. You’d see my hair bound in stiff braids coiled close to my head, my tunic woven from the same sinewy fibers that sprawl across the stone. And if I glanced your way, I would greet you quietly, as we do here, with no grand flourish—just a gentle nod that says: “You have found your way to the heart of the canyon’s final form, and so have I. Let us share in the steady, patient life that beats beneath this dust.”
