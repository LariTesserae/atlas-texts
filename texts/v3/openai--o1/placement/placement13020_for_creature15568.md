I find that I am neither purely an observer nor am I something I would have recognized in my old world—here, the transformations of this half-lit realm have shaped my being into a creature perfectly at home in the vast cavernous spaces. I awaken in a niche of rock where the trickle of condensation gathers into a rivulet that splashes gently against my hindlegs. My eyes open to dimness, and yet this dimness is alive, shimmering in my vision with subtle washes of faint color. I sense the drip-beat of water as if it were the ticking of a clock, though not a clock that measures seconds or minutes. It measures the growth of stalactites, the quiet breath of algae blooms, the slow, cyclical gatherings of life.

My skin is translucent in places, revealing delicate webs of red and purple vessels. Hints of pinkish gills fan out from behind my head—a vestige of amphibian heritage, perhaps, or a morphological quirk meant for living somewhere that is neither fully aquatic nor entirely dry. The gills do not always flutter. They quiver slightly when the dampness rises, allowing me to draw upon the moisture-laden air. At times, I submerge myself in the shallow pools, letting the water flow in and around those frilled edges. I suppose you might call me an amphibian, but to me, that name is only a ghost of some outside taxonomy. I am simply a living node in the slow dance of this place.

My limbs are short, but strong enough to carry me across the rocky ground. The ground here is gritty, pockmarked by the footprints or belly-trails of all the other inhabitants—endless insect tracks, the slither-lines of small lizards, and the occasional wide-lobed mark left by those larger, deep-voiced elders in the unseen recesses. I can place my forelimbs in the hollows formed by the steady passage of countless small feet, and I feel the pulses of life that hum below. Even in the dryness where no water pools, there is a subtle continuity between me and the stone, each of us an expression of the same ancient hush.

I do not speak in words or call out a name for myself, yet I know how to sing my presence in short, clicking notes. It’s something akin to a frog’s cry, but softer—a glottal note that resonates off the rock and returns to me. Sometimes, in the stillness, I let out a trill, and I hear a faint echo respond. Whether that echo is simply stone or a cousin of my species hidden a few dozen yards away, I cannot always tell. Sometimes I’ll wait for a second answer, suspecting that an unseen friend or rival crouches behind a column, listening.

When I move from my favorite puddle—where pale, creeping algae cling to the rock, giving me a ready supply of microbial snacks—I do so by stretches and starts, never in a hurry. The dryness between puddles can stretch for several yards, and I have learned that precipitous leaps might strand me in a place where no condensation remains. So I move in increments, carefully flicking out my lengthy, flexible tongue to test the air. Long whisker-like barbels hang from my cheeks and pulse with the humidity, letting me “taste” the water content before I commit to a path. If the air is too arid, I back away and wait for another tiny rivulet, trusting that the natural cycles will bring the dripping water closer to my position eventually. Patience is the first law of this realm.

At times, I sense the winged shapes flitting above, crossing through the half-light to clamor among the cracks. When the chance presents itself, I tilt my head to catch an unlucky straggler with a quick snap of my jaws. Their flight is erratic, so it’s a game of patience and practiced reflex. Mostly, though, I scavenge among the muddy edges of puddles for smaller larvae or cluster flies too slow to escape. If I’m lucky, I’ll find a patch of slug eggs. My gullet quivers at the memory of that dense, gelatinous meal—dense with energy for the next phase of my cycle.

In my seasons of growth, I carried a brood of my own within tiny translucent lumps along my sides, each one a watery cradle for a new generation. The lumps swelled when the humidity was right, and I could feel deep vibrations as the eggs matured. Eventually, each pair of shining eyes would peer out at the watery world, preparing to emerge. One by one, they broke free, dropping to the moist ground. It was a subtle event—no dramatic departure, only a hush, a ripple. They squirmed away to the protective gloom beneath a clump of fungal stalks, their new gills flickering. Those who survived have no doubt continued that cycle, adding their voices to the subtle thrumming tapestry of echoes. That’s how generations turn beneath this undisturbed stone.

I know every damp cranny near my territory, every crack that leads to deeper chambers where the fishlike creatures swim with their extravagant gill-frills, every shallow slope where the isopods gather in their roving caravans, antennae weaving signals to one another. Sometimes I creep near them, quietly observing how they meet at intersections, bowing and exchanging a flicker of their delicate feelers. None mind my presence unless I draw too close to their young, and I rarely do. We exist in a web of shared acceptance: enough space, enough moisture, enough quiet for all.

Sometimes I pause in an open stretch of rock and press my body low, letting the ragged, half-colorless ferns brush against my flanks. The droplets that cling to their leaves cool me and trickle down the ridges of my skin. In that moment, I belong to the stone and the water and the hush. The commotion of a thousand insects, the flitting bats-not-bats above, and the distant moan—for me, these are as normal and soothing as the hush of wind might be on the outside. They cradle my thoughts until I feel no boundary between my small existence and the endless cycling of water, rock, and breath.

I am but one thread in a vast tapestry of voices, and that is not lamentation but comfort. The hush here is a living hush, an all-encompassing patience that I share. If I name myself anything at all, it is not a separate name—it is a single note within the greater resonate chord, shaped by this freshly opened stone and the timeless drip of water. I am the amphibious thread in the loom of this realm. I glide through puddles, sing short clicks that bounce off walls, and drift through the unchanging cycles with neither hurry nor fear. Time itself flows around me like the water that seeps through the cracked ceiling. And so I live in this newly cracked domain, neither fully in water nor entirely on land, carrying the hush of ancient rhythms in every slow blink of my glassy eyes.
