I live in the high places where the stone is rawest—where new basalt can slice cloth and flesh if one is careless. My home is tucked within the westernmost district of the settlement, close by a narrow outcrop overlooking a sheer drop. From that ledge, I can watch the swirling clouds unspool like ghostly serpents, drifting through clefts in the rock. Some mornings, before the haze fully stirs with day’s first breath, I step out onto the damp basalt and feel the world tilt beneath my feet, as though this entire plateau still floats in the amniotic hush of its own becoming.

I was a child of the settlement’s rope-makers, but as I grew, I found my calling not in the twisting of fibrous strands but in the quiet companionship of the overhead vines. There, colossal insects gather, fractal-limbed silhouettes shimmering in the gloom. I have learned to recognize each species by the rhythm of its steps, the textures of its shell. Some droop from the vines like living chandeliers, their outstretched legs bridging one stone spire to the next. Others coil and uncoil with a ripple, so slight you’d mistake them for branches if not for the faint glints on their translucent wings.

I have become, in my own small way, their caretaker. Not a tamer, exactly—we do not tame these creatures, for they are far beyond us—but a friend who makes the trust between plateau and human a little sturdier. When the insects shed segments of their carapace, I climb up the slippery vines, carefully retrieving the abandoned shells. Our smiths prize them—carapace plates are light, strong, and oddly resonant when struck. The fragments become armor patches for those who must venture deeper into the forest, or delicate ornamentation around the city’s crude torches. It is delicate work, hopping from one protruding basalt ledge to the next, gathering shimmering shells while never startling their massive, luminous owners.

I am neither elder nor official. My voice does not weigh heavily in the communal gatherings. But I attend all the same, often perched quietly at the edge of the semicircle of stone seats while the elders discuss how best to divert the next wave of condensation into cisterns, or how to repair the stilted huts battered by storms. Though I do not speak often, I watch—ever watch—and bring what scraps of knowledge I can: how certain insects predict the arrival of seasonal rains by weaving sticky webs across open funnels in the vine canopy, how the faint clicking in the spore-caves sometimes heralds the departure of the wide-winged moths—those same moths we count on to pollinate the succulent tubers in the terraces.

Like most, I wear layered robes dyed in the colors of the plateau: deep charcoal with patterns of emerald and moss. A broad hood shades my brow, for the luminous spores I harvest each evening can be painfully bright when gathered in large clusters. At dusk, I venture along precarious footpaths that wind around rock towers and hidden gullies, guided by the faint glow of spore pods beneath overhanging rocks. Sometimes, I pluck the pods gently from the damp clefts to bring them back to the city’s planters, where we try to cultivate them in stone troughs brimming with filtered drizzle. Their bioluminescent glow brightens the settlement’s corridors with a gentle radiance—a counterpart to our flickering torchlight.

When I am not collecting spore pods or retrieving insect shells, I help coax water into our cisterns. Everyone does, at one time or another, because water here is precious beyond measure. We set out small troughs and ceramic basins in the corners of the city’s courtyards. At the first hint of a silver drizzle, I run outside with a funnel in hand, channeling every drop into the clay jars that line our workshops. In the hush of an overcast day, these droplets sound like tiny bells—a soft, persistent chorus that merges with the tapping of chisels on basalt. It is our music, ephemeral but consistent, binding us to this plateau’s rhythms.

Though it can be lonely to spend so much time above the rooftops and along the cliff edges, my heart feels most alive in those hidden corners. The hush is deeper there, broken only by the whisper of wind through spiny leaves or the furtive crackling of small amphibians among the ferns. Sometimes, a gargantuan moth will glide by, spraying a dust of shimmering scales across the gloom. Or a shadow will ripple across the rock walls—perhaps a silhouette of one of those tall, antlered creatures creeping toward farmland. In those moments, I pause, breath caught in my throat, as if I have suddenly become aware that the plateau itself is watching me.

By night, after my foraging is done and my rope-bridges coiled away, I descend once more to the heart of the city. The torches there cast dancing gold flickers against sharpened basalt columns and half-carved figurines waiting for the mallet and chisel’s final kiss. Often, I’ll stand for a moment in front of one of these half-finished statues—perhaps it depicts a sprawling insect or vine-wrapped cervid—and I feel a kinship so strong that I almost expect the stone to move. Under the swirling cloud cover, with the hush settled thick as moss over every surface, I sometimes believe that if I tried, I could speak with the plateau directly.

I sleep in a simple hut, its frame built around an outcrop that juts through the living quarters. My bed is a thin mat of reed fiber softened by moss spun into cloth, and above me, in the rocky roof, there is a small aperture, a window on the sky. Through it, I hear the distant sounds of giant insects scuttling overhead or the hushed flapping of nocturnal wings. Sometimes, lightning blooms distantly, painting the spires in brilliant white for an instant before the dark reclaims them. In that flash, the sense of strangeness returns—the realization that all of this might be a dream perched on a precarious ledge above reality.

But then morning arrives in a pearly glow, and I can see the elders already rising, children running to gather the biggest mushrooms from the moss banks, the farmland watchers guiding the antlered giants. I feel the basalt under my feet, newly wet from the night’s condensation, and know that we are indeed real—real in our daily tasks, our unspoken reverence for the swirling clouds and craggy spires. And in that tangible moment, my calling is renewed. I slip on my hood, tie my carved token around my waist—a spiral etched from a mantic insect’s shell—and step into the hush, part of this tapestry of raw stone, cloud, and living wonders.

This is who I am, in this hidden sky-bound realm—an insect-whisperer, a gatherer of luminescence, a walker of precarious edges. Just like the plateau itself, I feel half-formed and fully alive, perched between awe and reverence, forging my own small balance in a place that is both newly conjured and unthinkably old. And every time an insect’s carapace glints overhead, or a silver drizzle patters onto the basalt, I remember: here, in this place of hush and slow cycles, we are each part of something vast and intricate—a living mosaic on the world’s rim.
