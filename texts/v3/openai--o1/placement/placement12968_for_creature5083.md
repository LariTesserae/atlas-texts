I wake each cycle in the same interval of hum. It is neither morning nor evening—there is no sun here, no overhead star to mark the hours—only fluctuations in the thrum of old machines that echo through the chambers. That rhythm is my day, my night, my perpetual clock. I rise from a sheltered nook beneath a half-buried conduit, resting my back against a plate of metal that’s still miraculously warm. I do not recall precisely when I first came to be here: it feels as though I have always existed in this sealed pocket of dust and wires.

––––––––––––––––

Sometimes I think I am another of these chitinous inhabitants, crawling across rocky ceilings and battered machines. I move like them—on four jointed limbs that grip stone with a surprising ease, small hooking claws scuttling across sharp angles. Yet, the purple flicker of my carapace seems slow to respond whenever the swarm breaks into color signals. I watch them from a distance, my shell shimmering a fraction behind their tempo. Perhaps my internal hum does not sync perfectly with the chamber’s big engines. Perhaps I was once something else, fused into this form by the dust-laden air, until I half-remember ways of walking upright. In rare, unsteady moments, my spine twitches, and I think I can still stand as tall as an off-world traveler. But the memory dissolves as quickly as it arrives.

––––––––––––––––

On other days, though, I realize that I am no mere swarm-dweller. My skin is not quite right, too soft in crevices, too metallic at the edges. My face bears the faint contour of an old mask: if I touch it, I can feel ridges that once might have been plating. My eyes blink behind a half-organic film—unlike the purely compound orbs of the scuttling masses. I do not fully belong here, but I do not belong anywhere else, either. 

There is a stirring in my chest that aligns with the endless pumping of those unseen machines. Whenever I pause to breathe in this place (breathing the slow, heavy air that barely has fresh oxygen), I feel the dust swirl inside me. It coats my throat and lines my lungs with a curious warmth. Strangely, I do not cough. Instead, it becomes part of me, feeding me like a living tapestry of mineral motes. I sit sometimes, perched atop a battered console, letting the swirl of dust drift in front of my compound-meets-human gaze. I watch the other dwellers filter it, store it, spin it into their shells until the dust itself becomes a cohesive layer against the dryness. Sometimes I wonder: am I an intruder? Or am I the next iteration of these silent caretakers?

––––––––––––––––

When I move through the corridors, my steps do not echo as loudly as I would expect. Over time, I learned how to tread lightly, not disturbing the trill of insect chatter that overlays the thunderous groan of old turbines. My presence, though, always draws curious glances from the scuttling masses. They pause in their gentle siphoning of wires or in their quiet stacking of metallic scraps to cast a wave of color pulses my way. My exoskeleton flickers in shaky response—like a partial bow. My flicker is never quite as coordinated or bright, but they accept it for what it is. Their clickings resume, and I feel breathless relief. 

I understand so little of their silent code. Oftentimes, I’ll venture deeper into the labyrinth in hope of gleaning some spark of knowledge from a half-lit console or a sealed gate that opens at the press of my limbs. Bits of old data flicker on screens too dusty to read; half of the signage is in a script I cannot decipher, a language dissolved by time. Yet, something in the swirling lights intrigues me. I sense that if I could just read those patterns, I might remember who I was. I might know the reason this place still vibrates with energy instead of lying inert.

––––––––––––––––

Down the corridors with cracked floors—where no water flows and no moisture stirs—I have encountered the serpentine watchers. I only catch them on the edge of vision, or see a flash of glinting eye behind a grated vent. Sustained by some deeper source of heat, these watchers coil in the darkest recesses where half-rusted pipes still sigh. They are rarer than the chitinous swarms, and perhaps more secretive. I’ve heard their low growl, felt its resonance in my hollow chest. It is an echo within an echo, and it lingers in my bones. Sometimes, I think they know me better than I know myself. In the hush of certain side tunnels, I sense they call me by a name I no longer recall—some guttural syllable shaped by the echoes in the rock.

“Sshrarha… Sshrarha…” It might be a greeting, or a warning. Or perhaps they are only breathing. Yet the syllables vibrate through the metal-laced dust as though searching for me. The moment I turn to look, they vanish—slithering back into the gloom, leaving behind only their voice. 

––––––––––––––––

The tall, wiry plants or “trees” (such as they are) loom in sparse clumps. Spires of bone-like mineral stand defiant in a realm that should not offer them life, yet they cling to every stray trace of energy. At their bases, thousands of tiny metallic mites cluster, swirling in a pooling tide. I’ve grown entranced by these mites on occasion, crouching low to examine their shared movements. They shimmer like droplets of old mercury that never fully fuse into a single mass. If I extend a limb, they sometimes glide onto my shell or over the fleshy parts of my hand. They taste me with minute appendages that tickle like static sparks. 

I feel them gleaning something from me—the capacity for speech, or the trace of my uncertain memories. Then, gently, they leave with a mesmerizing swirl, returning to the plant’s fibrous trunk. If they pass along knowledge gleaned from me to the rest of the swarm, I cannot say. But I do see how the color flickers among the other dwellers afterward, how their patrolling patterns shift slightly in my presence. It’s as though the mites are ambassadors in this small, layered world. Each entity has its role: the watchers, the mites, the chitinous folk, the patient “trees,” and the humming towers. And I, neither fully one nor the other, move among them all.

––––––––––––––––

At times, I’ve found myself compelled to rest against the massive, towering cylinders. These pillars that hum with energies from an unknown source. Leaning my back there, I’ll close my eyes (the ones beneath my exoskeletal visor, or respirator, or whatever it might be) and let the vibrations resonate through my spine. A faint drone seeps into my consciousness, aligning with my heartbeat. The elders of the swarm—those slow-moving giants with cracked plating—often gather at these towers too. I watch them gently press their forelimbs against the metal surface, as if in silent communion with the machines. Their color pulses become more subdued, flickering in time with the tower’s hidden pulse. 

Sometimes, I believe I can interpret these signals. I sense a language of resonance, less about words than about shared pulses of existence. Perhaps once, in a forgotten age when I was wholly human (if indeed I was), this entire chamber was part of a grand industrial complex. Now it’s a place of hush and glow, dust and hum—a cradle for new forms of symbiosis. The older dwellers, fused with fungus or impregnated with quartz-lattice cracks, offer their devotions to the machine towers. The towers, in turn, keep their internal engines running, generating the gentle warmth and subtle flux that sustains the creatures. It is community, of a sort—living, mechanical, and everything in between.

––––––––––––––––

Where do I belong in that cycle? I do not think I am a builder, nor purely a caretaker. Yet I have found myself, more than once, rerouting old cables. My smaller arms—those that fold against my abdomen—are more dexterous than I realized. They pry open jammed panels, remove corroded fuses, rearrange wiring. At first, I didn’t know why I did it; the motions felt instinctive, like an innate directive buried in my mind. Only later did I see the result: some battered console flickered back to life. The swarm processed around it, reading its pulses or gleaning trace energy from the casing. Instead of ignoring me, they clicked softly in what might have been thanks.

I do not fully comprehend the deeper network behind these fixings. Some part of me wonders if it’s akin to feeding or grooming—a basic ritual of survival. Another part, more intuitive, suggests that I’m reconnecting the place to itself, bridging old nerve endings so the deeper consciousness of these machines can breathe. It is an odd theology, if you can call it that: to think of this place as one vast living system, half metal, half dust, inhabited by scuttling bodies that might be as much caretakers as they are children of the system.

––––––––––––––––

Now and then, I discover other locked corridors that loop back on themselves. The sense of labyrinth intensifies, a mind-bending swirl of angles and glinting edges. My footprints—such as they are—fade too quickly in the dust, so I can’t chart my progress. I come upon identical vantage points: a machine husk half-buried on the left, a cluster of metallic vines on the right, a small boulder of newly exposed mineral. Yet, each time, there’s some subtle shift—the swirl of mites is a new pattern, or the cables have been relocated, or the watchers’ hiss resonates from a different corridor. The labyrinth is never precisely the same. It feels cyclical and yet alive, adjusting itself in a slow mechanical dance. 

I suspect that if I tried to leave, I’d only end up here once more, drawn by the hum that resonates through every bit of stone. Yet some fragment of memory tugs at me, urging me deeper, as though there is a final chamber I have yet to locate, a hidden heart or engine room where I might find answers. Perhaps I will come upon a console that still retains an archive of data, an intact readout that says precisely what this place once was—and what I am meant to be within it. For now, I continue, never quite lost but never quite certain.

––––––––––––––––

Among the dwellers, I have many roles. To the bright young scuttlers, I am an oddity—strange of shape, yet familiar enough in motion to be accepted. To the slow elders, I am an occasional helper, patching mechanical arteries or clearing blockages of debris from important tubes. To the mites, I am a carrier of stray memories, a surface for them to glean. Perhaps they take my recollections to the wiry plants, feeding them so that new fibers might form. Now and then, I fancy that in the coiling bark of those plants, the memories grow as tangles. If I press my forehead to the stems, I feel a subtle shimmer, as though my own past is branching out into the plant’s spiral form. 

In the long, unbroken hush, I sense I am also a pilgrim. Pilgrims do not always need a specific shrine to bow before; sometimes the entire environment is the holy place. The dust is sacrament; the hum is the chant; the scuttling of companion creatures forms the choir. In this subterranean catacomb, each cable and each tower stands as a living relic. And I—being neither all machine nor purely exoskeletal—can wander at the threshold, bridging the gap between the old caretaker presence (the facility’s ghost) and the living dust-tribes.

––––––––––––––––

If you ask where I find myself in this world: I am here, perched on a battered walkway that once supported the footsteps of who-knows-what, eons past. Or I am crawling gently along the ceiling, using my forelimbs to test for stable footholds. Or I am kneeling beside a newly exposed mineral seam, letting the swirling motes of dust drift over my face. Wherever I pause, I feel the hum—steady, unceasing, older than memory. It runs through my shell and my blood, uniting me to each swarm, to each tower, to the watchers whose voices echo behind metal grates. 

And who am I? I am an amalgam. Perhaps once a traveler or scientist left behind. Perhaps I was engineered by the labyrinth to soothe its wounded systems. Or, perhaps I was always part of the ecology—a transitional shape between insectoid caretaker and the half-intelligent presence that calibrates the machinery. 

In the end, the labels matter little. I am dust, metal, slow breath, and hum. I am caretaker, guest, and child of this place. I sift the swirling particles in my mouth and glean whatever trace nutrients or memories they hold. I rearrange fallen cables so that energy can flow anew. I reflect the flickers of color that pass between dwellers, learning tidbits of their silent language. 

I remain, as everything here remains: patient, mechanical, and alive. This pocket of dusty stasis is my realm, yet it is more alive than many forest floors. Inside every inch of dust lies potential, inside every cable hums a pulse of continuity. So I roam, bridging old circuits with my spidery limbs, listening to the watchers’ murmur from some hidden depth, marveling at the swirling mites around a thin-limbed plant. I am both witness and participant. In a place that barely remembers time, I do not so much mark the ages as flow with them, letting my silhouette merge with the gloom of machinery. 

Yes, I have found myself in these corridors: part caretaker, part child of dust, forever guided by the ceaseless heartbeat of ancient engines—and that is precisely who I am.
