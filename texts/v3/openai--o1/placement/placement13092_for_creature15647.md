I think, in this slow hush of stone and drizzle, I would be an old keeper of the water channels—a figure half-blurred by years of mist, wrapped in a cloak the color of weathered slate. I can almost see myself crouched beside one of those shallow basins at the settlement’s edge, listening to the faint clank of the blacksmith’s hammer from afar, adjusting a small rock to redirect a trickle of water. Or perhaps I am not quite settled on that role yet; perhaps I started my days among the watchtowers.

Yes—when I was younger, my feet were firmer on the worn steps of the highest parapet, a place few visited except to watch the clouds roll through. Back then, I had a certain fierce curiosity about everything below: the distant valleys, the rumor of forests that seemed impossibly lush from this vantage. I’d ring the bell if I spotted an unusual swirl of clouds, or if I glimpsed an unfamiliar bird circling the plateau edge. I’d linger at the tower’s crest even once my duties were done, scanning the horizon in search of… I wasn’t quite sure. My child’s heart wanted something more, or else to see some change ripple across these settled stones.

But time in this place doesn’t race or shift so easily. My bones ached in the cool mountain air; the hush seeped into my voice, and the ring of the bell became a rarer sound. It’s not that I lost wonder—some of that amazement at endless cloud seas and faint sunbeams never truly left me—but I found a slower, steadier purpose, one that let me slip deeper into the rhythms of the plateau.

That’s when I started to spend more mornings with the elders who tend the water channels. They taught me how each rivulet is precious, how water can vanish overnight if not properly guided. We studied how a single stone placed just so might hold enough moisture for a small patch of onions, or how a shallow dip in the channel would keep a trickle from eroding its bedding. I learned that water, in a place like this, is akin to breath: subtle and easily overlooked, but vital. I saw how measuring out drops of runoff was an art form perfected over decades, how the entire settlement is essentially a masterpiece of slow, patient engineering.

In the hush of these days, I grew older. My hair thinned and turned wispy, as though inspired by the clouds that perpetually drift around our mountaintops. My cloak’s hem frayed, marking the years spent walking between damp moss and battered stone. I no longer rang the bell on the watchtower, leaving that to younger watchers whose eyes still shift with curiosity. Instead, my mornings became a communion with the small trickle of stream outside the gate, the place where reeds cluster and occasionally cradle a lost bird. Sometimes I carried seeds of gnarly shrubs from the battered slopes, coaxing them to take root near the water’s edge so that the blacksmith might have enough charcoal in another generation’s time.

But on certain afternoons, I drift back to the tower—my old vantage point—climbing step by slow step. The wind is keener up there, laced with a lingering chill, though it rarely cuts. I can see how the settlement’s buildings cling so neatly to the plateau, a puzzle of stone pinned together by centuries. From that height, the hush of the place reveals itself in layers: the hush in the vacant streets, the hush in the forging courtyard as the blacksmith’s hammer rests between strokes, the hush in each household where neighbors murmur over a shared meal.

And I realize I, too, embody that hush. My voice has grown so soft that a single conversation hardly stirs the air. Even my footsteps wear a path as quiet as distant rain. Perhaps that is how I came to think of myself as a keeper—of water, yes, but also of silence. I watch over the flow of drips and rivulets, and I feel within me the slow turning of this plateau’s long, unbroken cycles.

Sometimes, a memory rises of myself as a child, leaning from the tower’s edge at twilight, scanning with bright eyes for anything new beyond the peaks. I whisper a small greeting to that younger self, letting the hush carry my voice backward in time. I promise that child we are well here, that discovery can be found in small things—like the way water meanders, how moss can push its way through a hairline crack in stone, or how the hush itself can be so comforting when you learn its shape.

At night, after the faint hum of neighbors marks the end of another day, I like to sit where the plateau meets the stream, letting the slick stone soak through the seat of my cloak. The moon—on clear nights—hangs low above the peaks, sending a pale reflection dancing across the water’s surface. I peer into the wavering brightness and imagine that perhaps we in this settlement are our own reflection, a faint glimmer of calm in a broader world that rarely slows. Whatever life exists beyond the mist, I feel it must be turning on wheels of busier commerce, forging iron contraptions, raising towers of polished stone. Those are hearsay, half-myth. Here, we have no grand expansions, no churn of machinery. We have only the shaping of water, stone, and hush.

And I am content, not in a resigned way, but in the shape of an acceptance that is both delicate and unbreakable. If I ever ventured beyond these mountains, I might tell others of our plateau’s quiet stability. But then I realize: no notion of “telling” truly captures this hush. Words are too clumsy, too large. To dwell here is to learn a more subtle language: the hush that is near-silence but not emptiness, the hush that hears each shift of gravel underfoot, each breath drawn.

So yes, I would be a caretaker—of water, of the hush, of the centuries-settled stones. I would be a keeper of memory, though my memory might be vague on details, letting them slip into something that feels more like myth. I would be the one who listens when evening descends, who notices the change in pitch when the wind swirls through new cracks in the walls. I would be the person who, upon waking, carefully checks the channel that leads to the onions, adjusts a stone here, or pads bare steps along the plateau’s edge to see that the reeds haven’t been washed away by last night’s drizzle.

And in time, I would fade ever more into the hush myself—another soft presence among many. Perhaps someone younger, curious, might see me from afar, crouched by the water, and wonder at the gentleness in my steps. They might ask quietly, “Who is that elder, and what do they do?” And another voice might answer, “They tend the channels; they’ve always been here, guiding the water.” As if I, too, were cut from the stone, an ancient fixture in a place that changes only by the most patient degrees.

Yes, that is who I would be in this hush. It suits me: caretaker of the streams, sentinel of a slow equilibrium, quietly weaving my life into the lasting hush that blankets the plateau. And if others should pass by, or if rare travelers ever came to see this perched settlement, they would find me there, kneeling at the water’s edge, a worn cloak of slate gray around my shoulders—the color of mountains and sky, and of a hush that has endured for centuries.
