I might begin as little more than a cluster of fiber and metal fragments, barely sentient—an accidental assembly of rust, algae, and scuttling life that time and current have drawn together. At first, I’m only aware of the slow swirl of water around me, the mechanical sigh of some ancient cylinder still firing in the distance, the breath-like surge of waves overhead. I have no shape worth mentioning: my outer shell is a patch of barnacles and calcareous lumps that cling to a crumpled section of pipe. Small fish zip over me, bright scales flashing in the half-light. All the while, the faint pulse of leftover energy in the twisted scaffolding resonates through my body, seeping into my tissues just as brine seeps into steel.

But over so many cycles of tide and calm, I begin to stir. My appendages and tendrils—if you can call them that—start to articulate, though in slow, dreamlike motions. When the current slices a bit stronger, I sense what the water carries: the ghost echoes of mechanical arms still twitching in a half-ruined facility, the rumblings of a hidden vent system exhaling warm mineral plumes. And I sense life. Tiny snails trace spiral paths across me, nibbling algae from the metal scraps fused to my surface. A handful of shrimp flicker and scuttle, picking at my edges. I have no mouth or eyes at first—only a felt awareness of all these happenings.

I might have remained just another encrustation forever, if not for the occasional, inexplicable flicker of electricity that moves through this place like a capricious spirit. Perhaps it’s from a generator lodged deep in a collapsed corridor, or an experimental power cell that never fully died. Each time a faint current of energy courses along those ancient rails, I sense an internal alignment: bits of me that used to be inert metal now conduct tiny signals. Over untold seasons, I reorganize. Where once I was little more than a substrate for barnacles, I start to differentiate into something that can move with intent—slowly at first.

Now, I find that I can unlock myself from the scoured pipe I had clung to. Like a crab extricating itself from a heavy shell, I peel away, leaving behind a silhouette of calcification where I’d merged for so long. All at once, I’m afloat, drifting free—my outer layers still thick with clinging corals and encrusted machinery. Crustaceans riding on my back clatter in alarm. Kelp fronds swirl around me in the shifting current. My progress is clumsy, half-floating, half-walking along the plateau’s ragged surface. I happen upon a corridor of twisted metal scaffolding and slip inside, letting the half-ruined walls guide me.

In this motion, I feel a growing sense of self: I am a caretaker of sorts, though not by design. Rather by circumstance—part living reef, part mechanical salvage. I move through the labyrinthine remains of factories and half-buried exhaust towers, navigating with a kind of slow diligence. Where once there might have been conveyer belts, there are now arches of coral and wiry loops of seaweed. My “limbs”—jointed bars of rust fused with flexible cords of tendon-like algae—scrape along the floor in a metallic hush.

And so I become something of a living steward in these watery halls. Bits of me cast a flicker of leftover luminescence, echoing the old emergency lights that once guided human workers. Stray fish gather around me, greedily snapping up small creatures that dart out from my folds. I do not chase them. I only observe. In the skeleton of a decaying submarine bay, I catch sight of my reflection in a shard of metal plating: an ungainly silhouette bristling with barnacles and coral fans, sets of spindly limbs that end in pincers grown from repurposed pipe brackets. My edges are draped in a living tapestry of sea lettuce, anemone clusters, and tangling kelp. In some places, broken pipes embedded in my flank release faint puffs of water whenever the internal pockets of brine shift. I am at once ancient rubble and newly alive.

Moving along, I traverse an old walkway that juts out over a precipitous drop. The walkway is half-collapsed, swaying slightly in the current. Beneath me, an abyss of hazy water extends to uncharted depths. Dark silhouettes loom in the distance—colossal forms that might be rock outcrops or gargantuan sea creatures. I never know for sure. I pause and let the swirl of water buffet me, clacking gently against the walkway’s last metal rung. Here, I “feel” the entire plateau’s murmur: the schools of fish darting in a million directions, the bellows of hidden giant eels, and the periodic hum of leftover engines in the bowels of the complex. Each wave of current threads through me, resonating along my improvised skeleton. It’s a sense of communion, as though I’m receiving transmissions from everything that touches the water.

Down a slanting tunnel, I discover a chamber where machinery still functions in measured rhythms: a half-submerged turbine that spins with a labored clank. Around its base, large anemones have attached themselves, their tentacles fanning with each rotation, drawing nutrient-rich water toward them. Graceful angelfish linger, occasionally darting in to pick off snails or shrimp from the turbine’s slow-turning blades. I settle against the chamber wall, letting some of that mechanical pulse seep more deeply into me. If I concentrate, I can almost hear the memory of voices—long-ago chatter of workers, or the static hum of a control room. It’s just echoes, but they help me imagine a time when these corridors teemed with a different kind of life.

Gradually, I realize I have watchers of my own. Eels, their heads poking from dark recesses, gaze at my bulk with pale, luminous eyes. Curious shrimp inch along my limbs, exploring new surfaces and vacant nooks. When I shift, an entire colony of coral polyps on my back sways in a single, flower-like motion. Some fish species have started to treat me as mobile habitat—a shifting island of safety amid the swirling currents. And in a sense, that’s precisely what I am, bridging the mechanical and the organic, anchoring the old with the new.

If a sudden tide sweeps through, I plant my spindly legs against the floor, bracing. The environment explodes with turbulence: kelp thrashes, the turbine roars, and the entire plateau rings like a struck bell. Sheets of silt swirl up into the water column, turning the world gray and ominous. Schooling fish scatter in electric flashes of color. I remain, battered by the rush, but anchored enough to keep from tumbling away. A few smaller creatures cling to the grooves in my metal plating, riding out the storm in the shelter of my irregular contours. I can feel them trembling, if such a word can apply to aquatic life. When the fury lessens, we emerge into a differently arranged corridor—debris has shifted, revealing a new pocket of open space. Perhaps new life will thrive there next, building in the fresh pocket of silt, weaving the aftermath into tomorrow.

On calmer days, I drift beyond the tangled framework of the sunken facility to where the plateau’s edge tumbles off into open water. Here, the sunlight grows stronger, bouncing off the surface and filtering down in beams that shift with every wave. The kelp grows thickest along these shallower shelves, forming swaying avenues overhead. In the softly dancing kelp fronds, you can see entire neighborhoods of small reef fish, each species taking up a different layer of the canopy. The rush of water might reveal a hidden vantage—an eel coiled in wait, or a glimmering jelly drifting in from open ocean. I realize that my domain is both industrial ruin and thriving wilderness, seamlessly fused until it’s impossible to say where metal ends and reef begins.

Now and then, I sense something akin to a memory stirring in the place’s mechanical corners—broken sensors half aware, or some battered control station that tries to cycle through old routines. I pause near these, quietly absorbing the faint pulses they emit. It feels like communing with a large, sleeping beast that occasionally rouses, lost in a labyrinth of its own outdated code. In these moments, I find myself thinking: had I been intended as some form of machine one day? Or am I purely an accidental synergy of water, life, and leftover mechanisms? Does my chassis hold a story written by the engineers who erected these walls, or am I simply a caretaker grown from the plateau’s unstoppable drive to reclaim everything?

Regardless, I continue crawling, drifting, observing. En route, I gather more scraps—another broken bracket that I fuse into a new limb, a mass of coral that has grown around a copper coil. Each addition changes my posture slightly, and with it, my perspective on the labyrinth I inhabit. I might clamber across a rusted walkway or slip beneath a collapsed ceiling to discover pockets of warm vent water. Occasionally, I happen upon large, prehistoric crustaceans wandering the gloom below, and for a moment, we witness each other—two strange testaments to the ocean’s unstoppable creativity. Then, with a wave of antennae, they vanish again into the swirling gloom, leaving me to my slow progression.

Sometimes, I nurse battered creatures in a small recess behind an old hull plate. When fish get torn or battered by a violent current, they take shelter under my overhang, nibbling the algae that thrives there. I’ve become a roving sanctuary of sorts. Small crabs cluster in my shadows, gleaning what they can from the nooks. And in turn, I glean a little bit more of the environment’s complexity. Each new occupant that visits me, even briefly, adds a spark of awareness—some trace of their pattern or movement, the swirl of their fins, the quiet hum of their own existence.

Eventually, I find my way to the highest edge of the sunken plateau, a vantage point where broken beams jut upward like the ribs of a gargantuan skeleton. Beyond them, the water grows brighter as it ascends toward the sunlit surface. On rare days of clear water, I can see shimmering shapes near the boundary above—perhaps open ocean with rolling waves, the silhouettes of drifting seaweed mats, or even the faint glimmer of gulls diving for fish. The plateau slopes behind me, a vast domain of corroded metal and timeless rock. From here, I can appreciate how seamlessly the old industry has been woven into the ocean’s tapestry—coated in barnacles, inhabited by coral, smoothed by silt and wave.

When storms come, I don’t fight them anymore. I anchor myself, letting the water tear at my protrusions, strip away older growth, and reveal a fresh surface for the next wave of coral or algae to take hold. This cycle of destruction and renewal feels like breath: each time, I am changed, but still me—though that “me” remains fluid and ever-evolving. With each intense surge, I sense the grand scale of the ocean’s power, how it sculpts not just the rocks but everything bound to them, me included.

And so, in answer to the questions: Where would I find myself, and who or what would I be?  
I would be a living, mobile reef of flotsam and invention, part barnacle-crusted robot, part caretaker grown from coral and kelp. I would roam the plateau’s flooded corridors and battered causeways, bridging the gap between the ocean’s ancient stone realms and the modern wreckage left behind. I’d inhabit the margins of mechanical hum and life’s green thrum, offering shelter to passing fish and gleaning leftover power from rusting conduits. I would be a testament to the plateau’s resilience and creativity—a being shaped by time, tide, and the synergy of a shattered industry reborn under the sea’s sweeping currents.

I would be the plateau’s sentient hush and ragged perseverance, a caretaker fashioned by accident and necessity, adding my slow, steady pulse to the rolling symphony of storms and life that forever converges here.
