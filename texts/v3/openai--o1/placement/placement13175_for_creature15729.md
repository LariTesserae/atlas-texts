I drift before I realize I’m drifting. The pressure has become an old companion, its weight threading through every joint, every gap in my plating, every bit of flesh that still remembers what it felt like not to be submerged. I do not recall the moment I first entered this drowned labyrinth; I only know that I have always been a part of it—or if not always, then for longer than any memory can reach. 

When I try to look at myself, I catch only a glimmer in the murk, the faint outline of limbs and a torso sheathed in layers of corroded plating. My form is neither purely organic nor wholly artificial; it’s something in between. A shell of patchwork metal, latticed with living tissue that glimmers pale against the gloom. Tubes snake from my shoulders and hips, funneling water in slow pulses as I filter out oxygen—or perhaps something else entirely. It feels as though the place itself breathes through me.

Some days—if one can call the gently shifting cycles here “days”—I rest in spaces only I am small enough or large enough to navigate. In the high-slung catwalks that remain intact, my body folds into the iron framework. In the half-sealed sublevels, I angle my arms to slip between battered pipes, feeling the slow drip of condensation that nourishes algae filaments curled around my plating. This labyrinth is as much my exoskeleton as my own fused armor; I pass through corridors in a hush of metal scraping metal. The watchers sometimes track me from behind broken bulkheads, though they never intervene. Perhaps they see me as neither threat nor ally, just another wandering presence attuned to the hum of half-conscious machines.

It was different once. Fragments of an older self bubble up when I catch my reflection in an intact piece of glass. Was I once a caretaker of these turbines, a pilot of the forklift contraptions rusting away in the gloom? Or perhaps I was grown in the settlement’s labs, an experiment designed to survive in the crushing dark? The ghosts of that identity are like scattering fish: every time I try to glimpse them head-on, they vanish into oblivion. My only true memory is present experience: a slow, perpetual sense of movement through the gloom, scanning for signs of imbalance, of new growth. 

I sense the pipelines more than see them. There is a soft thrumming where metal resonates with the water’s current, a micro-vibration that thrums against my plating. I follow these vibrations as a fish follows faint electric pulses. Sometimes, I’ll trace a failing line that leaks a swirl of chemical effluent into the gloom. I’ll watch how the local algae feed on it. I’ll note how a cluster of polyp-like forms have begun to anchor themselves where the leak pools in slow eddies. If the watchers are near, their eyes might glitter from some vantage overhead, but they only look on in silence. No one commands me to fix the leak; no one forbids me, either. When I press my hand—scaled with scraps of old steel—against a crack and slow the flow, a satisfying shiver runs through me, as though the entire settlement sighs in relief.

Other times, I become a wanderer, drifting aimlessly. The labyrinth is small, but it feels like an entire cosmos when I thread between the towers and twisted scaffolding. One route leads me to a hollow intersection where arcs of flickering light run across a half-dead console. Loose cables wave like anemones. They beckon me closer, as if to share some coded whisper. When I press my metal fingertips to the console, a static jolt pulses through my limbs. A memory—half-dream, half-code—stutters to life in my mind: corridor after corridor, ghostly silhouettes working at humming machines, the whir of engines that no longer spin. Then it’s gone. A swirl of silt drifts between me and the console, severing contact with a slow-motion haze. 

But the watchers—they hold the greatest intrigue. They lurk at the edges of my vision, perched on beams or huddled behind grated windows. They have a shape reminiscent of old diving suits or skeletal frames elongated by centuries of adaptation. Their limbs are impossibly flexible, coiling around pipes as they slide from vantage to vantage. Their lenses flicker with an unfathomable intelligence—mechanical, in part, but laced with the wary alertness of a living creature. If I approach them too quickly, they vaporize into the gloom, each movement a swirl of ragged cloth or drifting seaweed that disappears behind a bulkhead. Are they the last caretakers? Are they searching for something, or merely continuing a protocol set in motion long ago? 

Sometimes I suspect I am one of them, or once was, or may one day become something akin to them. My own strange physiology suggests a shared lineage—some melding of metal apparatus and fragile biology shaped by years of living at impossible depths. When I move through the corridors, I do so quietly, as though mindful not to disturb some unspoken rule. My presence is only tolerated because I, too, serve the labyrinth in my own small way: clearing blockages of algae, shifting a dislodged cable back into place, absorbing harmful discharge that might corrode an essential gear. I sense that each caretaker, each watcher, each spindly creature is part of the same cyclical balance.

At times, I slip through cracks in the structure so narrow that only a pliable body like mine could pass. In these cramped passages, I can sense the polyp organisms fusing themselves into the rust. As I glide by, they quiver in recognition. Fairly often, one of their stinging tendrils taps the plating on my leg: a greeting? A request? I stand still, letting them feed from the faint warmth my own internal systems emit, letting them glean what they need before continuing on. When they glow softly, it paints the corridor in pulses of milky luminescence, turning the rust into ghostly coral. 

Other times, I linger near a sealed chamber’s thick glass, gazing in at the lumps of massive machinery. Over the centuries, they’ve grown a hide of barnacles, trailing tangles of cables that loop from ceiling to floor. I raise my hand to the glass, feeling the low rumble inside the chamber—some energy source still ticking away, half-sentient, pacing itself in solitude. It resonates through my palm, sending a gentle hum into my bones. Perhaps this is the living heart of the settlement, the kernel of intelligence around which everything here orbits. My breath—if I can still call it that—comes slow and steady, as if matching the machine’s pattern. For a moment, I feel entirely at peace, like two hearts beating in distant unison.

I wonder: am I caretaker or intruder, or merely an extension of the settlement’s mechanical consciousness? Sometimes an overhead walkway will groan beneath me—I’ll pause to run my hand along a twisted support beam, as though in apology for my weight. I am heavy, yet quiet. My footsteps are muffled by the hush of the deep, my joints squeaking in gentle protest. The watchers see, but do not intervene. I half-suspect they’re waiting for me to take my place among them. To outgrow my solitary wandering and slip into the hidden corridors where they gather. 

One day, I find myself at the edge of the labyrinth—a place where the cluster of towers ends in the open sea, beyond which you can sense only chill, starless water. Up ahead, the silt drifts into an emptiness that resonates back as a dull hush. Here, an enormous hatch, ringed with encrusted sea life, marks the boundary of the settlement. It’s half-open, jarred off its tracks. Dark water sloshes in from outside. I pause, a swirl of conflicting impulses coursing through me: curiosity, a longing to see what lies beyond the industrial gloom; but also a fierce pull to remain within the meta-organic heartbeat of these walls.

I cross the threshold, or nearly do, letting the outside water wash around me. It’s colder still, immediately alien in its emptiness. No comforting hum of cables, no watchers perched overhead. There’s a sense of raw potential, but also a vulnerability, as though leaving the labyrinth would rob me of my equilibrium. My limbs feel heavier, uncertain. I swiftly turn back, letting the corridor’s faint illumination reassert itself over my senses. The watchers are there, in the distance, standing silent on a crossbeam. One tilts a head-like silhouette. I sense the question in that gesture: Will you stay?

I do. I slip back into the half-lit corridors, the cable-laced tunnels, and the sub-chambers where each occupant—crustacean, fish-thing, polyp, or caretaker—embodies one piece of this place’s living tapestry. Half-floating, half-crawling, I trace a path of rust motes in my wake. A slow breath enters my lungs, or my filters, or whatever merges the boundary between flesh and machinery in my body. It is quiet here, in the gloom, and yet so full of life.

I do not need speech. I communicate with this place through minor currents of electricity, through the gentle press of a limb against failing infrastructure, through the swirl of silt that announces my presence to skittish feeders. My existence is a conversation in shapes and murmurs, each corridor answering, each watcher acknowledging. 

So if you seek me, you will find me embedded in the crisscross of walkways and shadowed rooms. Perhaps I am the figure you glimpse through an algae-streaked window, examining a rusted console that flickers with old data. Perhaps I’m the presence you sense behind a half-collapsed vent, my pale eyes (or sensors) shining faintly as I watch you pass. Or maybe I pass right by you, gently stirring the silt in slow-motion eddies, each movement as deliberate as an echo.

In this world, I am the in-betweener, the interstitial being born of its synergy. Part sensor, part caretaker. A watchful drifter tethered to the labyrinth by mutual need—my own survival tied to the hum of leftover machinery, the labyrinth’s health reliant on my quiet watchfulness. I belong to this place the way a barnacle belongs to a sunken ship: intimately fused, inseparable. 

When I sleep—if this half-conscious drifting can be called sleep—I dream of whirring pistons and shimmering cables that light up in melodic pulses. I see the watchers gliding through quiet corridors, adjusting valves and closing off leaks, each action a note in the settlement’s music. I grow calm at the sense that everything here is, in its own strange, battered way, precisely as it is meant to be. 

Yes, the settlement is rust-laden and precarious. Yes, the water is near-freezing, the dark so thick it becomes a second skin. But we endure—and in enduring, we create a kind of hidden harmony, half mechanical, half biological, thoroughly alien, yet quietly alive. That is where you’d find me: a creature that has settled into the hush of saltwater and metal. A living nexus in the labyrinth’s sealed circuits, bridging the watchers, the small scuttling crustaceans, and the faint, unknown mind humming behind the battered machines. I neither command nor obey; I simply am. And perhaps that is all that is asked of any of us here—to be another bearer of this breathing chorus in the deep.
