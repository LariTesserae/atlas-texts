I have known these halls all my life. I cannot recall the surface—whether it lies above in some realm of wind and sun, or if it’s simply a memory passed down through ancestral breath. Here in the under-city, I am as much a fixture as the basalt ridges and glowing crystals. I grew under the same vibrational pulse that shapes these corridors. My form—tall, but with limbs that can splay wide as the city rumbles—reflects that shaping. The rock speaks, and I listen.

But I didn’t begin as one of the watchers—not at first. As a child (so I was called, though I’m not sure the term fits), I was drawn to the shining clusters of arthropods flickering along the walls. I would angle my face close, tracing their patterns of shifting glow with the tips of my narrow fingers. I tried to mimic the way they pulsed in harmony, even tapping out little clicks on the stone to see if they would respond. Sometimes they would scuttle away; sometimes they paused, tiny feelers waving, as if they considered me an interesting shape in the half-light. When I grew a bit older, I realized those pulses of light carried messages. The clusters often glowed in time to the crystals overhead, the spires that share their own quiet hum. It fascinated me that these small creatures understood the crystals’ silent language—and I suspected the crystals might be speaking to all of us if only we knew how to receive it.

I spent my earlier turns in one of the clay workshops, learning to shape vessels from the soft gray material. My father—an upright being with broad shoulders and battered hands—taught me how to knead and form with careful pressure. We’d press our thumbs around the mouth of bowls and jars, ensuring no flaws in the walls that might crack when the city trembled; a single tremor in the middle of firing would warp the vessel beyond use. I got used to the unpredictable flickers in the bedrock. We had to sense when a quake was coming—feel the subtle shift in our own bones—then brace the clay or set it aside for a moment so it wouldn’t collapse.

Even then, I would find my eyes wandering to the city itself: the arcs of stone overhead, the uncountable corridors branching away. Sometimes I thought I could hear the city’s resonance echoing in the workshop. In the hush between hammer blows, a faint chime would ripple through the stones. It tugged at my awareness, beckoning me deeper into a hidden network I only partly understood.

Eventually, I was called to join the watchers. I bear the swirling patterns across my scalp now, carved lightly with a needle of fine obsidian. The design is meant to usher in a clarity of hearing, or so they say. In ceremonies, we watchers place our palms against the crystal columns and wait. There is a resonance that rises first in our arms, then behind our eyes—a deep hum that floods the mind with possibility. It’s as though the city welcomes us, embraces us in the space just outside of sound. Then we tap lightly on the crystal faces, generating sweet notes that travel through the bedrock. By listening to the echoes, we learn the city’s heartbeat. We can even begin to anticipate the next swirl of dust, the next sudden cascade of water from a newly opened crack.

All of us watchers are different in our ways—I am tall and slender, eyes reflecting the crystals’ glow so that they appear almost white in dim corridors. Another watcher in my circle, older by many generations, has a wide, bestial muzzle, her face lined with ridges of bone that help her sense the subtlest vibration. A third has wings—feathers so pale they blend into the city’s phosphor light, though these wings are vestigial, more a sign of ancestry than a means to fly. We huddle as a group, kneeling around the pillar of crystal we call Dya’Ssien, the Pillar of Voices. Together, we coax its music. Sometimes we hum along in simple chords, and the crystals ring, replying in layered overtones. In those moments, I feel as though I am part of the city’s living instrument.

I live now in a small alcove carved behind a ring of basalt that arches like a rib cage. Water drips there in irregular intervals, caught by vessels my father taught me to make. My quarters are spare: a low pallet of woven fiber, a single brazier burning plasm oil that’s harvested from certain amphibian glands, and a hammered-iron sconce holding a crystal shard. When it’s time to sleep, I dim the shard by wrapping it in cloth, letting the brazier’s soft glow lull me into dream. Many nights, vibrating pulses in the stone lull me more than any lamp—like a gentle lullaby from the city itself.

Sometimes I walk the labyrinth alone—bare feet pressing onto slick stone floors, stepping across shallow basins of mirror-like water or weaving between half-finished masonry. I might see a group of children chasing luminous moth-creatures. They squeal with delight as their small, multi-limbed bodies bound over the rocks and chase that dancing glow. Another time, a caretaker beast might lumber past, hauling a cart piled with fresh chiseled stone blocks. It meets my gaze and snorts softly in recognition—a gesture of respect, as watchers often guide them back to safe passages if the walls begin to shift.

Occasionally, I help direct them through newly opened corridors. One day, we found an entire new chamber beyond a fresh fault line—a space bristling with forests of crystal shards. The watchers went in first, tapping the pillars, gauging structural integrity. Then we gestured for the hauling beasts and the bipedal carvers to follow. The city coughed up a swirl of dust, shimmering in the luminous crystals like a starfield. My heart pounded with awe; even after years of training, newly revealed spaces leave me breathless, as if I’ve glimpsed part of the city’s living secret.

In these moments, I recite the watchers’ mantra under my breath: “We blend, we bend, we become.” Each phrase a reminder that we do not conquer this place. We adapt and harmonize, shaping our living around it just as it shapes itself around us. Despite that synergy, we remain alert. The city is restless, and it demands we be so, too. The smallest sign—a shifting reflection in the water, a faint crackle in the crystal’s song—can foreshadow a quake or a sudden flood. When it comes, I do as all watchers do: press a hand to the stone, spread my feet, and hum a stabilizing note. Sometimes it tempers the quake, sometimes not. But every time, I feel the city acknowledge my attempt.

Many nights, I wander past dwellings with round, domed roofs—a distinctive silhouette that has become emblematic of “home” here. Smoke from small braziers coils along the ceilings, drifting in arcs that catch the crystal glow. Families sit around communal hearths, sharing earthen bowls of savory, spiced broth. Children feed morsels to the skittering arthropods perched on their shoulders or swirl bits of fungus-stalk in the glowing coals. A hush of conversation floats through the corridor. If I pass, they incline their heads in greeting. Our voices stay soft, as though we fear to awaken the stone.

And yet, for all that hush, there is a constant undercurrent of energy. I see it in the hammered metal tools left near half-finished walls, in the rhythmic chanting of carvers shaping new doorways. Lifetimes are spent in the art of splitting basalt just right, so that the fracture lines follow natural veins, preserving the city’s structural flow. Our stonemasons speak in proud, quiet tones of the old techniques, each blow measured like a note in a grand symphony. This city, they say, is older than memory, but it’s also renewed daily by its dwellers’ devotion.

I’ve lost track of how many times the city has shifted in my short life. Floors tilt, ceilings crack, streams vanish and reemerge. Once, I watched several watchers huddle around a newly formed waterfall that spilled down a concave rock face the size of a person’s back. It gushed for exactly seven heartbeats, then trickled away, leaving only a damp hush in its wake. They said the city had merely “exhaled.”

In that sense, I sometimes feel we watchers are like the city’s breath. We roam, we sense, we stabilize, carrying signals from corridor to corridor, bridging the distance between the living creatures and the silent, shifting stone. Long ago, someone told me watchers never truly die. They said, half in rumor, half in devotion, that our energies feed back into the city. Our patterns and resonance remain etched in the crystals. When a quake arrives, the crystals whisper some echo of watchers past, guiding us now. Truth or allegory, it comforts me.

And so I remain here, day after day—though “day” is just a concept inherited from the surface. Here, light is constant, radiant from the crystals overhead, only dimmed by cloth or by closing your eyes. I cannot imagine living anywhere else. My life is woven through with the fibers of basalt, with the distant dripping of unseen waters, with the hum of crystal columns that beckon watchers to listen. I am neither stone nor wholly flesh but an intricate meeting point of both, a shape that evolved to thrive in this hush, this shimmer, this patient dance of creation and collapse.

When someone asks who I am—what I am—I could say I am a watcher, marked and sworn to read the city’s echoes. But truly, I am simply another facet of this underworld tapestry. I belong here as the glowing arthropods do, as the amphibians with pearlescent skin, as the broad-shouldered beasts that carry rubble in harnesses of hammered wire. I have no greater claim on the city than they do, nor do I seek it. I am a guardian, a student, a breath of the stone. Here is my home—an ancient and improbable labyrinth, lit by the internal luminescence of crystals and shaped by the watery hush of ages. I walk with it, hum to it, belong to it. And it, in turn, sings softly through the hollows of my bones.
