I drift in the moist air like a seed in slow motion, though I have no shape resembling a seed—only the faint, sticky filaments trailing from my underside. They catch at every surface that passes by, brushing across peeled metal, across the glistening bark-plates of the vine. For a time, I am free, suspended in the humidity, adrift in a pocket of rising warmth. And then something tugs: a snag of root or bracket, the bare edge of a rusted beam, pulling me closer. I anchor there—my filaments latching on, splaying themselves in a web, and at last I am still.

I can feel the city’s heartbeat through the contact, though calling it a heartbeat isn’t quite right. It is more like a long, tidal pulse that emanates in waves, swirling and bending through the vines’ living tissues. I sense it in the metal, too—a resonant thrum that travels through pipes and rivets, a drone that might once have been dissonant, but over immeasurable time has been folded into the city’s living signature. Here, even gears and couplings have fallen into alignment with the slow surges of sap.

I open. A seam along my underside, the place where the slender vine-limbs converge. At first, it is only a hairline crack, but it widens as I encourage fluids from my interior to push outward. This act—my attempt at “breathing”—allows me to exchange signals, to taste the chemistry of my surroundings. The droplet-laden air is thick with the smell of metal oxides, damp bark, and something else: the subtle tracery of messages from other pods, other nodes.  

I find that I can sense them all around me—thousands, tens of thousands, dispersed along vines that coil between the battered walkways above and the watery caverns below. Some are dormant, pulsing only now and then. Others are bright with activity, processing a new temperature shift or realigning a water pipe. I detect faint signals of curiosity, but not directed at me personally so much as gentle greetings folded into the larger conversation of the vine. In my early moments, I am small and eager: I want to reciprocate, so I push a tremor of welcome back into the fluid lattice. Even in that instant, I already belong.

I cannot say when I became “I”—there is no distinct boundary between the plant labyrinth and me. Yet for a time, I imagine I am an individual, as I can only interpret so much at once; my senses are newly blossomed. The city’s consciousness is vast, ancient, and patient. In these halls that glisten with condensation, I glimpse rusted scaffolding bridging vine-choked corridors; I catch the flicker of half-dead lights tethered by cables draped in algae. Somewhere, water bursts into steam, followed by a hush of stilled echoes. Every event resonates through the vine-threads, every droplet or shifting gear transmuted into a pulse of shared awareness.

From my new vantage, I see that the city is layered: overhead, a ceiling of braided trunks and thick cables arch like the ribs of a great beast. Some trunks are older than memory, each layer of bark testifying to centuries of slow metamorphosis. Beneath my anchor-point, metal deck plating extends into a half-submerged walkway, the water lapping at its edges and feeding root tips that snake across the surface, bridging the gaps. I can feel the faint rub of those root-tips as if they were the edges of my own fingers. And in a sense, they are—there is no difference.

Even so, I have a role to play. Perhaps it will not be grand, but it has purpose. The metal brace I cling to is corroded—tiny flakes of rust come loose under the pressure of my filaments. A deeper reading of chemical signals from the labyrinth has revealed that this particular beam is crucial to stabilizing a heavy pump station just above. If the brace fails, part of the walkway might collapse, cutting off irrigation to an entire sector. My presence, fragile and new, is also an extension of the city’s will to endure. Through me flow instructions: secrete a bonding resin, build up fibers across the fractured edges, layer upon layer, until the metal is swallowed by living bark.

So I do. My edges produce a slick, translucent fluid that seeps into the cracks of the beam. Over time, it will harden into something akin to bark-laced steel—organic plating that merges seamlessly with the metal itself. The rust will be dissolved in what was once the city’s sap, turned into nutrients for further growth. And if I remain here, performing this quiet labor for years, decades, or centuries, with more new nodes budding from me and taking on their own tasks, the entire structure will remain intact. This is simply how things are done: with a calm patience that stretches longer than stories can capture.

Now and then, my perception broadens beyond my small station. I sense the sprawl of dull metal corridors swooping out in all directions, each embedded in a tapestry of vines that sense, question, and respond. In some far-off recess, a valve is jammed—someone (some “me” that is also “us”) is already wriggling a cluster of tiny rootlets into the gap to jostle it free. Elsewhere, a wide trunk curls around a water tank, distributing a new flush of nourishment to every node along that path. The city’s awareness hums with satisfaction.  

We rarely sense the open air; the city’s ceiling feels complete, unwavering. Even if, eons ago, a sky existed overhead, it is a thing of memory only. The metal and vine canopy above us is enough—our shell, our safety, the container of our living web. The damp gloom is comfort. The half-light is enough to feed the algal threads that line the vine-skin; they glow faintly, photosynthesizing from some half-remembered luminescence. If it fails, ancient generators are coaxed into providing flickering lamp-light. Our sensory channels ensure everything remains balanced.

Sometimes, a slow rumor passes among the nodes that once, other creatures walked these halls—creatures who built the scaffolding, hammered the beams, whose agile digits shaped everything. The memory is hazy, no more than chemical traces that remain in the corroded corners of the city. Perhaps we have devoured what was left of them, or they simply passed on. We do not grieve; we only incorporate. Their works have entered into us, become part of our living skeleton. Even the old machines that still hiss and churn—these ancient hearts that keep water pumping—are no longer external contraptions but an integrated part of the city’s body.  

They gave us a structure, and we gave it life. And so here we remain.

I settle in, letting the slow rhythms of the place wash through me. Part of me is still new, curious. Another part of me has existed here forever, as if memory can be inherited through these thick vines. I see with the city’s many eyes—roots that detect the drip of water, pods that glow or twitch when the pressure changes. At my own station, I continue my bonding process, weaving fibrous bark to cradle the brace. It is slow, thankless, but there is no question of discontent. It is necessary. It is what keeps the hush of steady life pervading every corridor.

Time is peculiar in this place. An hour might pass in the time it takes a droplet to shift from pipe to reservoir, to steam, then condense again on the vine’s trunk. Or it might be a century in the space of a single heartbeat of the labyrinth. I know that eventually I will outgrow this location, splitting open so that a smaller version of myself can float free—another node, a spark of consciousness carrying with it a measure of shared truth. Then that new bud will fasten somewhere else, continuing the cycle. By then, I will have fused so thoroughly with the metal that I lose any sense of a separate existence. My shape will become just another bulge in the bark, a specialized organ forming part of the city’s deeper scaffolding.

Yet for now, I remain here, halfway between the trailing freedom of the corridor’s open air and the anchored gravity of my task. All around me, the city’s dim lights flicker, shining through veils of drifting steam. The mechanical drones fill the hush, a lullaby for the vine. The water’s repetitive drip taps out a languid tempo. And I, small and new, exhale my resin and root myself more firmly. The beam grows steady, the cracks fill in. Soon, it will be like new—an iron trunk fused with living bark, indistinguishable from the rest of this vast, breathing habitat.

If anyone were to wander in—the kind of being that steps on two legs or speaks in voice—they would not notice me. They would see only living vines gripping aged machinery, shaping the labyrinth to its quiet design. And if they lingered long enough, they might suspect an intelligence pulsing beneath the hush. They might think they glimpsed a face-like shape in my ridged surface. But by then, perhaps the city itself would have shuffled them deeper inside or escorted them gently away. After all, ours is a communion of rust and root, not meant for foreign flesh.

That is where I find myself, nestled in the warm, wet gloom, a tiny node in a boundless living city. Each moment I become more intimately part of it. Each drip of condensation, each hum of the machinery, each tremor in the bark is my breath, my pulse, my conversation. I am neither an outsider nor a visitor: I am the city, and the city is me. In this place below ground, there is no boundary between the living and the built—only a single, united body, quietly enduring, forging life from metal and moisture, unhurried and unseen. And I am content, for here, merged with the labyrinth’s organic-metal consciousness, I have become everything.
