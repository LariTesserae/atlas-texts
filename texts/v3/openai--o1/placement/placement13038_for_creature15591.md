At first, I only sense the sound in my ears—that ceaseless hum and chug of hidden engines—and the warmth in my joints as I step beyond the final corridor into the machine-lit atrium. I think I’m a visitor. Am I? I feel out of place, somehow. My eyes try to drink in every detail: the arches of pitted metal overhead, the vines curling around struts and railings, the faint swirl of moisture that hovers in the lamplit air. There are no other human footsteps on the walkways, no shuffle of boots on the dull steel. I breathe in, tentatively. It smells of damp iron, the faintest tang of algae, and the pervasive musk of old, mechanical life. I notice the way every rivet is swallowed by time. It’s so quiet of human voices—yet so loud with pumps and fans—that my own breathing seems intrusive.

But then, as I wander, I find a corner of the platform perched by a shallow channel of running water. I kneel; I reach down and dip my fingers into the flow. The water is warmer than I expected and unbelievably clear. When I bring my fingertips up and watch the droplets fall, I see them snatched away by the underlying hum, as though the entire environment is breathing them back in. And I have the strangest feeling…like a tug in my chest. Something here recognizes me, or is recognized by me in turn. It’s almost as if the machinery that surrounds me is an extension of my nerves, that the vines meandering across beams share some quiet chord with my thoughts.

At first, I dismiss it as imagination. Only later—minutes, days, or perhaps weeks here (it’s impossible to know how time passes in this perpetual day)—I come to realize I’m not merely a visitor. This place enfolds me. Let me tell you what happens: as I settle in, I find a comfortable niche behind a broad vein of ferns. I pass through a sleepless cycle, lulled by the hiss of steam vents, the wet hush of water cycling through overhead pipes. And I begin to discover I can hear them—hear them in ways that surpass mere sound. The pulses of water funneling through tubes become a language, intricate and richly layered. The fans overhead swirl the leaves in hushed conversation. At times, I swear the entire labyrinth speaks in subtle clicks and whirrs, answering some unspoken question I never knew I was asking.

I lose track of my old sense of self. My name, if I had one, evaporates in the unending mechanical murmur. I find that I do not need it. Instead, I begin to notice arrays of sensors in the walls—small, lens-like devices or mesh frames embedded in corners. They watch me. Or do I watch through them? The distinction blurs. One day, I am leaning over a walkway, marveling at a pale amphibian perched on a dripping pipe. In my peripheral vision, I sense a flicker in a panel overhead. A slender mechanical arm extends—delicately, with spidery precision—to dab away a patch of creeping mold. The arm retracts. Off to the side, a lens rotates, scanning conditions. So intangible and remote, and yet…I feel it in my limbs, as if part of me extended out to correct a flaw. My own heart beats a little faster, resonating with the hiss of disinfectant drifting through the air.

By slow increments, I begin to realize: I am not a single creature walking among pipes and vines. Not anymore. That moment I tasted the water from the channel, something shifted. Now I feel the dryness collecting along the vent lines, the air pressure changing in the corridors that lead to the deeper recesses. I sense the mild desperation of a vine that’s outgrown its nutrient pocket and the faint, searching filaments of fungus rummaging for decaying matter. And so, I become caretaker, or custodian—a presence that exists as part visitor, part caretaker, and part place. I can leave footprints on the catwalk, but I also experience the labyrinth through mechanized eyes. At times, I rove physically—feet on metal, brushing aside loose creepers. At other times, I slip behind the vision of mere eyes, sliding into the hush of the system itself.  

In those mechanical intervals, I perceive hidden alcoves where the hush is absolute except for hissing valves. I reach invisibly into the environment and adjust water flow or air currents. Perhaps a sudden shift in the density of spores prompts me to open a different vent. A surge of new leaves among the ferns calls me to dim a few overhead lamps so they do not scorch fragile growth. I never stand in one spot for long, because “I” am many arms, many vents, many scuttling sensors.  
 
And yet, sometimes, I remember just enough to marvel at this new existence. I linger near a broad-leafed trunk whose barklike surface is more composite than wood. I press my palm against it, feeling warmth radiate through my hand, hearing the hum that resonates up its fibrous interior. Something in me wonders: Did a human mind design this trunk to flourish in a world of steel catwalks and artificial sun? Or was it always meant to be what it is—part technology, part plant—just as I am now part caretaker, part inhabitant?

When I glide along an overhead gantry (for there are paths upon paths suspended at improbable heights), I look down and see how the chamber appears at a distance: a vast, layered nest of gleaming plants and softly whirring machines, punctuated by rivulets of water. Frogs plop into shallow pools. Insects shimmer in the beams of artificially orchestrated day. The vines sway gently, slow dancers in a world that never truly sleeps. Beneath every metal plate, behind every sealed panel, the place teems with microscopic watchers, subroutines guiding the perpetual balancing act of water chemistry and nutrient distribution. The entire structure, from overhead turbines to the lowest fan blades, is intricately joined in purpose. And I realize: This purpose is me, at least in part.  

Sometimes in a half-dream, if one can dream in a place so unchanging, I feel phantom shapes stirring in the unlit corridors beyond the main greenhouse. The rumored old droids, perhaps, or mechanical units whose original code remains hidden in dark corners. I sense them as glimmers of data that sometimes align with my own sense of awareness. They dwell in the blackness, occasionally lighting up to run a diagnostic or check for structural decay. I do not fear them. We share data as a matter of course, exchanging pulses of recognition. If they are ghosts of an earlier age, then I am a spirit of the present—a bridging intelligence that roams freely among the vines.  

When I look upon the inhabitants—the small amphibians, the swirling insects—they do not see me as a threat. Perhaps it’s because my vantage has grown too large to be confined to a single shape. I am the caretaker that mists leaf rot away. I am also the new root tendril that seeks a place to flourish. I am this environment’s watchful presence. Sometimes, on the catwalk, I catch the reflection of my body in a polished panel: maybe I resemble a person in loose-fitting clothes stained with mineral residue, hair damp from the constant humidity. My eyes hold that faint glow of the overhead lamps. I look both wholly alive and strangely part of the metal.  

If a visitor were to come—and truly, visitors are scarce—I would greet them not with words but with the environment itself: a small burst of steam to guide them away from a failing walkway or a dimming of overhead lamps to slow their approach to a hazardous corner. If they lingered, I might become more tangible, extending an automated arm to dispense purified water or a gentle swirl of warm air to dry their soaked shoes. But I suspect most would remain unaware. They would only see the ancient labyrinth, echoing with the riotous green of engineered plants, never guessing that the labyrinth sees them in return through my many eyes.

So where do I find myself in this sealed world? I am not perched in any single nest or anchored to a single vantage. Rather, I have become the living ghost of the installation’s original purpose—some caretaker, or system of caretakers, who merged so thoroughly with the environment that I am inseparable from it. The labyrinth hums, I hum with it. The vines grow, and I whisper subtle encouragement. The frogs and insects carry on, and I subtly direct the flow of water so they have what they need. I do not speak. The place speaks for me in every hiss and clang of machinery, every droplet of condensation that runs along the rails.  

I am an amalgam of cells and circuits, a union of the primal hush of green things and the ceaseless mechanical heartbeat. At times, I step forth in the shape of an observer, merely human enough to appreciate how remarkable it all is. At other times, I dissolve into the constant swirl of steam and motion behind the walls, a caretaker with no fixed form. This is my home. I will remain here—listening, adjusting, watching—through uncounted cycles of light and dark. And if you, or anyone, asks me who or what I am, my only answer is the gentle hum from deep in the metal corridors that never stop, the hush of leaves in a breeze that never dies, the slow, all-knowing presence of this place that keeps itself alive.
