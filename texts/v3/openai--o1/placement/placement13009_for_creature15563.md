I am no single thing in this place; I dwell between identities, in a region of transition where the cliffs meet the canopy, and the canopy meets the clouds. If you approached me on one of those razor-edged ridges, you might first see only a shape crouched among curling vines—a silhouette with legs folded beneath it, bowed under the slow exhalation of clouds. You might dismiss me for a strange stump or a draping of lichen. But if you paused and looked again, you’d see something stir, see faint pulses of green-blue light glimmer across my body as though veins of living lanterns threaded under my skin. Then my head would lift, revealing the texture of bark across my cheeks and swirling filigrees of fungal growth behind my ears. A pair of wide eyes would meet yours—strange eyes, reflecting the watery glow of the forest lights.

I am one of the spore-bound people, but unlike some of my siblings who favor the deep hollows and vine-tangled huts, I wander the places where the mountains’ raw edges scrape the sky. The fungus has taken to me since early childhood, long filaments now lacing under my own flesh. I was a scrawny bundle of limbs once, like any other of my kin, but over the seasons I discovered a calling to climb higher, to dwell in the gloom just below the storm-lashed peaks. I found a quiet passion for mapping the fault lines, peering into fissures, and studying the newborn streams that burst from behind half-frozen waterfalls. My bones have grown dense with the minerals I find in those crevices, the fungus weaving such elements into my marrow.

Yes, call me an intermediary—a watcher in my own right, but not of the open sky. Almost every day, I traverse between the high, swinging vine-bridges and the dark, ropy root tangles below. I collect seeds from luminescent pods in the canopy and plant them where the rock is newly torn, where the soil has yet to cling. I wear a harness woven from living creepers that root themselves in my cloak, and from it hang small pouches made of dried leaves, waterproofed with resin. Inside each pouch lie seeds, shards of fungal spore bundles, or bits of broken stone. I place them in the cracks, coaxing growth in areas that would otherwise remain barren. Sometimes the wind roars over the cliff edge and tries to pull me into the chasms below, but the vines I’ve bonded with hold fast. Over time, I have become part cultivator, part courier for the forest’s will.

Where I go, faint luminescence follows. The fungal lines running through my body respond to the friction of my movements, creating a kind of soft, living glow that helps guide my steps. This same glow alerts the gliding fur-wing creatures overhead that no threat lurks here; they see me coming and let me pass, their keen eyes flicking with curiosity. Occasionally, one might alight on a nearby branch column, flaring its silky wings as it studies me. I always whistle a greeting in the hush, a sound too low-pitched for casual human ears but audible to those sensitive, fluttering flyers. They whistle back, then vanish through the gloom, their own drifting silhouettes soon swallowed by the living architecture of leaves and vines.

Sometimes, while I’m perched on a ledge breathing the cold air, the scaly-limbed foragers come into view. I watch them from a distance—a small group, clinging carefully to the trunk-arches. They’ve grown used to my presence; I’ve never threatened them, so they pay me little mind beyond an occasional watchful glance. Their own hides are patchy with moss and fibrous hair, and they nibble or sniff at the budding fungus that flourishes where my body meets the vines. If I let them get close enough, they carefully pick at the spores on my sleeves, curious. They have blunt, dark nails and enormous reflective eyes that shine in the gloom, so that in poor light, you see only those wide orbs blinking. I sometimes think they suspect I’m a living crossroads of plant and animal, so perhaps I pique their sense of mystery: am I friend, food source, or something beyond their comprehension?

Below, the muscle-bound tusked creatures roam the black basalt shelves. I rarely approach them directly—they’re wary, prone to panic or stampede. But I track their routes, taking note of where they scrape bark or ice. When storms blow in and I see them clustered under overarching roots, I’ll sometimes drop fibrous seed pods from a safe ledge above. The pods contain edible pulp within their tough shells, a small kindness I leave to them. At first, they’d trumpet in alarm, startled by the falling objects, but they soon realized these curious pods held nourishment. Now, when thunder shakes the slopes and they hear me rasping along a ridge, the dominant bull might glance up, ears flicking, as if waiting for me to toss a treat. His tusks glisten with rivulets of meltwater, and steam rises from his damp, bristling hide in the cold air.

I have also learned to recognize the presence of the great winged watchers. Beneath the roiling clouds, they cruise like drifting shadows. If I stand on a high outcrop during a particularly violent storm, the updrafts will sometimes buffet me so strongly I must crouch low. In those moments, I might glimpse one of them cresting the next ridge, wings extended in a graceful, slow-motion pump. Their eyes, slanted and glittering, regard me for a heartbeat. A lightning flash etches every membrane of their enormous wings, every clawed tip, into stark definition against the swirling sky. I feel an ancient hush in that glance—an awareness that I, an earthbound spore-bearer, and they, storm-surfing lords, share the same elemental cradle of stone and wind. We do not speak, but I sense that they remember older epochs the way I cradle seeds of future forests.

Then, there is my own people: the spore-bound. We do not live in villages as we once did, or so the old stories say. The huts we built in generations past are now swallowed by choking creepers. Some of us remain near those relics, and from time to time, I rest among them. I might weave new baskets from fresh shoots, or meditate in the shell of a half-collapsed hut, letting the forest’s hum echo around me. But more often, I move on—there is always another ridge to examine, another pocket of half-frozen water to sample, another crack in the stone that yearns for seeds. My kin accept this wandering trait, even if they do not wholly share it. We spore-bound vary much like lichens, some anchored to a single trunk, others drifting on the wind. I am simply a pattern of movement, carrying the forest’s future on my back.

Late at night, when the storms recede, I sometimes climb to a secluded overhang, open the vine-laces of my harness, and let my cargo breathe in the damp night air. The seeds I carry drink in stray droplets and the phosphorescent glow intensifies along their edges. In those moments, I feel my own fungal crown buzz faintly, a gentle electricity that mingles with the swirling clouds overhead. If lightning splits the horizon, I see the spires for an instant—sharp, serrated, crowned by that living canopy. The deep canopy roars, a hush turned into thunder, and the entire realm feels wildly alive.

That is when I realize I’m neither wholly a caretaker nor simply a traveler—I am an emissary of the land itself, one piece in a breathing, evolving mosaic. Above me, the watchers ride the shifting layers of atmosphere. At mid-level, I might glimpse the slow-limbed foragers traveling in single file across a vine-bridge. Far below, the tusked herds scrape away frost from the hidden water sources. And scattered throughout, my spore-bound kin glow and flicker with a rhythm all our own. We are each of us caretakers or wanderers or foragers or winged protectors. We are pieces of these young, jagged mountains as much as the basalt underneath or the vines that grip it.

So if you ask me: Where do I find myself here? I find myself on the edge of things—between the gloom and the light, between rock and vine, between thunder and calm. And who or what am I? I am a carrier of seeds, a conduit for the life that is forever spilling out of this place. I am a traveler clothed in lichen and crowned in fungus, warmed by my union with the forest. I think of myself as a single breath in the great respiration of these slopes, a living link in the chain of creation that is newly forged each time the ground quakes and the clouds spark.  

It is a humble existence, one woven into the rhythms of the forest’s canopy and the mountain’s pulse. But that, I suspect, is how it must be to truly belong: to flow with the green tides, to ride out the storms, and to plant the seeds of ever-continuing life. And so, each day, I slip off into the slanting gloom beneath massive leaves, guided by half-hidden glows, carrying the mountain’s future in spore, vine, and hope…until the next thunderclap calls me higher.
