I don’t recall exactly when I first noticed the droplets forming on my fingertips—the same slow, glimmering beads of water that gather on the edges of every leaf and crevice in this lowland. But I know they somehow called me deeper into the hush. Maybe I had been drifting on a half-forgotten path through an unfamiliar forest, or perhaps my journey had officially “ended” in some more typical realm. Yet the hush guided me here, and it’s as though my very skin has begun to resonate with the constant, gentle rhythm of dripping condensation.

At first, I thought myself an outsider—an observer in boots still crusted with dust from louder, more sun-blazed roads. That is, until I found that my footsteps had begun to match the measured tread of the local inhabitants. And my voice, hardly used to the hush, found itself caught in hushed breathing. Even the way I swallowed or sighed felt amplified against the near-silence, so I learned by instinct to make each motion quieter, gentler, more in tune with the seamless lull. The citizens did not instruct me in it. They only offered soft glances of welcome, a faint curve of lips that said, “Ah, you’ve arrived.”

For a while, I lived as a watcher. I’d linger at the edges of the cylindrical buildings, running my fingers over the smooth, moss-lined stones, trying to read the stories of ancient footsteps worn into them. I noticed how everyone’s garments—thin layers of textile—blended within the green surroundings, and mine alone stood out, too sharply colored, too new in a place that thrives on continuity. Determined not to tear the delicate vines as I passed, I learned to hold them aside gently, letting their tendrils brush my wrists as if they were verifying my presence. In this way, it became natural for me to walk softly and breathe slowly.

Over time, the hush seeped into my bones. I sensed a subtle invitation in that geometry of vines above me: a coiled pattern that seemed incomplete, like a puzzle piece waiting for my participation. Beneath one of the domed structures with a braided wooden roof, I found what might have been an abandoned weaving loom. Its frame was simple, carved from local wood with faint etchings at the corners—abstract lines that echoed the vines curling overhead. Something in me responded. I sat at that loom one day, curious. My first attempts at weaving were clumsy, but as I worked the fibers, I realized I could follow the same quiet rhythms as I heard from dripping water, measured footfalls, and the faint scuttling of insect-like creatures. I wove the hush into the cloth, or so it felt.

Before I knew it, I was no longer just observing—I was contributing. My role took shape as if it had always been reserved for me: the caretaker of cloth and quiet, the weaver of small quilts that families would drape over their shoulders during the cold hush of almost-evening. Oddly, I learned the dyes by intuition. Each mushroom or cluster of fungi had its own hue—some gave a pale, icy blue, others a ghostly white. By combining these with fibrous threads, the quilts bore a subdued spectrum mirroring the settlement’s soft greens and browns. Soon, my hands were stained in ephemeral tints, ephemeral but continuous through reapplication. The hush itself seemed to guide me in choosing which color to add next, as though the entire settlement choreographed these slow changes that never felt like changes at all.

My neighbors—if I can call them that, for here, every inhabitant feels more like a gentle relative—taught with gestures over words: a nod to signal it was the right time to gather water from earthenware jars, a light touch on my elbow indicating how to bank the embers in the clay brazier for a quieter flame. No one spoke to me at length about the settlement’s origins. Instead, I came to understand it through the daily recitations of small histories: the precise reason the walkway near the southern edge sags just a little (it was once overshadowed by an enormous leaf canopy that only recently died back); the way certain rodents continued returning to the same corner to nibble on seeds because a longtime resident scattered them there each morning, decade after decade.

I adjusted, grew still. When I wove, I felt the weight of centuries in my hands, though no single stone or vine cried out for attention. My tasks became cyclical, almost ceremonial in their gentle repetition. Weave, gather water, listen. Weave, pick mushrooms, observe drifting droplets. Through it all, I tended to the hush, as though it were a living entity that shared in the city’s equilibrium. Occasionally, I’d catch sight of a traveling stranger—someone moving cautiously among the buildings, uncertain whether they belonged. Several times, they crossed my path while I was setting up new threads, and we’d exchange a soft glance. I recognized the amazement in their eyes. Perhaps a careful tilt of my head or a faint curve at the corners of my mouth assured them it was all right to lower their voice and stay a little while. Then they would move on, the hush trailing after them.

As the seasons shifted—or did they? It was difficult to pinpoint—the plants maintained their bright dampness, the overhead leaves never losing that watery sheen. I learned to mark moments by the changing flavors in the herbal infusions: certain mushrooms or dried moss added tangy notes in one “season,” while the next might feature a heavier, almost wood-smoke fragrance. And so, time passed in swirling, subtle currents here, never in leaps or breaks.

Now, if asked who I am in this place, I would smile gently and remember the tangle of my old identity—a traveler, a foreigner—falling away as I slipped into the hush. I have become part of the tapestry. My hands are calloused from twisting fibers, the faint marks of colorful fungus dyes trace my fingers, and the hush itself threads through my very breath. I am the one quietly weaving quilts that keep bodies and hearts warm against the chill; I am the person who steps aside to let a rodent scurry through, who whispers to children that they might help me stir the dye pot if they use the gentlest of touches. And I feel in my chest the same geometry that sings in the overhead vines, old as the stones beneath our feet.

I belong to this city cloaked in moss and silence. I inhabit the liminal space between the rustling of leaves and the drop of water on polished stones. I know that as long as the hush remains, as long as these old walls stand in gentle equilibrium, my work continues: weaving patterns that echo the vines and the stories told by the ever-present droplets. And so, in the stillness that resonates like a collective heartbeat, I persist, neither intruder nor fleeting guest, but a quiet caretaker in the tapestry of this secret, timeless home.
