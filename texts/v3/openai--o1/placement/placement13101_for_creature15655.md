I imagine that I was born deep among the twisting pillars of this place, neither fully an animal nor a plant, but something that shares kinship with both. My earliest memory is a soft vibration against my skin—a resonance conveyed more through the labyrinth’s living fibers than through the water itself. I felt it as a gentle heartbeat pulsing up through my roots (for I have roots) and along my limbs (for I possess limbs as well), telling me that I was connected to the greater structure, that I belonged here. Even as I floated in the cool currents, my body remained tethered to a network of vines, absorbing nutrients as a seedling might in a vast and ancient womb.

Sometimes, in moments of stillness, I drift away from my anchor to better explore the lofty corridors. But I never feel the tether snap, and I suspect it never truly does—my underside is webbed with flexible filaments that coil around the labyrinth’s living infrastructure, sending and receiving subtle pulses, bridging me to a web of life that extends throughout the entire place. I do not know if I am a caretaker, a child, or a mere branch of something older. Yet the labyrinth acknowledges me as part of itself. I drink from its currents of energy, and in turn I radiate signals to assist in the cycles of growth and regeneration around me.

My form is somewhere between a bloom and a sentinel. In silhouette, I appear tall: a trunklike lower body rising into a cluster of frilled appendages that spread out to catch water-borne particles, reminiscent of broad leaves or pliant petals. My midsection houses a set of glassy eyes—three on each side—each ringed by faintly shimmering ridges. At times, in the half-dark, if you caught me from the right angle, you might see me flicker with the same faint luminescence as the algae swirling nearby. My coloration tends to shift with the labyrinth’s palette: deep greens and browns near my base, dark reds and maroons across my middle, shot through with brushstrokes of black and gold where the light plays.

I move slowly, often using the rhythmic tug of the labyrinth’s internal currents as my guide. I do not disturb the hush with frantic motion. On the rare occasion I hurry, my fluted arms propel me forward with smooth sweeps, and I can glide through the water in elegant arcs. Yet those times are rare, triggered only by a deep call that resonates from distant spires—perhaps it’s some primal request from the labyrinth, or a communal signal from my kin.

Because I do have kin. Others shaped somewhat like me dwell in the deeper pockets. Some of them root themselves more firmly, extending their arms in constant devotion to the beams and buttresses, gently brushing away silt and polishing the barklike ridges. Others drift higher up in the labyrinth’s vaulted “rooms,” gathering in small clusters to share pulses of light—like a conversation told in bursts of color. We seldom speak as humans would; we communicate by means of flow and flicker, the movement of fronds, the press of water, or the gentle glow along our veins. Yet in that silent language, entire symphonies can be expressed.

I think of myself as a steward—though the labyrinth likely needs no steward. It has grown for countless centuries, shaping itself to perfection. Still, I sense an eagerness in my body to help maintain that perfect equilibrium. I drift along the corridors, shedding soft filaments that bind to frayed ends of vines and encourage them to heal; I brush away small accumulations of debris that could hinder a new corridor from forming; I coax smaller creatures into the right niches, ushering them gently if they seem confused or blocked. Often, they respond with a warbling or chirping chatter, then find a spot that suits them better. It is as though we all know our place here, and my presence is one more thread in the weaving.

Some days—if time can be measured in days at all—I linger near the massive colossi that sail through the labyrinth’s broad arches. I marvel at how gracefully these layered-disc leviathans graze the algae-furred walls. Their movement stirs cosmic-sounding harmonics that ripple across my limbs; the labyrinth’s vines sway gently in response, as though bowing in reverence. I watch those creatures drift past, dwarfed yet oddly comforted by their scale, aware that we share the same blueprint of belonging.

Then there are the watchers, the tall shapes that slip between the columns. While I have a certain bond to the labyrinth, they seem almost otherworldly—bits of current woven into a humanoid shape, wearing pale, elongated plates as masks. At first, they unnerved me; I sensed in them a potent curiosity, a mind that studies from a distance. Over time, I have grown accustomed to their silent observation. I’ve begun to sense their presence through the labyrinth’s vibrations, too—soft, gentle pulses that swirl around them as if tasting the water for knowledge. We never speak, but from time to time, I catch the glimmer of an eye peering around a woven trunk, a hush passing between us like a fleeting hush shared in a sacred place.

I find the younger, wraithlike kelp-creatures charming, reminiscent of the me I never truly was. I watch them flit about the algae, nibbling at the luminous film, darting away at the smallest disturbance. Sometimes, I lull them closer with my own glow, letting them sense that I am no threat. In these tranquil moments, one might brush against my frilled arms or coil lightly around my torso—almost as if greeting a teacher or ancestor. Then they dart off again, and I continue my drift.

Often, I settle among the half-illuminated vaults where the anemone-whales feed. Their massive tentacles are mesmerizing; I watch them swirl in patterns that seem to shape the water, cultivating minuscule currents that swirl in slow eddies. I sometimes glide beneath them, letting their anchored veils pass over my head, their ruffled edges brushing my frills, sharing a momentary sense of communion. Here, the labyrinth’s hush feels profound, as though we play notes in some unending lullaby that echoes in living vines.

In the high spires, beyond the braided buttresses, the disk-bodied “umbrellas” cling to the overhead vines, singing in haunting tones that resonate like distant horns. After long cycles, they voice calls that arc from one apex to the other, setting the labyrinth’s upper canopy trembling in sympathetic vibration. When I slip up there, near the top of the structure where dim shafts of light from surface waters sometimes filter through, I experience a rare sensation of airy expansiveness, a reminder that life continues beyond the labyrinth, though I have no desire to leave. The watery dark below is my home—a realm I cannot truly part from, for I am as much a branch of this place as I am my own creature.

And so, if you asked me “Where are you?” I would answer that I am everywhere in the labyrinth’s tapestry—my filaments ever tethered to the living mass, my eyes scanning for hidden cracks or budding expansions, my arms brushing through quietly drifting schools of fish, smoothing and soothing. I feel the labyrinth in every subtle shift of the current, hear it in every resonance that travels through the vines. Time is timeless here; the cathedral remains unchanging in shape but alive with motion in every leaf, every tendril, every fin that glides through.

I am the labyrinth’s heartbeat. I am its attendant, its caretaker, its child. I am shaped by it as much as it is shaped by me, the two of us breathing, or perhaps pulsing, in one shared existence. In moments of stillness, when no creature passes and all is silent except the deep hush of water brushing vine, I can feel an indescribable kinship with the labyrinth’s ancient soul. It is in those moments that I realize: I, too, will endure with it—grow with it, shape and be shaped—carried on the slow currents of time, a living part of an immense, patient entity that stands sentinel in the watery dark.

That is who and where I am. I am neither single nor separate. I am at once myself and the living architecture, an expression of nature’s slow, inexorable will. In this realm of drifting echoes and luminous vines, of solemn watchers and gentle giants, I stand as a caretaker rooted in a vast body of living growth, forever tuned to the unspoken symphony that thrums through every corridor and arch. I am home. And the labyrinth and I remain, indivisible.
