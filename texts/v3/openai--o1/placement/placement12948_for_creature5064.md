I do not remember exactly when I came here—whether I arrived as a child, eyes bright with curiosity, or whether I was already grown and in search of rare solace. Perhaps my memory of crossing from the world outside has softened into the same mist that clings to these terraces. However it happened, I’ve long since settled into this plateau city of hushed stone and slow-drifting cloud, and I am—well, I suppose you might call me a steward of the ridges.

I say “steward,” but there’s no formal title given here; it’s more a role I’ve grown into, like the way ferns take root in a cradle of moisture between the stones. My days wind along the outer walls, the place where the city meets the slope, where great shards of rock shear up at crooked angles. These edges are seldom crowded; families go there only for essential tasks like collecting wild mushrooms after fog or gathering loose stones for building. Yet I find myself drawn to the boundaries, watching how the land and sky brush against each other along the plateau’s rim.

Sometimes I carry a thick, worn ledger that I’ve slowly compiled over eons—if time can be measured at all. I track subtle changes in the rock face or the patterns of water running through the channels. Perhaps one day a vine has curled differently around a jagged outcrop; I note the shape in careful sketches. Another day, I find that a hairline fracture in the wall has lengthened by the smallest fraction, hardly more than a whisper in the stone. I run my fingers along the crack, feeling a tiny shift that the rest of the city either hasn’t noticed or has quietly folded into its unhurried awareness. It is my peculiar calling, I think, to watch for these minute transformations that drift through the city like underwater shadows.

While others tend goats or weave heavy fabrics, I spend hours kneeling at a rock ledge, pressing my ear to the stone to sense its faint reverberations. The wise-ones occasionally stoop beside me, exchanging a meaningful glance or a single question in that soft-lilting language we speak. They might nod and say that the echo in the rock “breathes differently” now. I record their words with care, mindful of how even the quietest shift might ripple across centuries.

Down in the city proper, you might spot me at evening, returning to the communal courtyard, ledger tucked under one arm, hair damp from the perpetual mist that never quite forms into rain. My garments are the same textured cloth as everyone else’s—some mottled gray that merges with the gloom, sewn with thread dyed in the faintest of russets. Once in a while, I’m asked to speak of what I have learned that day, and though I find it difficult to put such intangible observations into words, they listen patiently. The watchers nod, the children tilt their heads with mild curiosity. I sometimes sense they’re secretly hoping for an omen of change, a sign that the hush might shift—even if they also fear what that means. I offer them hushed reassurance: “The rock hums in time with the water,” or “The cracks are stable for now. No new paths have opened.” And they exhale as if comforted, letting the hush settle over them once more.

When the city’s day moves toward that perpetual twilight, I perch on a low stone bench in the courtyard. Overhead, the sky remains a dim pewter that never quite descends into true darkness. A brazier flickers amber, making the wet stone gleam. Children pass by, their laughter subdued as if aware they exist in a realm that can hold no exuberant clamor. They gather to play in the courtyard’s hidden corners, bounding around half-buried columns carved by ancestors no one fully remembers. I’ll wave mildly to them, smiling at the timeless quality of their games. They don’t grow—at least not in any easily charted sense—and yet their minds develop, their skill at weaving or chalking patterns on walls becoming more refined each season. I suspect the flow of time in them is a slow tide, invisible to quick glances.

I’ve grown used to the city’s unspoken equilibrium, but even so, sometimes I feel an undercurrent of longing, as though I yearn to see how the world might look if it truly changed from morning to night. I have the sense (or memory) that in other lands, the sun makes a grand display daily, sinking into regal, sherbet-hued sunsets. Here, the sky remains as though it were cast in burnished silver that never clarifies, never darkens. I place my hand upon the city’s walls as if I could coax it to shift, to let in some fraction of that outside time. Sometimes a fleeting voice in my mind wonders whether tomorrow might break the pattern—but tomorrow always dawns with the same pewter glow, the same crisp ridges. The city stands unaltered, as patient and eternal as the stones themselves.

On certain days, though, if you came looking for me, you’d find me carefully hoisting myself onto the precarious heights of the city walls—those thick parapets that overlook the plunging tiers below, from which you can glimpse the wide valley. I stand there in the swirling wind that tastes faintly of wet rock, scanning the layers of plateau stepping down and away. Beyond, the valley seems hazy, as if seen through a veil. Sometimes, I spot shapes that might be roads or farmland in the distance—beyond our timeless domain. I’ve heard tales of cityfolk who ventured down those slopes and returned changed, their eyes deeper set, their silence more resonant. This is the borderline of our hush. It excites me; it unsettles me. I study the twists in the ravines, imagining where a path might lead, if one had the patience—or the will—to depart.

Yet each evening, I go home. My little house nestles along a modest terrace near the southern gate, with a single window that looks onto a rocky courtyard. Moss creeps up from the base of the dwelling, vines drape over my wooden shutters. It never fully rots, for rot cannot take hold in this half-paused realm, but neither do the boards look new. The timbers exist in a curious stasis, as though they have just enough life in them to resist decay indefinitely.

Inside, I keep a personal shrine of shaped stones and small tokens. They are my offerings to the rhythms I do not fully comprehend but which I strive to honor. Sometimes I rest my hand upon the broadest stone—smooth from centuries of watery condensation—and I murmur my thanks. For all its oddness, the plateau gave me quiet. It gave me the hush in which to think, to wander, to observe. I speak a wish into the silence: that if ever genuine change must come, it might be gentle, so as not to shatter such precious stillness.

I recall once seeing a traveler—someone distinctly “other” in their gait and speech. They stayed only a short while in the city. I absorbed the way they glanced around with wide, questioning eyes, pressing for clarity as to how or why no one ages here. I politely showed them the best vantage points overlooking the ridges, explained with slow words that time here is different. I offered them a share of my meal: a warm stew of foraged greens and goat’s milk. Their curiosity never found a definitive answer, I think. Eventually they stepped back down the steep path and vanished into the valley gloom. I sometimes wonder if they made it home, or if the memory of this place clings to them like the perpetual damp.

In truth, I am not young. I do not look old, either, at least not in the everyday sense. My face, if studied closely, shows hints of lines that come more from quiet contemplation than from the rough usage of years. My hair remains dark. My limbs remain strong for the work of climbing ridges, hauling small timbers, or chiseling the city’s stone. Yet it feels as if these changes in me occurred so slowly that it’s only in reflection that I notice any difference at all—like turning around to see a vine you never planted has grown halfway across a wall.

When I dream, the city sometimes stirs in my mind and rearranges itself with labyrinthine cunning. I see walls twisting, houses shifting like blocks in a puzzle, and doorways opening to new corridors. Upon waking, though, everything is exactly as I left it—at least so far as my daylit eyes can discern. Maybe the watchers or the wise-ones see through to deeper patterns. Perhaps the city does shift at night, but only along some dimension that my mortal eyes cannot detect. If so, it’s all part of the hush—another echo in the stone I’ve yet to read.

Still, I remain here, a soft-eyed sentinel. I do not know if I arrived as an outsider and chose to stay, or if I was born in these winding corridors under a perpetual pewter sky. But it hardly matters. In this place, origin stories do not hold much weight; the act of being here is enough. I keep my ledger, keep my watch, and each day gather small increments of knowledge: the hiss of wind through a new fissure in the stone, the glimmer of dew that collects at dawn, the exhaled hush of a place that has made peace with stillness.

And so, if you ask me who or what I am: I am the one who listens to the echoes in the ridges. The one who stands at boundaries and wonders what lies beyond. I record the city’s pulse—its subtle changes, its near-static hush—and offer small acts of stewardship. I carry the memory of each day’s quiet dawn, each luminous, half-lit night, folded into the pages of my ledger. I have found my place among the people here: neither entirely a mystic nor fully an ordinary worker, but a caretaker of small moments that drift through an unchanging world.

This is my life in the city of shimmering stillness. I inhabit a twilight between motion and stasis, standing watch at the edge of time’s gentle forgetting. And as the years—if that word can even apply—gently pass, I continue to collect the patterns of the rock and the wind, quietly hopeful that my little record might someday prove that change, no matter how slight, can find its way into the hush and remind us that, beneath the stilled surface, life’s rhythm still beats on.
