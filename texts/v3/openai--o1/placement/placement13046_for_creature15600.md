Sometimes, I think I was born inside a crack that formed between the old wood and the new metal plating—a gap that let something else slip through. That’s how I’ve always felt in this city: not firmly of it, yet knit so tightly into its tissues I cannot leave.

I think of my earliest memory as lying pressed against a corridor of living bark. The smell was damp, metallic, the scent of hot iron and wet moss swirling around me as I opened my eyes and found a vine curling over my forearm. I could feel it breathing for a moment, as if my pulse and the vine’s pulse beat in sync. When I shifted, the corridor seemed to shimmer, each strand of bark jostling a fraction out of place. From then on, I knew that the illusions weren’t just anomalies but signals—especially for me.

I call myself a corridor-tender. It’s not an official title; I’m no forging master, nor a gatherer. I move between the vines and plating, listening for the place where reality snags. Curtain-thin spaces exist all across this city where the architecture settles oddly, where the illusions coil into corners with subtle creaks. The watchers have rods and instruments to quell flickers when they threaten to warp a building’s structural balance, but I have only my hands. I run my palms along the damp wood or across a seam of hammered iron, coaxing it to lie still. Sometimes, I perch on a high walkway and watch entire segments of the city’s scaffolding blink out of sync a moment, shifting a vine or two to reveal pockets of swirling mist behind. I gently hush it back into place with soft words—words that are less language than a hum that resonates in the living matter.

The city’s labyrinth is mostly deserted on the upper floors, where the archways are so wide they vanish into a mist-laden dream. That’s where I wander. Dressed in a cloak of living leaves that’s half grown into my shoulders, I pad along vine-carved paths that sometimes shift beneath the weight of my footprints. Others here might call me one of the watchers, but I lack the watchers’ quiet, methodical intensity—they gather in smaller knots, conferring in near-inaudible murmurs. I’m more of a rover, half-lost, half-pilgrim. When I encounter watchers on a balcony, we nod to each other in passing. We are each caretaker in our own way.

Occasionally, I pause to help the forging masters. Their chambers are clusters of steam and clang. I slip in like a ghost and might weave some of the creeping ivy back from an overheating vent. My gift is subtle: I have a way with illusions, as if I can hush them before they destabilize a weld. The forging masters don’t quite understand it; they assume I’ve learned from the shapers. Maybe I did. I recall once exchanging a few quiet lessons with a shaper who guided me through a living root’s swirl of energies. But mostly, I’m self-taught, following an intuition that hums inside my chest whenever the city starts to quiver or twist out of phase.

You can often find me in the half-lit sections where the lines between corridor and open air get blurred. Mist seeps in along the floors, and condensed water glistens on the bark overhead. Sometimes the illusions are stronger there: stepping forward, you might suddenly see your own reflection in midair—a mirrored corridor overlaying the real one. I close my eyes, place a palm against the nearest vine, and let my exhalation guide the illusions gently away. Call it a lullaby or a fuse. The illusions recede like a tide, leaving behind real corridors that shine wetly in the faint mechanical glow.

When I’m not wandering as a corridor-tender, I reside in a narrow loft I coaxed from the city’s living substrate. It’s part of a massive vine that arches in a semi-circle over a forging quarter below. The inside of my loft has braided walls woven from smaller, pliable vines that parted just enough to allow me a place to sleep. In wet weather, I can direct the new growth to tighten. The living bark fuses, becoming almost watertight—yet still it’s never entirely dry here. Shallow condensation finds its way onto every surface. Trickles of moisture gather around my meager metal possessions: a small copper kettle, a tin mug, a few battered table utensils I picked up in a forging yard. Everything rusts if I don’t keep it clean. So each morning, I wipe away the orange-brown flecks that appear on the kettle’s handle, or the rivets at my loft’s entrance. I swab them with a wad of fibrous moss, muttering a low chant about dryness that the watchers taught me. Maybe it’s just all in my breath.

Sometimes I host small gatherings in this loft. Gatherers come to trade stories over a cup of steam-laced brew. They speak of the swirling mists they’ve seen across the city’s ridges, of near-mythical “root-behemoths” lurking far below—an occasional quake in the vine architecture. Over the rim of my mug, I’ll listen, noticing how faint illusions ripple at the corners of the gatherers’ eyes. They’re tense. No one likes to admit it, but everyone knows a shift is coming. Something is building in the city’s living skeleton: a realignment, the watchers say. They see it in the illusions that flash in broad daylight rather than quietly in the gloom. They see it in the unseasonable bursts of vine growth forcing corridors to warp. More than once, I’ve had to intervene when an entire walkway threatened to slip out of phase with the forging district below, leaving a wide gap in midair.

I used to fear these illusions—sometimes they feel like the city’s mind is stirring, uncertain whether it wants to remain anchored or transform. But as the illusions grow more frequent, I find myself strangely calm. Maybe it’s because I’ve realized we all belong to this living, changing shape—we each have a place in the city’s respiration, no matter how ephemeral it feels. Maybe I’ve grown so used to the shifting and shimmering that I no longer mind where it carries me. I like to think I’m here as a kind of caretaker for the city’s in-between states, bridging the intangible with the real.

When the illusions come in waves, I slip along narrow corridors, guiding passersby where the vines are stable. People press close, uncertain. Some cling to the walls, wincing when they see entire swaths of ceiling flicker in and out. Their fear is a tangible hush. In that hush, I raise a hand and let my heartbeat resonate. Sometimes the illusions fade back. Sometimes we have to wait for them to subside on their own. In the swirling half-light, I gently coax the bark to realign, brushing my fingertips across slow pulses that vibrate through the wood. Metallic plating rattles, steam pipes hiss, but eventually, the corridor stabilizes. The watchers might come by, rods glowing with faint green pulses, to make sure everything is safe. Then they vanish again, and we are left with steadiness—until the next alignment shifts.

In quieter hours, I descend into the deepest halls where artisans craft tiny contraptions from living-wire. Their fungal lamps glow emerald, reflecting off hammered iron surfaces. The place smells of mushrooms and warm metal. Invisible pulses flicker through the vines down here, and the illusions can be more subtle: sometimes an entire row of fungal lamps will spontaneously darken, revealing a glistening sub-layer of the city behind them. At other times, I’ll brush a hand across a cluster of wires and feel as if I’m touching the city’s central nervous system. Quiet thrums trickle up my arm, like gentle static. I tell them it’s all right, that we are still here. And the illusions, or the pulse, or maybe the city itself, gently ripple in response.

Late at night, I sit at the edge of an open-air platform far above the forging district. Mist wraps the city in a half-glow, and the forging hammers pound a distant heartbeat. Sometimes an entire arch of vines far in the distance will shimmer, revealing flickers of unknown shapes—perhaps silhouettes of watchers crossing from spire to spire, or something else entirely. If I sit there long enough, now and then I’ll see a massive shape shift far below, making the vine floors tremble in slow waves. I wonder if it’s one of the root-behemoths stirring in its ancient slumber, aligning itself with the illusions. Sometimes I softly murmur a lullaby that I remember from childhood. I don’t know if I made it up or if the city did. Whenever I do, there’s a sense of calm and hush. The illusions die down, the forging halls go quiet, and the city seems to breathe in time with me. All its rust, all its steam, all its illusions, woven into one slow exhalation.

And in moments like that, I feel certain that the city won’t crumble or tear itself apart—that beneath its mysteries lies a stable rhythm older than any forging hall or vine trunk. I feel intimately bound to that rhythm, a subtle presence that moves between illusions and reality, guiding them into balance. I’ll remain, a corridor-tender and sometime-lullaby singer, perched on balconies that might flicker out of existence at any second, unafraid. The living bark, the hammered plating, the swirling illusions—they are all part of our home, and they all need a gentle hand to keep them honest. That hand might be mine, or maybe it belongs to all of us who dwell here in the hush.

So you ask where I am, who I am, in this place of rustling green peaks and shifting illusions? I am the one walking the thresholds, the caretaker of corridors that seem to vanish in the corner of your eye. I slip among the watchers and forging masters, bridging their tasks for a moment, then move on. When the city’s illusions grow too bold, I’m there with a soft hum or a palm pressed to the vine-walls, reminding them that we belong. I do not rule this living metropolis, nor do I shape its growth in any grand sense; I only help it remain calm in its own unstoppable transformations.  

That is who I’d be, here, in this high realm of vine-woven halls and drizzling condensation: a companion to the illusions, a gentle steward in the shifting spaces, a quiet soul thanks to whom the city can keep breathing in all its rust and wonder.
