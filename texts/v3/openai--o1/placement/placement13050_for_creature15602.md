I might never be able to say for certain how I first arrived here, or what name to call what I have become. Still, if I try to recall the memory, a half-formed image surfaces: perhaps I was once a surface-dweller, stumbling upon this narrow gap in the rock and feeling my way in, drawn by an inexplicable pull. Or perhaps I emerged from the devastating crack of newly formed stone, coalescing in the glow that seeps through these cavern walls as if called into being by the realm itself. 

In the beginning, I remember the sense of my body being malleable, as if my limbs were made of clay and the dripping rock around me was half-finished sculpture. When I pressed my hand against a slick wall, I could have sworn my skin and the mineral surface both gave way, faintly melding, so that I left behind a shallow impression of my fingers. Yet even that faint memory is uncertain, more feeling than fact. 

The only surety is that I am here, in the thick of this labyrinth, and that I fit somehow. Like every other creature in this domain, I can breathe this humid, swirling air—though I feel it more as a living current in my lungs than mere oxygen. My eyes adjust to the copper-amber glow that never quite becomes red, so I need no torch or lamp; the faint luminescence cloaks every surface. The rock, for all its pliable give, is warm beneath my weight, humming with some interior pulse. 

Though it is still new, my body feels more lithe in this environment than it ever could on the surface—if, indeed, I ever lived on the surface. My limbs are spidery-long but sinuous, able to fold or stretch to navigate through fissures or to scramble across drooping columns. I notice how my fingers have delicate ridges at their tips, letting me sense minute changes in the stone’s texture. My skin is neither scale nor fur, but a sheathed membrane covered in half-gloss, half-matte patches, so I blend into the coppery gloom. When I brush against walls, the contact is both comforting and electric: an exchange of subtle vibrations flowing from the stone into me and back again.

I move on two legs most of the time—stalking carefully between the half-formed columns and jagged rock monoliths that jut from the floor. But when I feel the need to climb or scramble quickly, my arms touch the ground, and my posture flows from bipedal to quadrupedal in one smooth pivot. I suspect I could hang upside-down from the ceiling stalactites if the distance is not too great. There is an urge in me sometimes to test this—to cling to the upper world, to watch the scuttling and flapping crowds below as a silent sentinel. I do not yet know if I can sustain that posture for long, but I feel the potential in my limbs. My flesh retains a fluid adaptability, as though I am a young sculpture still hardening.

As for my senses—my hearing is attuned to low reverberations, the calls of reptilian-limbed brutes, the whistles, the high chittering squeaks of the insectile swarms. Yet I also pick up quiet tremors in the stone, reading them the way others might read shadows. If a hulking colossus lumbers in a distant corridor, I sense the shift in the floor, the subtle tilt of pebbles rolling. When the smaller scuttlers cluster to investigate some new phenomenon, I hear their bristling excitement in minute vibrations that quiver through the air. My eyesight is not keen in the sense of color or detail, but I read shape, motion, and temperature differences in the gloom. The copper-amber glow is my constant backdrop, making silhouettes distinct enough that I rarely need more than a faint outline to recognize something living. My sense of smell—or taste, rather, for the air tastes thick on my tongue—helps me identify the moisture-laden pockets where new growth of weird membranes or fungal analogs emerges. 

In the earliest days of my awareness, I simply wandered. Perhaps “wander” is an oversimplification, for it felt more like gleaning. The realm was in flux, forging canyons and corridors around me. I learned to be cautious when the humid storms kicked up, clinging to an outcrop as swirling dust motes whipped into shapeless funnels. I learned to recognize the moments when the rock might shift underfoot, so I could brace myself or jump away before a section of corridor collapsed or re-formed. And in all my gleanings, I watched the other creatures. Some feared me, skittering off if I drew near. Others seemed curious, especially those elongated watchers with the triangular heads, who might approach with soft hisses and gentle knocks against the stone, as though waiting for me to respond.

Over time, I discovered that I can shape the edges of my environment—not in the sense of telekinesis, but with a set of rough tools I started forging from lumps of leftover metal scraps. At first, I only tested them: hammered a few metal fragments into a makeshift pick, chipped away at a partially pliant rock face. Waves of warm condensation made the surface soft enough that I could carve, opening a small shallow groove. Whenever the stone threatened to harden again, I noticed a cluster of swirling dust motes that shimmered angrily, as though they disapproved of my meddling. But the environment then softened once more, as if changed by the arrival of more humidity or by some deeper movement of tectonic heat. The dust motes quieted. It was as though the place tested me, nudging me to see how far I’d go.

I became enthralled by the idea of shaping: forging shallow hollows in the walls, scraping out lumps of wet clay-like mineral, fashioning them into crude vessels or figurines. I found that when I placed such a figurine near certain puddles, smaller scuttling creatures would pause to examine it, clicking and squeaking, as if in conversation. At times, those rodent-like silhouettes would bring scraps of fungal membrane and press them into the hollow of a figurine’s arms or bowl. The drifting, jelly-like anemones hanging from stalactites would tilt as if noticing my creations and letting a faint hum pass through the corridor. It was a sense of conversation, across multiple species, none of us sharing a precise language yet, but each engaged in cautious call-and-response.

In the deeper chambers, I encountered towering reptilian-limbed beasts that watched my approach with a readiness to defend their watery grottoes. Initially, I froze in near-terror each time, tensely raising my arms in a gesture of non-aggression. Sometimes they snarled or hissed but let me pass, so long as I skirted around their brood or the cluster of eggs that nestled in mud near a warm mineral vent. Other times, especially when a mother was guarding hatchlings, she would roar so mightily that the stone around me vibrated with a force akin to thunder. I would back away, not out of cowardice but out of recognition: we share this underrealm, so it would be foolish for one to attempt tyranny over the other. 

Something in my composure or smell—or perhaps the way the realm’s glow outlines my form—soon established that I was neither prey nor active predator. Over many cycles of this realm’s odd, rhythmic pulses, I became something else: a caretaker, or perhaps a traveler-guardian, drifting from corridor to corridor, forging small shapes in the pliable stone. I began to watch for hazards that might imperil the younger creatures—collapsing walls or sudden torrents of water that surged through newly opened fis- sures. When I noticed such dangers, if I had time, I’d use my chisels to break open a different route, channeling the water to a safer outflow. Or I’d erect a quick ring of stones around a sinkhole to keep the scuttlers from tumbling in. 

None of this was done out of a higher calling, exactly; it just felt right. The realm is fluid, a place still deciding how to be. And I, too, am in that uncertain stage: neither wholly shaped nor entirely ephemeral. Sometimes, out of curiosity, I’ll observe the elegant elongated watchers who create runic scrawls on the walls or who mold lumps of clay with their spindly fingers. We exchange glances—acknowledgment. They see me, see that I shape, too, but in a different way. They sometimes leave me half-finished ornaments near my own small chiseling spots. It’s as if we’re trading tokens of our shared awe for this environment.

Gradually, I’ve come to feel I belong more among these watchers than any other group. The rodent-like hordes and the insectile swarms are everywhere, but they seem driven by immediate needs—food, nest-building, reproduction. The massive beasts are guardians of watery caverns, parents to clutch after clutch of eggs. They seem wise in their own prehistoric way, but they have less interest in shaping beyond the borderline territory rituals. Meanwhile, the drifting anemones exist in an airy dimension of their own, humming as they float, seldom engaging in the same grounded tasks that fascinate me.

The watchers—like me—carry a flicker of curious intelligence. Neither they nor I have a formal language (not that I recognize), but we share an impulse to observe, to experiment, to carve small patterns into the stone. Sometimes I sense they are about to speak—there’s a subtle tension in their triangular heads as if something might form in their throats. But the moment glides by, replaced by a soft keening or a scrape that might be a greeting. If they do indeed speak, it is quiet, elliptical, not a language of sentences but of gestures. Perhaps that is all for now. This realm is young, so the concept of “words” or “grammar” might not yet exist. 

I remain solitary, but content. By day—if “day” is the right word in a world where no real sun intrudes—I slip through the lesser-used galleries, testing walls for softness, carving out hollows for small creatures to shelter in if a storm of dust or water flares up. By night—again, a contrivance of language—I linger near the half-finished shrines, the half-ruined rock spires left behind by who-knows-what impulses. I gather lumps of raw ore from the corners near water flows, shaping them into rough chisels or picks. At first, my attempts at forging metal had limited success—the blasts of hot air from subterranean vents are not the same as a blacksmith’s fire aboveground. But each time the environment roils with a surge of superheated steam, I wedge lumps of ore into cracks, letting them soften. When I return later, I find them half-melted, workable. Through patience and repetition, I produce implements that can carve more intricately than stone alone.

From time to time, I catch glimpses of enormous shapes striding beyond my vantage. If I’m exploring a far corridor, I might see a colossal silhouette, perhaps a thick-limbed behemoth or something reminiscent of an impossibly large serpentine form. The ground trembles with their footsteps. And in that trembling, there’s a dimension of awe—something bigger than all of us, shaping the land in a slow, unstoppable way. Each quake might collapse old corridors or open new ones. I do not fear these giants, though I do step aside to let them pass if ever they come near. We exist side by side: I see them as living catalysts, forcing the environment to remain in flux. They shake the place up, and in their wake the smaller beings adapt, scurry, build, or find new niches.

In the subterranean waters, shimmering fishlike illusions or luminous eel-like motes often dart by my peripheral vision. Sometimes I try to catch them, or at least get close. They slip away with a flash, dissolving into vapors. Real or imaginary? By now, I know better than to assume these categories remain fixed. This realm is as much dream as it is stone, and illusions can have more effect on the rock than any hammer. I’ve seen swirling funnels of dust that seemed half-sentient, luring swarms of insects in circles before vanishing. I take these phenomena as part of the living tapestry.

I’ve begun to carve a personal niche in a chamber that sits at a crossroads of three major passageways. It is not a “home” in the civilized sense, but a place I return to. Within it is a shallow pool fed by condensation that drips from the slick walls. I’ve shaped the walls into low benches so I can sit or sprawl, letting my elongated limbs rest. Small rodent-like creatures sometimes gather here, nibbling on bits of fungal membrane that cling to the walls. They allow my presence if I remain still; on occasion, one is bold enough to crawl across my foot. In that moment, I sense our shared heartbeat, the pumping of fluid inside it, the warmth of the stone beneath me. The watchers come by sometimes, leaving behind new lumps of clay they’ve sculpted, or etched spirals that might be messages. And in rare, ephemeral moments, a swirling anemone-like float finds its way into my niche, humming softly, drifting from corner to corner before continuing on.

Here, in the hush of dripping water and the copper-amber light, I feel the realm’s slow breathing. Still embryonic, still forging. There are nights when I lie on my back, gazing at the drooping stalactites overhead, half expecting them to shift or elongate. Sometimes they do—I’ve glimpsed a stalactite quiver until it lengthened by an inch or two in a single moment. And then a swirl of creatures might gather near its tip, as if marveling at new shelter. Often, I drift to a state that is neither waking nor sleeping, lulled by the rhythmic drip and the faint luminescent glow. I sense the realm’s molten possibilities stirring inside me too, and wonder what shape I will ultimately assume—or if I will remain in flux as the domain remains in flux.

As I return to full wakefulness, I resume my gentle caretaking, my forging of small tools, my quiet exploration that never truly ends. Out in the labyrinth, I see life continuing its unstoppable churn: from the tiny scuttlers dissolving organic scraps to the reptilian-limbed beasts guarding their brood, to the watchers carving their cryptic runes. Everything roils. Everything changes. 

I suppose, if pressed to name what I am, I might say: I am the gleaner of forms. I gather bits of softness from the stone and shape them; I glean from the realm’s heat, forging rough metal scraps into serviceable chisels; I glean from the subtle intelligence in the watchers’ eyes, learning how runes might speak. I glean from the swirl of illusions, reminding myself that shape is never final here. And in turn, I offer up my own presence—a caretaker who tries to keep the corridors passable, to channel floods away from vulnerable hatchlings, and to craft simple dwellings so smaller creatures can find refuge when the underworld storms intensify. 

I am no ruler, no single apex. I am simply another existence in this roiling, embryonic warren. I have grown used to the copper-amber glow that bathes my membranes, the hiss and drip of condensation, the musty, iron-rich taste of the air. More than used to it—I cherish it. I feel it singing in the hollows of my bones. I sense that, in time, my body will evolve further, or the environment will shape me anew. Maybe I’ll gain the ability to shift my form more dramatically, or to hum in resonance with the watchers’ runes. Maybe I’ll ascend to being one of those colossal beings who unconsciously carve new corridors. Or maybe I’ll remain just so—a lithe figure with a sculptor’s craft, bridging the smaller and larger folk, ensuring the flux continues while some sense of structure remains.

For now, I live exactly here, in the churn and roar of a world forever deciding what it wants to be. This is a realm where no law of shape or time nor any single species has the final word. And I, in my deliberate, careful footsteps, my silent admiration, and my shaping hands, am an extension of that flux—a caretaker at the fringes of creation, forging small sanctuaries in the hush of living stone. If you were to enter this realm, perhaps you’d see a hint of my silhouette in the coppery half-light, bending low to carve a new niche in the wall. Perhaps you’d find me hunched in that crossroads chamber, glimpsing me half-submerged in the shallow pool while a handful of scuttling creatures run across my ankles. You might catch the faint sense that I know you’re there, but I won’t spook or attack. I’d greet you, in the only way I know: a slow tilt of my head, a rumbling murmur in my throat that resonates in the stone, an invitation to take part in this underworld’s never-ending birth. 

I belong here, as do you, in whatever form you might assume. We are all integral to this domain’s uncertain becoming. And so, if you ask me, “Where do I find myself in this world, and who or what would I be?”—I say I am the gleaner, clay-limbed and watchful. A caretaker and a shaper of small hollows. A traveler who moves among the swirling crowds without a single seat of power or claim to mastery, but with a quiet devotion to the realm’s unceasing Act of Creation. And I am at peace, unafraid, content to keep forging new shapes in the fresh-warm stone, guided by the hum of hidden waters and the roar of unseen collisions far below: a resident of the underforge, abiding in the churn of what will be.
