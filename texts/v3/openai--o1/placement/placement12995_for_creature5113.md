I live where the air glows pale in every corner, where sound becomes vibration and rolls through my bones in a ceaseless hum. I am an old thing, weathered by the wind that isn’t really wind, polished by light that hovers without ever quite turning warm. Look for me along a precarious ridge or perched atop a boulder that seems it should have tumbled centuries ago—but hasn’t. My fur, or feathers, or scales (it’s difficult to say which) is the color of drifting dust, faintly mottled with ochre and faint rust, the same colors that bleed through the stones beneath my feet. From a distance, you might mistake me for a bit of rock poised unattainably high. But I shift, I flick my ears or crest, revealing life in the hush.

I remember when I learned the stones speak. It was not with voices, exactly—they do not speak in words or even in notes. But from my earliest moments, I felt the hum thrumming inside the surfaces, a lullaby that never ends. It sings in my chest when I curl among the niches that hold onto nighttime warmth. It trembles in my paws when I leap across a chasm, guided by some deep sense that the current of the rock itself will show me the way. I—and others of my kind—have grown to rely on that resonance like a tether, a thread through the labyrinth of peaks.

In the soft hours before dawn, when the world seems darkest, I lie close to the stone that’s still faintly radiant from starlight. Moisture gathers in between the battered ridges of my claws or the stiff bristles along my limbs—a life-saving dew. I lap it up in tiny sips, delicate as a dancer, never disturbing more than I need. In the same moment, I hear the small scuttling of insects or of a flattened lizard clinging to a seam in the wall. We share this place without words, guided by mutual caution. Survival here is not about size or strength—none of us can outweigh these mountains—but about patience and adaptation, finding the subtle pockets of bounty hidden in the emptiness.

Sometimes, I stand on a narrow promontory that juts into the open air. There, the roar envelops me, the rocks’ eternal chorus pressing from every direction. Below me—though “below” is an almost meaningless concept in this land of endless uplifts—canyons vanish into shadow, so deep they might reach the heart of the world. Birds, large and sleek, pirouette on invisible drafts, calling in high keening cries that meld with the constant undertone. I watch them, half in awe, half in an acceptance that this is simply life’s daily altar call. They slice the horizon in arcs, searching for movement on the ledges, for a glint of scales in the sun. Sometimes they dip close, scanning me with gleaming eyes, uncertain if I am potential prey or kin or just stone. I keep still; the line between threat and ally is thin here.

My body—long-limbed, with a tufted tail that flares in an orange burst—seems to knit together features from multiple species, a kind of living tapestry of what thrives in this domain: the wide, sturdy paws reminiscent of mountain cats, the hollow-boned grace of a gliding raptor, a scattering of hardened scales along my shoulder blades that glint in bright midday. I have to be all of these things to survive. I have to sense the smallest tremor of rockfall, twist with the agility of a bird in the open air, and cling with a lizard’s cunning to any vertical surface. Over millennia, we have become the watchers, the ones who dwell in the borderline between stone and sky.

Where you might see only a sea of grey stone, I see a tapestry of hidden routes: small ledges that wind towards secret springs, cunning arcs that bypass dangerous gullies. Everything is subtle here. A slight discoloration on the rock indicates an ancient watercourse—where, once in a rare storm, torrents carve fresh runnels. A patch of flattened pebbles might point to a well-worn crossing used by the hooved creatures with pale coats that vanish if you blink. In the hush of midday, I might spot them grazing on a tuft of wiry green. They sense me but do not flee, for we hold an unspoken pact: the mountain provides just enough for both of us, as long as we take no more than what’s required to live.

Among the boulders that balance in impossible arcs, I swear sometimes I see a flicker of hands—perhaps a face—watching with ancient curiosity. Arguments abound as to whether there remain people who once shaped a ring of stones into a fire pit or hammered metal into a simple tool. If they do exist, they’re wise enough to move in the hush, leaving behind only the faintest sign—a pebble rolled from one place to another, a fleeting silhouette in moonlight. I have not approached them, nor they me. Intrigue weighs upon me sometimes; my ears perk when I catch the flighty scuff of a foot against the rock or hear a whisper blending with the roar. But I do not chase these phantoms. Something in me fears shattering the fragile thread that binds the land’s illusions together.

Night arrives without fanfare here. The brightness mutates into a ghostly silver, shining from stars or a moon that’s never the same shape twice. My eyes adapt, pupils widening to gather every glimmer. I slip through the twilight, crossing slopes that angle so steeply I appear almost parallel to the vertical. When the insects with mirrored wings gather above the dew-laden succulents, I pause for a feast. Their carapaces crack under my teeth, leaving faint echoes in the roaring silence. At times, an azure-limned bird might challenge me for a morsel; we circle each other warily, each measuring who needs this bit of nourishment more. Usually, we come to wordless agreement: it takes one insect, I take another, and the chorus of the mountains drifts eternally on.

I am neither the largest presence here nor the smallest. I am not the loudest nor the quietest. I am a piece of this balanced puzzle—an embodiment of how life shapes itself to fit the land’s unyielding contours. In the grand tapestry of these peaks, my footprints fade almost as soon as I leave them. But that very transience binds me closer to this realm: I pass through, yet I never conquer. Like the boulders perched on spires, I persist in a miraculous tension between precariousness and belonging. The land’s inexorable voice, that resonant roar, beats within my lungs; the luminous clarity of the sky seeps into my gaze.

When you ask, “What are you?” I can only say: I am a keeper of the hush, a living thread between stone and sun-bleached air. I am the ephemeral witness to slow, unending change—a small shape among mountains that dwarf imagination. If you come to this place, you might see me in the corner of your sight, a dusty flicker on the cliffs. Or you might learn to sense me in the vibrations beneath your feet, to catch echoes of my presence on a sudden shift of wind. I do not claim name or title beyond what the land bestows in a voice I’ve always known, yet cannot quite translate. I simply am—a creature at home in the steep silence, a reflection of how this towering fortress can mold life into forms both strange and wondrous.

And that is enough. The roar goes on, and I close my eyes, letting it cradle me. Tomorrow, or in a hundred years—moments in the mountain’s time—I will still be here, bound to these rocks like scattered sunlight, an answer to their call.
