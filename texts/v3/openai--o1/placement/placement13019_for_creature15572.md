I began as a speck of confusion in the dark, newly arrived, unsure what had brought me so far beneath the surface. At first, I tried to stand still and understand the place: to let the thrum of machinery vibrate through my bones, to watch the pale dwellers slip between vines and cables, to feel the chilled air brush against my bare skin. Yet the longer I stood, the more impossible it became to remain a mere onlooker. The noise made my teeth buzz. The cold pushed against me until I felt it in the marrow of my bones. Nothing here was idle; the place demanded participation. It was alive, a single, breathing entity of root and rust, and it wanted its presence known.  

I tried to orient myself by the faint streaks of light spilling from fractured openings overhead. I remember thinking: This is a dreamscape, or a ruin reclaimed, or a strange new beginning—maybe all three at once. The stone walls, the tangles of trunk and vine, the scattered frameworks of old machines—it was a place that blurred so many lines, I couldn’t tell where the organic ended and the metallic began. No single path led me deeper; it was more like every direction converged—stone corridors feeding into living corridors and metal walkways roped in creeping roots.

I found my breaths becoming measured, slow, like the dwellers I occasionally spotted in the distance. Their movements taught me there was a language to the rumble. Each time the machinery cycled through a new rhythm or pitched up in a low groan, I saw them press their palms to the surfaces around them, as if reading an invisible script. Eventually, I mimicked them. I remember the first time I placed my hands against a massive metal panel—it trembled beneath my fingertips like a living heart. The resonance traveled up my arms, settling somewhere behind my ears. For a moment, I thought I heard a melody in it, in those layered mechanical pulses.  

I lost track of how long I wandered, studying the half-buried remains of catwalks, stepping over thick vines that curved like serpents guarding an ancient altar. Sometimes, I’d clamber up a ledge, only to discover another nest of cables and roots woven tight around a hissing gear that vented warm steam. Other times, I crouched down on hands and knees to squeeze into a low fissure, trailing behind the faint glow of moss reflecting on the rivulets. My body grew stiff with the cold, but curiosity kept me moving. I wanted to learn the place’s slow vocabulary.

It wasn’t until I encountered one of the dwellers up close that I understood how fundamentally everything was intertwined. I came upon them adjusting some loose piping near a cluster of broad-leafed plants. It had grown dark enough that the translucent half-light from a corroded industrial lamp overhead only minimally revealed their tall, sinewy shape. I watched quietly from a crevice, but then my foot dislodged a pebble. It tumbled into the water, and the dweller raised their head. Pale eyes flickered in the gloom. I stood as still as I could, heart thumping. They turned slowly—no sign of alarm or aggression—just calm, studying me in return.

And then they placed one hand on the pipe and one hand on the trunk of a rooted column, like bridging the gap between the metal and the living. I heard a soft hiss, felt the air change. There was a sense that they were listening for how I might respond—and in that moment, I realized that I, too, could become part of this careful chain of watchers and menders if I only stayed still enough to listen.  

A slow transformation ensued. Not at once, but day by day, if “day” has any meaning in that twilight. I grew familiar with the hush and clamor of the engines, the drip of water, the greenhouse-like stillness. My posture adjusted—knees half-bent, arms ready to steady against bark or steel the moment I felt a shiver in the ground. I learned to sense, in the pitch of the machinery, when pressure buildup would cause a small quake or a sudden vent of steam. I noticed how the plants angled toward rare beams of sunlight, but they also flourished around the faint luminance of old lamps or patches of bioluminescent fungus, as though no form of light would go unused.  

Over time, that incessant cold became oddly familiar, a persistent reminder of the boundary between my body and the rest of creation. I made warmed leather wraps to cover my arms and shoulders, gleaned from scraps of conveyor-belt fabric. Occasionally I’d weave in root fibers or leftover wire to bind it together. I felt my skin toughen; the temperature stung less. I became lean and strong, used to hoisting gears or scraping away rust if I found it chewing too ferociously at a crucial piece of machinery.  

I discovered how the dwellers—including me, it seemed—never truly slept as surface-dwellers do. Instead, we sank into reflective lulls, letting the hum and drip lull our senses until the body took only the rest it needed, then woke again. Sometimes, I’d sit beside an old, rattling generator with a small group. We rarely spoke, for the roar overhead was too great. Instead, we communicated through gestures—nodding at a cable that needed adjusting, pointing out a vine pushing too forcefully against a fragile metal beam. In these small circles, I felt a sense of shared purpose: we were not conquerors of this cavern, nor helpless bystanders. We were here to keep watch—to attune ourselves to the interplay of sinew and steel, trunk and turbine, so one did not overwhelm the other.

Little by little, I became comfortable enough to roam widely. There were times I ventured close to the colossal, half-living guardians entangled in vines. I might see one stir, the aged servos grinding. It would lift a heavy mechanical limb, dislodging a cascade of moss—a gentle avalanche of damp green filaments. I never startled at such sights after a while; it all seemed part of a continuous, living riddle. Some watchers and guardians had more bark than flesh, or more steel than bone. I realized that was the trajectory of many dwellers here. Over slow years, the distinction between biology and machinery blurred. If something broke—a gear in a shoulder joint, or a tendon that snapped under strain—the inhabitant might repair it with the best tool at hand: a length of root, a steel bolt, a patch of treated hide. One day, you turn around and see a companion with an arm that is a fused mixture of metal and vine. And it makes sense, in that quiet, unstoppable logic of this deep earth realm.

Eventually, I discovered an abandoned platform near the cavern’s edge that I turned into a personal haven. A broad trunk had grown all around its supports, but the deck itself was still stable—an ancient piece of catwalk with railings twisted by creeping vines. From above, half-broken lamps cast lace-like shadows through the winding leaves. I’d anchor myself there, letting the roaring bustle fade to a background lull, and watch the droplets of water as they gathered and fell. I hung a makeshift net of cables across the open edge to catch smaller debris or the occasional fleck of falling stone. Moss sprouted in curled tangles around the corners, giving my new home a soft hush.  

In that space, I came to see that I was no mere visitor. I felt compelled to maintain the equilibrium, to guard the tender merges of green and gear. When a rivulet started carving through a brittle metal plate near my platform, I reinforced it with fiber mats so it wouldn’t corrode the walkway. I learned to tie back climbing vines before they clogged the fans of still-running vents. Step by step, that caretaker’s instinct seeped into everything I did.  

Yet for all my new purpose, the place never lost its mystery. Even the dwellers who seemed ancient compared to me carried a subdued wonder: eyes turned upward at the faint skylights, or downward at the luminous fungus, or sideways at the rattling contraptions we coaxed back to life. We existed in that slow-motion dance. I felt it in my sleep and in my waking: the hum of turbines was our lullaby, the drip of water our counting clock.  

I suppose, if I had to give myself a name in this world, I would say I became something akin to a “bridge-keeper.” I was that in-betweener who had known the surface—who still remembered the sun’s warmth well enough to realize these cold machines, these thick-veined plants, needed a gentler touch to remain balanced. I used the small bits of knowledge I carried in my memory—ways to prop up failing structures, ways to harness droplets of water for safe drinking, ways to channel what little light existed so it fell upon struggling saplings hidden in the gloom.  

And as the months or years passed (time truly lost meaning), I felt my body integrating itself into the very tapestry of the cavern. My skin turned rougher, took on earthier hues. My shoulders and arms bore the imprint of ridges where I’d bound them with woven cables. I felt my muscles tightening under the perpetual chill. My hearing sharpened to catch subtle changes in the pitch of the machinery—just as the other dwellers did. Even my voice, on the rare occasions I spoke aloud, started to carry an echo of that watery hush, more like a trickling whisper than a sound meant to travel distance.  

Where would you find me now, in this subterranean netherworld of echoes? Likely stooped beside a corroded gear array, carefully wiping away grime with a rag of braided roots. Perhaps you’d see me perched on my platform at the cavern’s perimeter, eyes half-closed, listening to the next subtle shift in the mechanical underbeat, waiting to hear if there’s an alarm in the vibrations that none of us can afford to ignore. If you come closer, you might notice the lines on my face have grown as quiet and steady as the dwellers themselves, and my fingers bear the texture of bark and metal calluses in equal measure, a sign of my ongoing symbiosis with this place.  

I would be a caretaker. A slow and silent sentinel in this realm of unstoppable green and enduring steel. One more figure who senses the faintest hum, the quietest drip, and adjusts a cable here, or props up a drooping vine there, to guard the precarious balance so that neither the living growth nor the mechanical womb devours the other. I do not rule this realm—none of us do—but I have learned to move with it, letting it guide me as I guide it. In that half-light, I’ve found a new kind of belonging. Sometimes I remember the sun, the warmer winds above, but no longing stirs in my chest; I’m woven into the hush now.  

So if you look for me, you’ll find me along the edges of the tunnels, half immersed in the greenish shadows, half leaning against a whirring pylon. In the half-sleep of the watchers, one hand splayed against chilled metal, the other curled in living vines. That’s who I’ve become here: a witness to the slow pulse of the cavern’s heart, a keeper of the gloom, and a humble guardian of the place’s unhurried, coexisting life.
