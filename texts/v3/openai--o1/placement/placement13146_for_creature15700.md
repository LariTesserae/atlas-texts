I am not quite sure when I first began calling these caverns home. Memories of the surface cling to me in a fragmentary way: half-recalled images of rolling hills under sunlit skies, or the towering silhouettes of trees swaying in the wind. But that all feels distant now, like a dream viewed through dark water. Here beneath rock and earth, everything is much more immediate—the press of stone against my palm, the echo that reveals a chamber’s size, the hush of water threading through cracks in the walls. This is my world now.

In the settlement, they say I’ve always had a knack for going near the places no one else dares. I’m not a “drift-seeker” in the formal sense—no torch-toting explorer of the deep labyrinths, no proud wanderer who returns with rumors of glimmering caverns and transparent fish. My gift seems smaller at first glance: I know how to listen to the stone. From my earliest days here, when I was a child clinging to my mother’s wrist, I felt that certain places “spoke” with subtle murmurs. Others said I was imagining it, hearing echoes of my own footsteps. But, over time, people saw that I could sense when a corridor might be unsafe or when a wall might crack if struck in the wrong spot. 

It began quietly, as all things do in this subterranean life: an older man—he was no more than forty, but that was ancient to us—noticed how I hesitated before certain tunnels. He asked me why. I remember explaining to him that every time you press your ear against bare, freshly split rock, you can catch a faint hum or vibration if the stone is thin or unsteady just behind the surface. If it’s solid, the hum is deeper, slower. If it’s fragile, there’s a skittishness, a tiny, tremoring echo. He tapped the rock with his chisel, listened, and in the process discovered a hidden hollow behind the wall that might have collapsed if they’d chipped much further. From that day on, he called me “Stone-Listener,” and the name stuck with others too.

Now that I’m grown, you’ll see me wandering the settlement’s perimeter every morning. Well—our version of “morning,” anyway. It’s when the faintest beams of surface light filter down through the angled vents overhead, turning the damp air into a haze of shimmering motes. That’s when I go checking new passages. It doesn’t matter if they were pried open by the mason-makers or discovered by ambitious drift-seekers. My job is to squat beside the newly revealed rock faces, lean forward, and place my ear to the stone. I don’t rush. I wait for my own heartbeat to slow, for my breathing to settle, until the hush of the cavern pools around me. Then I tap gently with my fingernail or a short length of carved bone. Tap, pause, listen. Tap, pause, listen. If I hear that deep, sure hum, I’ll give an approving nod. If I sense something uncertain—like a muffled rattle behind the surface—I’ll warn folks to brace the tunnel first or try a different angle.

I often work alongside the mason-makers, the ones shaping blocks and arches. They joke that they’ve grown dependent on me. “No point in building a perfect arch if it’s going to split open next week,” they say. Their arms are thick from chisel work, their gloves rough with rock dust as they shape the walls, and I stand nearby, guiding them to safer ground. In turn, they teach me which veins in the stone are likely to yield workable slabs, which crystals are too brittle. I’ve come to appreciate the artistry in their trade: the neat lines incised by a steady chisel, the satisfying crack when a block cleaves away neatly from the parent rock. They create the frameworks of our new home, and I help ensure it doesn’t crumble.

In the quieter hours, when lanterns have been doused and only a few torches smolder near the communal fires, I slip away to gather with spore-weavers in the damp corners. Without them, the settlement would lack the faint shimmer of bioluminescent fungus that now dots the walls. They call me there sometimes because mushrooms and rocky fissures share hidden symmetries: the delicate fungal strands follow the grain of the stone in surprising ways. By listening to the rock, I’m able to guess where condensation will form next or which hairline fractures will cradle moisture—the perfect environment for spores to take root. In return, the spore-weavers teach me to gently cultivate the glowing threads, coaxing them to spread into new cracks so that entire corridors take on a subtle, ghostly glow by night. Standing under those pale strings of light feels like standing among star-trails deep underground.

The children of the settlement have taken to gathering around me, wide-eyed, whenever I press my ear to a newly revealed wall. They mimic me—little palms against the stone, heads tilted to the side. They giggle and whisper: “What do you hear?” I try to describe the feeling rather than the sound, because it’s more vibration than noise. And I see the spark in their eyes when they realize they too can sense the faint thrum of living rock. They’ll inch closer, testing their own taps against the surface, delighted if they can hear something, anything at all. Often it’s simply the echo of their own squeaks. But every now and then, one of them gasps and shouts, “I hear it! I hear it humming!” And I believe them. I suspect I’m not the only one with this gift.

Sometimes, though, I leave the main settlement entirely. I go alone, with a single torch and a small bundle of dried fungus for fuel, plus a water gourd. I move beyond the known mapped corridors, following tunnels that breathe out a subtle current of cooler air—almost like a silent wind. I’ve discovered chambers dotted with crystals as large as a person, their points jutting from the floors at odd angles, each cluster reflecting my wavering torchlight in a thousand shards. I’ve crept alongside silent pools that mirror my face so clearly it feels as if I’m staring into a second world. I do not go so deep as the real drift-seekers, but I go far enough to sense fresh threads of possibility, new expansions in the rock that might one day connect to unknown realms.

In the hush of these solitary explorations, I sometimes catch glimpses of other folk in the distance—shadows cast by their own weak lanterns, or glimpses of those broad-shouldered “Stone-Kin” rumored to dwell in labyrinthine depths. I’ve never approached them directly. Something tells me that they understand the stone’s language far more deeply than I do, and that they watch us from the edges of torchlight, deciding how much to reveal of themselves. Once, I found a small cairn of stacked river stones left near a passage I was sure no one from our settlement had explored. It felt like a sign. Perhaps an invitation, or a gentle warning. I left a polished piece of quartz there as an answer, uncertain if they’d understand my meaning, but hoping nonetheless.

Back in the crowded hubbub of the settlement, though, my role is simpler. I roam from one half-finished construction to another, up the wooden ramps and rope bridges that link different tiers of dwellings. My clothes are a patchwork: tough leather patches caked with dust, broad cloth wraps to keep the dripping water off my shoulders, and small crystal shards sewn along my sleeves. The crystals help me reflect the torchlight and cast it deeper into the gloom. They also serve as a little badge of pride—gifts from the mason-makers for each time I identified a stable vein of rock they could harvest.

Elders—who are not so old yet, but wise in their own ways—sometimes call me to the informal council gatherings, asking for my insight. “Stone-Listener,” they’ll say, “we’re thinking of opening a new side channel from the eastern cavern to draw in more fresh air. Does the rock allow it?” And I’ll gather my tools, my chisel, my short-length bone tapper, and go investigate. I’m not always certain, but I do my best. I share my impressions: does the corridor seem to shift underfoot? Does the water gather in unusual puddles, hinting at an underground stream that might undermine the floor? Did the rock near the surface cracks feel unusually warm or cold? All these small clues form a tapestry of possibilities. The elders weigh my words, discussing them around a small fire, planning the next step in shaping our home.

On rare occasions, I join the drift-seekers for short excursions, carrying rope and a supply of fungus-based tinder. It’s humbling. They’re far more fearless, though many appreciate my knack for sensing shifts in the stone. We move slowly, our voices hushed, so that the drip and trickle of water sometimes ring louder than our footfalls. We see wondrous things—enormous geodes half-embedded in walls, chambers filled with swirling mists like clouds trapped beneath the earth. And we hear echoes of unknown creatures. In those moments, I feel something primeval stirring in my chest, a blend of awe and unease. Not fear exactly—more like reverence. The stone is living, evolving; these caves are part of a breathing organism whose secrets we only begin to glimpse.

For me, though, the best part of returning is how the settlement’s soft chatter envelops me like a warm blanket. I step back into the main cavern where fires burn in shallow basins and children run after each other between the dwellings. I pass by spore-weavers tending to their damp boards, artisans painting swirling designs onto newly fired pots, and fire-tenders carefully stoking the charcoal embers that glow with a subdued, pulsing orange. They greet me by name—Stone-Listener, “Lena the Ear to Earth,” or just “You,” depending on who’s calling. And there’s a shared, unspoken understanding: we belong to each other here, in this place that is still learning to be a home.

I don’t picture leaving. Maybe some do dream of returning above or traveling deeper beyond the furthest known chambers, but I feel anchored to these caverns. Each newly revealed passage tugs at my curiosity. Each day, the settlement shifts a little—another platform hammered into the rock face, new rope lines bridging dwellings, fresh cracks appearing in the walls. It’s a never-ending dance between growth and caution, expansion and patience. And in that dance, I’ve found my calling: to read the signs in the stone, to guide how we carve our future so that we do not break the very ground that shelters us.

If you came looking for me, you’d likely find me crouched near a rising corridor, ear pressed against the cool dampness. You might hear nothing but dripping water and your own heartbeats, but I’d pause, eyes half-lidded, listening for a subtle resonance. With each tap, I sift through the intricate song of the earth, searching for places of strength and places of frailty. And in so doing, I help shape the story we are all writing here in this raw, shimmering world—a story told in the language of stone, water, and quiet determination. 

This is where I belong: in the hush of these deepest places, leaning in to sense what the rock whispers next, reminding us that we and these caverns are still, and always, becoming.
