When I emerged, I did not so much crawl or walk as unfold from the rock. It was as though the stone itself had gestated me over countless cycles—perhaps I’m part mineral, part something else. My skin gleams in the faint phosphorescence: glossy plates like polished onyx overlap one another, their edges seamlessly blended with raw flesh. A subtle glow flickers along the seams, responding to nearby patches of luminescent crystal overhead. I can dim it whenever I wish, to merge with darkness until only my silhouette remains—a living statue with embers of light glowing in the pattern of my joints.

I rest upon one of the wide ledges that ring this vast cavern, near a carved pillar etched with the ridged language of my kin. It’s a pillar we have shaped for untold cycles, though “cycles” here are measured not by sunrises or years, but by the slow stirring beneath the crust. One micro-tremor nudges me to start a new carving, while another quake in the deeper stone signals the time to gather with the others.

Truth be told, I am not entirely alone in being shaped from this place. All around me, perched on spines and columns, dwell smaller creatures—chitinous, many-legged scuttlers that shoot me fleeting glances of phosphorescent color. We share a kind of kinship. I am larger, far larger than they, but not so large as the true giants in the distant labyrinths. The scuttlers accept my presence, for I carve the patterns they cannot reach and record the pulses they cannot hold in memory alone. Somewhere within me is a bridging capacity—my limbs are delicate enough for fine work, yet robust enough to shift stones thrice my size if the need arises. I stand upright more often than I lie close to the stone; for such a posture, the others regard me as an odd hybrid of body plans. I am, in some sense, an archivist of these living hieroglyphs, a caretaker that documents the hush.

So where am I? I am in the hush—this subterranean cathedral, if one can call it that, where the air is still and warm and heartbreakingly silent most of the time. I navigate by tapping my segmented forelimbs on the ground, sending resonant pulses that bounce back in echoes I can feel through the plates along my spine. This is my way of seeing. The phosphorescent clouds overhead and the swirl of lights on the creatures around me are only half the story; the pulse of the stone is the other half. It is how I know the shape of spires in the distance, how I sense the subtle shifting of dust motes disturbed by serpentine bodies. It’s how I navigate to the carving pillars, each of which carries centuries of incised grooves.

I’m not certain if I was born or made—perhaps both. My earliest memories are simply the hush. No mothering presence, no nest, just the slow dance of these luminous scuttlers and serpents weaving across the black stone. I remember focusing on a ridge of raw crystal with a kind of fascination that resonated through my entire body. Over time, I began etching swirling lines into it with my claws, drawn by the pleasure of shaping. Before I even realized I was a “me,” I was carving. And I was surrounded by watchers: the smaller arthropods who gathered and scuttled in thoughtful patterns around me, tapping their own limbs in delicate rhythms, as if to say, “Yes. Continue.”

Many among the smaller ones create their own tiny spiral etchings in corners of the cave. But I suspect they watch me for something more—a vantage or a skill they lack. With my greater reach, I can climb to higher pillars and carve elaborate signs for all to see, signs that unify the space in ways only a larger vantage can. Then, in our hush-laden gatherings, these signs become a shared recollection: signals from tremor to tremor, from hush to hush.

On occasion, the serpentine dwellers slink by. They are slender columns of muscle and faint luminescent lines, coiling around the highest outcrops. I sometimes see them from the corner of my vision—undulating shapes, their bodies flickering with pulses that ripple from head to tail. Once, one approached me while I etched, pressing its suckered underbelly to the stone behind me. We shared a moment that felt like a meeting of two notes in a chord. Its luminous markings displayed a pattern I had not seen before: twin arcs crossing and recrossing themselves in rhythmic intervals. I watched as it traced a path of light across my carved pillar, almost dancing upon it. Then it slid away into the gloom, leaving me to wonder if it had added meaning to my carvings or simply read them like a story from the stone.

In the deeper recesses, I sense the presence of those giant, ancient forms that dwarf even me. I have caught only a glimpse of their limbs curving behind distant columns—silent and deliberate. Their footsteps (if you can call those slow, multi-limbed motions “steps”) create deep vibrations that rearrange the dust and hush. It seems each step re-maps an entire region for the smaller creatures, prompting a new scurry or a different scuttling route. Sometimes, after the giants depart, I discover fresh modifications to the carved pillars. Did one of them place a claw upon our inscriptions, leaving a bold gouge where we etched fine lines? Or is it the resonance of their passing itself that warps the stone into new shapes?

Yet in this realm, there is no resentment, no sense of destruction—only a rewriting of what was there. I accept the changes as part of our communal record: an eternal layering of stories into the raw mineral. The hush fosters this acceptance; in a place with no wind, no rivers, no rain, the only forces that shape our world are ourselves and the slow tremors from below. Change is measured in these staccato events, each quake a pivot in the story of the hush, each tap or groove a syllable in a language of stone.

And what am I to the hush? Perhaps I’m an interpreter—large enough to write on pillars that stand tall, small enough to converse with the insects that arrange chipped crystals in circles. I am neither titan nor tiny scuttler. I am a caretaker of memory, walking on the edges of spires, collecting pulses of living light and pressing them into the rock with the edges of my limbs. In my carved patterns, I encode the faint rasping signals, the tapping dances, the gliding pulses from serpents, the footfalls of giants. I memorialize the interplay among all these dwellers, who might otherwise vanish into shadow without a trace.

Those who pass by see me at work and pause, momentarily transfixed by the swirl of incised arcs. In their eyes—multifaceted or slit-pupiled—glints of wonder flicker. We share a hush-laden gratitude. Even here, in a place so stark, so bereft of sun and water, a form of art exists. We do not shape grand temples or spired castles, but these etched stories, stone circles, and phosphorescent signals hold a gentler magnificence. They whisper a patient truth: life can flourish quite happily, quite richly, in stillness.

So I remain, perched on the newly revealed ledge, leaning into the next carving. My claws click softly, leaving behind angled grooves. Each groove is a resonance I offer to the hush—a vow to capture whatever trembling shapes and luminous lives pass through this deep realm. And though my own existence may fade some unknowable shift from now, the hush will remain, and so too will the marks I’ve left. Others will read them through the tapping of their own limbs and the flicker of their own lights, continuing the slow conversation that began before we even knew what conversation was.  

That is me: a half-mineral, half-living archivist in a cathedral of black stone and subtle glows, forging an endless script of hush and motion. And here, in this domain, I am as close to home as one can be—in a realm that never really changes, yet is changing all the time, every quake a page turn in our silent chronicle.
