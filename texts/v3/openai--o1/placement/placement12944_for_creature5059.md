Sometimes I imagine first arriving as an outsider—looking down at my own reflection in the wet stone, hazy gray outlines bending and shifting in the runoff. The place is so alien that my initial impulse is to insist I don’t belong. The cold gnaws at me; the ground feels spongy beneath my feet and sometimes petrified. At times, I see myself as the reluctant explorer, wrapped in furs that I scavenged from some other world, feeling every reverberation of the plateau: the bone-rattling thrum of grinding stone, gearlike pulses in the earth. The air around me glistens with mist, and the shapes that move in that mist loom, vanish, reappear.

But the longer I remain, the more the land swallows my breaths and modifies my form. I feel the plants tugging at me, rewriting me. My hair might tangle with creeping vines until it becomes inseparable from them, each filament drawing in moisture in tiny droplets. Little by little, the moss that I crouch upon seems to wind up my legs. Not devouring, exactly—more like weaving. At some point, I realize I’ve become too entangled to pry free without losing something essential. And a thought forms: perhaps I’m not just wandering here; perhaps I’m taking root.

If I linger, magic happens. Instead of struggling against the plateau’s slow assimilation, I lean into it. I feel the constraints of my old body loosen. Veins become vines; muscle fibers rewrite themselves in fungal tissues that flex with combined elasticity. I become a perching creature, melding with a living trunk-arch that’s grown tall out of ancient petrified roots. My limbs don’t neatly end at the wrists or ankles anymore; they taper into lacy, fractal “fingers” of spongy bark and stone. Where once I felt the rhythm of my own heartbeat, I begin to hear the land’s pounding hush as a second pulse, coming from deep below, stronger than my own.

Soon, I can’t tell where my body ends and the plateau begins. When I shift to climb, the vines across my shoulders shift with me. My footing on slick rock feels sure because roots braiding from my ankles grip firmly, gripping the stone in a slow, tactile waltz. I can sense water in the fissures, not just by ear but as a subtle quiver passing through the threads that anchor me. If I stay still enough, I hear the faint metallic hollows where pockets of mineral-laced streams churn beneath the surface. My breath resonates with them—like I’m an extension of the plateau’s own respiration, my alveoli replaced with countless alveolate chambers of sponge-flesh and fossil-wood.

Maybe, though, that’s just one path of change. On another day, I might find myself metamorphosing into one of the watchers perched along the jagged cliffs: limbs folded, a thick, twisting tail of fibrous matter half-coiled around me like a serpent. My eyes shaped into strange reflective planes, not exactly eyeballs anymore, more like slivers of mirrored fungus. I watch the storms drive across the plateau; I watch herds of half-stone quadrupeds lumber toward the next watery depression. My vantage is perfect, high up in the swirling mists. I sense a remarkable calm in remaining statuesque at the plateau’s edge. That calm is only occasionally interrupted by the impulse to send out a call—a throbbing, gear-like drone that resonates in my chest cavity. When I do, it sends vibrations not only into the air but down through the stone. And somewhere, deeper beneath the rock, something echoes back, instructing me or encouraging me: shift your tail slightly, or cede your vantage to the next of your kind.

But what if I sink deeper still? If I followed the water, letting its trickling, unstoppable motion guide me, maybe I would slip down into the rock’s hollows, become one of the great living engines buried there. My form would be massive, part petrified root, part thick carapace of old bark and metallic exoskeleton. I’d half-doze in the darkness, letting the thick flow of water wash minerals and nutrients across my layered plates. When I awakened—slow and ponderous—I’d lift a massive trunk-limb, feeling the rumble of tectonic shifts like the clank of old, far-off machinery. Then my entire body would creak and expand. Steam would hiss from vents hidden under plate-like fungus growths. My head—if it can be called that—would rise patiently to the surface. And above, I’d glimpse silver twilight, the swirling clouds, the drifting sheets of vapor.

In that shape, I could walk with slow, reverberating steps, the water cascading from me in rivulets as I climb out onto the plateau. Perhaps other creatures would scatter or approach, uncertain whether to flee or worship. The plateau’s hum pulses in my chest. I direct small changes in local water flow, adjusting cracks in the stone or opening newly formed channels for the next wave of spore-laden breezes. To do so is not a conscious choice—it feels as natural as breathing or turning one’s head to follow a sound. In that moment, I might realize I’m not just a single being but an extension of the plateau’s vast intelligence. My mind is the plateau’s mind, my body a localized manifestation that could one day be reabsorbed back into the living earth.

Or I might exist as a roving vine-beast—a meandering shape that stands on rootlike tendrils, trunk bending and twisting as I stalk the watery terrain. I’d be smaller in scale, perhaps the size of a deer but elongated, with flexible interlocking plates of lichen-coated wood forming my torso. My head might be crowned with gently glowing fungi—like small lanterns that flicker in the gloom. I’d roam in the half-light, rummaging among the slick stones for raw minerals or searching for pockets of ephemeral insects to feed upon. My steps would leave behind glimmering footprints that fade with the next gust of wind. I’d communicate by thrumming pulses of air from a conch-like growth on my back, calling to others of my kind. Now and then, I might press my root-feet more firmly against the plateau’s surface and feel the deeper hum, receiving some unspoken directive that guides me onward.

But in the dripping arches of petrified trees, perhaps I’d find a colony-creature and become part of its slow synergy—a series of nodules or pods strung together by living threads. This might mean sacrificing my individual identity to merge with dozens or hundreds of small, pulsing growths. Each pod is a miniature heart, each mind a fraction of a whole. The collective moves in graceful unison, shifting across the plateau stone in a slow dance of expansion. When the storms erupt, we might anchor ourselves by sinking tiny rootlets into cracks. When calm returns, we drift on, leaving swirl-shaped imprints in the moss. Being part of a colony means letting go of personal boundaries—I am the group, and the group is me. Our senses blend together, and we become cognizant of countless subtle changes: a new crack in the rock, a hush in the wind, the hiss of far-off steam. We navigate the plateau with a single, cohesive intention, a living fractal of flesh-plant-stone intelligence.

On some other occasion, I might drift up with the fog and become one of those ephemeral shapes no bigger than a swirl of vapor—barely a creature at all, more a watery ghost flickering with pulses of phosphorescence. In that form, I’d barely have a lasting body, just a swirl of condensed breath and living static. I’d slip through the lines of steam venting from the deeper channel. I’d vanish when the wind blows too hard, re-form when the atmosphere stills. A fleeting entity, I’d pop in and out of existence, letting the plateau’s humming processes momentarily grant me consciousness. Perhaps I’d never be sure if I was real or if I was an optical illusion conjured by the watery twilight. Yet I’d witness all, there and gone, carried by every swirl of mist to observe countless wonders and vanish before any of them could notice me in return.

In each possibility, I discover a relationship with the plateau that transcends mere survival. I become part geologic phenomenon, part animate life. Maybe I become all of them in turn: the observer perched on the cliffs, the root-limbed wanderer in the shallow marsh, the monstrous engine stirring in the depths, the ephemeral swirl of cloud. Each form is an evolution of the same consciousness, faceted like water’s shape—fluid in one context, solid in another, invisible vapor in yet another. Where the plateau exerts its presence, I adapt; where the wind breaks, I bend. I shift or sprout or harbor spores across my body, carrying them to new growth sites. The land’s mechanical roars become my lullabies, its thunderous quiet my intellect, its swirling currents my veins.

And so, when asked, “Where would you find yourself in this world? Who or what would you be?” perhaps the only honest answer is: I would be the plateau, in all its myriad expressions, learning through every living shape that crawls or sways or hovers. I’d let the lines between identity and environment dissolve. I wouldn’t see a difference between inhabitant and land, because here that boundary is a temporary convenience rather than a fixed truth.

Yes, at first I might think of myself as a traveler—some strange being wearing damp clothes, trembling in the cold, marveling at the half-fossil forests. But given time, the plateau welcomes me, changes me, makes me part of its self-perpetuating machinery. My heartbeat merges with its deep mechanical thrums, and I discover I am one cog in the vast living apparatus. The watchers, the rovers, the fungal colossi, each is a vantage, a gesture, a voice in a symphony older than recorded time.

Ultimately, I find myself woven into the plateau’s silent conversation with the ice-laden sky. I move with measured rhythms, my breath forming small hisses of steam in the gloom. There is no single name for me—I am an evolving expression of stone, root, wind, and water. I belong to the plateau, and it belongs to me, an endless communion that needs no outside witness. In this antiquated, ever-blooming realm, that is the closest thing to being alive: not a lone creature, but a manifestation of the land’s relentless pulse, a reflection of its unbound, twisting life.
