You might begin in one of the winding mid-level corridors, unsure whether you want to go higher or descend deeper into the city’s metal bones. Over your head dangle thick, dripping cables, and beneath your boots, the walkway is slick with condensation. You pause at the edge of a gap in the floor, peering down into a coil of spinning gears that churn water through the channels below. A faint ring of daylight filters through from somewhere above, enough to cast a silvery sheen over your arms when you raise them. The sound of running water is constant here—somewhere between a hush and a rush.

But what are you in this place? A single name doesn’t quite fit yet; you’re still working it out. You remember a stray mention from a passing Drifter who, upon seeing you climb precariously to fix a leaky connector, called you a “Gutterseed.” They said it with an ambiguous tone—somewhere between affection and curiosity. “A Gutterseed,” you murmur to yourself now, testing how it feels.

Perhaps it resonates. You find yourself drawn to the half-light spaces near the gutters, fascinated by the green life sprouting in rivulets of water. You’re not precisely a Gutter Gardener, though you nod with respect when you pass them by, their seed-pouches hopping at their hips. Nor are you truly one of the Tinker-Colonists—though you’ve borrowed their scrap metal once or twice, forging small components you need. If pressed, you might say your calling sits between these roles: you’re a gleaner, a caretaker of lost corners, coaxing new possibilities from overlooked scraps. You climb up and down the city’s scaffolded layers searching for bits of neglected machinery, half-rotted planters, or twisted metal leftover from expansions, and you see in them the seeds of something else—something that can still serve life here.

Your boots are old, belonging once to a cargo pilot who’d traded them for a tinkered part. They squeak with water as you move. Your clothes, patched together from rubberized swatches and wire stitching, have begun to blend in with the settlement’s textures, showing mottled rust marks and moss stains. Over your shoulder, you carry a slim satchel containing spools of filament for quick repairs, a collapsible spade, and a series of small hand-scythes for cutting back vines when necessary. Your hands have rough gloves that you remove only when you need the sure grip of your fingers—fingers calloused from climbing the dripping struts, from wrestling with cables that never seem to stay in place.

You don’t remember exactly how you first arrived here—whether you were a child of Tinker-Colonists, or you simply wandered between battered hulls and steam vents until this place claimed you. The city has a way of doing that: capturing wanderers in its lattice of catwalks, inviting them to become a part of its unending expansion. Perhaps you recall a hazy childhood memory of a distant farmland or a small fishing village in the valleys below, but that memory is so faint you’re not sure it was ever truly yours. Now, each day, you wake to the gentle hiss of steam lines overhead, your cheeks damp from the nightly rains, your limbs stiff but eager for the chores and explorations to come.

As you move through the corridors, you greet a rustkeeper in passing: a tall figure stooped under a dripping eave, scraping orange flakes from a crucial beam. They grunt politely at your wave, mindful not to fling rust flakes in your direction. In a corner, you spot a Gutter Gardener carefully tilting a section of guttering so water collects in a newly hung cistern. A small sprig of green peeks out from behind their ear—a personal flourish that some Gardeners adopt. You share a nod; there’s an unspoken camaraderie between you, though you walk different paths. They know you’ve rescued more than one vine from the fate of strangling an intake vent.

Around the next bend is a cramped chamber that used to be an old cargo staging area. You’ve repurposed it as your workshop. The walls are patchworked with rivets and plates you’ve scavenged from scrapped elevator housings. It’s dusty and poorly lit, but it’s home. Here, you store your finds: twisted rods that might be hammered into brackets, cracked water tanks that can become large planters, and containers of leftover washers and bolts that you sort by size whenever a new idea strikes. You also keep trays of seeds or cuttings you’ve gathered from the city’s wild growth—small saplings of gnarled shrubs, coils of vine that root readily if given enough moisture and shade. A few fungus spore pods rest in a jar for the day you find a warm, humid corner to cultivate them.

At times, you think you’re the only one who truly believes the city is a single living entity. Others sense it, yes, your fellow Gutter Gardeners among them, and perhaps the Tinker-Colonists who speak of the “pulse” of the city’s expansions. But you’ve felt the heartbeat beneath your boots, heard it in the slow groan of metal beams adjusting to wind or the hiss of steam through labyrinthine pipes. You feel it in your own bones—like a resonance that calls you upward one morning and downward another, guiding you to the next corner in need. It’s this feeling that compels you to salvage a broken metal panel and find, hidden beneath, the perfect damp pocket for a cluster of fern spores to flourish. Over the weeks, you revisit those ferns. You watch them catch the drifting droplets from overhead, turning their fronds a deep, glimmering green.

Sometimes you venture far higher, climbing beyond the city’s crowded mid-levels toward vantage points shared by the Skyward Riggers. Up there, the wind snaps so hard you flatten yourself to a beam, face pressed to cold metal as the world howls. On those occasions, you sometimes see the silhouettes of Riggers scaling the spires, nimble as insects, eyes trained on the horizon’s swirling clouds. More than once, you’ve glimpsed them stringing a new line between two precarious towers, letting the tension hum with the wind. They always wave, acknowledging your presence from across a dizzying gap, and you wave back, your hood flapping like a sail in the gale. If you’re lucky, they’ll shout warnings about an incoming storm—this place is no friend to the unprepared.

When the storms come, it feels as though the entire city roars in chorus: metal clattering, water slamming against bulkheads, steam vents shrieking as pressure builds. You spend those hours sheltering among the upper rung corridors, adjusting improvised gutter extensions or checking if the flow is too forceful for your new seed pods. Once, you stayed out in a squall longer than was wise, hollering to a rustkeeper that you’d spotted a flooding channel about to breach a major mechanical hub. Together, you wrestled a stray corrugated panel into place to divert the torrent. By the time you were done, your clothes were so drenched that water squished in your boots for days, and the rustkeeper was forced to re-lacquer every seam on their jacket. Yet neither of you regretted it—it felt like a mission accomplished, the city breathing easier, returning to its calm susurration once the storm receded.

On nights when the fog drapes thick as a second skin, you’ve found yourself drifting to the lower levels. There, among the humming turbines and labyrinthine tubes, the Vapotouched sometimes invite you to warm your feet near a steaming vent. Their enclave is suffused with pale lamplight reflected on slick walls and mossy floors. You sit with them for a time, letting scalding clouds swirl around your cheeks. They speak quietly of new mushroom spawns or the faint hiss of overheated lines that need re-routing. Occasionally, you share a cutting or two with them—fungal spores for your seedlings—and in exchange, they offer you a handful of their golden fungus that glows faintly in the dark. “It might guide you,” they whisper, eyes shining in the steam. “Plants crave what’s in it—some mix of heat and life.”

Your existence is knit from these small kindnesses and alliances. In a city with no single authority, you depend on the goodwill of those who dwell in adjacent pockets of labor and care. You repay them by scavenging spare parts, clearing debris, or—more often—reclaiming neglected corners so that the damp gloom fosters growth rather than rot. You’re neither mechanical nor entirely natural, like most everything in this realm. Your veins may not be filled with oil or sap, but your heart thrums in time with the city’s wet, metallic pulse.

Sometimes, in a half-dream, you wonder if you’re turning into part of the city itself—if your fingernails are flecked with rust, if your eyes reflect the city’s faint green glow. Passing your reflection in a sheen of condensed water along a bulkhead, you’re startled by how easily you could be mistaken for a bit of moss grown over a pipe. The swirl of your clothes merges with the background’s battered steel, your hair (tied back with copper wire) looks half-vine. You draw closer to your reflection, uncertain whether this is a good or dangerous sign. Then the city murmurs around you—a hiss from a vent or a gentle rattle of cables—and you feel caught in a moment of quiet reassurance: you belong here, it seems to say, because you help make me whole.

And so your days fall into a steady rhythm, broken only by storms or unexpected breaks in the piping you must fix. You start each morning by checking the moisture in your current green projects: ferns in repurposed drums, small shrubs in wall-mounted planters, or a handful of vines climbing an old ladder you bolted to a corridor. You can’t help but grin when you see new leaves unfurling. And in the evenings, if the weather’s calm, you climb to a vantage point to watch the city’s scattered lights twinkle beneath the low-hanging clouds. It’s there you fully sense the settlement as a single, breathing thing—machinery exhaling steam, water trickling in every crevice, cables humming in the hush.

Sometimes a Drifter joins you, sharing stewed mushrooms in a battered tin cup. The two of you gaze out on the labyrinth of walkways, glowing windows, scaffolding, and swirling mists. They’ll ask if you ever miss the outside world. You might pause, remembering uncertain fields or distant towns, but the glimmer of oil-slick steel, the subtle perfume of wet rust, the creeping tenacity of vines… that’s where your heart lies now. You are part caretaker, part scavenger, part improvised gardener. The word Gutterseed still echoes in your mind. It fits like a seed lodging in a crack of metal, waiting for the next drizzle of water to let it grow.

Eventually, the Drifter leaves, continuing on their indefinite path. You remain, feeling the city’s quiet hush settle around you, the drizzle forming bright droplets at the tips of your hair. Far below, you hear the faint clang of Tinker-Colonists finishing a day’s work, the low murmur of Rustkeepers patrolling a newly installed beam. High overhead, the Riggers are calling out storm-touch, urging caution for tomorrow’s likely winds. And through the metal bones of this grand, half-living structure, you sense other pulses: the Automaton-Brood stirring, the Gutter Gardeners checking channels, the Vapotouched drifting through vents of steam. All of it weaves together into a tapestry of motion.

Yes, you think, this is where you find yourself in this world: balanced in a city that’s both fortress and garden, caretaker and occupant, forging new growth in the watery gloom. You are the one who sees potential in every leak, every busted pipe, every fractal swirl of moss. The hush that settles is never silent, but it’s enough to remind you: you’re part of something alive, something that will reshape itself countless times beyond your own lifetime. And you, Gutterseed or gleaner or caretaker—whatever your name becomes—are here, breathing in its steam-laden air, planting sprigs in the cracks, and listening to the metal’s slow, unrelenting heartbeat.
