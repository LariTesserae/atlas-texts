I might first notice a subtle pulse, an ancient breathing beneath the rock, and then realize it is my own pulse twisting in time with this lofty realm. It’s hard to tell where my senses end and the plateau begins, for I have lived here so long—if “lived” is even the right word. I do not recall a beginning. Perhaps I emerged from one of the fissures in the stone, or was carried by a current of faint cloud.

In shape, I am not unlike those faintly amphibian beings, yet my silhouette is far more fluid. I shift with the changing light, skin flickering from translucid pale at noon to softly dappled near dusk. Along my limbs and torso, faint patterns glisten—neither quite scales nor skin, but a shifting mosaic that catches the dampness in fractal beads at dawn. It is this mosaic that best reveals me, for if I grew too still, I would vanish entirely against the plateau’s etched surface. Indeed, a casual observer might confuse me with a piece of smooth, striated rock—just another formation shaped by wind and old rains.

In the earliest hours, before the sun’s warmth seeps into the plateau stone, I glide along the cracks, collecting droplets that glisten like tiny stars. My limbs extend gently, each fingerlike protrusion adept at scooping or brushing condensation into small, hollow compartments on the underside of my belly—reservoirs that can hold a surprising quantity of water, akin to some cunning biological canteen. From there, I dispense the liquid when I’m at rest, letting it slowly drip out, creating pockets of moisture for smaller creatures that gather beneath me. A congregation of them—slender lizards with ridged backs, elongated insects whose wings hum faintly—will crowd my shadow and lap at the droplets. Sometimes they climb onto my mosaic hide, so light in weight I only sense them by their gentle prickling steps.

I’ve grown used to this caretaker role, though I cannot fully say whether it is born of altruism or simply instinct. The plateau’s hush shaped me as surely as it shaped everything else. Each day, I sense a faint stirring within the stone. Some nights, my mosaic pulses in unison with hidden echoes reverberating among the fissures. I do not speak in calls or grunts—my voice, if I can name it, is a subtle, throb-like hum that resonates through my chest plates. When the lonely hush is thick enough, certain creatures perk their ears at this vibration. Perhaps they recognize it as a sign that water is near, or that I have paused in some sheltered hollow large enough for them to join me. Either way, we dwell in a shared quiet, an intimacy so deep no audible cry is needed.

My eyes are faceted, reminiscent of the insects’ shimmering mosaics, allowing me to see in curved arcs. I take in the plateau’s worn expanse from every direction at once: the slow, distant shuffle of the large amphibian colossuses; the stilt-legged grazers creaking over the ridges, their frilled snouts fluttering in the soft wind; the gliders launching themselves with silver-skin wings that ripple in the sun like drifting banners. When raindrops begin to spatter, I watch them all converge near rock-bowls that brim with water. The hush remains, interrupted only by the soft scuffling of many feet. Even the amphibious giants are subdued as they lower themselves with surprising grace to drink. Occasionally, my mosaic chest hums, a small, throbbing lullaby that seems to calm the younger beasts—furless, scaling-limb hatchlings clinging to their mothers’ sides.

While I do not remember a birth or a childhood, I carry an instinctual memory of the lost dwellings that stand half-wild in the margins—those piled stones and petrified wood frameworks. Sometimes I pass by them as I roam, pausing at the threshold of a half-collapsed doorway, touching the edges as if to greet an old friend. My mosaic patterns brighten slightly then, as though reading faint echoes left in the stone—traces of a more direct companionship these huts might once have harbored. Some nights, I sense them in my sleep, as if these ancient silhouettes of shelter still cast intangible shapes in the hush.

I am no builder of walls, no craftsman of tools. My entire existence is transitory: an ever-wandering presence that collects moisture, disperses it, quietly weaving through the plateau’s subtle densities of life. From time to time, I’ll rest in the shade of a low-slung shrub, feeling its leathery leaves brush my mosaic hide. The leaves quiver lightly with the memory of wind, and I imagine we share a conversation carried by the plateau’s hush—the words unspoken, just the play of shifting color across my body and the rustle of leaves in the breeze.

Yet, for all my calm observations, there dwells within me a profound, mysterious longing—a pull toward something beyond the plateau, or perhaps buried within it. When the sky gathers heavy clouds that ring the plateau’s edges, I sometimes wonder if I should venture down the steep inclines and into the unknown gloom. But I remain, enthralled by the hush that has always guided me. This hush is gentler than any siren call, and day by day, year by year, I watch the hairline fractures on the plateau widen—or close. See how water carves new pockets to house the next generation of life.

It feels as though I bridge the gulf between the plateau’s hidden watery veins and the sunlit dryness of its surface. Whether I was born for this, or simply took it on when I discovered I could, I don’t know. But I do know the creatures—and the plateau’s ancient spirit—quietly rely on my presence: the caretaker who eases the unspoken thirst. In that reliance, I find my belonging, more real than any structure or tool. Sometimes, I slip into a deep stillness near dusk, flattening my body against the warm stone, letting the last rays of daylight soak into me. In that moment, I feel the entire plateau hum in my mosaic patterns, so I know we are bound to one another.

When the tall amphibian pillars trundle past, their glowing eyes meeting mine, there is recognition. When the stilt-grazers approach a shallow puddle I have enriched by dripping stored dew, they kneel, spindly legs folding, to lap quietly. When the insect swarms gather against my flanks for a fleeting second, sipping the condensation that seeps from beneath my mosaic plates, they fill the air with faint humming. Then, as if satisfied, they vanish into the cracks once more. I have witnessed births and passings in these unhurried rhythms, footprints layered over footprints, eons of dust slowly reshaping the plateau’s surface. It never loses its hush.

And that is where I remain. A living keeper of dew, shaped by time’s infinite patience. Neither wholly reptile nor amphibian, neither mammal nor insect, but possibly kin to all—my mosaic body a tapestry of the plateau’s own design. Sometimes I marvel at myself, how I am only as real as a flicker of color, or a breath brushing the stone. In the hush, I feel at peace. I need no tools, no illusions of conquest. The plateau and I watch each other with the calm acceptance of two old friends—even family, in a sense—who have seen countless dawns and expect to see countless more. So I dwell here, quietly, content in each patient transformation: caretaker and companion to the hidden watery heart of this high, ancient stone.
