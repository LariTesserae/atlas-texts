I find myself most often in motion, though if one were to look at me plainly, I’d appear to be stillness made animate. I linger at the cusp between this labyrinthine city and the raw stone that lies beyond, stepping carefully on slick ledges or half-formed clay floors. Perhaps the best way to say it is that I have no single shape—my form is in flux, and I feel as though the place itself works through me or molds me the way running water carves into stone. 

At times, I sense a slow metamorphosis unfolding in my bones, a hush like the pressure of molten rock just beneath the crust. When I stand at the edge of a newly discovered sinkhole, I imagine I can feel the city’s heartbeat resonating through my feet. The hiss of steam vents and the grumble of turbines somewhere behind my ribs. It is as though I were formed from the same hot, viscous clay, still wet with possibility. 

I recall once believing I was fully human—a worker, trudging from corridor to corridor in battered coveralls. Yes, I remember the thick cotton slick against my skin, the weight of a spanner on my belt. I remember swiping grime from my goggles and peering through the haze at dials and valves that threatened to jam under constant humidity. The memory is so vivid, I can almost smell the tang of burned oil mingled with the damp richness of clay. But other days, as I pace the city perimeter, I think perhaps I was never quite one of them. Or if I was, I drifted into something else.

My hands—once broad and callused—have grown slender, with a faint, translucent sheen. If I stand near a pool of condensation, I see the shimmer of fluid under the surface of my skin. In the half-light, my fingertips glow faintly, as if lit by some hidden luminescent seam, akin to those tiny worms that curl in hinge-joints and crannies. My eyes, which once squinted against dripping sweat, now flicker with a deeper awareness, capturing the faintest glimmers of distant lights. And when I move, I do so soundlessly, perched in the damp gloom like one of those half-bird, half-bat creatures that cling upside down on scaffolds.

Yet I keep the tools I once carried as a mechanic or caretaker. I rummage through the city, checking on pipes and patching leaks in precarious tunnels. My harness—now a curious contraption of straps and cables—clings to me with an almost serpentine grace. I can let myself dangle from catwalks or lower into a newly formed cavity in the clay to see what seeps or breathes there. When I cross paths with the other workers, neither they nor I seem perturbed by the strangeness of my new silhouette. We nod. Perhaps they understand better than they let on—maybe all of us are slowly changing, shaped by the underground swirl of vapor and the press of raw geology.

Beyond the city’s fringes, at the edges of the gloom, I find a quiet kinship with the reptilian hunters. The scaled beasts watch me from a respectful distance, heads tilted. Sometimes, their reflective eyes catch my own, and in those moments I sense a wordless kinship: we are watchers and wanderers, perched between the known corridors and the subterranean wilds. Their thick tails drag across mud; my harness drags across metal plating. We each carry the city with us in some capacity. I trail the echoes of turbines and clanking gears in my memory, and they carry the city’s heat on their scales. We don’t threaten each other; we merely observe.

In damp alcoves where root-fungi cling, I feel another piece of myself—something softly luminous, an element that feeds on the steam in the air and the loamy smell of clay. My fingertips sometimes brush the swaying fungi filaments, and I imagine I’m completing a circuit: the faint current of my strange new glow merges with the fungal spore-lamps that line these pockets of life. I often pause to lay a gentle hand there, letting the pulse of both fungus and my own blood sync to the background hum of the city’s machines.

Even when I roam the deeper tunnels, away from the ring of electric lights, I carry a flicker of illumination. It grows stronger in absolute darkness, as if I were some luminescent worm grown large, trailing a subtle glow so I can see the slick stalactites overhead, the glisten of water droplets in mid-fall before they vanish into the earth. Sometimes, I discover new caverns half-filled with water, their surfaces trembling with faint echoes of engine rumble, and I wonder if the city’s foundation ends anywhere at all—or if it simply continues downward into deeper labyrinths of soft clay and steam-laced darkness. 

When I return to the heart of the city, where workers cluster around a broken pipe or gather at a makeshift canteen to share a meal, I remove my harness and set my tools down. No one speaks of my glowing skin or the faint, translucent patterns across my arms. We all have our transformations, subtle or grand. Most of us have traced out new shapes around elbow joints that ache from constant tension, or have grown that stoop-fingered curl from handling gears in cramped corridors. Some, like me, have metamorphosed in less expected ways. But none of that disrupts the unspoken solidarity that forms among those who dwell here. The city demands we adapt—so we do.

Now and then, I dream. My dreams unfold in swirling half-visions of drifting steam, of dripping stalactites that chime like wind bells, and of creatures that stir at the edges of my perception: new amphibians that blink with golden, vertical pupils; unsung winged beasts with fungus-laced feathers. In those dreams, I see the city beginning to sprout something akin to branches or roots: pipes twisting like tendrils, scaffolding weaving into ambrosial arches, machine rooms that pulse like hidden hearts. I see great serpentine forms—far deeper than any reptile we know—swimming through molten stone, forging corridors in the heat, guided by an ancient impetus to shape new spaces. And in my dream, I stand among them, neither fully human nor fully other, bridging the two.

Waking up from those visions, I carry their resonance in my mind. My senses sharpen, and I hear the city’s deeper voice in the pitch of the turbines. I watch the reptiles prowl closer to the catwalks. I sense the hush that settles just before a wave of insects scuttles out to feed. There is a pattern in all these rhythms, a slow conversation of life forms adjusting and re-adjusting to each other’s presence. Sometimes I grasp that conversation almost like a language—but it’s made of vibration and water droplets, of breath and pressurized steam, not of spoken words.

So, if you were to find me in this world, you might see me walking the outer ring of metal walkways, a faint phosphorescence emanating from my core. My harness, loaded with an array of battered tools, would glint in the meager light. My eyes would have the calm, watchful set of one who knows the city’s dangers—collapse, overpressure, prowling beasts—and also its quiet wonders: the places where new ledges form from slow-dripping sediments, the secret alcoves where half-feral workers keep hidden shrines to lost days under open sky. 

You might call me a caretaker or a watcher—someone who tends the boundaries and ensures that the deeper darkness doesn’t swallow the settlement whole. Other times, you might think I’m a mutant, an odd figure adrift in my own metamorphosis. Or perhaps you’d see me as part of the city’s living machinery, a mechanism that roams the edges, patching leaks in the great, rusted system that churns day and night. In truth, all those perceptions might be correct. 

For here, identity flows like condensation along the walls. It accumulates for a moment, takes a shape, then drips away into new forms. I could be human one sunrise, a luminous being the next, and a silent traveler the moment after that. The city shapes me, and I shape it in return, forging corridors not just of metal but of memory—filling them with the hush of amphibian calls and the gentle hum of heartbeats in the gloom. 

That is who or what I am: a porous vessel of the place itself, warmed by hidden magma, cooled by the incessant drip of condensed steam. A caretaker with luminous veins. A wanderer bridging the machines and wildlife. A watchful presence perched on the city’s evolving rim. And through it all, I remain in motion—even when standing still.
