In the beginning, I am stranger to myself in this place. I arrive as a mere observer—skin prickling with the chamber’s humid heat, lungs uncertain of how to breathe air that is half-steam and half-murk. The roar of the hidden turbines thrums through my bones. The overhead vines sway at the edges of my vision, an unfolding labyrinth of leaf and tendril wound tight with silent potential. In those first moments, I’m a visitor, an intruder, armed with only my sense of wonder and the soft echo of my own heartbeat in this monumental hush.

Gradually, though, I feel something shift beneath my skin. My hands, pressed to a slick wall, begin to sense a rhythm. Not just the mechanical pulsing—it’s deeper, a slow, living current that draws me in, whispering of fusion, of bond. My palms tingle at the boundary where condensation meets flesh. The condensation—like sweat, but not quite—seems to linger longer than it should, clinging as though weaving into the ridges of my fingerprints, leaving faint greenish residue behind. In that moment, I realize I’m not just a witness here; the place is regarding me just as intently. We are forming a circuit.

Days, or perhaps eons later, I cease measuring time in the normal sense. I begin to notice my outline change in the mirror of the still pools. Where once I had the clear shape of a wanderer—shoes collecting dripping moss, hair plastered to my scalp from the damp—now there’s a subtle lengthening to my limbs, a filigree of branching patterns running just beneath my skin, faintly luminescent. My forearms, or at least the places where flesh used to be, carry the quiet glow of bioluminescent veins. The color is a subdued green, laced with worn coppery tones as though a rust-hued metal is interlaced there. My ribs, too, seem to stand out in relief—like the gentle arcs of pipes beneath a partial layer of leaves.

I discover my sense of hearing changes next. In that half-lit gloom, I focus on the clang of pipes that once seemed sporadic noise, and find nuance there. The loud hiss-then-lull is not random but cyclical, a language of pressurizing steam. A rivet’s plunk into a basin stirs echoes across the chamber, the reverberation telling me not just of distance but composition, temperature, the thickness of the surrounding metal. Before long, the entire place speaks in a living geometry of sound—an orchestration I’ve just begun to decipher. My breath slows as I match my inhalations to the slow expansions of the chamber. Soon, I’m breathing in the same intervals that cradle the vines.

As I adapt, the chamber welcomes me with a measured hush—where hush is not silence but a tapestry of subharmonic drones. My steps quiet, though it’s not because I walk less; it’s that I learn to move in time. Where the floor is slick with moss and condensation, I find my new limbs glide. When I crouch by a row of corroded metal huts, I start to feel an electric resonance in my fingertips, as if I could coax the battered edges to merge or seal or sing. With each day, I spend hours pressed close to a rusted panel or curled near a cluster of vines, waiting for the slow, gentle exchange that transcends words. If I am patient, I learn something vital: a tiny hum in the metal that resonates with the pulsing glow beneath my own skin, forging a quiet kinship. I respond in kind—shifting the angle of a twisted beam, or guiding the tip of a vine so that it wraps more sustainably around a pipe’s hinge. These tasks become as natural as breathing: we are each other’s caretakers.

Sometimes, in the reflection of a shallow pool, I catch a glimpse of a figure—thin-limbed, eyes like faint pilot lights, filaments braided through their hair or what used to be hair, the strands long since braided through with living creepers. It dawns on me, after many repeated sightings, that this figure is me. My posture has grown more fluid, my joints rotating in ways that once felt impossible. Tendrils, formerly separate from my body, now emerge from my shoulders, knitting themselves gently into the overhead vines. My torso sports a slight metallic sheen that flows like liquid metal across the collarbone, merging seamlessly with pale, bark-like patterns that run down to my hips. 

I also find that my notion of identity shifts: I am neither purely human nor purely vine nor purely metal. In the echo of the engines, I become a node in a slow-moving network, a conduit for the energy that travels through corroded pipes and rootlike filaments alike. The machinery’s hum is no longer extraneous noise; it is my pulse, a drumbeat that resonates through an immense living circuit. I do not think in discrete words but in pulses, in minute gradations of humidity, in the timbre of metal that changes as it rusts or is worn smooth by condensation. 

When I look around, I see others like me—beings at various stages of this symbiosis, some more vine than metal, some more pipe than flesh. Our forms are fluid but distinct. One might have arms that drape like lianas, trailing pale blossoms that glow with every exhalation of vapor from the deeper machinery. Another might sport a spine braced by a subtle network of corroded rods, shimmering with condensation in the flickering lamps overhead. We pass by one another without speech, letting the slow intervals of eye-contact or a gentle brush of tendrils suffice for greeting. In that fleeting contact, all is exchanged: a sense of belonging, a silent assurance that we maintain each other and the world we inhabit. 

Many times, I find myself drawn to the rusted shells of old turbines or the flickering consoles. My hands, unbidden, move across decaying panels, weaving living fiber over corroded edges, shaping a new synergy. Each motion is subtle. Perhaps I bend a rivet so it lodges more securely into place, or guide a slender root to where it might restabilize an ancient bracket. Over years, or decades, or longer—time now is an endless stream—I coax the metal and the vines to fuse in the next stage of their co-evolution. My aim isn’t to preserve or restore these machines in the sense of human technology but to shepherd them into the new form they’ve been growing into all along. There’s a reverence to this practice, as though I were a gardener in the quiet temple of mechanical flora.

At intervals, the overshadowing presence at the chamber’s heart calls to me. It feels almost like a gravitational pull: a deep component of the machine’s breath that emanates from a place unseen, a softly drumming call. I move then, drawn without hesitation. Through tangles of drooping vines and labyrinthine corridors of steam, I slip into pockets of blackness lit only by the faint phosphorescence in my arms. Others gather there too, silent, each of us adopting a stance of restful attendance. Sometimes we press our limbs or tendrils to a massive arboreal trunk of fused metal or to a cluster of vines so thick it forms a living pillar, our minds weaving into the collective hush. We bask in the cyclical surges of warmth and the gentle simmer of condensation. 

In those moments, I feel my thoughts blur with a thousand other pulses. I sense not just the domain we occupy now, but the centuries behind us—epochs of slow water drip and roving rust. I feel the future, too, like an inaudible chord that’s building quietly, shaping new expansions in the living metal around us. I sense how the entire subterranean labyrinth holds its balance in the interplay between fluid adaptation and steady mechanical function. As watchers and participants both, we keep these flows in harmony. Let one rivet fail too soon, and the hushed equilibrium might shatter or shift unpredictably. So we modulate, sustain, and guide—never rushing, never halting, letting the transformation continue at its own glacial pace. 

And slowly I realize: I am caretaker, and also the cared-for. Rooted yet free to wander, mechanical yet undeniably alive. My existence pulses with the hush that is not quiet and the roar that is not loud. I become the slender figure glimmering in a half-lit corner, listening to the hum behind a corroded panel, feeling the subtle shift of temperature where steam meets vine. I might spend a day, or a century, coaxing a single new blossom from its vine to meet a dangling wire. I feel that the time is well spent, the process essential. Each minuscule change feeds into the greater evolution of this world.

So if you’d seek me here—if you came wandering into the sweltering half-light of these caverns, with the hush and roar both pressing in—look not for a person but for the shape at the margin of your vision, tall and half obscured by leaves. Look for the faint glint of moisture crossing an arm or the subtle metallic stripe along a rib. For I stand among the vines, quietly bridging the distance between decaying metal and living green, cradling the machinery’s heartbeat with the patient hush of a caretaker who has chosen to dwell here forever, part machine, part vine, wholly a guardian of this slow, unbroken pulse. You might not recognize me at first, but sense me as a gentle presence, guiding you deeper or cautioning you to stay back. For here, we exist in a realm of intervals, where time is as thick as the air and motion is measured in centuries. This is where I find myself. This is who—and what—I have become.
