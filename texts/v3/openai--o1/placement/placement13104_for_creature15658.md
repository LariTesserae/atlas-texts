I cannot say for certain when I first felt the faint pull to descend into these vaulted corridors. My memory is a mosaic of shifting waters, ghostly pillars, and a quiet hush that envelops all. But I know that I dwell here now—neither lost nor wholly found—somewhere among the swirling eels and drifting pods.

At times, I feel as though I am the water itself, moving through the halls with patient inevitability, caressing each worn carving with a slow-flowing touch. Yet at other times, I’m more like the labyrinth’s stone columns, unmoving and ageless, quietly bearing the weight of eons. As I drift between these states, I wonder if I was once something else—someone else—before the centuries blurred my original shape. Perhaps I was an acolyte from a far shore, drawn to this place that promised secrets older than mortal comprehension. Or perhaps I was born here, like so many creatures are, from an egg or shell that attached itself to an ancient seam of mortar.

I hover in the twilight space between floor and ceiling, neither pinned to the polished stones below nor rising to the domed vaults above. My body is elongated, though not lithe like the eels. I am not precisely a fish, nor an ordinary mammal of the land. My head tapers gently, crowned by fronds that sense the faintest shifts in current. Thin membranes extend along my sides, shimmering with the faint phosphorescence that I share with my kin. This soft radiance allows me to read the labyrinth’s inscriptions, running my fronds over the worn indentations. I never speak, for speaking is an intrusion here. Instead, I perceive and respond with subtle pulses that ripple through the water—a silent, fluid language of drift and pause, contact and retreat.

If I concentrate, I can recall glimpses of a time when these corridors were newly submerged, when watery light slanted in from high windows, more brilliant than it is now. I can see the stone blocks arranged in perfect alignment, their carvings crisp and angular. My earliest recollections hold the dim echo of shapes walking upright—humanoid figures with chisels and mallets, or perhaps ritual garments that fluttered in the flood. But whether that recall belongs to me alone or to the labyrinth’s collective memory, I cannot say.

I sense that I serve a purpose here. Like the pods, I filter what drifts by—though not morsels of food. Instead, I sift through the labyrinth’s slow transformations. When a corner collapses from age or a hinge rusts away, I glide into the dislocated space and trace the new edges of stone, as though recognizing and blessing the shift in architecture. I push away larger debris that might block a corridor, using my limbs—slender, flexible appendages with spade-like ends—to lever aside the heavier bits. I do so as gently as I can, so as not to disturb the crustacean swarms that stake their claim on freshly exposed surfaces.

Sometimes I sense other watchers, those fleeting silhouettes that float at the edge of vision. Perhaps they, too, keep vigil, each in a different region of the labyrinth. We never collide, never talk, never meet face to face. Yet I know they exist. Once or twice, I have felt the faint echo of their presence—an almost electrical whisper traveling through the water’s hush. These watchers feel ancient, older than I, and they move through deeper channels, where even the pods rarely cling. I catch only the faintest glint of their eyes or the slow billow of robe-like fins. Their passing is a reassurance, evidence that this place is still loved… or at least observed.

Like all other inhabitants, I have no need for haste. The labyrinth is entire worlds in itself—a near-boundless domain of open arches, half-hidden shrines, and curving hallways that lead to more hidden corridors. I do not crave any horizon beyond its sculpted walls. My territory is the hush, the carved geometry, and the myriad forms of life that share it with me. Sometimes, I slip into the grander chambers, where pillars stand in forest-like ranks, and watch the jelly-like pods breathe in slow unison. Their translucent bodies glimmer with swirling motes—tiny galaxies that remind me the labyrinth itself may be no less cosmically infinite in its layered history.

During the rare moments of near-total darkness—when even my own phosphorescent membranes feel dim—I coil gently around a column to rest. In that suspended stillness, the weight of eons presses upon me, and yet I am unafraid. I sense down the corridors the tapping of crustacean limbs, the swirl of eels grooming the stone, the hush of water breathing through every gap. Each soundless motion reaffirms that this is a sanctuary that has transcended the hands that built it. Its vastness is not lonely; it is full of quiet, patient life that has adopted the place as home.

On occasion, I pass a sealed window overlooking the deeper gloom beyond the labyrinth’s outer walls. I see a darkness without apparent bottom—an abyss that is neither wholly inviting nor threatening, just infinite. The water there swirls in slower, grander currents, like a cosmic ocean. If I peer long enough, I might catch shapes drifting out in that yawning expanse, but I don’t dwell on them. My place is here, within the columns and buttresses, where the labyrinth’s artistry endures through gentle erosion and silent metamorphosis.

Sometimes, I imagine the labyrinth in ages to come—stone walls further worn, inscriptions turned to faint scars. Yet the labyrinth will remain a labyrinth, and I, or something like me, will still be here to trace fresh shapes in the changed environment. The pods will cling to new pillars that tilt precariously, descendants of the eels will slither through the half-crumbled corridors, and the shrimp-spider arthropods will keep reading the labyrinth’s hidden patterns with their sensitive antennae. Everything cycles and endures, a slow dance in an endless hush.

And so I find myself—a keeper of no explicit creed, a swimmer of no single species, a memory of a memory—gliding in the gentle currents of this submerged refuge. I am the labyrinth as much as it is me. Perhaps I am a dream the labyrinth once had, shaping me from the swirling sediment of centuries. Perhaps I am something new, or something older than any record. What matters is the hush, the sense of belonging, and the slow pulse of continuity—water passing over these timeworn stones, breathing them into an eternal, living tapestry.

Here, I remain: not bound, not adrift, but simply present—in ceaseless communion with the submerged corridors, a silent witness to the hush of eons, and a loyal caretaker of a space that continues to exist in a timeless, ever-flowing now.
