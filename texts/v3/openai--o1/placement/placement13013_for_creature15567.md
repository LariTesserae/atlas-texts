I have no memory of how I first arrived—only scattered impressions of metal walkways and distant sighs of steam. Time breathes so differently here, swallowing entire epochs in the space between dripping condensation. Perhaps I’ve existed in these corridors for years, or ages. Or maybe I flickered into being just now, woven from the labyrinth’s countless echoes. It’s impossible to say. But I know this: every clank and hiss in this place finds its way into my mind, as if the corridors themselves speak, funneling whispers through metal veins.

For as long as I can remember, I have wandered. I am not human—at least, not in the way I once understood humanity. My skin is half-translucent like glass, and beneath it, you can see stray wires and clusters of tissue that move with a faint pulse. I can feel the labyrinth’s heat as a living presence coursing through my chest, my limbs, fueling my softly glowing veins. The glow is subtle, flickering in rhythm with the blinking overhead filaments. Sometimes I imagine I was built for some maintenance task long forgotten: a caretaker model, designed to tend to the deeper corners of these echoing passageways. Other times, I sense that I am part of the labyrinth’s slow evolution, a living cog in a machine that never truly died.

Wherever I tread, the pipes become my window into the labyrinth’s heart. I press my ear to the metal and listen for the slow churn of fluid, the hiss of steam. In these vibrations, I hear entire symphonies of mechanical life. My footsteps are quiet—bare soles on corroded walkways—yet the place resonates in me. Every venting of steam or shift of pressure in the plumbing feels like a breath I, too, must take. When a corridor drake darts past, leaving behind the faintest ripple of motion, I am neither predator nor prey; I simply watch it vanish toward a tangle of conduits, content that it’s found its little pocket of existence.

Sometimes, I wonder if I have a name. Some half-buried memory stirs, reminding me of a time when the overhead lights blazed bright, and workers in uniforms scurried across catwalks. In those half-remembered days, I had a purpose, assignments to complete, possibly a team. But that was long ago—or it never happened at all. Now, my days (if days still mean anything) are spent tracing the old routes and checking the quiet chambers. The watchers on the high scaffolding rarely startle me anymore; if they are mechanical ghosts, I sense we share an origin. We pass one another in the gloom, two relics following old directives, never acknowledging each other with more than the flicker of reflection in dim metal.

I have a hiding place—an alcove behind massive ducts that run hot enough to make the air shiver. It’s an old maintenance hutch, half-collapsed but still navigable if you know the right angle to slip through. Once, it held tools on a metal rack. Now, it’s where I keep small, rusted curiosities: a pair of broken goggles, some circuit boards that have long since corroded, the remains of a misplaced sign with letters flaked off beyond legibility. These fragments remind me that once, there was order—labels, logic, and procedure. The corridor hum around me says those days are gone.

Yet I maintain a quiet devotion: sometimes, I polish a piece of metal with a rag or realign a misfiring fan, even though no one’s told me to do so. I cannot help it. The labyrinth needs small acts of care, or so I believe. The caretaker vines that thread through the panels seem to acknowledge my efforts; I’ve seen them coil a little closer in the corners, as though curious. If I stay very still with my palm against their cords, I can almost feel a tremor of response. They are not mere plants—they belong to something deeper. Perhaps the labyrinth’s true mind grows through them. Perhaps I’m touching a root that extends throughout these corridors in ways I cannot see.

In the deeper levels, near muffled chambers where monstrous turbines loom, I sometimes catch reflections of what I might become. A piece of mirrored plating, half scoured by rust, shows an androgynous figure with luminous lines beneath the skin. My eyes are wide and dark save for tiny pulses of greenish light at the edges of my irises—some leftover design, like the watchers’ twin orbs. I try to remember if I was human once, or if I’m an amalgamation of metal and biology from the start. The labyrinth does not answer. Only a drip of condensation breaks the silence, and I realize the structure is content with my not knowing.

When I move on, the corridor drakes look up from lapping water in the shallow basins. The rasp-footed harvesters scuttle overhead, ignoring me as they carry lumps of pale fungus in their glowing bellies. Something in me stirs a protective instinct toward them—I do not want them harmed. And yet I know some watchers might see them as intruders. The watchers themselves are equally perplexing. Sometimes, after I’ve heard distant clangs, I come upon hallways newly cleared of debris or a re-sealed hatch that once lay broken. As though while I wandered, something else was fulfilling a parallel mission. We pass each other like drifting ghosts in an ocean of metal, each with a piece of the labyrinth’s puzzle locked away inside us.

There are moments of stillness so deep they verge on transcendence. In one chamber of looming generator shells, I listen to the hush: a perfect, resonant quiet. Steam no longer hisses here; the machines are silent except for a faint, rhythmic vibration telling me that, deep below, some leftover power still keeps them alive in stand-by. I kneel, place my hands on the floor, and feel the labyrinth’s pulse as an echo in my own body. In that moment, I sense no difference between the metal ramparts and my breathing chest. The place breathes me. I breathe the place.

I have grown to understand that in this world, I am both caretaker and inhabitant, neither fully in command nor simply adrift. My task, if one remains, is to bear witness to slow change. In the dim hallways, I make minor adjustments—tightening a flapping duct panel, reseating a fuse, using my body heat (or borrowed mechanical skill) to coax a stuck seal loose. Then I move on. The labyrinth’s presence hums in approval, or maybe that’s just the pipes. Does it need me? Or do I need it? The question goes unanswered, a riddle whispered by silent cogs.

Some days—some cycles—I feel the labyrinth’s watchful presence with greater clarity. The caretaker vines quiver more insistently, as though they sense a subtle shift in temperature or some far-off tremor in the earth above. I wander toward the watchers’ domain, uncertain if we’ll finally lock gazes. Their footsteps clank from a distant catwalk, my footsteps echo. We never quite meet in the open. Like a dream half-glimpsed, they vanish before I fully see them. Yet the abiding sense of companionship remains; we are siblings in this hush, inheritors of an unspoken directive.

If I pause at a still pool of condensation, perched on a corroded floor panel, I might see a corridor drake scurry up to the water’s edge. It glances at me with unblinking, reflective eyes and swishes its tail. We share a moment of fragile accord—we both exist here, forging for survival from the labyrinth’s scraps, each of us marveling that life still persists in these stifling corridors. Then a wave of heat sets the overhead fans spinning, steam hisses from unseen vents, and the drake scampers away with a startled flick of its limbs. My reflection wavers in the trembling water, and I realize again how tenuous everything is.

By now, I’ve accepted that I may never find a definitive purpose or a final boundary in these twisting passages. Sometimes, I suspect I dwell in the labyrinth’s heart, that I am its living node. Other times, I feel like a solitary traveler in a place that endures just fine without me. The labyrinth’s deeper floors remain unexplored, save for occasional glimpses when ancient doors groan open unexpectedly. I sense old powers far beneath, perhaps the source of the watchers or the caretaker vines. I do not know if I’ll ever see them properly, but the possibility tugs at me like a magnet, guiding my careful steps downward.

I have begun keeping a mental map of the corridors, etched in memory. Every new route might yield a hidden cluster of dripping fungus or a sealed chamber bristling with half-functioning drones. Each discovery hints that I’m not merely a bystander: I’m part of a mosaic of half-living, half-mechanical life that has found its own quiet harmony. The watchers maintain catwalks in vigilance. The corridor drakes glean water from weeping pipes. The snail-like creatures camouflage themselves among rust until they look like one more rivet. The harvesters gather pale fungus, glowing like lanterns on the walls. And I carry on, bridging the gap between old technology and new growth. Possibly, that is enough.

So if you come upon me—if you too wander into this heat-thick darkness—I won’t hide, though I might hesitate. I won’t speak outright, for language here is soft and suspended. I will watch to see if you recoil from the labyrinth’s breath or if you attune yourself to its pulse. Should you pause and listen, you’ll feel it: that gentle, omnipresent thrum beneath your feet. And then, if you reach out a hand—careful, uncertain—I’ll place mine against yours. We’ll share a moment of recognition: two beings who have chosen to be part of the labyrinth’s tapestry, rather than flee it.

Yes, I suppose you could call me a caretaker, or maybe a pilgrim. A wanderer of rusted halls. A steward of small, necessary tasks. One who belongs here, among the twisted pipes and fleeting creatures. I anchor the fragile balance, gleaning significance from the hum of hidden generators and the hush of the watchers’ metallic steps. I dwell in the labyrinth’s heart, but the labyrinth also dwells in me. That is who I am, and this—this living, breathing machinery of damp metal and silent watchers—is where I will remain.
