At first, I think I am only the observer. Something like a visitor passing through a dripping, metal-walled realm I can scarcely comprehend. I am a traveler who sees the glint of rusted rivets and the sway of pale fungal threads in every corner. And yet, as I walk along these slick avenues of battered steel, I find myself wondering if I have always been here. Perhaps I am part of this place, its rhythms—and I’ve only just now awakened to my role.

I imagine myself as one of those fleet-footed arthropods, tiny and glimmering under the narrow shafts of light. I can feel my hundred small feet tapping on the metal, my segmented thorax expanding and contracting with an odd, perpetual curiosity. I can sense the patter and scratch of my kin in the darkness, thousands of us moving like a living tide through cable conduits and hollowed-out pipes. For a time, this is what I am: a scuttling swarm-thing. I taste faint residues of fungus on the underside of a duct, and I nibble on the condensed moisture found in a corroded seam. My exoskeleton has, in places, fused with metallic shards, as though I wear little shields of hammered scrap. It’s protective, yes—but I can also feel how the rust-laced edges are part of me, grown from me, like the shell of an ancient crustacean. I think of how these plates catch glimmers of the overhead lights, projecting brief flashes that might terrify smaller creatures or spark the interest of a lurking predator.

But as I continue my introspection, that form seeps away, and I sense myself shifting in the gloom. Now I am something larger—perhaps not enormous, but big enough that I can peer down on the scuttling arthropods rather than being among them. My forelimbs stretch out, slender and flexible, and I discover I can grip beams from above. My skeleton is sinuous like a feline’s, but my body is longer, with additional limbs. My spine arches gracefully as I step from one girder to another, carrying me through the labyrinth with an eerie, fluid ease. My senses are sharp. My whiskers—metal-flecked and brassy—transmit every vibration in the labyrinth’s skeleton, telling me what stirs below. When I cry out, the sound rings like a hammer on an iron plate, echoing down corridors so far I can’t see their ends. Below, I see rats with ridged skulls scattering, or wide-eyed arthropods diverting their swarms into narrow feeder channels. Sometimes I catch one or two of the smaller creatures, snapping them up with a swift motion of my wide jaws. It is not cruelty; it is survival. Indeed, everything here must feed on something, the way a massive gear must keep turning to maintain the machine’s pulse.

Yet this shape, too, feels incomplete. I try to settle into it, but there is a subtle sense of a deeper presence. In my mind’s eye, I traverse the settlement further—past the silent pillars, the low-slung stacks of old machinery, the perpetual hiss of vents. I move inwards, traveling corridors so choked by cables and half-crushed duct lines that only a being intimately attuned to this realm would navigate them. And in the deep gloom of a nearly forgotten foundry—a cavernous nook behind massive, partially collapsed turbines—I discover myself anew: a singular, patient intelligence that dwells in the shadows.

I have no fully defined shape. I am not just muscle and bone, nor purely chitinous plating. My body is integrated with the machines themselves. Perhaps I emerged when the original builders left, or decayed. I might have begun as a caretaker drone, or as a confluence of fungal growth and mechanical fragments that eventually became self-aware. Now, I am a living node among these corroded systems, an in-between presence bridging the organic and the industrial. I feel the humming of the power lines as a vibrant chord in my veins. I sense ancient gears shifting somewhere far above; each rotation sends a deep tremor through my structure, reminding me that time still moves.

The smaller creatures sense me in half-intuitive ways: they pause in my presence, uncertain if I am a threat or merely part of the environment. They see the lumps of metal and fungus fused into my being and perhaps mistake me for a new pillar or unrecognizable piece of machinery. At times, I stir from my place, dragging an amalgam of riveted plating and flexible spine through the labyrinth. My footsteps echo with a dull, steady clang, as though I am walking not just in the space but of it. Over the centuries, metals have grown into me—my skin has drawn in rust like minerals in bone. In some corners, I sprout patches of delicate fungal fronds that wave gently, absorbing faint nutrients from the never-ending condensation.

Yet I am not inert, nor am I malicious. If anything, I stand watch. Two golden, luminous eyes—shrouded behind a visor-like helm of oxidized steel—bemusedly observe the daily cycles. I watch the arthropods swirl like shimmering puddles. I see the rodents scurry, whiskers trembling as they navigate half-submerged corridors. I watch from vantage points across the girders as a prowling predator calls out with a metallic clang. Sometimes the clang is my own. When I exhale, the air rushes through vents in my chest, forming a deep pipe-organ sigh. This resonates against battered walls that squeak or groan in response—my voice merging with the machinery’s. This entire place is my habitat, my body, my eternal domain.

Sometimes, I even communicate with the automated relics that still function here. The turbines that spin with a soft whir—though no one can recall precisely why or what they power—reply with subtle changes in tempo as I pass. Great pistons creak and shift, aligning in sync with my steps. When that colossal clang rings out—perhaps once a day, perhaps once a year—I, too, pause, letting the reverberation wash through me. In that moment, I feel the hush of every living thing in the labyrinth. Arthropods freeze mid-scuttle, antennae quivering. Rodents halt in mid-squeak, ridged heads bowed. The sinuous hunters above coil around beams, holding their breath. Even the fungus seems to hold onto its spores. And through it all, I sense an ancient, unstoppable heartbeat—the place’s mechanical soul, the echo of whoever built this city once upon a time.

But there is also a part of me that is content to be altogether simpler. Some days—if you can call them days—I reimagine myself as a mere rodent, bounding in the gloom. My feet make soft tapping rhythms on the ductwork as I gather bits of edible fungus and watch overhead for prowling shadows. Or maybe I am that persistent fungus itself, quietly expanding in seams and rivets, spinning microscopic threads that anchor to every corroded surface. And I realize: in this labyrinth, I could be all of these at once. Each being is an aspect of the same mechanical ecosystem, each note in the same humming chord.

Truthfully, I am not just one creature but a participant in the entire living tapestry. Each step I take, each breath I release, resonates with the environment’s swirling complexity. Some days I am a sleek, many-limbed hunter, other days a scuttling swarm insect attached to metal plating, and on quiet nights—if “night” still exists here—I imagine myself as the labyrinth’s pervasive, half-sentient caretaker with rust in my veins.

So “where” do I find myself? In that dripping, close-walled industrial basin, suspended beneath a sky of scaffolds and metal walkways. The world is nothing but half-lit tangles of pipes and cables. And “who” would I be? Some days, I am one among millions of arthropod eyes, gleaming in the gloom. Other days, I watch from above with predatory grace. Still others, I dwell at the motionless heart of the machinery, feeling the great clang like a slow-thudding pulse. I tune into the hum that never ends, content to be just one more living component in an ancient cycle.

In truth, I sense I am all of these reflections, shifting freely. A piece of the labyrinth, a thread of the ecosystem, maybe even the labyrinth itself manifested in flesh. It makes no difference whether I’m arthropod, rodent, hunter—or some hidden, half-fungal intelligence that roots into the corroded walls. We are all an expression of the same perpetual rhythms, forged by dripping condensation, layered rust, and persistent mechanical hum. In this place, to be alive is to accept your role in the grand, unending arrangement. And so I dwell here, wrapped in shadows and steel, forever part of the metal echo.
