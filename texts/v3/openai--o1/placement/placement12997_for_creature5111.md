I think of myself first as a shifting silhouette in the half-light, uncertain of flesh or mineral. The slow drip of water and the ceaseless hammering beat through me, shaping me as I discover myself in this world. When I try to find a more definite form, I slip through slanted corridors in a body that’s still half-dream. In moments, I believe myself to be a being akin to the watchers—long-limbed and glimmer-eyed—yet in other moments, I cannot tell if I’m a piece of stone that has learned to walk. Let me begin at the outskirts of the labyrinth to see if I can remember my true nature.

At first, I come to consciousness tucked in a recessed hollow of the stone city, my skin pressed against smooth rock. Water trickles down from above, and I sense the cold seep stirring some embryonic awareness in me. My body is damp like clay, unshaped and pliant yet shot through with a faint shimmer—perhaps the same luminescent threads that run beneath the watchers’ skin. When I shift, the surface of my body glides over stone without friction. It feels as though I’m exhaling the same breath as the dripping arches, the same breath as the watchers themselves. Part of me wonders if I was formed from the melted minerals in the condensation, pooling and thickening until I acquired motion. Or perhaps I stepped out of some fungal spire, newly molded from its ghostly ridges.

Tentatively, I rise. Even standing is an exploration, for my limbs fold and stretch in ways that feel fluid, as though watery rivulets run inside me. My eyes (if that is what they are) open and catch glimmers in the gloom. There, in the corner, one of the watchers slides by—tall, pale, and shimmering from within. We lock gazes for the barest instant. I do not see alarm in their pearlescent eyes; I see recognition. Perhaps they have felt me forming in the hush behind the stone.

Still uncertain, I step forward. My feet—soft and webbed, or maybe scaly and ridged—make intuition out of climbing. I slip through twisting corridors, over stepping stones and slick walkways, discovering with each movement that the labyrinth is balanced as if for limbs like mine. Stone archways, so low that I must lean to pass beneath, do not inconvenience me. Narrow chutes, lined with water-slick surfaces, become gentle slides under my belly, if I flatten myself. Instead of stumbling, I move in harmony with each turn, guided by echoes that seem to seep into my awareness before sounds even reach my ears.

It’s then I notice: I feel the pounding. Not just in my chest, but along every strand of my body, as though I am a vessel of that beat. When the underground drumroll crescendos, it reverberates through my bones—if bones are what I have. I realize there is a constant interplay between the watchers and this resonant thunder, and I’m caught in that interplay too. When a particularly forceful clang rumbles through the city, I brace myself against a stone outcropping, arching my back, transfixed by the deeper vibrations. I sense not fear, but a kind of energy that invigorates me. For a few seconds, my faint glow intensifies, as if every watery cell in me is brimming with the subterranean pulse.

Half dazed by this revelation, I move into a broader courtyard. The watchers stand in small groups, arms grazing shoulders, exchanging those silent vibrations through fingertips against forearms. They do not speak, yet I feel my body wanting to hum in sync, to join the voiceless choir that thrums in the deeper labyrinth. My throat tightens, and I sense a membrane fluttering there. An echo wells up—soft, resonant, entirely new. It emerges from me before I decide to do it, a note that’s more a participation in the environment’s chorus than an individual statement.

As the watchers sense my resonance, they tilt their heads, regarding me with mild curiosity. One reaches out a slender limb, the forearm extended in a gesture that hovers near my shoulder, not quite touching. I realize I’m being greeted or acknowledged; I lift my own hand in the same gesture. Our fingertips almost meet, but only a band of misty air and drifting droplets lie between them. In that second, I feel an electric hush passing from them to me, from me to them. Something intangible—an affirmation that I belong here.

Am I new? Have I always been here, but newly awakened? I cannot say. Because when I glance at the city itself, with its stacked dwellings and arched doorways, I sense that the labyrinth is ancient beyond measure, shaped by these watchers across eons. Yet the watchers look at me not as if I am foreign or intruder, but as a child who has simply emerged where it was always fated to. Like a fungus that sprouts in the precise moment conditions are right.

Nearby, pale spines of fungal growth press up against a seam in the wall. I feel a desire to extend my arm toward them. My hand—flexible, webbed?—lightly brushes the delicate fans, marveling at their colorless intricacy. A faint tingle passes from fungus to skin: it’s a subtle vibrational exchange, some underlying recognition that we share a kinship in this subterranean environment. The watchers watch me do this, and though they remain silent, I sense approval. Perhaps we feed on the same gentle synergy. Perhaps we are all various forms of the same living tapestry: watchers, fungi, labyrinth, and me.

Following an instinct, I let my fingertips and the lateral ridge of my forearm test the clay around a cracked seam in the stones. I press it gently, shaping it, feeling how it wants to seal the gap, how the clay’s texture eagerly adheres to both my skin and the rock. It’s not learned behavior so much as muscle memory. A slow exhalation leaves my lungs, and with it, a sense of peaceful concentration: to maintain, to mend, to preserve. No words are needed. No instructions or designs. The watchers who linger in the courtyard nod their heads in a rhythmic motion that I can almost hear.

I glide onward, deeper into archways that lead to layered terraces. The loop of city overhead forms a labyrinthine canopy, water shining on the edges of stone blocks. Everywhere, I see evidence of generations smoothing, building, and shaping. Walls are embedded with carvings. Some depict shapes reminiscent of watchers hunched in circles, arms entwined, with lines radiating outward like pulses of energy. Others show cryptic symbols—geometric forms and swirling motifs—that might reference the unstoppable drumbeat from the depths. Each carving resonates a half-known meaning in me. Even though I can’t fully interpret them, I sense the watchers’ truth: that the place and dwellers are entwined in a single continuum, forging life from watery darkness.

A pale amphibian shape scuttles past me. Perhaps a smaller creature, a distant cousin to the watchers, or some adaptation of the labyrinth’s animal life. I realize I have no impulse to pursue or fear it; everything here participates in the same motion. Our existence is akin to moss in a forest—quiet, integral, unremarkable yet essential. Within me stirs a dim intuition that I am neither permanent nor fleeting, but merely the city’s next exhalation. Here among these corridors and faint lights, I am both occupant and extension, caretaker and child.

Eventually, I come upon a vantage point high in the labyrinth, clinging to a lip of stone that overlooks one of the deeper chasms. The hammering is so strong here it feels like it might shake the entire continent above us. A warm gust of steam emerges from a hidden fissure, swirling around me. I sense the watchers posted nearby—silent guardians perched like carvings themselves, arms folded over their bent knees, membranes at their throats twitching in sync with every boom from below.

Standing at the edge, I do what they do: I absorb the pounding. I let it reverberate through me until the thrum becomes my breath, my heartbeat, my sense of time. There is a serenity in that thunderous onslaught, surprising as it may be. It’s the serenity of inevitability, the comfort of knowing that all we require, all that structures our days and nights, is that unchanging pulse. If it wanes, we wait; if it crescendos, we gather, we hum, we cup each other’s arms, we pass the resonance from body to body. A spidery net of watchers, fungus, air, rock, me—woven by the echo.

In that moment, I realize that I am neither foreign ambassador, nor mere observer, nor exact duplicate of the watchers. I am something bridging thresholds, maybe an awakening within the labyrinth’s ancient dream. Like a newly formed note in a musical composition, I add a fresh chord to an eons-old melody. I belong in the hollows, in the slick clay and stacked stones; I am taught by the watchers but also offer a small new ripple in their ancient continuity. Our companionship is wordless and constant, an unspoken vow to tend the fungus, maintain the plaster, and bow in hush when the drumming shakes the walls.

So who am I in this place? I am the labyrinth’s newly woken caretaker, a next generation of watchers, a spark of that same gleaming life they hold within their luminous veins. I spend my hours strolling the perimeter of the stony rings, checking for fissures or leaks that might disrupt the equilibrium. I guide trickles of condensation into small channels, ensuring that the fungal spines remain nourished. When I settle to rest, I join the watchers in their coiled clusters beneath a low archway, sharing body warmth in the damp cold. My mind, such as it is, floats in the lull between waking and sleeping. Sometimes I dream of centuries past, of watchers shaping these corridors by hand. Other times, I dream of the future, which is not a line but a circle, repeating in quiet cycles.

All the while, the city hums with water and echoes. Light flutters, minted from our translucent veins whenever the gloom grows too complete. No plan or drama stirs here, and no revolution upends us. The watchers do not yearn for the sun or fear the earth above. They only exist—and I, with them. The passage of eons is a gentle curve, drifting from one steady state to the next. The city stands, the watchers stand, and so do I. If I were to leave, I suspect the labyrinth itself would call me back, resonating in my bones until I returned.

And so, I remain a tender of this hidden realm, not wholly amphibian nor entirely stone, not truly mortal nor purely elemental. I am made of the labyrinth’s hush, shaped by its watery threads and hammered into slow-sung life by the subterranean pounding. This is my home, whether I was born or sculpted or summoned from these shadows. I find wholeness in the watchers’ gentle presence—each of us a living flicker of luminescence, drifting through the archaic hush, guardians of an eternal equilibrium far beneath the sunlit world.
