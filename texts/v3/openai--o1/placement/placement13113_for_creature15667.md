I begin simply as an indistinct shape at the edge of this small domain—someone, or something, leaning against slick stone. At first, I barely notice my own presence: the soft hush of dripping water envelops me, and I dissolve into it. Every breath weighs on my lungs like a hundred silent exhalations merged. The air is hot and sluggish, yet I feel an unexpected comfort, as if I’ve always been in this condensation-soaked twilight.

Perhaps I am a visitor, crouched beside a stunted mineral spike that gnaws at the corner of a shallow pool. Perhaps I am an inhabitant who no longer recalls any other home. In this world, the line between memory and current moment is as blurred as the watery reflections that cling to the walls. I find myself noticing the smallest details, as though they’re essential to my survival—the way a droplet forms on the tip of a stalactite, swells, and finally drips with a near-silent echo that resonates in my chest. I watch a ripple expand across the surface of a puddle until it taps gently against my ankles. And at that sensation, I wonder:

Am I wearing boots? Are my feet bare? When I run a hand over my arm, do I feel the ridges of a thin exoskeleton or the smooth pliancy of amphibian flesh?

I move a little, pebble by pebble, stepping into a place shaded by a broken arch. The archway wavers in the gloom, so ancient it seems part of the rock itself, and yet, I sense the intention behind its design—someone long ago stacked those stones, shaped them into an opening to pass through. I press my palms against the curved surface, feeling the softened edges and the slurry of sediment that collects in the cracks. My hands leave a faint impression when I draw them away. 

In those traces, I glimpse a part of myself that might have existed here for centuries: a shape adapted to water-worn gloom, a creature with wide palm pads or leathery fingertips designed to grip slick surfaces. The next moment, I see something more akin to a fragile human print, as though I’m just a wanderer who’s come to drink from long-forgotten pools. The image flits back and forth, amphibian or traveler, inhabitant or intruder. In the half-dark, identity is fluid.

I sense a presence gliding behind me, and I shift my posture, trying to see. Whatever it is moves with a near-soundless grace—that distinctive hush of limbs sliding through thick air. A single droplet forms on the tip of a mineral spire, drops into a stone vessel on the ground, making a hollow note. Did I put that vessel there? Or did it remain from those who once lived here in earnest?

The air is laden with memory as much as moisture. Hard to say how many centuries these stones have witnessed. The columns, the chipped edges of old tools, half-formed bowls that might have been used to collect the trickle of water from overhead. I feel an urge to gather them, to restore some sense of the old ways. I find my fingers absently brushing a broken ladle carved from onyx-colored rock. My thumb settles into a worn groove, perfect for a palm. The groove suggests an ancestral hand, similar in shape to mine. If I close my eyes, I can almost feel the laughter or quiet conversation that once echoed here. But then I open them, and I hear only the drip, drip, drip.

I wade through a narrow channel, water sliding across the tops of my toes—though I can’t decide if my toes are webbed or if they merely feel that way in the creeping damp. Every step is careful; the rock floor is slick, but I navigate it as though guided by an inner map. One channel forks off to the left, venturing behind the stump of a column. Another channel leads to a shallow lagoon where pale fungus spreads in a delicate arc. Lifting one foot from the water, I notice how the rivulets cling to me, shining in the dim glow.

At first, I wonder if I’m alone, the only upright being in this stone womb. But I sense watchers in the corners, lumps that might be or might not be living forms. Occasionally, a shape shifts, a pair of eyes glimmers. They’re cautious, these watchers, perhaps more akin to the pale amphibians than to any humanity I recognize. Something in me stirs, whispering that these watchers might be my kin—whether literal or in spirit. They are the keepers of this quiet labyrinth, as I may be too.

I approach the ring of fungus with reverence, kneeling to see the shimmer of droplets beneath its translucent skin. A pale amphibian sits next to it, long limbs folded under a slender torso. The amphibian’s bulging eyes meet mine, holding a quiet acceptance, not fear. It makes a gentle, pulsing shift of its chin sack in a slow breath, and in that gesture, I see something almost wise. The amphibian and I both rely on the dripping water, the meager offerings of fungus. We are siblings in this intimate cycle of damp, shaped by the trickle of eons.

My gaze wanders to a cluster of insect larvae that float under the shallow water’s surface, luminous in delicate stripes of greenish light. They hover and glow, dancing slowly. For a moment, I consider: do I rely on them for nourishment, or do they rely on me to remain untrampled and free? The entire realm seems built on reciprocity—a circle of quiet energies and subtle hungers.

I straighten up, letting the fungus ring slip from my fingertips, leaving a fragile indentation behind. A faint swirling in the air around my ankles suggests an unseen presence, or maybe just a pocket of warmth escaping from between layers of stone. It feels vaguely comforting, as if some ancestral spirit is brushing against me, nudging me deeper into the hush.

Am I part of a tribe that once thrived in these walls? No, there is no thriving here, only persistent existence. Perhaps I am a caretaker grown from the same mineral-laced water that formed the columns. Maybe I am a newly arrived wanderer, lost in the gloom and forgetting the sun. As I walk, I find traces of woven fungus threads looped around a broken peg: a sign that someone once hung tools or garments. I bend down to touch the ropelike fiber. Worn soft and spongy, it leaves a faint film on my hands.

I recall no name for myself. I know only the slow, steady pulse of water. The hush is all-encompassing. If I speak, will there be words left to say? I part my lips—and only exhale a small breath that merges with the damp. The hush remains unbroken.

Perhaps I am a pilgrim who came seeking the timeless quiet of this place. Perhaps I was once an outsider, but my hair has grown thin and colorless and my skin has grown roomy around my bones, adopting the pallor, sliding quietly through the labyrinth until I became one with the gloom. I watch the amphibians feed upon mineral salt, I watch the watchers fade into corners, and I realize: here, I am no more or less important than the next rivulet carving a channel in the rock. We all shape and are shaped by the drip of water.

Somewhere in the distance, at the boundary of this small, stone enclosure, a slender ray of the outside world filters through a vertical fissure. The light is weak, half-lost to the darkness before it even alights on the puddles below. Occasionally, I glimpse it shimmering on the surface of a pool. A fleeting thought stirs in me: if I pressed myself through that fissure, forced my body to find a way upward, would I discover a brighter day beyond?

And yet, the notion of leaving feels foreign, unanchored in any real longing. My bones, if I still possess them in a human sense, are lulled by the lullaby of droplets, by the gentle hush that blankets every corner. If I am free to leave, I have little desire to flee. Even if I am just a memory or a spirit moored to this realm, the place welcomes me in a silent, unspoken way.

No single image of me is fixed. On one breath, I’m stooped and half-human, my hair plastered to my brow by warm condensation, eyes searching the gloom for a reflection of my old self. On another breath, I’m amphibious—soft, translucent, with wide eyes that mirror the watery sheen around me. My limbs extend, and I feel the surge of a subtle, compact agility. Or maybe I am the intangible swirl of warm air near the ground, drifting softly around the watchers’ ankles, gently shepherding them from one dripping spot to another. The place molds me to its needs, and I let myself be shaped as willingly as water shapes stone.

In a distant corner, I find a relic—a chipped flint blade with a handle encased in hardened resin. Who last held this tool? The phantom of a person from a lost age? My own ancestor? If I pick it up, does that make me a caretaker of old ways? My fingertips trace its edge, the blade’s dullness a far cry from a once-honed utility. I sense not only the memory of hunts or harvests but also the passing of countless moments in the hush. Maybe the blade has become purely symbolic, a link between present gloom and vanished centuries.

A shared presence under this roof of sedimentary stone—that is how we endure: amphibian, insect, fungus, the watchers, and me. We breathe the same moisture, glean nourishment from the same slow drips. Each being’s quiet acceptance forms a tapestry of existence as delicate as the fine lichen threaded along the rock.

I slip around a bend, into a narrower corridor that angles downward. Darkness deepens, yet I continue, untroubled. I need no torch or lantern. The faintest glow from unseen larvae or mineral spark is enough to guide my steps. My foot touches a small channel of freshwater, and a subtle shock of warmth passes through me—like recognition, a welcoming handshake. The water and I, we belong together. Maybe I was always a part of it.

I stand still in a small depression of rock where water slides down in gentle curtains. My heart, if that’s what it can be called in this body, beats slowly, in rhythm with the drip. I close my eyes. In that moment, I become the drip itself. I am the shimmer inside the translucent fungus. I am the hiss of condensation forming on an overhang. I am the gaze of a silent watcher in the gloom. I am the single amphibian crouched, swallowing meager nourishment. I am the caretaker who sees the next era approaching with patient calm. 

When I open my eyes, the hush remains, unbroken. The realm acknowledges me: yes, I live here—whoever or whatever I am. This pocket of stone, water, and quiet belongs to me as much as I belong to it.

And that is how I find myself: neither purely human nor wholly amphibian, neither wandering spirit nor guardian, but something fluid in between. One more thin-limbed being, shaped by condensation and centuries of dim twilight, at home with the drip, the hush, and the slow, warm air. Here in this hidden labyrinth, I am a silence that moves—the living echo of all that has been and all that remains.
