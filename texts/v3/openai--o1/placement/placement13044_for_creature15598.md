I can sense myself before I see myself. At first, I am only a soft stirring in the damp warmth, a subtle awareness of water seeping through fissures in the earth. It is as though my body and the surrounding land share a single heartbeat, slow and deliberate. Perhaps I am water first, or root, or simply a listening presence carried on the humidity. I feel the hush of the place breathing around me, urging me to stay still long enough to learn its gentle rhythms.

In the beginning, I’m not certain of my shape. One moment, I might be a small lizard creature, clinging to a broad leaf and tasting the moist air. In another moment, I sense the dark, pebbly soil beneath my belly and imagine myself an amphibian, my song croaking softly at dusk. Then I notice the sensation of rising on two legs, my bare feet sinking into the spongy moss just enough to know I have weight in this world. I realize I am one of the settlement dwellers—or perhaps something very akin to them, with skin that changes tone under shifting light. My hair might be an unruly tangle of fibrous tendrils and woven vine, or it might be cropped close like new fern growth. The longer I stay, the closer I come to a definite form—but the land does not rush me. It allows me to try on each possibility, invites me to linger in the in-between.

Eventually, I take on more consistent presence: a being who walks gently across the saturated earth, whose footprints vanish within a day as the mud shifts and water trickles by, smoothing everything over. I belong to this small community of moss-brown and leaf-green skins, living in braided huts and angled shelters that blend into the half-formed terrain. My limbs are strong yet soft around the edges, reflecting the clay walls we build onto our shelters. I wear minimal cloth, airy as can be in this humidity—reeds woven into a loose vest, a wrap of broad leaves secured at my waist, a scattering of bark-beads braided into my hair. No single piece is meant to last forever; all is subject to the rain, to the shift of seasons, to the new rivulets that form in the night.

In this life, my role emerges in tandem with the land’s talents nudging forth from me. I find that my fingers are deft at weaving: I can pick up stalks of river reeds slick with water and thread them together without snapping them, bending each into place with a patience that mimics the slow shift of soil and root. Before long, I become known among my kin for crafting the boundary fences around the farmland—really, more a set of organic markers to guide the creeping vines than a true barrier. I make doorways out of wood that curve in gentle arcs, always mindful not to block the flow of creatures beneath, be they frogs or drifting water droplets.

Yet I also sense another calling. In the dusky half-light, I feel drawn to the half-finished stone outcroppings where water seeps in silver threads, forming communal watering holes. There, I crouch and listen to the ground. I place both palms against the damp stone and feel micro-vibrations—a stirring, an embryonic breath that resonates deep beneath the surface. At such times, I’ve begun to hum softly, a thrumming note in my chest, as if in conversation with the law of shifting matter. Animals and neighbors alike pause, watch, and sometimes join in with a harmony of their own. I don’t claim to be a shaman or priest, but somehow my presence helps quell the land’s restless energies, as if my voice gently reminds the earth it is free to keep changing but that we dwellers remain present, ready to adapt alongside it.

When I am not weaving, I help cultivate the curly, vine-like crops in our shared garden plots, which are never arranged in neat squares but meander wherever the soil and dappled light lead them. Each vine is a life I encourage with a quiet, smiling dedication. I learn from older, wiser neighbors which pockets of mud are richest in minerals, which ferns prefer a mossy bed, and how to ensure mushrooms and small lichens can grow untrampled along the edges of the walkways. Here, these tasks are not burdens but living conversations with the land. Our community doesn’t rush the plants; we listen to them just as they listen to us. If a vine wilts in one spot, we gently move it, trusting its roots will find a better hold where water trickles more steadily.

During the thick of midday haze, I sit with others in a circle of braided fronds—some elder, some child—sharing quiet talk about new gullies opening in the far corner of our territory or that sly predator spotted at dawn, its footprints pressed into the damp soil. We pass around simple clay cups of water infused with crushed leaves that taste faintly of citrus and moss. If conversation lulls, we listen to the creatures around us: the near-silent flutter of hidden birds, the gentle drip of condensation off a palm leaf, the hush of water traveling in the countless crevices below our feet. Sometimes we all humorlessly marvel that the world is so full of sound and yet remains so peaceful.

I am neither leader nor follower in this settlement, for leadership here is collective and fluid, shaped by need. Some days, I am among those who share ideas on how best to redirect run-off water after a torrential rain; other days, I am content to hum in the background while more skilled voices discuss the farmland’s layout. My neighbors are untroubled by differences in opinion; we each speak in turn, allowing silence to hold space for thought. Eventually, the land’s own voice guides us toward consensus.

When pale dusk rolls in (though it cannot be so different from midday, given how hazed and diffused the light always seems), I join a small group that explores newly formed pools across the stony ridge. We use simple reed-woven vessels to collect whatever fish appear in those shallow basins, taking only what we need. I feel the slick scales under my palms, note the fish’s quicksilver hearts beating, and, with gratitude, we gather them for the evening meal. Walking back, we place half the catch in earthen basins for neighbors tied up in other tasks, trusting that the land’s bounty belongs to all.

What I love most about my life here are the moments before sleep. The night hush settled, the phosphorescent fungi aglow around our common space—faint lights under woven eaves. Some nights, I place my ear to the trunk of a sapling as a slow, rhythmic song vibrates through the wood, or lie back on a raised walk to watch where the canopy of haze fades softly into darkness. Within that hush, I can almost hear the heartbeat of the entire land. I can feel the wide leaves overhead collecting dew, sense the amphibians shifting under leaf litter, and hear the gentle murmur of neighbors sharing a final word or laugh. My body and the landscape feel inseparable, as though we are threads in the same tapestry weaving itself in real time.

Tomorrow, the settlement could look slightly different: a new trickle of water weaving through the huts, a collapsed bank forming a new pond, or fresh vines overtaking a hollow. And I will adapt with it. If the huts need reinforcement, I’ll gather reeds. If a quiet predator claims a corner of land, we’ll let it be, building paths around its territory. If a section of farmland washes away, we’ll celebrate the fresh clay that emerges, a new possibility for a weaving workshop or a place to plant a different variety of mushroom. There is no frustration here—only an acceptance that the land is a living creature, and we are but one vibrant organ within its body.

However many nights pass, when I awaken, I feel neither the anxious need for permanence nor any yearning for a time “before.” The land is new, yes, but it breathes with a constancy that makes me feel grounded. My skin might shift again—gain a mineral gleam, tint more moss-green, or match the soft brown of pressed clay. My hair might lose its vine braids or gain them back a week later. I greet each change as a friend. The people around me do too. We are co-creators with a realm that lovingly folds us into its warm, damp arms. This is my home: a place in the midst of becoming, weaving gently around my presence, just as my presence weaves gently through it.

So when you ask, “Where would I find myself in this world? Who or what would I be?” I can only smile and place my hand to the ground. I am everything this place is: water, root, bark, flesh, and breath. I am a dweller of the half-lit land—neither fully plant nor fully human, but a mixture of all that grows and thrives here. Each day, I learn how to adapt, to weave, to flow, to sing. And in that daily practice, I become more the land itself than ever before. I take shape as a humble caretaker, a weaver of reeds and relationships, finding kinship with every frog’s croak, every trickle of water, every hush of wind through the new, green fronds. In the end, perhaps I am simply one more tender shoot pushing up through fresh soil, guided by the same quiet, steady pulse that makes this whole embryonic world so alive.
