I might only begin to understand what I am when, in the darkness, a long, living tendril brushes across my shoulder. It’s a vine as broad as a small child’s forearm, slick with condensation, rustling gently against the fibrous clothing that clings to me. I stand amid the gently undulating hush, water lapping at the roots beneath my feet. My senses come online slowly: I feel the cold in my bones, the moist chill creeping through my fingertips. And yet I detect a pulse here, something that resonates with an internal rhythm—not just my heartbeat, but a subtle something else that seems to flow through the living architecture itself.

As I take my first steps, I understand I’m no visitor. Not truly. My respiration syncs with the trickling water, as though I have always known to breathe in, breathe out, at precisely the same intervals as the rest of this hidden world. My limbs feel elongated, elongated beyond purely human norms, yet flexible. Swaths of loamy moss drape me, part garment, part second skin. If I run my fingers across my arms, I find patches of slick bark-like texture, small ridges that stretch when I bend. More curiously, my hair—if I can call it that—has the texture of fibrous, root-like strands. They coil around my scalp, collecting beads of moisture, funneling them down toward leaflike structures at my collarbones. It’s all perfectly natural, as though I was born with it.

I move across a narrow boardwalk made of interwoven roots. With every step, the walkway rocks softly, as if acknowledging my passage. I pass a cluster of fungus-lamps, their blue-green glows highlighting the glimmer on my damp skin. Here, I linger. I can’t help but watch how tiny motes of light reflect in the water’s surface—like floating stars compressed into a soggy cosmos. Something in me recognizes their patterns. With a gesture, I brush one of the fungus-lamps gently, coaxing it to vibrate. A low glow intensifies for a moment, then fades. I whisper to it—though I’m not sure if it is to the fungus-lamp itself, or some piece of my own memory that’s stirred by the faint luminescence.

In the distance, two of my kin—if that is the word—gesture for me to come closer. Their bodies are similarly clad in swaying moss garments, with spore clusters sprouting gently along their shoulders and hips. Pulling my cloak tighter, I pick my way across the walkway. The boards bend and shift, but they hold firm, as if the vines sense my presence and brace themselves. When I join the two figures, they tilt their faces toward mine. Though their eyes are wide and dark, I glimpse reflections in them: the same curious shine I sense in my own. We are watchers, my mind tells me—though who or what we watch over remains an unspoken truth.

We speak no words just yet. Instead, one of them taps at a braided rope with deft fingers, sending vibrations along its length. I can sense some signal traveling far, down past the trunk-ramp that spirals below. The rope twangs softly, lost beneath the constant drip-drip of the canopy’s rainfall. One of my companions raises a hand, palm outward, and in the hush I can hear another drip gather at the tip of a leaf overhead. That droplet falls precisely into the open palm. They bring it to their lips, tasting the water—testing for purity or maybe some subtle shift in the bog’s chemistry.

We continue forward. Beneath us, a hulking shape sloshes through deeper water. I tense, but my companions do not. They merely pause, allowing this broad, furred quadruped to pass underfoot. A faint moan escapes its muzzle, and its clouded eyes flicker up at us. In that moment, my breath catches. Some feral part of me expects to see animal hostility, but instead, there is a communal recognition. The creature knows us—knows we are watchers, perhaps. It heaves a great belly-sigh and trudges on. My mind tugs with the memory of having fed such creatures lumps of peat or small fish from woven baskets, in an unspoken exchange of respect. The memory is mine, or ours, or the bog’s.

We walk on, weaving in and out of thick curtains of living vines. Some vines appear to shift as we pass, revealing glimpses of smaller folk hidden behind them. The smaller folk peer up with wide, curious eyes. They seem to measure my figure, my newness. A child among them steps forward to see me. On their slender arm is an armband of fungus filaments swirling in delicate patterns—both ornament and warning, as the filaments can sting if handled incorrectly. I bow my head, acknowledging that curiosity, offering a subtle tilt of my shoulder. The child nods back, seems to smile, then recedes into the tangle. I understand: we share the same home, but each of us holds our piece of it differently. Their corner is that cluster of root-hollows where families huddle close to the warmth of peat-fueled braziers. My place is not so enclosed.

Farther along, the walkway opens onto a wider platform circled by tall, living reeds. Hollow stems catch each wisp of wind, producing an eerie, resonant music. I feel the chord in my chest. It is a chord that reverberates through me, as though I can sense the entire canopy’s exhalations condensed into these reeds. This is a place of gatherings—a place to share vital knowledge and to guide new growth. I recall that I, too, have a role here: the braider of trunk-knots, the caretaker of vine pathways. I do not know how I recall it, but with each step, a deeper recognition settles inside me.

An elder stands near the platform’s center. Their face is furrowed and etched with age, but greenish lichen patterns swirl across their cheeks, bridging the lines so that each crease is more like a river channel than a wrinkle. A large staff in their hand is carved with twisting motifs that echo the shapes of the overhead canopy. They beckon me. And I—knowing no other course—approach.

They say softly (or I hear softly), “Come and tend this place.” Their voice is half breath, half hum, as if shaped by the streaming water. “The roots shall listen to you; the vines consult you. Remember how to guide them.”

I close my eyes momentarily. Memories trickle in: kneeling in the bog’s shallows, coaxing slender shoots to twist and bind in designated patterns, shaping half-formed trunks into living walls. It’s not so much commanding the plants as conversing with them. Showing them where the settlement needs a walkway, a shelter. Respecting what they need in return: nutrients, space, a certain pattern of water flow. My companions stand nearby, their presence a comfort. For we all share the task of guiding growth. We watch, we listen. We measure the pulse of water, sunless though it may be, to glean the proper conditions for new structures. Our footprints are ephemeral—washed away by the next deluge—yet the living architecture we coax endures.

I approach a young trunk; it’s already as thick as my arm. Gently placing both palms on its surface, I feel the swirling energy within. The trunk is still malleable, its wooden fibers not fully hardened. A subtle push here, a guiding twist there, and the plant begins to curve. In the near darkness, soft glimmers of phosphorescent insects gather around us, like watchers bearing witness. Then, almost imperceptibly, the trunk yields to my direction, forming a slight arch. One of my companions murmurs approval. We are shaping a new entranceway—a corridor that will run between two existing huts, bridging a frequently-flooded gap.

As the trunk takes shape, I sense that intangible awareness in the vegetation. The vine-lattice overhead ripples gently, as if breathing an affirmation. Far below, the trickling stream intensifies, bringing fresh water that flows around the roots. Nearby, an amphibious being with webbed hands trudges up from the deeper mire and checks the newly placed reeds, adjusting them for balance. We exchange a nod of understanding. No single species could maintain this place alone. Here, each of us has a vantage, a skill, a small magic of our own.

From time to time, I glance upward, marveling. The canopy is woven so tightly that the sky is a near-constant twilight, but I’ve learned to see in the dark. My pupils dilate, and I become aware of shimmering arcs in the gloom—creatures crossing from one trunk to another, translucent shapes flitting among the tangles. The elder greets them silently with a small movement of the staff, as if to say: We know you’re here. We welcome your presence, so long as you mean peace.

Peace and caution—these are the twin currents that carry us. There is always a hush, not of emptiness but of abiding respect for every inch of living matter around us. Our entire city is a collaboration, and we are duty-bound to preserve it. Even the largest beasts that wade through the pools share in the bargain: they do not crush our huts or uproot our braided walkways, and we do not poison them or drive them away—unless threatened. On that platform ringed by reeds, vital negotiations take place: which new growths to prune, which corridor to let the vines reclaim, where to guide the water next. That is my role, specifically: to interpret the signals from the plants and shape them gently in service of all.

As the hours pass, the hush becomes gentler. A faint breeze stirs, whispering through the reed-pipes. My eyes flit across the settlement: those coiled huts of braided leaves, the half-submerged footpaths, the tall spires of living wood, the scattering of phosphorescent insects that flicker at roots’ edges. I realize I have always lived here, in some essential sense. Even if my memory is new, my body and spirit belong to this place. My role is caretaker of the living roots—part weaver, part diplomat with the green world. 

Perhaps I might take on more roles over time. I sense I am young in my own way, though my limbs feel ancient. Maybe I’ll learn the deeper secrets of spore-based potions from the robed elders. Or roam beyond the settlement with the beast-like dwellers who gather in the watery outskirts, gleaning new resources from the labyrinth outside. I might discover how to coax phosphorescence from mushrooms to light darker corridors, or how to read the shifting swirl of water in the gullies for signs of an incoming flood. Each day, this bog metamorphoses anew, forging new passages, challenging old structures. And each day, I metamorphose alongside it.

Yet as I stand here, listening to my breath in unison with the vast hush, I am content with simply being what I am: a tall-bodied caretaker, half clothed in living vines, half fused to the memory of water and root. When others pass, they drift close and sense the subtle hum in my bones, the hum that tells them: This is one of the watchers of the living walls, a guardian of the quiet corridor. They acknowledge me with faint smiles or slight bows, trusting that as they sleep tonight, the canopies above will remain steadfast, the walkways unbroken. 

And so I maintain this existence in the hush and the bog, bridging the deep green realm overhead with the watery labyrinth beneath. I feel the vines flex under my palms, watch the water gather, sense the hush pressed close. The cold is bracing, but it no longer feels hostile. It’s simply the atmosphere of our home, an intimate place that demands respect and grants wonder in return. I let the hush enfold me, content to be part of its living tapestry—listening as the roots whisper secrets of new branches to braid, new life to cradle, old growth to release. 

I am simply here, part of it all, shaped by it. I am a soul who has made a silent pact with the curled leaves and the shimmering water, an ally meant to guide its living flesh into forms that shelter us all. That is who I am. That is where I dwell—in the hush, in the bog, with vines that bend in welcome as I pass.
