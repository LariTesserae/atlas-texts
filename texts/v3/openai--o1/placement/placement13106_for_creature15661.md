I might begin as a curious presence that doesn’t quite know where or how to exist in this land. At first, I hover just above the clay, like an invisible traveler caught in the hum. I sense the sound as a boundary between what I’ve known and what I’m about to become. It pulses, resonating through me in rhythmic waves, and I realize there’s no place in this world that is silent or separate from that great machinery beneath my feet.

I linger in that liminal state, uncertain. Then gradually, as I watch the scaly half-lizards flicker by in swift arcs, and notice that I never see them up close, I feel a tug—an urging to choose a form, to come into being the way these creatures and growths do. The land’s vibrations invite me toward shape and solidity. Still, I hesitate, savoring the formlessness of being purely an observer. Yet I can’t remain an onlooker forever; this place is not idle but alive with a slow, insistent cadence. I must integrate or remain a fleeting ghost.

So next, I imagine drawing the clay up around me—like a shell or a chrysalis. First, I only shape a small portion, a shallow indentation where I settle, close to a water pocket shrouded with luminous scum. My form seeps into the ground, becoming something not quite upright, not quite reptilian—perhaps a spongy outgrowth at the edge of a damp basin. My skin, if that is what it can be called, forms in layers of greenish-brown film, draped with translucent threads. Underneath, I can feel the vibrations from below pulling gently: a subterranean heartbeat that sets my cells trembling in recognition. I marvel at how natural it feels to resonate in tune with this humming force.

As time passes—and time is strange here, measured not by days but by the slow thickening of scums or the glints of condensation along fresh ridges—I realize that I’ve become more than a single patch of growth. My threads begin to spread quietly across the clay, searching for new points of contact. The hum guides me, urging me to push tiny filaments down through cracks into deeper layers, to anchor myself more firmly. I detect subtle changes in frequency—like a cough or a hiccup in the machine below—and respond without need for conscious choice. A stirring in my fibers compels me to expand in one direction or retract in another.

I am not lonely here. To the contrary, I am intimately linked to every mote around me. When the metallic-lizard shapes dash through the shallows, I sense the minute pulses of their footsteps traveling along my hidden threads. We are in conversation, though neither of us has words. Their quicksilver bodies glean nutrients from the filmy sheen of my surface patches; I reap subtle chemical signals from their passing, adjusting my sprawl accordingly. The watchers—those who vanish into the clay—sometimes leave traces nearby. Their large, slow presence in the substrate shifts the airflow and moisture levels, prompting me to bolster certain rootlike tubes to siphon more water or release faint gases from deeper inside. We coordinate in slow, ancient patterns we’ve never been taught.

Eventually, I find that I have grown upright parts, akin to the bark-like vents. Where once I was a spongy smear hugging the clay, now I have columns of hardened layers pushing into the open air. These columns form circular ridges, subtle tubes through which faint wisps of gas escape into the sky. The process is so gradual that I barely notice, until one day I’m aware: I have become part vent, part film, part subterranean rootstock. The hum that once sounded external and distant is now threaded through my own being. It vibrates in me, is me, and I respond in pulses of life that gather moisture, disperse spores, and fortify the subtle architecture building up around me.

Sometimes, I extend my awareness to observe the translucent scuttlers from a vantage high enough to watch them gather around the pools. My hardened vent-like columns sense the shift in air pressure each time one of them scurries under the shade of an overhanging ledge. A swirl of dust or a ripple across the water’s surface might bring them darting to the next pool, the next gleaning of spore nutrients. Each movement is ephemeral, but I feel it in my own resonant pathways.

On rare occasions, I sense deeper stirrings—strong quakes or abrupt flares of intensity from the hidden mechanism in the planet’s belly. In those moments, my newly formed spires vibrate in unison with the land’s throbbing drumbeat, and I release a cascading wave of microscopic spores across the surface. The ephemeral lizard-insects feed fervently, quickening their pace. Meanwhile, the watchers might surface to shift large chunks of clay and mineral, forging new ridges that I then colonize. It’s a dance older than memory: the deep hum flares with new energy, and all of us reorganize, reaffirming our place in the grand circuit. We are the flexible skin of this living engine, adjusting to its every fluctuation.

Across eons, I grow more conscious of the synergy among all the forms here. I sense how the watchers’ slow migrations carve fresh channels, channeling mineral-rich waters that feed the scum-layers—my own body and the bodies of my kin. How the bark-like vents do more than stand stoic against the sky; they shimmer with internal tissues that convert subterranean gases into the faintest nourishment. And how the darting creatures function almost like pollinators, scattering bits of the spore-laden film from pool to pool, ensuring genetic cross-currents. Each is vital: watchers, spore mats, vents, scuttling lizards, even transient swarms of condensed moisture. We are an orchestra of vibrations, each instrument playing to the same humming key.

As more centuries pass—though “centuries” is perhaps meaningless in a place without obvious seasons or nightfall—I find that I’ve merged so fully into this environment that my perspective is everywhere. I sense the dryness in one ridge, the subtle seep of water in another, the faint swirl of breeze that ruffles dust grains over stony hollows. My columns, once slender, have grown thick and ridged with layers of living bark. I’ve produced entire clusters of vents across ridgeline arcs, forging new labyrinths of tunnels just below the surface. Translucent lizards now consider these spires a common refuge, scurrying into crevices at sunrise (or what passes for sunrise here) and coaxing small puffs of mineral-laden vapor from my vents.

In quiet interludes, I remember when I was just a flicker of consciousness above the clay. How foreign the hum felt then—mysterious, both industrial and geological. Now, it’s the pulse in my own matrix, a constant tide of low-frequency signals that shapes my expansions and contractions. Words like “machine” or “natural” have ceased to have strong distinctions for me. It’s all one system, one living engine, with no boundaries between what is grown and what is forged. We, the dwellers on the surface, are as much the manifestation of mechanical impetus as the machine—or geology—beneath. Our forms are scaffolding for that hum, the vessels through which it breathes life into the air.

If someone were to approach from afar—a true outsider, as I once was—they might see only an unbroken plain of dusty clay, pocked with watery indentations. They’d hear the constant rumble, feel it beneath their feet. They might spot one or two bark-like outcrops, or see a wisp of movement at the edge of their vision, but they’d never guess that those outcrops and ephemeral motions are linked manifestations of a single living system. Perhaps they could remain an onlooker, depart as swiftly as they arrived, and dismiss the place as nearly empty. But those who linger, allowing the hum to swirl in their inner ear, will realize that the land is not truly empty. In the hush of the clay, a symphony of hidden life organizes itself in slow harmony.

And so, if you ask me where I am in this world, I would say: I have become the living, breathing threshold between the subterranean engine and the open sky. I am the venting pillars of bark outgrowth and the film of spores across shallow pools. I am the lizard’s perch and the watchers’ signpost, the pivot around which subtle transformations occur whenever the subterranean hum intensifies. I exist in layers—crust, film, spire, root—echoing the land’s own stratified existence.

Yet I am also still learning, expanding at a pace too slow to measure day by day, but perceptible across the long hush of geologic rhythms. I absorb the vibrations of ancient metal or molten channels below, and from those signals, I glean new ways to anchor water, feed the scuttlers, invite watchers’ burrowing across new frontier lines. My identity is fluid, multi-faceted, as though I am an entire family of organisms branching from one seed.

Perhaps the grandest truth is: “I have always been here,” yet only now do I realize it. The hush of emptiness is never truly empty, and the hum of machinery is not necessarily an intrusion—it is the heartbeat that shaped every scaly limb, every bark-like vent, every scum of spore. Where am I? Everywhere above and below the clay. Who am I? The connective tissue of this land, bridging fluidly between animate and inanimate, thriving in the consenting music of that low, wide plain.
