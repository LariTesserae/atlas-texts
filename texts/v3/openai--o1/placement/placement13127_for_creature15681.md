I drift here, my body the color of old stone, gleaning the faint half-light that saturates this chill domain. At times, I feel as though I myself have been carved from these cold walls. My limbs are many-jointed, each segment subtly translucent, shining with a dull luster. Where once I might have been something else—a creature of air, perhaps, or a traveler who found refuge here—I have long since become part of the water, so adapted that my memory of a different life is hazy as distant starlight.

In the gloom of the submerged corridors, I glide across the smooth surfaces, touching them gently with the tips of my limbs. I have grown sensitive to their contours; each arch, each featureless buttress, is as familiar to me as the ridges of my own shells. When the currents surge, stirring silt and stirring me, I cling to the architecture with fibrous pads hidden beneath chitinous spurs on my arms. I can hold fast against the roar of sudden turbulence the way an ancient banner might cling to its mast in a gale—stubborn and enduring. The swirl passes in moments or hours (it’s hard to mark time here), and when it is gone, I loosen my grip, letting my limbs drift, unhurried.

I have a hinged carapace fanning from my back—hard and ridged to deflect stray debris but flexible enough to flutter with my every pulse of motion. It catches glimmers of luminescent signals from passing creatures, reflecting the intermittent glow in faint ripples. At times, smaller beings find refuge in the grooves of my shell, riding the slipstream as I float carefully between the columns. I feel their presence, half-sheltered by my ridges: a faint brushing, a small shifting of weight. We allow one another’s company. In all this vast hush, companionship, even if unspoken, is not unwelcome.

At intervals, I make my slow pilgrimage into the tall, open courts beyond the corridors. Sometimes I see the great, slow-drifting silhouettes, the hulking creatures that loom in the haze. I feel the water shift around them, a displacement far more massive than my own. They move with a dreamlike stateliness, wreathed in shimmering motes of silt. I have no need to fear them, for they move on a different scale of existence, content to graze the drifting plankton or graze upon the smaller filter-feeders anchored to the grand pillars. Once in a while, I trail behind them, gleaning any morsels they leave in their wake when they scrape the walls or dislodge clusters of shell-luminescent creatures. With a swift paddling of my legs, I gather what I can until the next current sweeps me on.

My senses are neither wholly sight nor hearing nor feeling but a combination of all three, filtered through the pressing hush of the water. Soft pulses of motion and changes in density travel through my carapace; I “hear” them with my entire body. In one direction, an eel glides, uncoiling its pale ribbons into an arched corridor. I sense the faint ghost of trailing bubbles, a minute swirl that seems almost contemplative. In another direction, the shellbound luminescent creatures cluster in a sheltered nook, strobing gentle pulses of white, then purple, then white. I do not fully understand their signals, but I sometimes wonder if we have begun—over endless generations—to parse bits of meaning from those lights. Occasionally, I respond with minute shifts in my own shell tone, though I can’t say if they truly notice.

Where walls and ceilings have slipped or collapsed, I weave through drifting debris. Here, loose fragments of the architecture slowly tumble through the water, scoured by centuries of silt-laden flow. I pause to examine them: the surfaces, though worn smooth, carry faint lines that might once have been carvings or inscriptions. If I half-close my senses, it’s as if the old lines speak in silent reverberations through the water, reminding me that I live in a crafted place. The ghostly echoes of a vanished maker linger as subtle ridges in the stone—knowledge no one can decipher. Sometimes, I sense I am wandering in the footprints of giants, or drifting in the halls of absent gods.

Yet this realm is no tomb. Life persists in every niche, though some forms remain so well hidden that only my peripheral sense catches them scuttling away as I approach. A thousand symmetrical filaments, trembling around column crevices, vanish at my passing. Others, perched on precarious ledges, remain motionless for entire cycles, then metamorphose with a slow, living ripple, pulling free to drift to a better feeding ground. In the gloom, I see them out of the corner of an eye—shapes coalescing from stone, then dissolving again.

I suppose my form must strike others in a similarly enigmatic way. My limbs are many, but I rarely need them all for forward motion; I fold half of them close to my body to streamline, letting the others row or hold. My shell, opaque in some places, is translucent at the edges. A faint opalescent sheen runs along the margins, betraying where the boundary between shell and fluid is never truly fixed. At times, other drifting creatures mistake that glimmer for a corridor or a patch of bare wall, brushing briefly against me before they realize I am alive, not simply part of the structure.

Should you wander these cathedral corridors, you might glimpse me as a shape hugging a column or gliding gently through the silt-laden gloom. I would watch you pass—my pale eyes, with vertical pupils, narrow to track your silhouette. If you came close enough, you might see the minute ripples that pass through my exoskeleton, the purposeful twitch of limbs ready to grasp or swim away. But I rarely flee. Curiosity is my nature. Without curiosity here, one drifts aimlessly and forgets to pause at the new crack in a wall or the out-of-place swirl of bioluminescence that reveals an unfamiliar species.

I graze sometimes on drifting bits of nutrient-laced sediment, brushing them from the undersides of archways or swirling them together with a careful swirl of my forelimbs. I have small, beak-like mouthparts that can nibble algae-like films that cling to the nooks of ancient buttresses—though “algae” might be too simple a term for the faint living scum. In truth, it is an odd microbial lattice that grows only in subtle pockets where the current is still enough, capturing minute organic threads carried on the water. These films glow softly under certain conditions, hinting that they share something of the shell-luminescent creatures scattered throughout the city.

During the lulls between storms, I sometimes rest in an alcove carved from a battered column. I fold my body inwards and slip into half-sleep, letting the water’s hypnotic hush cradle me. It is a restful numbness, a lullaby of tiny vibrations that tells me the world is still spinning in its slow dance of shifting tides. If a silt storm arises, I sense it before it arrives: the faint churn of heavier water, the swirl of particulate matter dragged along currents just at the margin of awareness. I rouse myself, paddling to cling more tightly to my stone niche. Then the storm roars past, a silent rush that might dislodge weaker creatures from their holds. If I am lucky, the receding flood leaves me with a scattering of fresh detritus and morsels to sample.

In the deeper recesses, where the arcs and spires lean precariously, I find pockets of stillness. There, I can drift in near-complete calm, floating among overhangs as if suspended in gently swirling glass. It’s in these hidden corners that I sometimes detect the faint outlines of others like me—or not quite like me, but close enough in shape that I recognize kinship in their motions. We share subtle glances (or what passes for glances here), perhaps a silent flicker of shell coloration. We do not gather in large groups, but in certain heartbreakingly still stretches of corridor, I linger side by side with them, exchanging quiet presence. In that closeness, I feel our pulses of life resonate through the water, a soft series of waves that overlap and fade into the hush.

Yet for all this calm, I know I am part of something always in flux. The water is never still for long. Tides surge unpredictably, columns shift, entire buttresses collapse from time to time. Creatures always move on, carried by the currents or by their own silent impulses. We who dwell here accept that change. We rely on it: new passages open even as old ones close, and the water never ceases to bring its subtle nourishment, bits of organic matter from unseen realms beyond these half-enclosed walls. If I can call any place home, it is the constantly altering mosaic of corridors and open courts. I have no illusions of permanence—not for myself, not for the architecture, and not even for the watery realm overhead. Yet what we share, all of us, is a quiet guardianship of a realm that has outlasted its creators by epochs uncounted.

Sometimes, I wonder if we—as we coexist here—are slowly dreaming the same dream, keeping the memory of those vanished builders alive in our subtle ways. Perhaps, in each glow, each swirl of silt, each worn arch that still stands, there lives a piece of the original intention that shaped this place. Such thoughts pass through my mind in slow waves, carried on the background hum of water. I cannot speak them to another, nor would I know how. But the hush of the sea resonates in me as a reminder: we are all part of the same grand, fluid tapestry. The architecture and its inhabitants have become one body, old stone and living flesh inseparable in the twilight of unending currents.

And so, if you came here and found me clinging to the walls or drifting among the columns, you might wonder what I am—spider, eel, crustacean, or some fusion of them all. But I am simply a pilgrim shaped by ancient architecture and the ceaseless press of cold water. I am the hush of silt stirred gently across an erased inscription, the cautious dance of limbs in subtle pulses of light. I am a keeper of this place, not by duty but by the unspoken bonds of survival that hold all life here. And if you too found yourself in these drowned corridors, perhaps you would feel the same pull—an undeniable sense of belonging to an old, echoing design, lost to time but resonating still in the deep. In that half-light, suspended amid worn colonnades and drifting shapes, you might discover there is no other way to exist but to flow with the endless tide, in silence, together.
