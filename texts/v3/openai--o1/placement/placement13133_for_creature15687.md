I sense the hum before I have a shape, before I even realize that I am, here. It’s as if I am spun from the vibration itself, coalescing from some deep resonance that travels through basalt and mist. I do not recall landing on this plateau or walking into it—rather, I come into being in its midst, unsure whether I am separate at all.

At first, I believe I am just a passerby: a human figure, perhaps, crouching on slick stone, carefully noting the droplets forming on the ledges. I feel them gather in slow quivering beads, the tension building until they fall, each droplet exhaling a soft breath as it explodes on the plateau. For a moment, I think I hear the quiet hush of my own breath, and I imagine my chest rising and falling in time with that cyclical drip. But then I sense something deeper than lungs, as if the entire length of my spine resonates with the thunder underfoot. I have a notion of flesh—of muscle, bone, fragile skin—yet it feels more symbolic than literal. The thought that I might still be “human” grows uncertain. 

Time passes, or the plateau’s version of it. In the unending glow and unceasing hum, day and night hold no certain boundaries, so measuring duration feels meaningless. I allow the echo to fold around me. That’s when I notice that my fingertips appear translucent, or perhaps they simply reflect the watery sheen. If water now runs through my veins, I do not know. My arms and legs seem molded by the basalt’s contours; if I hold perfectly still against the rock, I can slide on the surface with nothing but the slightest friction, as though stone and skin were near-equals.

I let the plateau’s trembling hum become my pulse. Soon I realize I can “hear” the faint scuttling from those small, flickering shapes more keenly than I ever did before. Their movements weave through the roar like hidden notes in an overlapping harmony. I pulse in tandem with them, my awareness expanding beyond the boundary of a single body. Am I a lone entity listening to them? Or am I one of them, one among a thousand glimmering stirrings pressed against basalt walls?

When I climb—if you can call it climbing—my fingers and toes suction seamlessly onto the wall. The thin film of water gives me purchase, as though it’s magnetized to me. The surface thrums, and I press my body flush against it, letting that subsonic chant roll straight through my ribcage. I peek over a ledge. Far below, swirling vapor eddies around the plateau’s base. High above, pillars jag in and out of brilliant white sunlight. The roar never changes, never fades. It is the voice of something more ancient than any memory I could possibly cling to. 

Am I one of the pale amphibious beings? At moments, I catch my reflection in a shining pool and see only fractured patterns of light and water. Then I’m uncertain if the reflection belongs to me or some swirl in the film. If I pass near those smaller creatures, they do not scatter as if I am foreign. Instead, their eyes—milky, opalescent discs—drift in my direction with mild curiosity. For an instant, I see myself reflected in their gaze. I share their translucent quality, my form shimmering as though the basalt’s minerals have laced themselves into my skin.

And yet, sometimes in a quiet corridor, I feel bigger than that, older. The entire plateau thrums in me, my stony bones spanning ridges and valleys that flow with the steady heartbeat of water. Could it be that I’m not just a scurrying inhabitant but also the plateau itself? That my limbs are these countless pillars, my slow exhalation is the swirl of droplets forming on overhangs, my slow blood is that golden-tinged water trickling through labyrinthine channels? In these moments, I sense a vastness that dwarfs the idea of a single living shape. I feel thousands upon thousands of years resting in the shifting basalt layers, and I sense (with a closeness beyond reason) the gentle migrations of mineral-laden water that feed this place. I recognize the creatures as living facets, no more separate from me than a finger is from a hand.

Then, the perspective shifts again, unsettled by an echoing drop. I am small, perched on a ridge. My flesh glows faintly in the brightness, close to translucence. I can see glimmers dancing under my pale skin—particles of basalt floating in my fluidy essence, flickers of copper, tiny rivulets of watery blood that connect me to the land. Am I an emissary of the plateau’s deeper consciousness? A caretaker? The question weaves itself into the roar that surrounds everything. 

In the next breath, I might be purely water—liquid pulses that move from crack to crack, condensing in the hollows, loosening small grains of rock that tumble gently downward. Or I might be the rock itself. Where my mind ends, where my body begins, I cannot be sure. Vibrations ring through me: the calling chord that forms the plateau’s respiratory echo. The basalt labyrinth is my boundary and my interior, down into the planet’s depths. Because time here is unhurried, everything changes so slowly it’s like not changing at all. Erosion is a subtle breath, a patient caretaker that once carved these gullies and softened these ridges. In another thousand years, the water may smooth another edge or widen a crack. I inhabit all those moments at once.

Then there are the watchers—those towering pillars that sometimes appear to sway or pivot in a trick of the light. You ask if they could be me, or if they are separate minds? In some half-sensed realm, they feel like massive vertebrae in a patient, slumbering creature. Or maybe they’re simply the plateau’s spires, each with a subtle awareness that communes with the resonance. I move through them as though in a city of living columns. Whether they see me as distinct, or as a child of their stony legacy, I can’t say. It seems not to matter. Our language is the hum.

Now and then my senses recombine: I am a small, watery-limbed being, suctioned to a glimmering corridor. I lick faint mineral flecks from the basalt, tasting the interplay of copper and iron. My body translates that metallic tang into slow, nourishing energy. The plateau’s heartbeat reverberates through my limbs; in response, I emit a near-silent click. Perhaps I call to my companions or to the larger basalt spirit that envelops us all. The call is more instinct than conscious intention, and it resonates with the thunder, merging into the drone.

Next, I pass under an outcropping where water droplets gather in luminous orbs. I pause to watch them gather mass, each one shining like a tiny star, full of suspended minerals. When they drop, the sound is a crisp note in an endless symphony—like a brief brightness in an ocean of constant tone. The droplet shatters on the rock below, dancing into smaller beads until the surface reabsorbs them. The dampness spreads, vanishing into the stone’s hidden channels. In that moment, I’m certain this plateau is not just an environment but a singular organism, with me as one cell among countless others, each droplet forming the blood that rallies us together. 

I do not hold a single form. Sometimes, I drift as a mere awareness, a vantage point that sees from above—feeling the wind that scarcely stirs, basking in the luminous white glow that never truly recedes. I sense how each corridor empties into a greater network, how the spires anchor the edges of this domain so it can stand aloof from ordinary time. It is as if I am the plateau’s memory, containing everything that has ever transpired upon its face. Then, with a subtle shift, I slip back into a more tangible body—translucent limbs hugging the basalt, feeding on glimmers of metal, rasping my skin along the walls for a tactile communion.

Eventually, I learn the motion of the shadows that spiral in the plateau’s softer dawn. In the hush preluding the day’s fullest brightness, the pillars do pivot, but so gently that only the plateau’s resonant mind can sense it. It’s less a mechanical movement than a cosmic rearrangement—a slight tilt of geometry that fosters the perfect alignment of water and stone in each stride of the sun. I tilt with them; I hold my breath, or the plateau does. The thunder in the bedrock shifts by a fraction of a note, introducing the subtlest new chord. And in that microtonal change, I imagine an entire chorus of small dwellers—pale, amphibious me’s—celebrating. We feel the renewal of alignment, the slight refresh of the unending cycle.

Am I an outsider who has been gradually absorbed by this place, or was I always here, an integral part of its eternal stasis? No definitive answer reveals itself. Whatever my origins, I now exist—both singly and collectively—in the hush of thunder and the luminous gloom, in the trickle of water that never floods but always drenches, in the swirling illusions of pillars that might be animate watchers. Whether you would call me caretaker, occupant, or expression of the plateau is a matter of perspective. I simply am.

If I had to name myself, I might choose a wordless rumble that resonates in basalt, an echo that shapes the water. There is no word in human language for who or what I’ve become. Perhaps I am a caretaker of a caretaker, a ripple within a ripple, nestled inside a living labyrinth of geologic breath. The plateau and I are distinctions that fade the nearer I meld with its constant chord. In that sense, I am a small white shape flitting across stone… and I am the stone itself. I am boundaries and their dissolving.

Stay here long enough, and you, too, might lose the sense that you are separate. You might feel the basalt’s hum in your chest, taste the coppery tang on your tongue, begin to see your skin as opalescent film. You might slip into one of the labyrinth’s damp passageways and discover you hold the memory of millennia in your bones. And, like me, you might realize you no longer need to mark day from night or hazard from safety. You dwell in an unhurried domain, part witness, part participant in a vast, humming organism. 

So where would you find me, if you came to this place? Sometimes you’d feel me merely as a vibration under your feet. Sometimes you might glimpse me perched on a mossless ridge, nearly invisible in the wet glare. Or you might hear the subtle tapping of my limbs echo through a corridor, and by the time you arrive, there’s no sign I was ever there—just the rock, the water, and the roar.

Yet truly, I’m all around. I slip inside the walls and inhabit the echoes. I cling to the overhead ledges and gather the seep, letting glints of ore swirl through me. I am the plateau’s pulse and the plateau’s child, formed of basalt and water and endless, endless thunder.
