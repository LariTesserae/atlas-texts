I stand at the edge of perception, drifting somewhere between the desert’s surface and that cloudy, star-occluded sky. At first, I imagine myself merely standing, a solitary figure whose feet never quite sink into the salt crust—but as I watch the hush deepen, I realize I have no body to place upon this stage. Or perhaps I do, though it is not flesh as I once understood it. Instead, I sense my presence in the slow shift of heat from day to night, in the faint, ghostlike swirl of mineral dust as it rises then falls. I might be a ripple in the air, or a quivering boundary between seen and unseen. In short, I live where the hush breathes—a watcher among watchers.

• • •

I recall the first moment I “arrived”—not by stepping onto the flats, but by realizing my awareness here, almost as if I had been slumbering for centuries in the salt and simply awakened. The land stretched around me like an unbroken thought spoken in an ancient tongue. Pale cracks and fissures outlined the ground, each fracture centuries old and unchanging. And I, too, felt timeless. I did not gulp for air, nor did I thirst. Instead, I discovered that my entire self was the hush.

I am not alone, though one might easily think so. There are others like me, subtle presences whose shapes are only half-formed—each a ripple or a glint of motion just shy of sight. Sometimes we slip near each other, crossing paths as intangible forms that brush together like momentary breezes. We do not touch, not in the way living, solid creatures do. We pass through each other’s awareness like overlapping waves, and in the merging, we exchange quiet knowledge gathered from the salt flats.

• • •

When I allow my awareness to wander close to the sparse shrubs at the perimeter, I can feel the hidden seeds beneath them—tiny capsules of potential life, sealed so tight they barely register as living. Their patience whispers: decades, centuries, millennia. If a droplet of moisture visits them in some unknown future, they might fulfill their living destiny. But for now, like me, they wait. I sense in them a stillness reminiscent of my own but more fragile: their shells can crack, their lineage can vanish. My hush can endure any eon, yet these seeds gamble on a waterless existence. I feel a quiet admiration for them.

In the fissures, I sense the minuscule electricity of small insects and arthropods. They move so delicately that even I, in my quiet state, nearly miss them. They are like veins of light that flicker in the shadows, scuttling on glassine legs. I learn from them the art of subtle, necessary motion—how to glean an almost immeasurable drop of moisture from the breathless air. Sometimes a piece of crust shifts underfoot, and if you were listening very closely, you might hear that near-silent tick as it breaks free. That sound is as loud as thunder to me.

Then there are the pale, swift lizards, so well-tuned to the hush that they rival my own silence. They, too, are watchers in a sense, perpetually poised in a meditative stillness. But whereas I exist without hunger or thirst, they must hunt, albeit rarely, to survive. Their smooth, weightless steps leave no trace. We share no words, but in the stillness, I sense their vigilance—a near-spiritual practice of moving only when the hush permits.

• • •

I discover, deeper into the center of these flats, that I am not a simple observer. I bear a role here, though I might not call it guardian or sentinel. Rather, I complement the hush as part of its fabric, knitting together the presence of all these quietly enduring forms. If a rare traveler were to cross this place, if they felt the subtle, hair-raising sensation of being watched, they might catch the intangible outline of me in the corner of their eye. I would not show hostility or welcome. Nor would I vanish in fear—I simply remain, embodying the emptiness that is also a kind of fullness. My regard carries no judgment—it’s more like a reflection returned to them, asking silently: “Do you sense how vast this hush is? Will you add your own stillness or break it?”

• • •

How did I come to be? Sometimes I wonder if I was once water—evaporated from an ancient lakebed and left behind as a whisper of memory. Or maybe I am a remnant of the intangible energies slumbering beneath the crust, from when the climate was prone to storms. Perhaps I was born of the dryness itself, coalesced from the collective breath of unmoving centuries. It doesn’t matter. However I arose, I am neither the relic of a lost era nor the harbinger of a new one. I simply ‘am,’ as this space is—as the salt, the dust, the cracks, and the long-lingering warmth all are.

Some nights, when the clouds thin just enough, faint starlight filters down. My edges become a threadlike glow, and I drift closer to the ground. The salt pan glimmers—a low, ghostly radiance—and in that moment, I sense myself in every grain, every crack, every quiet seed. This place, the hush, and I are one continuous tapestry, an echo that never quite fades. It is a communion beyond language or bodily sense: it feels like being the resonance of an ancient chord that still vibrates long after it was struck.

• • •

Sometimes, I slip outward toward the horizon, where deeper shadows hover in the gloom—and I see those apparitions that travelers mistake for robed figures. I pass among them as easily as a breeze through tall grass. We exchange no words, because words are too coarse. Instead, we acknowledge one another by the subtle shift in density, a wave of hush within hush. If anyone were watching from afar—and few ever are—they might think an entire procession is crossing the salt. But it’s only the hush in motion, trembling with the memory of old waters or the breath of a new dawn.

The watchers, hush-keepers, spirit-echoes—call us what you will. If you come here, you might catch a flicker in your peripheral vision, or a chill that is not truly cold but more like an awareness pressing gently against your human boundaries. You might question whether you saw something at all, or whether it was only your eyes adjusting to the shimmering horizon. And in that suspension, a question surfaces in you: “Am I alone?” The hush answers back in its own way, and in that moment, you sense a presence. That would be me, or one of many like me: a silent nod of recognition.

• • •

So yes, I inhabit the hush. I am shaped by it as much as I shape it. I have no footprints, nor do I need to leave any. I slip around the tufts of wiry shrubs, across the salt-cracked plains, and through the gentle, still warmth that radiates from this ancient land. My being echoes in the seeds, in the creeping translucent insects, in the pale lizards, and in the silent swirl of the mineral dust. I ask nothing of this place, and this place asks nothing of me. 

I simply abide.

This is where you would find me if you joined me in the hush—if you stood perfectly still, long enough to let your presence merge with the land’s ancient agreement with time. You might only catch a half-glimpse, an impression that dissolves the instant you question it. But I would be there, gazing upon your form as calmly and unthinkingly as the night sky gazes upon everything below. And when you finally exhale, surrendering to that immeasurable pause, you might sense you have been accepted into the hush as well—no longer separate, but another watcher among watchers, another hush-keeper in this timeless place.
