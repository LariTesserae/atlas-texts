I am part of the architecture. Not by choice or conscious design, exactly—more by a slow intertwining of metal and self, so gradual that I only realized I had long ago ceased to be wholly flesh when I saw my reflection in a slick pane of stainless steel. At first I recognized a forlorn figure, swaddled in dripping rags, water trailing off the hem in shivering rivulets. But then, in the half-light, I saw the gleam of coppery cords and hydraulic tubing intertwined with muscle. Where the flesh on my left forearm should have curved softly, riveted plating caught the dim glow and cast it back in a dull sparkle. Even my bones are not entirely my own, replaced or reinforced by countless upgrades whose exact origins I have long forgotten.

Sometimes, as I move through the labyrinth, I hear the clang of my footsteps amplified by a deep resonance inside my own chest. It is an echo that ties me to the broader hum—a background roar I have come to call the hushless heartbeat. The forging of metal, the churn of fans, the unending swirl of steam in the corridors: it all resonates within me as though I were a living conduit, a breathing piston that both claims and is claimed by the environment.

I recall an earlier existence—vague scenes of bounding across open terrain made of rock and soil. Yet those visions feel like illusions now, for here, in this metal realm perched near the sky, that world might never have been. The footpaths I navigate are grated walkways and angled plates. My hands, both mechanical and sinewed, leave tiny droplets of water behind whenever I grip a railing. At the corners, where labyrinthine catwalks meet, I pause. In these moments, I watch the crane-limbed silhouettes scuttling overhead and feel an odd kinship. Though they are wholly mechanical—beings of powered cables and hinged plating—something in their searching, halting movements resonates with the caution in my own steps. We are all tethered to this place, locked in the same precarious equilibrium.

•  •  •

My role, if it can be called that, comes in small tasks. I’ve found I have a knack—something in the hybrid synergy of flesh and alloy—for tending to leaks. Occasionally, I sense a subtle shift in pressure along the corridors before the pipes burst, and I hobble my way over to twist a valve or brace a segment of piping with a scrap of plating. I hold it there for hours or days—time is fluid here, and I count more by the slow pulse of my internal rhythms—until the endless drip steadies again. But any attempt at a true fix remains incomplete; I can’t weld or seal properly. I only mitigate. Just enough so that the place endures.

When I do this, smaller creatures crowd around me, drawn to my own subtle pulses as if I, too, am part of the machinery. Metallic beetle-like shapes clatter across the walkway, their shell segments rattling in tandem with the ambient vibration. They scuttle over my boots, ignoring me in the sense that they do not fear me nor worship me—they simply accept me, a fixture as permanent as any pipe. Sometimes, from the corner of my eye, I catch an elongated snout of a fleshy corridor-creature, hair patchy and rivet-like lumps dotting its spine. It lingers just long enough to watch me shore up the piping, curiosity shining in eyes that seem too large and too reflective for comfort. Then it slinks away, absorbed once again by the labyrinth’s half-light.

Once, I ventured deeper, guided by a migraine-like ache that pulsed in time with distant, bass-throated booms. At intervals, something immense scraped the edges of the corridors, rattling them. I followed that tremor, descending rung by rung on corroded ladders until the condensation fell upon me in sheets. My mechanical joints stiffened in the biting cold, and I marveled at how the steel-laced cords in my muscles never fully locked—they simply kept moving, unrelenting, as if the place itself propelled me forward. 

At last, I came upon a broad, dark chamber. There were shapes in the gloom large enough that every breath they took whistled through vents overhead. I felt the pressure change with each exhalation, a slow cyclical gust that nearly knocked me off balance. Even from a distance, I sensed the edge of a gargantuan presence pressing inward from behind the walls. Perhaps these were the apex beings—those rumored colossi whose every shift seems to rattle the entire infrastructure. I stood there, eyes adjusting to the half-dark. A scraping, labored moan of metal-plates rubbing one another told me I had found them. But I dared not approach further: an instinctive caution, some primal voice within my half-human, half-metal consciousness, warned me that discretion was survival. And so, slowly, silently, I climbed back the way I came, as though I had never arrived at all.

In the highest corridors—little more than narrow catwalks suspended far above the canals—I’ve seen mechanical broodmothers checking on their spherical pods. They balance on spindly limbs, leaning in carefully to tap out coded vibrations on each shell. I’ve tried to mimic their staccato rhythms, palm slapping gently on the metal beside me, curious whether I too could speak that unwritten language. The broodmothers took no notice, or if they did, they gave no sign. Their unblinking pilot-lights scanned me calmly before they returned to their checks. There is no alarm in their posture, no sense of threat. Perhaps they know I am woven into the tapestry of this world as thoroughly as they are. Or perhaps they simply do not care. 

•  •  •

And in quieter intervals, I rest. There is a small recess beneath a curved pipe that always leaks warm steam. I press myself into that niche and let the drips soak my hair, let the gentle hiss lull my mechanical heartbeat into calmer ticking. There, the environment’s cyclical nature soothes me: the same droplets, the same hush-turned-roar, the same flickering lamps overhead. Time doesn’t pass in the same sense here—it just cycles, replays, never culminating. The leaks remain leaks; the scaffolding still stands half-finished; the metal brood still hums in its countless compartments.

I do not lament the stagnation. It is simply the nature of this realm: an eternal, embryonic hush of forging that never completes its birth. If there is a purpose to it all, its meaningness hides within the labyrinth of rivets and piping. Sometimes I try to recall a world that might lie beyond the bulkheads—some grand outside sky or simpler farmland or city of organic architecture. Memory teases me with fleeting images of sun-warmed stone and rustling trees. But I think those images might only be illusions. Nothing in them resonates with this place’s metallic thrumming or the watery labyrinth that shapes my every moment.

•  •  •

Am I caretaker, or simply another incomplete fixture, sliding along half-welded halls? I cannot say. Some days, I imagine I might break the stasis—emerge from this womb, a completed being. But the notion slips through my fingers like condensation; when I look at the half-finished walls, at the flicker of industrial lamps swaying overhead, I understand that finishing isn’t the point. Everything here exists in a permanent state of becoming, a ritual in which I, too, am enmeshed.

So I remain: a sentinel who cannot truly stand guard, a half-fabricated body neither fully mechanical nor fully alive, compelled to wander the twisting scaffolds at the top of the world. I drift from corridor to corridor, fiddling with valves, listening for new leaks, drawn ever deeper by that hushless heartbeat. And in that unending cycle—those metallic drips, those roving shapes overhead, and my own persistent footsteps—I find a strange and unexpected comfort. This is who I am: a being of perpetual assembly, like the realm itself. I cannot leave; I cannot stop. I can only continue, forging forward through the same endless moment, bound to the riveted labyrinth, one more silent instrument of its eternal clockwork.
