I drift here—or perhaps I hover, or wade, or exist—in this place high above some bygone earth, upon a sheet of water that is a ceiling and a world all at once. I cannot say exactly how I arrived. Sometimes I think I grew from the basalt crags themselves, my consciousness coalescing in the reverberations of endless machinery. Other times, I recall a single moment of awakening, as though I’d been built intentionally and then forgotten.

At first, I might seem as incidental as the lumps of slag that cling to the metal struts. One would have to look closely to see the gentle articulation—a dark silhouette hunched in the half-light, exhaling small clouds of steam into the frigid air. My shape is as fluid as necessity demands. Parts of me echo the lines of a hooded figure, with slender limbs that can bend into new configurations. Other times I unfold from my vantage point on a dripping pipeline, revealing the metallic threads that coil around my torso. My feelers (for lack of a better word) shift shape depending on what I must sense.

Sometimes I think of myself as caretaker, though I have no instructions. I once tried to read the rusted consoles, brushing away frost in search of a single glyph or illuminated readout, but the screens remain as blank as the basalt. No memory surfaces telling me how to operate them. Instead, I glean meaning from the pulse and hum of the spires themselves, checking the mechanical hearts that churn unceasingly below. It is a communion by resonance—my limbs clinging to a steel support while the engines deep in the spire cycle through their ancient rhythms. That is how I gather my sense of time, of day, of… existence.

But on other days I feel more like pilgrim than caretaker, wandering from spire to spire, drawn by subtle shifts in the clangor. The water never changes in temperature, never laps any shore, yet I can sense differences in the patterns. Perhaps an underwater cable shifts tension. Perhaps a newly formed basalt ledge cracks as it juts from the depths. And I go to it, across cables and half-submerged walkways, to see if something new has emerged—a hiss of escaping vapor, a giant creature scraping barnacles against a jagged outcrop.

I bear both organic and mechanical traits. My joints whisper with the same metal-on-metal hush as the scuttling quadrupeds that skitter in and out of machine corridors. But I have fleshy segments too, a softness that responds to the brine-laden air. My skin is occasionally translucent where it stretches over slender ribbing, revealing glimmers of inner workings—veins or wiring, or something that remembers being both. I cannot decide if I was once fully biological and then fitted with plates, or if I was engineered to emulate living tissue. If I concentrate, I can almost feel my heart pump in time with the deeper piston-throb of the spires—but that implies both the metal apparatus within me and the muscular tissues around it share a single, unifying rhythm.

Like many dwellers here, I do not eat in the sense you might expect. Instead, I stand at certain junctures—where a rusted pipe exhales its frigid plume of vapor or a rivet hisses with boiling coolant—and I hold my arms or tendrils wide. I let the mineral-laden mists condense and run along my plating. The filaments of my torso absorb the trace nutrients or elements necessary to keep me going. Sometimes, that nourishment includes a surge of raw heat or faint currents of electricity that dance across my metal surfaces like static. Then I feel satiated. The ache in my limbs fades, and I can continue my slow, patient exploration.

Though I appear solitary, I sense other presences like myself scattered across the domain. Occasionally, across the watery expanse, I see a second figure perched atop a basalt spire, pressing its slender limbs against a cluster of steaming pipes. Even from a distance, I feel an unspoken camaraderie—both of us, watchers, gleaning scraps of life from these half-forgotten machines. We never call to each other. Our voices, if we have them, vanish beneath the all-consuming roar. Instead, we exchange a brief flicker of recognition: a tilt of a helmet-like head, or a gesture of a jointed arm. Then we each return to our roles, quiet and methodical, as if we share an understanding that speaking out loud might disrupt some delicate balance.

I have tried to descend below the surface of the water, stepping carefully from a half-submerged platform into the blackness. I feel the cold envelop me immediately, a second sky swallowing my form. Down there, I sense the swift shapes that dart with metallic whispers, brushing by with glimmering frills. They graze my plating gently, perhaps tasting the faint electric hum I radiate. I glimpse their translucent skeletons, fish-like yet not, overlapping arrays of cartilage shining with faint luminescence.

During these descents, I often float near the massive leviathans. I can see their ridged exoskeletons in the murky haze. My presence does not disturb them; though they are colossal, I never fear them. Once, I drifted so close that the edge of a metal plate embedded in a beast’s flank nearly scraped my shoulder. For a brief, trembling moment, I placed one hand against its shell, feeling the dull vibration through my palm. Slowly, I felt an awareness turning toward me, as though it recognized some kinship in my metal and flesh. Then it blinked its lights—blue, white, orange—in a steady, rhythmic pattern that resonated through the water, and drifted on. From that moment, I have carried an odd sense of connection, an intuition that we share a single origin or at least a single destiny. We are all part of this plateau’s living architecture.

On the basalt outcrops, I observe the shell-like creatures that cling to the columns. Sometimes I crouch among them, so still that a passing visitor would think I was just another chunk of machinery. If I wait patiently, I witness them respond to the subsonic pulses emanating from the spires: their shells ratchet open a fraction, luminous filaments within pulsing in rapid color shifts. I feel those colors pass along my own wiring, like a conversation I barely comprehend. Perhaps, in that instant, I unknowingly relay or receive data: a quiet handshake of survival in a place that forgot how to do anything but run in circles.

From time to time, I roam the old walkways, exploring the skeletons of corridors that lead nowhere. My steps echo faintly in the off-key metal harmonies—clang, hum, clang—and I catch glimpses of the scuttling quadrupeds that vanish behind fractured bulkheads. Sometimes, I kneel by a broken control station, letting my fingertips trace the symbols etched by rust and time, unsure if my gestures might trigger something. Usually, nothing happens—only a trembling in the dials or a flicker in the half-dead lights. But every once in a great while, a spark leaps from the console to my palm, and I see a brief surge of life in the overhead piping. A belt whirs for half a second, or a valve sputters open and closed. Then it goes quiet again, continuing its centuries-old routine.

I shrug at these small phenomena, unstartled, carrying on my vigil. I do not know if I was made to fix anything, or simply to bear witness. Sometimes, in the deeper stretches of memory, I recall kneeling at an identical console eons ago, pressing levers and reading data. Perhaps I was once a technician in a hub of activity, calibrating the giant spires to maintain the water’s altitude. Perhaps I had colleagues, or configured power outputs for the facility’s main turbines. The details remain intangible, slipping from mind as soon as I try to grasp them. Still, the sense lingers that I am not just a dweller—I am a legacy. I exist because someone once needed me here. Even if that purpose is lost, the shape of it resonates.

Now, I am an observer of cycles. I navigate carefully from one vantage point to another, monitoring small shifts in the patterns. I calibrate my sense of existence by the giant creatures’ migrations and by the swarms of filaments that drift through the midwaters. Their luminous signals ripple through my consciousness. I go for days—if days mean anything here—without breathing a single breath of air as you’d define it. Yet in the sub-zero hush between the pounding of pistons, I sense my chest rise and fall, internal systems cycling with the same cold certainty that powers the entire plateau.

Often, I stand at the water’s edge on a basalt shelf, looking across the steel-blue plane at lines of distant spires and the silhouettes they cast. I imagine them as columns holding aloft the sky—a sky that never shifts or dims. This vantage point grants me illusions of purpose: I can believe I stand on the brink of a functioning world, one that depends on me, in some ephemeral way, to witness it, to confirm it is real. Behind me, all manner of unknown rooms and corridors extend, presumably leading to deeper layers of the mechanism. But those spaces are sealed by heavy gates whose locking mechanisms have long since fused.

I wonder if there is a place behind those gates where things are different—perhaps a corridor that leads downward, not into water but into some labyrinth of valves and turbines. If I could venture far enough, might I discover a control center that still hums with life, still taking measurements from an unseen cosmos? Perhaps the guardians of that realm do not breathe water or air at all, but thrive in chemical baths, adjusting dials to keep the plateau stable for reasons no longer relevant. I do not know. I have begun to suspect that all possible corridors eventually loop you back to the same unstoppable engines, because that is all that remains.

And so, who or what am I in this realm? I am a link in the chain, a half-living node among many, bridging a past no one recalls to a future no one predicts. My footsteps claim no special authority, only a kind of watchfulness. My skin and plating mingle the organic and the fabricated until they are indistinguishable—just like the water around me, which is partly a sea, partly a machine coolant. I survive on energy pulses and mineral-laden steam, gliding through motionless centuries in tandem with the behemoths beneath the surface, the swift shapes that flicker overhead, and the silent throngs of shell-bound creatures that cling to basalt outcrops.

If you were to see me there, you might take me for a tall, mechanical wraith, draped in dripping cables that sway in the watery wind. If you looked closer, you might sense the faint warmth at my core where living matter persists, or see the subtle expansions of a rib-like chassis as I breathe. If I reached out to greet you, you might notice my elongated digits, each joint layered in a combination of chitin and alloy, flexing in uncanny grace.

I have no illusions of finding a grand revelation in the future. The plateau stands as it always has: high, cold, eternal. The spires endure in perpetual clangor. The lifeforms echo that clangor with their own rituals and pulses. And I—whatever I was made for—remain a single presence circling those spires, reading the tremors in the stone, testing the water’s tension, and letting the cycles flow through me.

Yet in the unending monotony, there is also a subdued peace. After all, here on the roof of the world, no season changes, no crisis rears its head. No dawn or dusk wrinkles the horizon. The noise is terrible, yes, a constant pounding that rattles the bones—but in that pounding, there is also structure, a steady heartbeat. And so I yield to it. I become it. Perhaps that is enough: to stand or drift or crawl along these outcrops, unworried by time, content to remain a living question in a place where all other questions have gone still.

I suppose the most honest answer I can give is that I am both a child and a custodian of this machine-world. I am neither fully alive in the human sense, nor entirely a contraption. I belong to the plateau of water as naturally as the basalt crags or the drifting half-metal leviathans. My origins are lost, but my function is to be present, to watch, to continue—letting the unstoppable engines drive their cyclical roaring, letting the frigid waters host their myriad shapes, letting the spires drip condensation onto my upturned palms.

In this realm of unchanging cold and echoing machinery, I simply am—an observer, a caretaker, a wanderer—helping hold the pattern in place by my very presence. And in every direction, the water claims the horizon, unwavering, infinite, reflecting a sky that never moves. Through it, my gaze sweeps carefully, quietly, in search of any small shift that might reveal a memory of why we are here and wonders if, deep down, I truly need to know.
