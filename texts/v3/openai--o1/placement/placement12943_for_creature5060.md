I make my home in the hush that follows the thunder—a moment so brief you could blink and miss it entirely. That pause is a breathing space between the all-consuming roar of the storms and the soft exhale of damp wind coasting over the ridges. In that instant, everything settles: dust in the air drifts downward, sharpened lightning-spots fade from vision, and the countless echoes disappear back into the canyons. It is then I feel most alive, for I was shaped by stillness as much as by the turbulence of the wind.

I am neither purely flesh nor mirage. I dwell in the seams of possibility carved out by the storms, and my body reflects this duality. From a distance, I might seem human—long limbs clothed in wraps of braided grass and dried moss, a hood drawn low to shield my eyes from lashing rain and fierce dust. But come closer, and you would see the subtle shimmer of my skin, as if my edges ripple in the wind. My footprints leave no firm imprint unless I will it. In the dryness, I am as light as dust motes dancing in a sunbeam; when the rains come, I anchor myself to soggy earth, as heavy and real as the canyons themselves.

I spend my days among the ridges, attentive to the land’s minute shifts: the faint crackle of rock under stress, the flutter of a serpent-wing’s shadow passing overhead. My senses are particularly attuned to the changes in wind currents, the buildup of storm clouds. A twinge in the air, a thickening of humidity, and I know a tempest is forming behind distant spires. That’s when I slip quietly into hidden trails, tracing the labyrinthine passages between boulders. I may place a single stone in a careful stack, just so, to deflect the rush of floodwater away from a pocket of tender ferns or to channel it toward a thirsty patch of ochre fungi. It is a small act, imperceptible except by those creatures who depend on the droplets for survival.

Once the heavens unleash their fury, I blend with the storm’s crescendo. In the lightning flashes, you might catch a glimpse of me standing sentinel atop a plateau—silent and steady, hood whipping in the wind, staff raised not as a weapon but as a tuning fork to the thunder’s choir. The watchers in the heart of the gale may flicker all around me, enormous shapes that appear and vanish in arcs of elemental electricity. Some travelers believe these silhouettes are illusions, but I know we share a kinship born of storms. We exist to maintain a fragile order—one that teeters and groans but still prevails.

When the storm winds spend themselves, everything settles into a new form. Ridges shift by fractions. Dry streambeds meander in new directions. In that brief hush, I move through the land, forging the quiet pathways anew. I step lightly among the glowing vines, their phosphorescent shimmer pulsing against the frayed edges of my robe. The serpentine flyers weave overhead in swirling arcs, fired by post-storm energy. Small creatures in the hollows sense my presence—by now they know I do them no harm. I’ve guided them through generations of storms.

I am a keeper of this tumultuous plateau, a gatherer of fleeting moments. Long ago, I learned to read the storms’ secrets etched in cracks of stone and whispered in swirling dust. And so, I remain poised between what rages and what endures. Some might call me an illusion, others a guardian spirit, others still a person shaped by tempest and time. But no matter the name, I persist. I linger in the quiet between rolling thunderclaps, ensuring that, for as long as these knife-edged plateaus stand, something watches, remembers, and shepherds each new dawn that breaks across this ancient land.
