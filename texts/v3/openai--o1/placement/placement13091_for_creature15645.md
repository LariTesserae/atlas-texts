I think I arrived here as a wanderer long before I knew why. I might have slipped into view with the swirling mist, half-seen like the watchers themselves, until one of the Plateau Folk—an elder with a linen-wrapped staff—found me hunched against a mossy outcrop. Perhaps I was carrying only a small leather pouch of seeds and a few precious keepsakes. I don’t recall precisely the journey that led me here, but I remember there was an overwhelming sense of calm when I first saw the waterfall plunge into the gorge below. It was as though the air itself whispered, “Yes. Rest.” And so I staggered forward, half-frozen by the damp wind, and was welcomed into their circle.

In the early days, my presence must have been startling. The Plateau Folk rarely host outsiders, and I worried I would not belong—especially with the watchers just out of sight, slipping through the clouds. Some said I carried the smell of the lower valleys, too pungent in this realm of perpetual mist. Others only looked at me with soft curiosity. Yet no one turned me away. They lent me a rough-hewn blanket and showed me how to weave the fibrous reeds that grow along the plateau’s rivulets, so that I might craft my own garment to keep out the chill. Their acceptance felt as gentle as the fog itself—as inevitable.

I have since chosen to live in a narrow crevice off to one side of the settlement, half hidden by a dripping overhang of basalt. It’s almost a cave, but with enough open space that I can place a small ring of stones for a hearth and let the smoke curl out into the sky. Here, the plateau’s constant hush presses in. Sometimes, I stir from sleep at night because the wind thrums against the stone in a way that vibrates through my bedroll, filling my ears with an echo that makes me feel as if the mountain is speaking. At first, I felt fear—a sense that I was trespassing on something sacred. But slowly, in the everyday rhythms of eking out life at the plateau’s edge, I’ve come to see it as an invitation rather than a warning. I still sometimes find it astonishing, how it can feel both profoundly lonely and intimately connected.

I’ve become a forager of sorts. By day, I wander the steep slopes, balancing on slick ledges to gather the succulent vines that the Plateau Folk prize for their berries. I’ve learned to spot the faint color changes on the leaves that signal a nutritious cluster. In the cracks of new stone, I search for tiny mushrooms with stems like translucent glass, which the villagers boil into a simple but nourishing soup. Everywhere I step, water beads on every surface, and I move slowly so as not to slip. I can hear the watchers calling—those haunting, half-bipedal shapes glimpsed through the swirling fog. At times, I imagine they are guiding me to the ripest vines, but perhaps that is only my wishful, mountain-infused imagination.

In the evenings, I exchange my harvested finds for bread-like wafers made of ground tubers or tangy drinks the villagers ferment in hollowed wooden casks. Sometimes I join small gatherings at their central courtyard, though I mostly linger at the edges. I like to watch them from a slight remove: how they fold themselves onto the moss and hum quietly to the rhythms that echo through the plateau. Their chants weave into the perpetual thrum of the waterfall, and I feel my heart catch in my throat as the sound resonates in my chest. In those moments, the difference between my heart’s pulse and the mountain’s hum seems negligible—everything aligns in a single chord. It’s startlingly intimate, unifying, to feel that my body and the geology around me share one living resonance.

I’ve grown accustomed to their custom of gentle returning. One does not merely take an armful of mushrooms; one offers a portion of peat back to the earth, or leaves a small carved stone among the tangle of vines. So I’ve also taken to carving. I started with small bones or bits of soft rock, incising swirling lines reminiscent of the wind’s path along the plateau. My fingertips grew calloused from chiseling with copper tools, but over time, my skill improved. Now, I often work late into the misty nights, shaping small tokens—smooth spirals etched with half-imagined patterns that I’ve glimpsed in the watchers’ movements or in the swirl of water. Before dawn, I place them near the hidden pools where the amphibian-like creatures dwell. Sometimes, in return, I find a curious shard of colorful stone left on my threshold, or the faintest trace of footprints that are not quite human.

There came a day, not long ago, when I realized I no longer felt “new” here. I was kneeling at the edge of a shallow ravine, coaxing out a cluster of succulent leaves, when the goats—tall, thin-limbed beasts—emerged around the corner, gazing at me without alarm, as though we had known each other for ages. That little exchange of trust was a revelation: I had become woven into the plateau’s tapestry, not just an observer. At night, I sometimes sense that the watchers’ calls do not fade when I draw near but linger—an invitation, perhaps, to follow them into the deeper clefts where the mountain hum resonates like a carefully plucked string. The villagers and I rarely speak of this openly, but I’ve seen them glance at me with a knowing look, as if they sense my curiosity. They only ever say, “Let the mountain guide you if you are ready,” in that gentle, unhurried way that suggests time here is an endless tapestry rolling out, not a resource to be hoarded.

In those hushed hours just before dawn, I often rise and walk to the plateau’s edge. The sky is usually draped in cloud, but when the wind cooperates, I can see glimpses of ridges stretching below, vanishing into hazy distance. It is then I feel most deeply that I’m part of something half-formed, living shoulder to shoulder with the mountain’s slow transformations. I bow my head to the watchers—wherever they may be perched—and listen for that low thrumming tone that confirms the mountain is indeed alive, and it knows we dwell upon it.

I suppose I am neither Plateau Folk from birth nor total outsider now. I have become something in between—an apprentice to their ways and a wandering spirit who found a home in the swirling fog. I take no name other than what they call me: “the one who arrived with the seeds,” for I share freely the seeds I carried from the lower valleys, hoping that some might root in these thin, mist-shrouded soils. If I am honest, I think I continue my traveling—even if only across the plateau’s small expanses—because something in me resonates with the motion of water and the constant drift of clouds. I have no illusions of taming the place, but gently coaxing my life to entwine with it, singing softly with the wind and stone.

Sometimes, in rare moments, I have a vision of what will become of this plateau. The settlement might grow, or perhaps remain just as it is, quietly thriving in the permanent hush. The watchers may one day reveal themselves to walk openly among the huts, their fluid shapes exchanging calls with the humans. New ridges of rock might rise from the gorge, bridging chasms that are now impassable. I don’t know—and I’m not sure I need to. The Plateau Folk have taught me to linger in the moment, letting life unfold slowly, attuned to the living heartbeat of water dripping onto stone. It’s enough to wake each day and find the plateau still breathing beneath me.

So here is where you’ll find me, for as long as the plateau allows: living in the deep hush of half-formed land, a quiet occupant among the rock-and-rope dwellings, sharing in soft chants and faint flickers of peat fires. I’ve taken my place alongside the folk who listen, the watchers who wait, and the stones that hum. My life now belongs to that in-between realm, swaddled in clouds, shaped by hand-hewn copper tools and the slow, patient drum of water meeting stone. I suspect that this is the closest to home I have ever come.
