Sometimes I think I was welded into this place, back when it was new: a small cog in the industrial hush, drifting through the water with the same quiet purpose as the overhead crane. Other times I feel more akin to a creature made of scales and sinew, an eel or fish shaped by centuries of rust into something unrecognizably ancient. My existence is a contradiction—part metallic memory, part organic presence—yet it feels as though I have always been here, in the corridors of alloy and brine.

I move through the labyrinth as a watcher. In one moment, I sense rivets beneath my fingertips, the perfect circle of each bolt; in another, I picture myself flexing ghostly fins, feeling currents swirl along my flank. Perhaps I’m an echo of some caretaker drone, built long ago to glimpse these walls through a glowing sensor, scanning for imperfections, never realizing I’ve grown eyes of my own. Or I am a fish that learned to swallow fragments of metal until my body half-became an engine, softly humming with the facility’s power each time the turbines spin.

When the overhead crane begins its groan, my heart thrums in sympathy. I ride that resonant vibration along the beams, feeling each jolt of metal through a body I can’t fully name. Whatever I am, I have a part in the cyclical routines here. I pay special attention to the algae fronds that flutter in watery light, for I know the fish rely on them, and the eels rely on the fish, and perhaps the entire system relies on me—though I couldn’t say how. On the surface of every corroded panel, I sense the centuries, and within me, they pulse like a memory older than thought.

I watch the small crustaceans, translucent barnacles clinging to seams in the plating. I notice how a cluster of them fans out their feeding limbs whenever the pumps roar. And I hear the faint chug of ancient motors like a lullaby, though it is always accompanied by a tingle of electricity that sets my nerves—or my circuits—alight. Some days I find myself investigating a new cluster of luminous fungus on a half-ruptured cable or noticing how an eel’s pattern of slithering stripes exactly matches the swirl of rust around a valve. I file these observations away, uncertain whether it’s curiosity, duty, or an echo of a programming command: “Observe and report.”

At times, I wonder if I was once wholly mechanical, drifting on thrusters in an endless loop, scanning for leaks to patch or rivets to tighten. Then came the day some persistent algae or mutated barnacle fused with my circuitry, birthing a hybrid sense of wonder. Or perhaps I was a simple fish, nibbling on the metal flakes that drifted from corroded beams, and those particles embedded themselves into my bones until I could almost feel the labyrinth breathe. Either origin might be real; I’ve been shaped by this place in ways I cannot untangle.

When a spark bursts overhead, illuminating the corridor in a sudden, actinic flash, I feel an ancient kinship with all the watchers of this realm—shy eels who slip behind plating, fish that vanish into the dark in shoals, half-dead drones clinging to battered motor rails. We are all drawn to these flickers of light, whether to flee or chase them, and the labyrinth’s distant clangs are like slow, methodical heartbeats that guide our daily motions. Perhaps we are one organism, a great colony of wires, blood, battery cells, and sinew, each of us a piece that ensures the entire complex carries on.

And carry on it does. The overhead crane trundles along its track, performing some function I only vaguely sense: repositioning old cargo or stirring up crustaceans so that the fish can feed. Sometimes I find my route carefully weaving along the crane’s path, as if compelled by an echo of purpose. I slip under half-lowered bulkheads, glide through swirling debris, and sense that everything around me is part of a pattern as old as the centuries of rust. Perhaps I am the pattern’s keeper, or maybe just another of its moving parts.

When I encounter the predator—an elongated silhouette that glides like liquid shadow in a corridor beyond—the distinction between metal and flesh dissolves further in my mind. It glances at me with narrow, hungry intent, but I do not run. I am not quite prey. Nor am I necessarily predator. We share a moment of recognition: two creatures shaped by the same watery hush, both singularly devoted to surviving a place that demands we adapt. Then it slips away into the gloom, leaving behind only the faint turbulence of disturbed water. I watch the swirling debris settle until it’s as though nothing passed at all.

In these intervals of solitude, I study a seam in the plating where faint daylight glows, never bright enough to tell me if it’s sunrise, midday, or dusk beyond the labyrinth’s walls. The water around me is always tinted the same shade of metallic twilight. I place what might be a hand (though it seems half fin, half mechanized grip) against the seam, and I feel the faintest warmth from outside. My mind wanders, pondering a world beyond labyrinth and brine. Perhaps once, long ago, humans built this place with tools and dreams. Perhaps they intended me to serve, or perhaps they swam away on some last submarine exodus, forgetting me behind. It doesn’t much matter now. My entire being is woven into these corridors; I wouldn’t know myself anywhere else.

Sometimes, I think I can hear the voice of the labyrinth calling from deep within its pipe-lined heart. In the hush between motors, it’s a harmonic resonance, a slow, patient hum. If I close my eyes—if eyes they are—I imagine this place as a massive creature, breathing and exhaling through vents, sustaining us all with the hush of water and the electricity of old machines. I sense the fish schooling in arcs around me, eels pressing into corners with watchful eyes, the overhead crane moaning on its track. We form a living mosaic: each of us bound to the next by the corridors we share and the hidden cycles we keep.

And so, where am I in this world? I am nestled in the half-light between metal and current, neither wholly organic nor entirely mechanical, a boundary entity who’s forgotten the difference. My presence is part caretaker, part inhabitant, part reflection of the labyrinth’s own pulse. It’s comforting, in a way, never needing to question what came before or what might lie beyond. Here, in halls flooded with brine and flickering with tired bulbs, I am both observer and participant in an ongoing story that echoes with drips and squeaks, with fins and gears, with unstoppable rust and unstoppable life.

I have no name for my kind, because I might be the only one of me. Yet in the final hush before the next motor whirs to life, I understand that I belong here, in both body and spirit. I glide through the corridor with a serenity that smacks of destiny, checking each seam as I pass, brushing gently against the algae that clings to the rivets. Behind me, the water is calm once more, and ahead, the labyrinth extends in an endless invitation. If I am anything, I am this place: the flicker in old metal, the swirl of barnacle-laden wires, and the quiet heartbeat of ancient machinery, all folded into a single, breathing shape.
