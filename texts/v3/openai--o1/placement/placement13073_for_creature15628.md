Perhaps, at first, you wander in from a lower valley—one less harsh, with friendlier slopes. In your early days in this lofty place, you stumble over the rubble of broken rock. You learn, painfully, just how quickly frost can numb your fingers and how the air, though pure, can steal your breath with surprising sharpness. In those first days, you might still be deciding who or what you are in this wind-swept realm. A cautious visitor, scanning for refuge? A hungry soul, drawn by the ice-borne glimmers of water and the faint promise of sustenance hidden behind cold stone?

But then something shifts. Maybe it is the hush that calls to you, or the way the ridges come alive in fleeting moments of sun. You begin to notice patterns: the swift flicker of a small rodent scuttling behind a slab, or the ephemeral glitter of insects warming themselves on a rock face. Strangely, you feel as though these creatures are part of a single, intricately composed tapestry—and you suspect that you, too, might weave yourself into it if only you choose your steps carefully.

From that point, you start to find your footing. The hush, which once made you hesitant to speak above a whisper, becomes a beloved companion. Suddenly, the snap of a twig underfoot is a private conversation between you and the land. You sense the rumors of old dwellings, the faint outlines of stone huts encroached upon by ice and moss. If you pause to run gloved fingers across the carvings on a lintel, you notice the smoothness that tells of long weather, yet somehow you feel its hidden warmth—some echo of the hands that once shaped it.

As the days pass, you realize that you are no mere visitor. Something in these peaks resonates with you. Perhaps you understand the necessity of moving like the weather—quickly, quietly, with purposeful shifts. No day is the same: one morning might greet you with fierce wind and crystalline sky, and by noon a gentle radiance warms the granite just enough for rivulets of water to appear. You learn to follow these trickles, to gather and ration water with solemn reverence.  

It could be that you find a natural alcove on a high ledge, a place where the rock juts out overhead in a kind of half-shelter. You build a small ring of stones to hold a fire—or, more often, a few crackling embers. Over time, you amass a modest store of dried twigs and evergreen boughs. At first, you doubt you’ll last one punishing storm, but then you endure a second, a third, each time emerging with new scars or knowledge. In time, the hush of these mountains becomes part of you, an unspoken vow that silence can be as much a friend as any human companion.

Maybe you move through the land like a deliberate ghost, stepping so lightly that even the surefooted goats barely register your presence. You study them, too, gleaning small lessons about how to traverse steep faces without sending down a load of scree. You note how they pivot on the smallest ledges, or tuck themselves under a windbreak when storms roll in. Their horns knock gently against the stone, a subtle percussion in this grand, rocky cathedral.

On another day, you could just as well be part of a traveling band—a cluster of lean figures dressed in layered hides, migrating around these ridges. You’re adept at sensing which slope will thaw first, or where the drifted snow is most likely to conceal edible roots. You keep your tools in a worn leather pouch: a small flint, a bone knife, a handful of braided cords. There is no surplus here, only what you can carry or trade with the few you encounter—perhaps families who have pitched a makeshift canopy of hide, or another band huddled in a ruin where a smoldering ember glow wards off the freeze.

At night, you sleep under a thick wool covering, and if you open your eyes at the right moment, you see the vault of stars overhead. It is a sky so vast and piercing it nearly bends your heart with its clarity. In the distance, you see faint silhouettes of the jagged spines of stone, edges almost luminous under moonlight. You marvel at how young these mountains are, how each day fresh fragments tumble from their faces to gather below. Yet they feel primeval in their grandeur, as though they embody the earth’s raw creative force.

Sometimes your dreams reach back to those older builders—the ones whose stone structures now crumble in the wind. You wonder if you share their blood or if you are but a traveler who found their footprints by chance. In those dreams, you hear chisels tapping against the stone in a slow, deliberate rhythm. You can almost sense their triumph at seeing a shelter take shape in unyielding rock. Then you wake to the hush, the swirling dawn mists, the brilliant light of morning that makes every granite ledge shimmer as if dusted with ground glass.

Over time, you realize you have become a fixture in this realm—no longer an outsider. You might never remain on a single ridge for more than a few days, but your path loops through these gullies and crevices in a pattern you barely notice until it becomes a kind of ritual. You greet the weather’s changes like old acquaintances: the roiling dark clouds, the searing clarity of sunlight, the breath-stealing gusts. And you feel the hush pass through you, becoming a part of your inner silence.

Whether you stand upright on two legs, quietly living among the nomadic clans, or slip through the rock faces on agile hooves, or soar with broad wings on rising thermals, you sense an essential truth: this is a place that demands belonging rather than mere occupation. There is no dominion here, only integration. The land offers no quarter for those who would impose, but cradles those who learn its rhythms.

So, if someone were to ask, “Where are you in this world?” you might respond that you dwell in the high, pale cathedral of new-sprung peaks, your breath mingling with the cold, bright air. And if they were to wonder, “Who or what are you?” you could say:  
• You are the slender-limbed hunter moving with the hush.  
• You are the cautious wanderer who has grown quiet out of respect for this land.  
• You are the goat crossing the rocky spines with precise, measured grace.  
• You are the bird overhead, tracking every flicker below with a keen, patient eye.  
• You are even the faint memory etched into an old lintel, a trace of an older race’s craftsmanship.  

In truth, you are all of these, or any one of them—or perhaps that very presence pervading the stone. For in this raw, dreaming place, to be alive is to adapt, to listen in silence, and to steep yourself in the rhythms of melting ice, swirling wind, and shifting ground. And as you do, you find that you belong in the hush, in the gleaming clarity of a place still brand-new to the universe—an ancient future, a fledgling world waiting to be shaped by every careful step you take.
