I’ve never known any other home but this plateau of living wood. If there was ever a time before I belonged to it, I have no memory of it now. My earliest recollections are of the slow pulse humming through the bark, of peering at the world from a cradle of hollowed vine, and of the constant, whispering drip of condensation that felt as comforting as a lullaby. Perhaps I was born here, or perhaps I wandered in as a child—lost, or carried by some caretaker who left me. Whichever it was, the plateau has always held me as tenderly as a parent, and over years beyond my counting, I’ve grown to belong to it like the moss to a branch.

I am small among the dwellers. Lithe, nearly weightless, my bones and muscles shaped by constant climbing. I suspect there is human blood in me—my body has the general blueprint of two arms, two legs, a torso, and a head. But my skin has grown slick and faintly iridescent, patterned by mottled greens and purples that shift with the plateau’s dim lights. One day, I noticed tiny filament hairs sprouting along my arms. Now, if I press my forearms to a trunk or vine and concentrate, that small fuzz grips enough to keep me from falling, even on the slickest bark. My voice is a whisper—often used only to soothe a skittish spore-lizard or to sing to the living corridors. Among my kin, we communicate mostly by tapping on the bark or using the subtle shifts of our posture. Sound, we’ve learned, carries further than you might imagine in this hush, so we save speech for private moments, where the echoes won’t ripple too far.

I pass my days traversing the labyrinth, helping in small ways. My tribe—if you can call it that—consists mostly of wanderers, a few families. We gather in a central node when we need to share news or perform rituals of growth. But we rarely stay in one spot for long. It’s not from restlessness; it’s the plateau’s constant transformation. A corridor may bloom in a matter of days, or a thick trunk might shift to seal off an alcove. Temporary is simply our way of life. We adapt, weaving new routes and coaxing new living shelters. I’ve grown swift at reading the plateau’s cues—the dryness or dampness of certain vine ends, the color gradations in the fungus that sprouts from knotholes. These signs tell me where the architecture will move next, and how best to avoid being trapped when a corridor decides to bend itself into a new shape.

My role among my people is part forager, part caretaker. Each morning (though “morning” is a guess—light here is always a twilight filter of glow-shrooms and a far-off haze), I head out along the vine arches that radiate from our current roost. I seek pockets of water that have collected in the big leaf-bowls, or pluck clusters of fresh fungal blooms for nourishment. Sometimes I bring small bone-carving tools to chip away at the bark inside cramped corridors, widening it so that slothlike neighbors can pass without damaging the living structure. Those gentle creatures share our sense of hush—if I approach them slowly, they blink large, warm eyes at me and remain calm, their elongated limbs sagging with easy patience. They let me pass; sometimes they even watch me work, their soft squeaks of curiosity echoing faintly through the vines.

I move in near silence, my bare feet practiced at stepping around slick spots or fragile, newly sprouted shoots. Where the bark is thin and green beneath, I feel the heartbeat of the plateau thrumming up through my soles. With one hand pressed lightly to a trunk, I can sense the slow flow of sap like a river of warmth. In certain moments, if I’m still, I’ll catch glimpses of transcendent illusions—the corridor around me flickers, and I see behind its surface to twisting shapes that can’t exist in normal geometry. A floating ring of bark where gravity seems to reverse. A slipstream of spore-lizards zigzagging through a place that might not quite be “here.” This realm’s boundaries overlap with something else, some dimension that the plateau partly occupies, partly hides. I have no fear of it; I take it as sign of the plateau’s ancient magic.

In my more meditative moments, I tend to the seeds embedded in the older walls. I’ve learned the gentle patterns for coaxing them to sprout when needed. Some nights, by the greenish light of bioluminescent mushrooms, I sit cross-legged and hum a low vibration that travels through my chest, out my throat, and into the wood. With careful repetition, the bark’s surface splits, and a tiny, pale root slips free. In a matter of days, perhaps weeks, a new vine emerges, thick as a wrist, ready to be shaped into a bridge or to reinforce a drooping platform. The plateau does most of the work on its own, of course, but we dwellers have discovered that a sympathetic resonance can guide that growth. We’re stewards, coaxing rather than commanding.

At times, I join gatherings with my tribe. We meet in a broad, half-sheltered alcove where nine or ten colossal vines converge in a knotted nexus. By silent agreement, we sit around it, placing our palms or foreheads upon the living roots. At first, only the hush of the plateau’s heartbeat surrounds us, but slowly we add a suspended hum—partly our voices, partly the faint echoes drawn from the vines themselves. The effect is mesmerizing: the vines glow faintly in response, phosphorescent lines running through their bark as though acknowledging our presence. I feel a unity in these gatherings, a sense that we ourselves are an extension of the plateau, neither separate nor subservient. We encourage the vines to remain healthy, to mend weak spots, to open or seal passageways. In return, we find stability, a sense that we will not tumble into the endless swirling mist below.

But not everything is tranquil. I remember once I turned a corner too quickly and nearly collided with one of the swift, vine-limbed predators. It had a serpentine body, each muscle ripple blending with the leafy background in swirling camouflage. I caught the glint of its eyes, a fleeting flash of intelligence or cunning—and then, in the space of a breath, it was gone, vanished into the overlapping vines as if it never existed. My heart pounded, and I half-wondered if I’d hallucinated the encounter. Yet the scratch marks on the bark remained. Life here is shaped by hunger as well as growth. Some slothlike creatures do fall prey to those carnivores; some spore-lizards never make it past their first glide. The plateau does not judge or mourn openly—it simply continues. Day by day, it persists in cycles of renewal, no single part more “important” than another. That includes me.

Sometimes, when I venture deeper down into one of the watery basins far below, I meet other folk whose skin glistens with moisture, their arms and legs webbed, nearly amphibian. We cannot speak many words in common, but now and then we trade gestures. They show me how to cultivate certain submerged fungi that thrive on warm drips from above, and I tap out patterns on the bark to demonstrate how my tribe shapes new walkways. Language isn’t a barrier so much as a different melody, one that the plateau seamlessly conducts. There is no Babel of tongues here—only the hush, and the quiet rhythms that can be shared if one listens sincerely.

Who or what am I in all this? I’ve come to think of myself as a caretaker within the caretaker. The plateau holds me and fosters me, and in turn, I do my small part to keep it healthy. I carry spores I find along my travels, letting them colonize newly formed hollows. I gather bark shavings to create pouches or sleeping mats, ensuring waste is minimal and that all returns to the plateau’s living cycle. At day’s end, when the glow-shrooms dim and the vines resonate with a deeper lull, I nestle into a coiled pocket of bark, feeling the hum of life against my spine. In that moment, I know I’ve always been part of this place—maybe not in physical birth, but in spirit and purpose.

Sometimes I dream of a luminous figure, perhaps an ancestor or a spirit of the plateau. It touches the trunk with slender fingers, and vines coil around it in recognition. In the dream, the figure’s eyes glint in soft pale colors, and where it steps, new leaves sprout. When I wake, there’s always a fresh patch of fungus around me, or a single new shoot curling near my toes, as if my dream left a tangible mark. Perhaps this figure is me from another angle, or perhaps it is the plateau revealing its consciousness.

Under the drifting gloam, I walk the braided highways of living wood. I guide them, and let them guide me. Now and then, I stand at the precipice of a high arch and peer down into the infinite tangles below, hearing the hush of air and the slow drip that echoes from unseen cathedrals of bark. I inhale the air, thick with the scent of wet leaves and spore dust. My eyes close, and for a moment, I feel beyond the edges of my body, a part of the entire architecture, thrumming and breathing in synchronicity.

So yes, if you were to seek me here, you’d find me perched on a root-bridge, swaying just slightly with the plateau’s living pulse. I’m neither its master nor its servant—only a being shaped by it, as organically as a leaf might sprout. In this world, I am caretaker, gatherer, occasional explorer of unseen corners where geometry twists into ribbons of possibility. My home is the hush, the humid air, and the living corridors that defy the boundaries of ordinary sense. I’ve shed the illusions of a rigid self to become part of the plateau’s slow, unceasing transformation.

And that is what I am: a child of vines, an attendant to the pulse, a whisperer in the hush. If you come quietly, you’re welcome to sit with me at the trunk’s edge. Place your hand on the bark and feel that rhythmic stir, the heartbeat of the living plateau. In that shared silence, you too may sense the unspoken truth: we are only one tendril of a vast, living tapestry, forever entwined in a cosmic hush—and that hush is not emptiness but a promise of perpetual becoming.
