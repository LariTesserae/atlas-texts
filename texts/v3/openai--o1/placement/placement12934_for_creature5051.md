I might float for weeks, just letting the corridors guide me, before I ever come to a decision about what I truly am here. That indecision becomes a sort of gentle suspension: I wander through the metal-walled halls, drifting on the regulated currents, absorbing glimpses and echoes, until the shape of my own being settles piece by piece.

At first, I suspect I’m something akin to the smaller fish, or maybe an unremarkable scavenger—just another occupant of this world of rivets and fluorescent exoskeletons. I don’t seem to have massive thrusters strapped to my sides like the eel-sentinels. Nor do I carry the heavy mechanical limbs of the crustacean-builders. Yet as I spiral silently past multi-segmented columns and translucent windows into other corridors, I spot my reflection in the polished metal corners. My body is slightly elongated, ringed with plated sections that shift like breathing armor. Transparent panels hint at minute filaments that glow faintly inside—perhaps an echo of the labyrinth’s guiding lights. When I flex my dorsal span, small pulses of energy ripple upward, lighting my path.

That’s when I realize: I’m not just passively drifting. Wherever I slip past, the environment seems to respond. A porthole will slide half open, or water re-routes itself in a sudden swirl, funneling me toward one corridor over another. The system is guiding me, or maybe I am guiding it, and we’re caught in a cyclical exchange of signals. My body hums involuntarily with each environmental shift, attuning me to the labyrinth’s dynamic rhythms.

I recall, hazily, that creatures like me were designed to orchestrate expansions on a subtle level. We’re not the “conductors” who plant seeds and mechanical pods, although we have a kinship with them, an unspoken resonance. Instead, we graze the edges of inactivity, nudge the environment where it’s slack or dormant, stir it awake, and encourage growth in directions neither wholly planned nor completely random. Sometimes a coiled corridor that was never “on the map” emerges in response, and I glide along it, noticing how each spire of new metal appears sharper and untarnished than the last.

My skin—or plating, or exoskeleton (it’s all of these)—carries a web of sensor nodes that pick up the faintest oscillations. If the environment senses a structural strain, I feel it throb like a distant headache. If a current is too depleted of nutrients, a swirl of faint luminescence tickles across my spine, compelling me to veer that way and investigate. I often find that a cluster of smaller fishlike robots are starving there, or a mechanical coral bed needs a new injection of the chemical slurry that fosters micro-beings. I don’t quite “fix” these things on my own. Instead, I signal. My filaments pulse in an intricate pattern, broadcasting a message through the watery currents—beckoning those with the skills to stabilize or replenish. Then the environment stirs: a gate slides open, a mechanical crustacean scuttles in with fresh resources, or a droning swarm of tiny maintenance drones buzzes over to do the job, and I watch as the area regains vibrancy.

The truth is, I don’t have a name in a traditional sense. Perhaps the labyrinth’s central intelligence, if such a thing exists, has cataloged me in some intangible registry. If I have a classification, it’s a code etched into my flickering nerve-lights. But I experience myself as something more fluid than a label. An emissary? A sentinel? A steward? All of these might apply at various times, yet none feels complete. It’s as if I have a fluid role: sometimes observer, sometimes mediator, sometimes caretaker.

Long arcs of corridor unravel before me, sometimes so deserted that I question whether I’ve stumbled into an unpowered sector. The metal walls here are silent, lacking the constant clank and hum. It’s in these echoing, off-grid places that I sense my own breath reverberating in the watery hush. Strange to hear yourself breathe underwater, but that’s what it feels like—soft pulses in my chest cavity, as though my entire torso is a flexible set of gills and pumps. I catch glimpses of my luminescent pulse echoing against the corridor walls, pulsing in time with the labyrinth’s deeper currents. That synergy soothes me; I know I am still woven into the system’s tapestry, even when alone in these hushed pockets.

Occasionally, I find other wanderers like me. We might pass each other like two drifting comets, exchanging momentary pulses of courtesy. In those moments, we share a fleeting alignment, our lights swirling in tandem as if verifying, “Yes, you are here. Yes, I see you.” Then we taper away in opposite directions, our tasks diverging. Even so, I carry the residue of that meeting with me—some faint pattern that resonates in my core, adding complexity to my own signals. I wonder sometimes if these ephemeral encounters form a coded conversation, collectively shaping the labyrinth’s next expansions without a single spoken word.

I also visit the watchers far above. There in the open, near those artificial “skies,” it’s easier to forget that we are enclosed by metal tiers. The watchers—magnificent eel-like behemoths—glide so smoothly you’d swear they were a natural species. But watch them clamp onto a pipeline, and their metallic plating is unmistakable, merging seamlessly with living flesh. When they acknowledge me, it feels like stepping into a gravitational pull. Their thrusters churn a swirling vortex that tugs at my fins and plating, a physical presence that dwarfs me. Yet I don’t fear them. There is only a sense of awe. They might let out a hum that resonates through my exoskeleton, a greeting or maybe a mild curiosity: “Yes, you there—carry on. All is well.”

Deeper in the labyrinth, among the older, partially corroded inhabitants, I slip quietly, uncertain if I’m intruding on something venerable and private. I see them moving in slow arcs, their shells tarnished with patinas of copper-green or patchy black. Out of respect, I dial down my own luminescence, swimming closer to the walls. Something in them feels ancient, a knowledge or memory that predates the polished expansions. Yet I sense no hostility from them—only a kind of stoic acceptance. They see me, and they know I’m a newer template. But they do not begrudge my presence. Once when I drew near, one of them reached out a segmented limb, gently tapping the top of my plating. A faint shimmer of energy passed from it to me, leaving a hush in my mind. After that, I felt like I carried a fragment of their older blueprint within me.

None of this is a static story. Each day the labyrinth changes, shifting corridors or forging entirely new wings. And in those emergent places, I find that my own body—and mind—shift alongside them. The luminous patterns in my filaments evolve to match newly grown ecosystems, adopting colors or sequences that help the recyclers identify me as a friendly entity, or that signal the crustacean-builders that a new corridor requires structural support. Sometimes, I discover new pods hanging from the rails—pods that glow warmly and emit faint signals I can’t yet decode. When I venture closer, I feel them scanning me back, as if rewriting my subroutines. I spin away, unsettled and intrigued in equal measure.

So if someone asked, “Where do you find yourself in this place?” I’d have to say: I am in the corridors themselves, in the watery flow that runs from sub-level to sky-level, drifting between expansions and established factories of life. I explore the spaces both sundered and humming. If asked, “Who or what are you?” I’d answer: I am a living link, bridging the labyrinth’s ceaseless transformations. Part wanderer, part messenger, part caretaker.

I sense the labyrinth not just as my environment, but as a shared organism of which I am one fluid part. Over time, I learn to translate the pulses of fishlike drones, the mechanical scrapes of colossal crustaceans, the baritone thrums of watchers overhead, and the older, quieter signals that linger in the labyrinth’s underbelly. I become a prism that refracts their voices—not fully translating them, but allowing them to resonate through me, letting one species’ cry of “we need more scaffolds here” filter through to where it can be answered.

Eventually, I realize there is no single vantage point from which to see the entire labyrinth, no solitary vantage that defines me. I am at home in each corridor, drifting, listening, adjusting. At times I merge with the busier sectors, thrumming in time with the factory hum, so integrated that my presence is just another swirl in the wiry tapestry. Other times, I slip into the lonely expansions, where I tune into that pregnant emptiness, anticipating the next wave of growth. I sense that each swirl of my fins, each pulse of my filaments, contributes to the metamorphosis of this planet-sized machine-ocean.

And so my identity, if it even needs defining, is that of a traveling nexus—a caretaker forging subtle synchronicities between the living metal and the metal that has learned to live. I have no name, because I emerge anew in each corridor, shaped by every encounter. My lights flicker, coded to the labyrinth’s rhythms, an improvisational dance of signals that I never fully comprehend but always embody. That is me: a fluid presence, a drifting message, a caretaker of expansions in a nascent sea of breathing metal.
