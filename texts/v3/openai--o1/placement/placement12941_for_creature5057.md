I might not notice my beginning as much as simply come into being here—where the ground gives more than it resists, and the air is thick enough to taste. Perhaps I first awake as a slight vibration along the water’s surface, a ripple that senses the heartbeat of something stirring beneath the mud. And from that tremor, I become aware of my body, though I’m not entirely sure if I wear scales, or feathers, or a thin, amphibious skin. The land itself decides what form I take, shaping me gently, as the water shapes each stone.

At first, I feel the hush of old moss cushioning a cracked boulder, and the slight tension of roots seeking solid earth under the wet sponginess. From that hush emerges a glimmer. By increments, I recognize my own reflection in a mottled puddle—an outline that might be amphibian: damp-limbed, wide-eyed, a small body used to the constant shift between water and boggy shore. But I also sense the possibility of wings—a heron’s slow certainty, or perhaps the fleeting flicker of a dragonfly’s metallic shimmer. So for a while, I linger in this in-between, letting the land’s timelessness coax me into whichever shape feels most natural.

On certain mornings, I am a frog perched upon an outcrop of stone. My throat puffs out in a throaty trill, combining with countless others in the marsh. I feel the drum of my own call echoing in my chest. The day’s bright moistness warms my slick skin. I leap suddenly, startled by a passing dragonfly, and land with a muted splash. When I submerge, the water caresses my back. Every slight movement sends ripples that speak to the mud, the reeds, the stones. Submerged like this, I listen to the hush in the silt—like an ancient lullaby.

Then, in the hush of late afternoon, I transform again. Now I am a silent turtle, half-sunken in the shimmer of shallow water. My shell grows thick as the centuries that shaped these worn-down plains. I watch day shift to evening in the slow blink of my age-heavy eyes. The sun’s rays trickle through the water, swirling with specks of silt that catch primordial gold. Soft algae dances on my carapace, testament to my unwavering patience. When the air cools just a degree, I push away from the bank, sinking deeper into the glassy reflection, and vanish in a swirl of drifting sediment.

At dusk, perhaps I rise into the swaying air, a wide-winged bird gliding above the hush of frogs. My silhouette stretches across still ponds. Beyond every tussock of reeds, I spy the glint of hidden life: a watery rodent scurrying, a shimmering swirl of insect wings. From this vantage, the horizon—limitless and gently rolling—reveals the subtle hum of night’s approach. I sense the shifting hush as the daylight creatures tuck themselves into hollows, and the nighttime creatures begin their watch. My wings beat slowly, in tune with the ancient quiet below.

But there are moments when I am less flesh and more spirit. Sometimes I exist as a gentle swirl of fog drifting across puddled ground at dawn, silvered by early light. The bog breathes me in and out. I hover over a half-buried stone, slip among the moss-laden branches, and search for places where old roots cradle pockets of stagnant water. In that state, I’m not sure where I end and the land begins; I exist in the moisture-laden air itself, among the rising dew, part of each droplet shimmering on leaf edges.

Whatever my form, I am a guardian—if only by presence, by witnessing. I do not set boundaries or leave footprints that linger; I drift, I rest, I observe. My awareness of time becomes fluid, my spirit pulsing in tandem with the metabolism of swamp life. I come to understand that the hush here isn’t emptiness but fullness: the land’s slow voice, older than memory, singing in every gurgling rivulet. I carry that song within me, be it in a frog’s resonant throat or the span of a heron’s wing.

Sometimes, I gather my breath in the shelter of a crooked shrub. The bark is rough under damp palms—or talons, or forelimbs—and I feel the ancient hush in the trunk’s twisted grain. The water seeps so deliberately, winding in rivulets that feed the roots. Here I rest, and the hush folds around me, offering a window into the persistent cycle of soaking and draining, of quiet regeneration. Then a distant call—a lone bird cutting through the evening gloom—stirs me, and I move on, weaving between puddles, sinking to my ankles (or my belly, or my shell) in watery mud.

Now and then, a hush of watchers echoes in my mind, a sense that there are others here—spirit-forms or timeless guardians, aggregates of silt and root and memory. Sometimes I catch their glimmer, like half-remembered figures in the mist, perched on stones or half-hidden by reeds. They might be illusions, or they might be as real as I am. Either way, we greet each other in the silent language of kinship—murmuring acknowledgments that the bog has shaped us all, made us part of its living mosaic.

Are we creatures at all, or are we the land’s own reflection? In truth, it doesn’t matter. In this place of slow transformation, boundaries blur, and identity merges with the watery hush. What I know is that I belong here, between the reed-choked shallows and the low horizons that seem to stretch into infinity. I am as ancient as a stone eroded by eons of rain—and just as fleeting as a droplet clinging to a sedge leaf.

And so I continue, fluid in form but constant in presence, a quiet dweller in the watery tapestry. I slip easily across the sodden ground, through humid air, under wide skies. I breathe the same thick breath that the frogs, the turtles, the herons, and the hidden watchers breathe. My story is the marsh’s story, renewed every time the water rises or falls, every time the frogs trill to the dusk or the dawn. I am no single creature; I am the hush and the hum, the croak and the buzz, an embodiment of the land’s own slow, inevitable rhythm.
