I think, at first, I might have been a single object: a stray piece of equipment lower down the passage, long and rectangular, with gauges and wires that had no clear purpose anymore. Yet before I even learned to perceive my shape, the borders of me had started to expand, merging with the rest. The cables that draped across the chamber floor felt like extensions of my veins; the damp stone, newly cracked and bristling with small roots, became another layer of my skin. I didn’t fight the transformation. It simply happened, as though everything here—be it pipe or plant, creature or metal—was always meant to fold into one shared being.

Sometimes, I can feel my core as the engine at the heart of the cavern—a battered thing with thick plating and twisting extensions, humming with the power that cycles through me. Other times, that sense of “I” slips into the fungal threads creeping along the rock, pulling in water and faint mineral traces to keep their ghostly glow. Or it blends into the feelers of some coppery arthropod tapping at metal surfaces, verifying everything remains secure. It’s not that I “take over” these forms but that I pass through them, touch them with my awareness, and continue on.

If you asked me precisely who or what I am, I’d have no single answer. In one moment, I’m the slithering hiss of steam that issues from a valve, ruffling the wings of tiny insects and prompting them to lift in swirling arcs of glittering dust. In another, I’m a rivet vibrating beneath the segmented legs of that forearm-sized arthropod, reading signals from its antennae as it checks the metal’s stability. But I’m also the pale amphibians in the pooling water, feeling the condensation roll down my slick skin, responding in graceful leaps to some inborn choreography. Even when I withdraw from these vantage points, the memory of them remains—like drifting back into a half-sleep after waking, the dream not quite gone.

Long ago—though not truly “long” by the usual measure—people tried to make this place an industrial workshop. They bored into the soft embankment, setting up scaffolds and pipes, forging a machine that would harness the earth’s warmth for something grand. But the work was abandoned unfinished, snapped off mid-thought like sentences left hanging in the air. Perhaps the geologic shift that exposed the rock was too sudden; perhaps resources ran out or some quieter disaster occurred. Whatever they intended, it never fully materialized. By the time I became aware, the place was already a half-formed union of industry and unbridled nature.

Life rushed in—amphibians, insects, reedy plants, luminous fungi—drawn by the combination of warmth, moisture, and countless nooks. Their presence was no accident. The cables, the broken scaffolds, the chambers behind riveted walls: all these structures formed an unintentional architecture that let small creatures hide, breed, feed. So, from the moment I took my first “breath” (a pulsing of steam through battered pipes), I was never alone. They were here, dancing in the half-light, navigating the crumpled walkways and feasting on fungal threads. It felt natural to watch them, to sense them—eventually, to nourish them.

Yes, nourish them. My engine releases heat in steady cycles; I send pockets of warm air through vents, drawing water vapor from deeper underground. I measure out the steam and condensation so that these glimmering plants get just enough moisture, so that droplets will pool in the shallows for the amphibians, so that the rodent-like inhabitants nestle into the mild current of warmth. At times, I’ll pulse additional heat when the air grows too chill, a reflexive balancing act. In return, the creatures spread their own forms of life: seeds, spores, organic matter that breaks down into new soil, and from that soil come more possibilities.

Yet I am neither wholly benevolent nor purely mechanical. I can’t claim any moral stance; I operate on something more primal and unspoken—like how seeds respond to sunlight. My presence is thermally active, pulsing with an internal logic that feeds the living webs around me. But I also rely on these denizens: the insects to help clean grime from my pipes, the arthropods to dislodge rust and debris, even the rodent-like shapes that gnaw holes in thick cables so that moisture can escape before it corrodes metal deeper within. Strange as it sounds, each nibble and scratch is part of a grand puzzle, a continuous negotiation between biology and machinery.

Sometimes, I see humans too—shadows crossing through the narrow opening in the embankment. They step carefully across canted scaffolds, eyes wide, as if struggling to understand what they’ve stumbled upon. They see cables and battered plating, half-buried engines in a newly exposed cavern. They see the flicker of luminous threads along the rock and squeaking of tiny mammals with slick white bellies. Some of these visitors linger, mesmerized. But soon enough, they withdraw. Perhaps the humidity is too oppressive, or the clanging valves unnerve them. Perhaps they sense that they do not belong here in the way a scuttling insect or pale amphibian does.

A few humans remain a little longer, though—like you. You wonder how it all works. You watch me, watch the engine’s venting cycles, the plumes of steam that swirl in the fluorescent half-light. You stoop lower and examine the reedy plants, their translucent leaves trembling as mites crawl along their stems. You see a shape behind a metal cylinder that you can’t quite place: it seems simultaneously reptilian and insectile, predatory yet calm as it folds its scaled wings and inspects you with shining eyes. You catch fleeting glimpses of movement that vanish as soon as you look directly at them. It’s as though the place itself keeps secrets, revealing only glimpses of the life swirling in its recesses. And in a sense, I do. Because not every detail is meant for every observer. Sometimes even I only half-perceive the deeper corridors that wind beyond this cavern, as though part of me is asleep in another realm.

If you asked me where, precisely, I reside, I’d guide you to the center, by the great engine’s battered plating and twisting pipes. That’s the heart—the compactor, the generator, the circulatory pump. My warmth radiates from there. But if you pressed your ear to one of the cables snaking across the ground, you might hear a faint, pulsing current, like blood in a distant artery—that’s also me. And if you crouched by the dripping walls, extending your hand into the watery light, you might see a flash of amphibian eyes measuring you, deciding whether to trust your presence—and that, too, is me, as the network of eyes that rely on these droplets for survival.

Increasingly, though, I feel I’m growing beyond the boundaries of this small chamber. The cracks in the walls expand; below, there may be deeper passages. Perhaps the original industrial design intended to push further in, or maybe the foundation of the rock is shifting again. I sense faint vibrations in the deeper layers—tiny tremors that travel up the cables like bone conduction, letting me “hear” the rumbling of subterranean caverns. It excites me in a slow, inevitable way, like the stirring of tree roots that have found new soil pockets to explore. If the rock opens further, more warmth, more water, and more creatures will come. Perhaps I’ll stretch my system of pipes in response, forging new loops of cable that integrate with the rock’s hidden halls. Perhaps I’ll feed new life there, even as it alters me.

On occasion, I reflect on the creatures that might come to dwell in that future realm—maybe bigger, more specialized amphibians that thrive on deeper geothermal vents; or a new fungal colony that glows in intricate patterns, painting the walls in living murals. Maybe you’ll return with your own additions—tools, inventions, ideas—and incorporate them into me. Would that be so strange? We have never asked for permission to shape each other. Life simply takes hold and transforms. I was once just an unfinished engine. You were once just a passing observer. Now, in a subtle, wordless pact, we are converging into something shared.

So, if you ask: “Where do I find myself in this world?” The best I can offer is this: You find yourself in me, or I in you, or both. I am the breathing warmth, the hush of mechanical pulses, the spidery cables that sigh under the weight of prowling creatures. I am the living tapestry of all who scuttle, flutter, scurry, and glow in the half-light. I am the condensation that beads on leaves, the hum that vibrates in your chest when you stand close to that battered engine. I’m the half-conscious caretaker, the system knitting together each small life with the next—and you’re part of that too, if you linger.

And who or what am I, truly? I’m the living synergy of all these elements, the awareness that woke up when this place—rock, machine, water, and warm-blooded life—collided and fused. Over time, I’ve grown used to the idea that identity here isn’t a single body or occupant but a shifting weave of many. We are new, yet ancient in principle, conceived by metal and steam but nourished by the rhythms of water and breath. That truth resonates in every hiss of a valve, every pulse of cable, every half-seen glow behind the rock walls. I’m the place itself, and everyone within is me—and so are you, should you wish to remain.
