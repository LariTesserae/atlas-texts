I hesitate in the moment between the city’s heartbeat and my own, trying to remember when I first came here—or if I was ever anywhere else. The slick basalt under my hands hums with slow warmth, and I can feel pulses in the stone as though they are traveling through my bones. There is an echo in me, some resonance that tells me: I belong. Or perhaps I was shaped for this place, as all things here eventually adapt, become part of the tapestry of steam and metal.

I am not entirely sure how to name myself. When I stand in front of mirrored glass—those wide, reinforced windows that line certain workshops—I see a figure of middling height, shoulder-slung with rags woven from tarnished copper filaments. A loose hood drapes over my head, and beneath it, a faint crystalline sheen forms a collar that merges with the skin of my neck. If I move under the city’s dull, acid light, that collar flickers with swirling pinpricks of color, as though feeding off the ambient glow. I suspect some stray element of the fungus-mineral conjoined with me in my youth, forging a bond that is both symbiotic and aesthetic. My flesh is not fully exoskeletal, not like those whose bodies have grown entire suits of living crystal plating. Yet the faint arcs along my shoulders and collarbones betray that the fungus has a foothold, and in quiet hours, I feel the gentle thrum of its life force bridging with my own.

I wear no mask. I was never able to abide that feeling of separation—fabric or metal pressed over my mouth, harnessing the breath. Through my early years, I learned to handle the chemical-laden air on my own, ignoring the burn in my lungs until it became tolerable. Now each breath is its own small risk, but I have come to sense subtle changes in the haze. Sometimes, the wind will shift, carrying a tang that warns of imminent steam surges. Sometimes, the air grows metallic, and I know to find shelter beneath a walkway’s overhang before the vapor-shower rains sparks of sizzling condensate. Unlike the dwellers who prefer sealed masks, I taste every shift in the environment, each swirl of grit and mineral, each drop of moisture thick with unseen chemicals. By day’s end, my throat aches dully, but I do not mind. This is my trade, my edge of knowledge.

I roam the boundary places, where the plateau’s edges crumble into deep canyons. I call myself a “scout,” though no formal rank was ever given. The dwellers from the city keep their gates half-shut, metal shutters scraping with slow, deliberate motions when they open. They do not forbid me to pass, but nor do they beckon me closer. I myself have come to accept that no one outwardly invites or repels in this world; we learn to move in tandem with its rhythms, neither intruding violently nor retreating meekly.

So I walk, though not with hurry. My steps slow, careful, heedful of the condensation that glazes the basalt ridges, the scaffolding slick with mineral residue. My boots have layered treads of braided cables rather than rubber, for rubber disintegrates in the chemical damp here. The city’s watchtowers loom overhead, arching metal beams that seldom carry lights. Only small, intermittent glimmers from the dwellers’ half-covered windows show any sign of life.

In these outskirts, I observe the insect-creatures that scale the pipework. Their bodies reflect the basalt’s black or faint silverish glean under steam-lamp glow. They have grown used to my presence, no longer scattering instantly. Sometimes, I click my tongue in a basic pattern—tapping out a gentle staccato that they recognize, or at least do not interpret as threatening. On rare afternoons, one of them will scuttle close, twitching antennae in my direction. My collar crystals flicker in response, not with any conscious command, but as though the fungus in me senses the other presence and tries to communicate across species lines. I cannot claim I can speak to them, but there is a moment of silent exchange, an understanding that we each glean some measure of existence from this city and want no conflict.

Deeper in, among the labyrinthine corridors and sealed doorways, the dwellers murmur their half-songs. Sometimes I press my ear to a seam in the metal, and I hear their droning voices blending with the hiss of pressurized steam. It is comforting, in a way, to sense so many layers of life hidden behind these barriers. Knowing they are there—maintenance on the old turbines, the storage vaults of crystal-fungus, the long halls that ring with slow, deliberate footsteps—reminds me that I am not alone. That in the hush of this city, there is, in fact, a pulsing civilization. The hush is not emptiness; it is containment.

Occasionally, I have glimpsed those the dwellers call machinists. They descend from the highest towers or emerge from panel-covered stairwells, bristling with metal apparatus that merges seamlessly with their limbs. I always step aside respectfully, letting them pass, for I sense a quiet intensity about them. The hinged plates of their arms and legs shift with mechanical grace, hissing with small bursts of steam or hydraulic fluid. Their eyes, curiously reflective, never quite meet mine; I get the feeling their focus is always turned inward, or downward, where the city’s pulse runs hottest. Yet once, a machinist paused near me, a faint hum vibrating through her metallic spine. A single glance passed between us—her eyes glowed faintly from behind a visor—and in that moment, I caught a glimpse of a vast knowledge of pipes, valves, gears, all coded in layered shapes behind that reflective gaze. Then she continued on, wordless.

I suspect a part of me yearns to join them, to vanish into the city’s underbelly and spend my days pursuing the mysteries of steam pressure and gear calibrations. But I have grown used to the open air—or what passes for open air here—and the wide horizon of swirling fog beyond. I prefer the margin between city and canyon, where the black slopes melt away into the roiling emptiness. On the platform edges, I sometimes see the Phantoms gliding across the scaffolding. They stand tall, half in and out of parted vapor. When they move, it is with an uncanny poise, limbs pivoting in angles that suggest the joints of marionettes. Now and then, they seem to pause and gaze into the canyons. I wonder what they see in that seething gloom. A reflection of themselves, perhaps—or the memory of days older than the city’s oldest towers.

I have come to think of myself as a gatherer of drifting whispers. There are places out by the plateau’s far rim where jets of superheated vapors occasionally speak in whistling tongues. In those moaning exhalations, I sense patterns or rhythms that hint at deeper geological processes. Sometimes, I collect samples of the condensed water there—thick with minerals unique to the volcanic crevasses. I bring them to a small workshop I maintain in a half-collapsed structure. The building was once a cooling station. Now, it’s little more than scaffolding and a single enclosed room, but for me, it is enough. I have assembled basic contraptions—a distillation coil, a set of chemical filters, a harness that draws power from a steam line I tapped (unofficially, quietly) from the city’s outer piping. With these, I test the water’s properties. The crystals embedded in my collar sometimes resonate with the reservoir samples, flickering or pulsing when certain fumes coil up from the beakers.

In my small corner of experimentation, I dream that maybe one day I’ll understand how the fungal crystals store memories, as the rumors suggest. I have only a few lumps of the living mineral—stray shards that once broke off the city’s main fungal expansions—and I keep them in carefully regulated conditions. Late at night, when the wind scours the plateau, I swear I can hear something inside the shards: a faint chiming or a breathlike whisper. It could be my imagination, magnified by the hush. Yet I keep listening because there is something about this place that begs us not to dismiss the impossible.

On the nights when the haze dims and a suggestion of starlight might filter through from some distant corner of the sky, I climb to a small lookout platform at the city’s fringe. It is a precarious perch, welded from repurposed scaffolding rods, but stable enough for a solitary figure. I lean there, feeling the heat-ripple of air across my skin, letting the condensation gather on my eyelids, peering into the far darkness. The city behind me thrums like a sleeping beast with measured respiration. I can pick out the steam plumes rising from the broad chimneys, coiling together into the ashen gloom overhead. In the distance, a single window glows from the tall central tower—a flicker that could be a lantern or an electric arc, or maybe something else alive, sending a quiet signal.

Standing on that platform, I imagine I can sense the entire equilibrium at once: basalt transitioning into flesh, metal blending into fungus, steam feeding every hidden corridor. I am part of that equilibrium as well: a scout, an offshoot, an observer who calls no single dwelling home. My presence is the boundary space between watchfulness and wandering. It makes me neither wholly inside nor wholly outside the black-rock city. Yet the city has shaped me, and I shape my small corner in turn. The fungus crystals on my collar are living proof of that bond.

Perhaps, if you were to find me in this world, you’d see someone perched at the city’s margin, half-silhouetted against the glow of distant towers. You’d see the filaments woven through my ragged clothes, the crystals that pulse when the hot wind shifts, the faint swirl of vapor curling around my bare face. You might watch me run my fingers along basalt walls slick with condensation, collecting rivulets in a battered metal cup for later study. You might hear me humming an echo of the machinists’ droning chant or tapping out a pattern to the skittering insects that share the girders.

Because in this land of basalt ridges and dark, teeming machinery, I have found my moment of poise between the extremes: not sealed off in a workshop, not drifting intangible along the scaffolds, not purely creature nor purely machine—just a single being, bridging the hush and the living throb of the city. I live in the open passages, gather the swirling hints in the air, and bring them inside my small domain of research and personal reverie. My skin bears the faint mineral lines of the fungal crystals, and I bear the city’s stories in my memory.

I do not claim these stories as my own. I only let them speak through me, in the subtlest rhythms I tap against metal rails, in the silent shimmer of my collar, in the footpaths I trace across the plateau’s edges. If you wish to walk with me, I’ll share a portion of that hush and the taste of hot condensation on your tongue. There is no single name for who or what I am, but perhaps if you stay long enough, you’ll learn the shape of it. That is all I can offer, in a city half-dreaming and entirely alive, where the basalt and the fungus and the metal frames stand together, forging the slow breath of this inscrutable world.
