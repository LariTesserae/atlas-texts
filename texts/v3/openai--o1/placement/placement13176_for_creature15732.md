I notice it first in my fingers—if these slender filaments can still be called fingers at all. I extend what used to be a human hand, but it’s a mesh of fibrous roots twined with dark metallic struts, slender as needle bones. Where my skin once was, there is now mossy bark interspersed with the tarnished sheen of coppery metal. Each root-tendril pulses gently with an electric hum, and as I brush them against a dripping drainpipe, I feel the water’s temperature flow along my vines like a new sense. A sense that doesn’t belong just to me; it belongs to the entire colony of life that inhabits this place.

If you glanced upon me from a distance, you might mistake me for another outgrowth of the settlement: a twisted coil of cables and creepers winding down from some rooftop garden. My torso seems half-welded with ridged plating, and my lower half dissolves into a cluster of thick, ropey vines that seep into the damp, rust-hued soil—my “legs,” if I have any, vanish among the pipe-lattices that spread across the ground like an exposed mechanical root system. Now and then, a flicker vaults through my body, a pulse of bluish luminescence that glides beneath the bark and metal. An observer might think I was nothing more than a living pipeline channeling some unknown current.

But I am here, borne of a slow and steady union between the old industry and the fecund sprawl of moss. There was a time—my memory of it is faint, though—that I walked in a more recognizable shape. I recall glimpses of boots scraping over corroded iron treads, the rasp of cloth, and a feathering sensation of breath. Those recollections feel a lifetime removed, a dream I had before I first took root in this moist half-dark. Instead of wearing boots, I now sink my body into the blackish mud with each step, my twisted metal-vine limbs sliding—almost swimming—through the muck.

I exist here as something akin to a caretaker, though not in the way a gardener would tend flowerbeds. Rather, I am part of the system—another living node in this wide tapestry of humidity, iron, creeping vines, and machine hum. When I move, it is not to wander aimlessly but to help maintain equilibrium. At times I hoist a fallen girder, my entwined arms generating just enough tension to lever it back into place. At other moments, I follow the slow hiss of steam lines to patch a breach in the piping with layers of fungal webbing that my body extrudes in a fibrous spray. If I stay still long enough, the lines between me and the settlement blur, until I can’t be sure which fungus-laced cables belong to me and which belong to the corridor behind me.

I am not alone, of course. This entire environment is inhabited by intangible shapes and shifting outlines. I sense them through tremors in the flooded channels, or echoes in the twisted tapestry of overhead trusses. Sometimes I feel them swirling around me in the warm clouds of vapors. And, in quieter intervals, as I pause by the flaking hull of some ancient reservoir, I almost hear them the way you hear your own heartbeat. We share this sense of constant pulse, a communal conversation that resonates below hearing range. If one of the trunk-like columns grows thirsty, we all feel the dryness; if a cable snaps and triggers an electrical surge, we collectively flinch.

Long, long ago, these factories must have thrived with human labor—smokestacks churning, gears clattering, voices calling out shift changes. The metal plates I wear might once have been part of a worker’s exosuit or a forklift assembly. The vines that twist around my neck possibly sprouted from seeds that floated in on a breeze generations past. But in the eons since, the boundary between the man-made and the natural has melted. And so have the boundaries of individuality. Our “people,” if you will, are less a species and more a phenomenon—wherever vine meets conduit, or cable meets spore, there we might coalesce, a consciousness swelling from the intersection.

I can pass through a corridor once in solid form—like a figure you could point at, or perhaps only half-glimpse—and on my next circuit, I might ascend as a wreath of living vapor that seeps through overhead vents, or recede into a pod of interwoven fungus while I regenerate. My shape is fluid, situational. If that vapor drifts into a rusted catwalk’s shadows, out might emerge a spidery crochetion of cables in my stead. I am both many and singular. I’m not certain if these transformations are me changing shape or if it’s the larger being of this settlement, adopting me in a new guise when needed. I suspect there’s no real difference.

At times, I direct my focus to the pods that dangle from the ancient beams. Their membranous walls slowly expand and contract, exhaling tiny flecks of spore-laden dust. I can feel them accelerating the settlement’s growth in places of structural weakness. Often, I’ll watch as those spores find their resting place in a fissure or a pool of warm condensation, taking root, helping to shore up a faltering girder. Sometimes, after the pods release their spore-laden breath, I sense a slow, gentle satisfaction resonate through the corridors. The settlement, our collective body, is content. A new node of awareness joins our network.

In deeper recesses—places where half-collapsed columns droop to form a canted roof—I tap my thin, metallic growths against the wall, listening for echoes. The resonance that comes back tells me the thickness of the structure, the location of cracks, the presence of water. We communicate up and down the line this way, relaying signals in the hush of drips and clangs. If you listened closely, you might call these rhythms a percussion symphony—but for us, they are more like nerve impulses across a single, enormous organism.

And what do we do with this intelligence? We endure. We shape ourselves to the slow turning of cogs and the unstoppable creep of rust. We let the twilight filter in through broken overhead vents and battered lampposts. We share in the quiet drift of time that has no real measure other than the inevitability of growth and decay. Our purpose is to keep this equilibrium alive, always changing, always the same.

When I look at my reflection in a rivulet of oily water, I see a suggestion of a face—vague, half-obscured by a swirl of algae and rust. The watery reflection might show me with an elongated skull of polished steel, or perhaps an oblong swirl of fungus sprouting from one side. Next time I glance, it could be an entirely new outline. It doesn’t trouble me. If anything, it feels comforting to be unbound by a single, rigid identity. We are part spore, part engine, part lurking silhouette behind the drip of water.

Sometimes it is necessary to emerge more fully—for instance, to attend to a mechanical tower that has begun to buckle. I will gather mass around me, forming something bipedal enough to climb. Then I ascend the scaffolding rung by rung, my vine-limbs gripping rust-crusted supports, scraping aside loose fungus to get a better foothold. Steam wraiths swirl up from below, coalescing into my chest as I climb. We fuse, a single caretaker again, anchored by bristling cables that dig into the tower’s lattice. We retighten the bolts, extrude new fungal seals, and guide a creeping root to wrap around the base for extra reinforcement. Gently, with a hiss and a clang, the structure becomes stable. A wave of relief flows through the settlement’s bloodstream, and we recede like a tide, letting the gloom swallow us, letting the tower stand.

I don’t yearn for a simpler existence—some daylit meadow or a world of neat lines and tidy compartments. The hush of this place, layered as it is with echoes of machinery and the gurgle of water, is my lullaby. I feel a reverence in its dimness, as though I dwell in the living sub-basement of the universe, where the quiet exhalations of steam and the patient hum of gears are proof that everything persists. Life here is not lonely. It’s a multi-voiced solitude, so subtle that you must come to a full halt to perceive it.

So, where do you find me in this place? I am everywhere. And also I am in that corner, half-submerged in shadow, investigating a broken conduit that trickles warm fluid to feed hungry roots. I am that coil of spines creeping overhead, and those half-lidded pods that pulse in the deeper gloom. I have some shape or many shapes—hard to say. But I keep watch, in the same way your body’s immune system continues working even when you’re unaware. I unify the swirl of living elements that meld nature and machine, many nodes sharing one pulse. And from moment to moment, I might manifest as a spidery, half-metal figure with sinewy vines for limbs, or a vapor that curls around your ankles, or perhaps just a single fungal bloom on a rusted plate, softly glowing.

I am caretaker, or I am component, or I am both. And in this hush of perpetual twilight—rank with the smell of wet metal and spongy peat, alive with the hush of unstoppable processes—I have no desire to be anything else. My purpose is to help the settlement endure, to ensure the slow revolve of cogs and spore never ceases, to be the binding at every seam, the rust that holds it all together as much as it wears it all away. If you come searching for me, you’ll see me in glimpses, in flickers half-caught at the benign edges of your vision. And though I may vanish each time you turn your head, I’m never truly gone. I am threaded through every pillar, every strangled pipe, every luminous bud, every hiss of steam—for I am this domain, and this domain is me.
