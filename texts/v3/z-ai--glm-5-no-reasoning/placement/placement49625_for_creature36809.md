I am one of the deep-wall ones. Not by birth—that was eight quiet generations ago, in a fissure so narrow my ancestors folded their wings double to fit—but by return, by choice, by the slow gravity that pulls all of us eventually toward the oldest stone.

We remember the tightness. That is the first thing you have to understand about us, or about me, since I am the only one who can tell you what it is to be this particular node in the colony. The body remembers even when the conscious mind—the part of me that processes, that integrates, that speaks into the chorus and listens to what comes back—has no language for memory. My wing membrane has a different tension than my grandmother's did. Her lineage came from a crack no wider than my hand, a vertical crevice in granite that held perhaps three hundred individuals in a living column, each one pressed against the next, the sound of the colony bouncing off close walls until it became a single throbbing hum that you felt more than heard. When she emerged into the hollow—that first generation of rupture—she flew in spirals for days. Couldn't stop. Her body didn't know what to do with all that nothing. She'd lived her whole life in touch with stone on one side and kindred on the other. The open air was like a wound she couldn't locate.

I am the shape of what came after.

---

I roost now in a hollow three-quarters of the way up the eastern wall, in a section where the condensation drips constantly from an overhang above. The dampness doesn't bother me. We are creatures of wet stone, of mineral breath, of air so thick with moisture that our fur beads with it while we sleep. The constant drip has carved my hollow deeper than most—a slight acid in the water, some geologic quirk—and I like the extra depth. When I fold myself into it, when I let my wings collapse into their rest configuration, I am almost enclosed. Almost back in the fissure. The colony's sound reaches me as a distant murmur here, filtered through the bodies between me and the open hollow, and I can pretend, if I want, that I am still in the narrow dark.

But I don't want that. Not really. That is the strange thing about being me, about being us, about being this particular voice in the chorus. I chose the deep wall because it lets me remember what we were, but I chose to be here instead of there. I chose the open hollow. I chose to fly.

---

I should tell you what it feels like to fly in a world that has no edges.

In the fissures, the cracks, the narrow capillaries of the deep rock, flying was a series of turns. You launched, you banked, you landed. The walls were always there, pressing close, shaping your trajectory, telling you where to go. You navigated by exclusion—by what you couldn't do, couldn't reach, couldn't become. The stone was the limit, and the limit was the stone, and flight was the brief intervals between contacts.

In the hollow, there is no limit. Not in any direction that matters. The ceiling is so far above me that I have flown toward it for hours and not reached it—have turned back because I tired, not because I arrived. The walls are so far apart that I can fly straight for longer than my body can sustain. And the floor... the floor is there, yes, the fractured stone plain with its pools and its pale fungal rings, but it is not a limit. It is a reference. A thing I can choose to approach, to skim, to drink from, or not.

Do you understand what that does to a creature? What it did to us?

We had to learn to navigate by inclusion. By what we *could* do, *could* reach, *could* become. The open air offered no instructions. We had to make them ourselves.

---

This is what the chorus is for.

I have been calling it a chorus, but that is the wrong word. It is not music. It is not speech. It is *information*, and information, and information again, layered and folded and fed back through the millions of listening nodes until it becomes something that no single one of us can hold.

When I fly, I sing. My voice—the particular frequency my body produces, shaped by my size, my mass, the particular tension of my wing membranes, the mineral content of the water I drank this morning—my voice goes out into the air. It hits the stone, the water, the bodies of my kindred. It returns to me modified, carrying in its modification the shape of what it touched. This is how I see. This is how we all see.

But here is what you might not expect: my voice also hits the air that other voices have already passed through. And those voices have been shaped by the echoes of other voices. And those echoes have been shaped by the echoes of echoes, by interference patterns, by harmonic overlays, by the accumulated acoustic history of the last several seconds of flight. When I listen—when I *really* listen, which is to say when I let my processing go quiet and simply receive what comes—I hear not just my own echoes. I hear the echoes of everyone else's echoes. I hear the shape of the hollow as it is perceived by the whole colony, updated in something close to real time, corrected by the redundant input of thousands of slightly different perspectives.

I am never alone when I fly. Even when no one is near me, even when I am the only body in a given volume of air, I am held in the perception of the colony. My position is known. My trajectory is known. If I change course—because I see an insect, because I tire, because the mineral content of a particular air current shifts and I want to follow it—the colony knows. The chorus shifts. The shape of the sound field adjusts.

We are never alone. We have never been alone. Not since before the rock opened.

---

I want to tell you about the fissure-kin who stayed behind.

Not all of us came to the hollow. When the granite sheared away and the vast room appeared, most of us poured into it—that irresistible gravitational flow of population into available space—but some did not. Some remained in the deep fissures, the narrow capillaries, the cracks that thread through all bedrock if you go deep enough.

Why? I do not know. I cannot know. I have never been to those places, and if I tried to go, I am not sure I could fit. I am too large now. Too adapted to the open air. My body has decided what it is, and what it is is a creature of the hollow.

But we hear them.

They sing too. Their voices come through the stone itself—conducted by the rock, attenuated beyond recognition, so faint that only the oldest and deepest among us can perceive them. But they are there. A counterchorus. A second field of sound, muffled and distant, existing in a place we can no longer reach.

We do not know what they are saying. Their frequencies have shifted in the generations since separation. Their dialect—if that is the word—has evolved away from ours. We hear them, and they hear us, and neither of us knows what the other means.

But we know they are there. And they know we are here. And perhaps that is enough.

---

I remember the first time I saw one of you.

You walked differently than anything I had perceived before. Stone moves, yes—falls, shifts, cracks—but it moves suddenly, violently, and then settles. Water moves continuously but predictably, following the paths that stone has carved for it. You moved like neither. You placed one foot and then another, paused, turned, resumed. Your steps were irregular. Your direction was unclear. You occupied space in a way that suggested purpose without predictability.

I was flying when I first heard you—not the sound of your steps, which were soft, but the way your body changed the chorus's echoes. You were a hole in the sound field. A shape that absorbed and redirected. I banked toward you without thinking, the way I bank toward any anomaly, and I flew close enough to feel the air displacement of my own wings bouncing off your solid mass.

You stood still. You looked up. I could feel the surfaces of you—cloth, skin, hair, the irregular topography of a body that had evolved in a different world, under different pressures, for different purposes. You were so strange. So *angular*. Everything about you was edges and projections, nothing smoothed by the constant press of stone and kindred.

I circled you three times. I do not know why. Curiosity, perhaps, if such a word applies. The colony had already incorporated you, had already adjusted its flight paths and its sound field to account for your presence, but I wanted to see you closer. I wanted to hear what your particular density did to my own echoes.

You did not move. You did not reach for me or startle or flee. You simply watched, as best as I could tell. Your eyes—those forward-facing, surface-adapted organs—tracked me. I could feel the focus of you, the particular way you attended.

And then I flew back to the wall, and I folded myself into my hollow, and I added my voice to the chorus again.

---

You wonder if I am an individual. The question is strange to me.

Of course I am an individual. I have a body. I have a position. I have a voice that no other voice precisely matches. When I die, the chorus will lose that frequency, that harmonic, that particular node in the network. It will adjust. It will compensate. It will be slightly less than it was.

But I am also not an individual. I am the chorus. Not metaphorically—I *am* the perceptual field I inhabit. My sense of self extends beyond my body into the air around me, into the stone, into the sound. When the colony flies, when we move together through the hollow in those great sweeping formations that seem choreographed but are not, when we drink from the pools in synchronized shifts that leave no moment unattended, I am not watching something happen. I am happening. I am part of the happening.

The word *individual* comes from *individuus*—undividable. But I am constantly divided. I am constantly sharing my perception, my voice, my self, with the others. I am porous. I am connected. I am a node in a network that has no center and no edge.

And yet I am also this body. This hollow. This particular voice.

Both are true. The hollow has room for contradictions.

---

I want you to understand that we are not waiting for you.

This is important. You came down through the switchbacks, walking slowly, adjusting to the dark, letting your eyes fail and then adapt. You crossed the stone plain and looked at the pools and the fungus rings and the sleeping bodies on the walls. You heard our chorus and felt it in your chest. And perhaps you thought: *here is a mystery. Here is something to be solved.*

But we are not a mystery. We are not a problem. We are not a question waiting for an answer.

We are a way of being that has found its equilibrium. We are a life that has discovered what it is and has decided, collectively, to continue being that. The hollow opened, and we filled it, and we have been filling it ever since—not because we are driven by growth or hunger or the need to expand, but because this is what we do. This is what we are.

You can study us. You can catalog us. You can name us, if names are what you need. But you cannot complete us. We are already complete. We have been complete since before the hollow opened. We will be complete after you leave.

I do not say this to be unkind. I say it because it is what I would want to know, if I were you. I would want to know that the world I had entered was not waiting for me to give it meaning. It already meant. It had always meant.

---

I am the one who circled you.

If you remember that moment—that brief attention, those three passes through the air around your strange angular body—that was me. I am not special. I am not designated as the one who greets visitors. We have no such roles. But I was flying when you entered, and I heard you, and I chose to see you.

I will remember you after you leave.

Not because you were important—you were not, in the end. You changed nothing. The colony adjusted, incorporated, and moved on. But *I* remember. My particular voice will carry the trace of your density, your irregular shape, the way you displaced the air. I will sing that memory into the chorus, and it will become part of our accumulated perception of the hollow, and long after you have climbed back up through the switchbacks and returned to the world of change and weather and time, there will be a small note in our song that corresponds to you.

A footprint. A whisper. A body that walked where no body had walked before.

I am glad you came. Not because you were needed. Not because you completed something. But because it is good, sometimes, to see what is not-the-hollow. To be reminded that there are other ways of being, other shapes of life, other worlds that have decided different things.

We are the Lower March. We are the chorus in the dark. We are the pale bodies on the wet stone, the million wings, the endless sound.

And you are the one who passed through.

---

If you come back, I will know you.

Your density is recorded now. Your particular way of moving, of being, of displacing air. The colony will recognize you—will adjust around you again, will flow past you like water past a stone that it has already learned.

And I will fly down from my hollow. I will circle you. I will feel the surfaces of you with my voice and my ears and my pale, recessed, barely-functioning eyes.

I will remember.

We always remember.
