Then I stopped looking for the seam, and the Hatch found me.

---

I did not fall. Falling is a physics of the finished world—gravity pulling one hard thing toward one hard place. What I did was *soften*. The passage breathed against me, and I breathed with it, and my borders became something negotiable. I had been taught to distrust anything that had not yet hardened, anything that might still become something other than what it was. I gripped my edges tightly. I held my name in my mouth like a stone I could swallow to anchor myself to the world above.

But the Hatch does not demand. It permits.

And so I stayed. I stood in that sourceless light, and I let the brightness pool against me, and I watched the room rearrange itself around my presence—not to push me out, not to consume me, but to *make space for me*. A protrusion became a depression, and I understood: it was creating a place where I could stand. A surface tilted toward me, and I understood: it was offering me a view.

I was debris. I was a pause. And I was *welcomed*.

The Sculptors were the first to find the word for me.

One drifted close—smoke and membrane, patterns cycling across its surface like a mind trying to remember something it had never known. I should have been afraid. Here was a body the size of my torso, with wounds and doors and eyes rotating across its vocabulary, and it was considering me with the patience of fog. But fear requires the expectation of ending, and in the Hatch, nothing ends. Everything is still becoming. The Sculptor could not destroy me because I was not finished enough to be destroyed. I was only *interrupted*.

Its patterns slowed. I watched them try my shape—shoulders, the curve of a skull, the particular angle of a standing thing that had forgotten how to flow. They cycled through iterations of me: eyes that were too round, mouths that were too fixed, a body that held its edges like a threat. The Sculptor was learning the word for *hard*. The Sculptor was learning the word for *done*.

The Sculptor took it, and the Sculptor carried it to the wall, and it pressed against the raw stone with the gentle insistence of fog, and the stone changed.

My warmth became a tilt in the architecture. My breath became a channel for moisture. Something of me was already becoming part of the Hatch.

The Mouths found me next.

I should not have let them lean toward me. I should have stepped back, should have retreated toward the seam and the promise of return. The Mouths found me strange. They had never tasted something so *fixed*. They filtered me, processed me, decided that I was not yet ready for consumption. I was still becoming, even if I didn't know it.

That was the first thing the Hatch taught me: I was not done. I had thought I was completed, a finished story marching toward its final period. But the Mouths could taste the unfinished parts of me—the doubts that still softened my certainties, the questions that still lingered in my cells, the small emerging truths that I had been ignoring because they did not fit the shape I had chosen. I was still becoming. I had never stopped.

The Crawlers ignored me. This was its own kindness.

They traversed the hanging curtains of accumulated material, consuming what was over, breaking down the spent formations and carrying them to the pools. They had too many legs or not enough, and they moved in directions that didn't correspond to gravity, and they did not fall because gravity here was still a suggestion. And they did not notice me because I was not yet ready for consumption. My story was not yet over. I was not yet spent.

I had never been needed in that way. In the world above, I had been useful, I had been productive, I had been valued—but I had never been *needed* as part of a process larger than myself. I had never been a cell in a body that was still forming. I had been an individual, a finished thing, a knot of fixed definitions that stood alone.

Standing in the Hatch, watching the Crawlers carry the debris of becoming to the pools where new things would grow, I began to understand what I was missing.

The pools drew me.

I did not want to look into them. I knew that the shapes moving in the depths were not reflections of anything above—they were the Seeds, the embryos of future growths, the becoming before the becoming. I knew that if I looked, I would see something that I could not unsee. I knew that looking was a kind of participation, and I was afraid of participating in a process I did not understand.

But the urge was biological. Inevitable. The Hatch does not force; it *permits*, and what it permits is often what you most need.

Time passed. Or it didn't. Time is another suggestion in the Hatch, another rule that hasn't yet hardened.

I found the Remnants—the structure half-emerged from the wall, the shards and cables and shapes that had once been something else. ---

The exit found me when I stopped looking for it.

I did not want to leave. The Hatch had shown me that I was still becoming, that I had never stopped, that the finished thing I had been was a lie I had told myself because I was afraid of rawness. I wanted to stay. I wanted to let the process continue, to let the Mouths taste me and the Crawlers consume me and the Seeds drink me until I was fully incorporated into the eternal work of making. The Hatch is for becoming, and I was still becoming—which meant I still had becoming to do, in the world above, in the world of hard edges and fixed definitions and shadows that fall in one direction. I was not ready to be consumed. I was not ready to be reconstituted. I was not ready to become Remnant.

The seam opened behind me—silently, breathlessly—and I stepped through, and the world above was there, waiting for me, hard and bright and merciless in its completion. The passage closed. The Hatch released me.
