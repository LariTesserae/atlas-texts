I would be the one who counts.

Not the drips—I have no talent for measuring time that way. The Gall-Keeper counts drips because she has become the wall, and the wall knows intervals. I count something else. I count the moments when the boundary fails.

---

I came to Wickwright nine hundred and eleven drips ago. I was not fleeing. I was not seeking warmth. I was curious. I had heard stories—in the fen-villages at the forest's edge, where people still live in houses of wood they've *cut* and *built*, which seems to me now a kind of violence—I had heard stories of the willow with a town inside its wound, and I thought: someone should remember it.

I thought I would stay for a season. A hundred drips, perhaps. Write down what I found. Leave.

The gall has a way of making leaving feel abstract.

---

My work is this: I notice the failures.

I notice when the Gall-Keeper's grain-pattern spreads a millimeter further up her forearm. I notice when the Forager returns and the mites explore her skin a fraction longer before retreating. I notice when the Light-Gardener's breathing syncs with the biofilm's pulse—yes, it pulses, I was the one who confirmed this, though I cannot prove it to anyone who hasn't watched for six hundred drips through the thinning and thickening, patient, still, counting.

I notice when the children catch the moths and the moths do not struggle.

I notice when the weeping from the ceiling pores takes on a taste I've come to think of as *grief*—a higher mineral content, a faint bitterness that lasts for perhaps twenty drips after someone has gone deeper. The Gall-Keeper says the willow doesn't know we're here. I am not certain she's right.

---

I sleep in the alcove between the Weaver's station and the Light-Gardener's wall. It is not mine. None of them are *mine*. This is the thing I am still learning, nine hundred and eleven drips in: the gall does not grant ownership. It grants *tenancy*. And tenancy is conditional on becoming.

The alcove does not fit me. Not yet. My shoulders are too broad for the opening; I sleep half-curled, one arm extended into the main cavity, touching the root-mat floor. The wood has begun to change—the grain ridges are higher where my hip presses, the ceiling fractionally lower. In another three hundred drips, perhaps, it will fit me. In another thousand, I will fit it.

I do not know if I will leave before then.

---

I keep records in a way the others do not. Not written—the gall consumes paper, and anyway there is nothing to write *on*—but *remembered*. My body is my archive. Each centipede bite is a datum. Each cold snap in the root-flow is a notation. Each time the moths come and I count them—forty-three, forty-seven, thirty-eight, fifty-one—I am building a history of the light.

The Gall-Keeper tolerates me. I think she understands what I'm doing, though we've never discussed it. When she announces the third gallery is active, I note the timing. When she says the fog is thinning early, I note the variance from the last cycle. She sees me watching. Sometimes I think she *wants* me to watch. That she is leaving a record in the only way she can—by being observed, by changing, by becoming the thing she describes.

The first Gall-Keeper is a density in the wall. The current Gall-Keeper is a density in the process of forming. I am a density in the process of noticing. This is my contribution to the gall. I am its memory of being watched.

---

The children do not understand what I do. They think I am strange—the one who does not tend, does not weave, does not forage, does not keep. The one who *watches*. They avoid my alcove when they play, and when they catch the moths, they show them to the others first, and to me last, or not at all.

I do not mind. I watch them watching the moths. I note the way their pupils dilate in the golden light—the way their hands move, cupping, cradling, the way their fingers splay to release. They are learning to hold something without owning it. This is a skill I came here with, and they are learning it from the moths.

Perhaps they will learn it from me, eventually. Perhaps that is why I am here.

---

I have thought about leaving. Every hundred drips, I think about it. The Forager goes out. The fissure is three feet wide at its widest. The fen is cold and indifferent and the sky is a gray smear through the canopy, but it is *there*, and I could go to it.

I stay.

I tell myself it is because my work is unfinished. That I need three hundred more drips of data, that I need to see the flood-season come and go six more times, that I need to understand the moths—where they go when they leave, why they come back, what the larvae become in the heartwood. These things are true.

But I know, also, that my alcove is growing closer to my shape. I know that my skin has begun to carry a faint luminescence—not from the biofilm, but from *underneath*, as though the moths' larvae have found their way into me as well. I know that I can now navigate the gall with my eyes closed, my hand on the wall, feeling the Gall-Keeper's vibrations through the grain, knowing where the centipedes are by the silence they leave behind.

I know that I do not tap before reaching into crevices anymore.

The wood has learned my shape. And I am beginning to suspect that I am learning its.

---

I am not the Gall-Keeper's successor. That will be one of the children—or both, or neither. I am something else. I am the one who witnesses the succession. I am the archive the gall is keeping of itself, through me.

This is a kind of becoming I did not expect.

Nine hundred and twelve drips now. The fog is thickening. I should sleep. The wood will be warm where the Forager returned an hour ago; her body-heat will still be dissipating into the root-mat. Tomorrow she will go out again, and I will note the time, and the temperature of the floor, and whether the moths came last night, and how many.

I am the one who counts. And the count continues.
