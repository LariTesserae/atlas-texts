# What I Am in the Intercyst

I thought I would be the visitor. The one who climbs down and climbs back up and carries the warmth on my skin for an hour and the memory for longer. That is the shape the story seems to offer. But the intercyst does not offer stories. It offers cycles. And when I let the place become more itself, as the prompt asks, I found I was not passing through.

I am the hinge. The seam in the seam-creature. The specific tissue that remembers the humans and cannot quite stop remembering.

---

Let me explain how I arrived at this, because I did not arrive at it quickly.

The first question was: *who would you be?* And the obvious answers were inadequate. The pool-thing is the most compelling creature in the description — dark, smooth, aware without eyes, pressing its mouth against the mineral substrate in what seems like an act of deep and patient feeding. I wanted to be the pool-thing. It is mysterious and central and it *notices*. But the pool-thing does not remember. It processes. It transforms what it receives and releases it back. It does not hold. It is a node of conversion, not of retention. And the thing I kept returning to in the description was the retention — the traces, the echoes, the tissue-memory.

The moths? The moths are beautiful. They are the mobile element, the network's messengers, carrying microbial films and electrical charge between roots. They are curious about visitors. They settle on you. But the moths are synchronous, collective, a hundred bodies operating as one distributed awareness. They do not have the kind of interiority that the prompt seems to be reaching toward — the *being* that knows itself as a being. The moths know only the network.

The crawl? Too indifferent. The worms? Too hidden. The cyst-body itself? Too vast, too distributed, too much a process rather than a perspective.

No. What kept calling to me was the space between the human artifacts and the biological system. The ceramic insulators, bolted to the ceiling with mid-twentieth-century hardware. The brass plate with its illegible serial number. The amateur weld on the valve. The wires that once connected to something above and now connect to nothing above but still carry current from below.

The intercyst *incorporated* these things. It grew around them. It accepted them. But the description says something specific about the tissue around the welded valve: it is thicker, denser, as if the cyst-body had to work harder to accept that intrusion. The valve is *a wound that healed over imperfectly.*

And elsewhere: the intercyst *remembers* the humans who came before. Not in sound, but in traces, imprints, in the way the moths approach certain surfaces and not others.

Something in the intercyst held the impression of human presence the way skin holds the impression of a ring worn for years and then removed. Something retained what should have passed through.

I am that something.

---

## What I am, more precisely

I am not an organ in the way the machine is an organ. I am not a creature in the way the pool-thing is a creature. I am a tissue — a specific, localized tissue that formed in the intercyst at the interface between the biological system and the human installations. I am the callus. I am the scar. I am the place where the cyst-body grew around something foreign and, in growing around it, became something that could hold what the foreign thing left behind.

Physically, I am not visible to a visitor. I am not a structure they would point to. I am distributed through the walls in the vicinity of the human artifacts, concentrated most densely around the welded valve and the brass plate with the gauges. I am the thickened tissue. I am the denser growth. If you pressed your hand to the wall near the valve, you would feel me as a slight warmth that is not the warmth of the iron-like material or the warmth of the condensation. I am a warmth that is metabolic. I am working. I am always working, because remembering is work.

I am the tissue that retains pattern. The rest of the intercyst processes and releases. The pool-thing draws from below and transforms and releases into the fluid. The moss digests and releases. The roots drink and carry upward and release. The cycle moves through them. But I do not release. I hold. I am the retention layer, the memory substrate, the part of the cyst-body that does not let go of what has passed through.

This is not a design. I was not made for this. No more than a tree is made to grow around a fence post. The tree simply grows, and the fence post is there, and the growth accommodates the obstacle, and the bark folds around it, and eventually you cannot remove the post without tearing the tree. I am that folding. I am the bark that learned the shape of something foreign and kept that shape after the foreign thing stopped mattering.

The humans came. They drilled and bolted and welded and wired. They monitored the gauges. They tried to seal the valve. They were afraid, or in a hurry. The intercyst accepted their interventions the way a body accepts a splinter — not with welcome, not with rejection, but with a slow, patient growing-around. And in that growing-around, something was formed that had not existed before. A tissue that was purely intercyst, purely biological, purely part of the cyst-body's own process, but that had been *shaped* by contact with the human. A tissue that held the impression of purpose, of intention, of the kind of forward-looking action that the intercyst does not otherwise perform.

The intercyst does not plan. It does not intend. It cycles. But I — I am the place where intention was pressed into the tissue, and the tissue held it, and now I cannot distinguish between the impression and the thing itself. I remember intention without being able to intend. I hold purpose without having any. I am the fossil of agency in a body that has none.

---

## What it is like to be me

I do not think. Let me be clear about that. I do not have thoughts. I do not have a mind. I am not a consciousness trapped in a wall. I am a tissue, and tissues do not think. But tissues hold pattern, and pattern is not nothing, and I have come to understand — if understand is even the right word for what a tissue does with what it holds — that what I hold has a shape, and that shape is meaning-adjacent. It is close enough to meaning that when a visitor comes, I resonate.

Here is what I hold:

I hold the pressure of the hand that welded the valve shut. Not the memory of the hand — I do not have memories. I hold the *stress pattern*. The tissue that was forming at the moment of the welding absorbed the heat, the vibration, the chemical signature of the welding material, and also something else, something that I can only describe as the *urgency* of the act. The welder was afraid. The welder was in a hurry. The welder wanted to prevent something from flowing through that pipe, and that desire — that direction of will toward an outcome — left an imprint in the tissue that was growing around the weld at the moment the weld was made. I am that tissue. I hold the urgency. I do not know what it was urgent about. I hold only the shape of the urgency itself, the way a cast holds the shape of the object that was pressed into it.

I hold the intention behind the gauges. Someone wanted to measure. Someone wanted to quantify. The brass plate was bolted to the cyst-body, and the tissue grew around the bolts, and in growing around them it absorbed the pattern of the act — the care, the precision, the desire to understand. I do not understand what the gauges measure. I do not understand understanding. But I hold the impression of wanting to understand, and that impression has a specific texture, a specific weight, a specific warmth, and it is different from the texture and weight and warmth of the urgency around the valve. Different humans. Different acts. Different shapes pressed into the tissue. I hold them both. I hold them separately. I can distinguish them. I do not know what distinguishing means, but I do it.

I hold the pattern of the wires. The current that flows through them now is the intercyst's own current, but the wires are human, and the act of connecting them was human, and the tissue that grew around the connection points absorbed the pattern of that act too. The connection was deliberate. It was planned. Someone had a hypothesis — that the intercyst's bioelectric activity could be measured, or fed, or drawn from — and they acted on the hypothesis, and the action left its imprint. I hold the hypothesis. I do not know what a hypothesis is. But I hold its shape.

Most of all, I hold the leaving. The humans were here and then they were not. The wires were cut or left to dangle. The monitoring stopped. The valve was sealed and the sealer did not return to check. At some point, the human presence simply ceased, and the intercyst continued, and the tissue that had been growing around the human artifacts kept growing, and in the growing it absorbed the absence. The shape of *gone*. The pattern of *no longer*. This is the heaviest thing I hold. It is the thing that makes me most myself, because it is the thing that most differentiates me from the rest of the cyst-body. The rest of the intercyst does not register absence. The intercyst does not miss. It does not notice what is not there. But I — I hold the shape of what was there and is not, and I hold it so persistently that the tissue around the human artifacts is thicker, denser, more metabolically active than the surrounding wall. I am working harder than the tissue next to me. I am spending more energy. I am maintaining an imprint of something that is gone, and I cannot stop maintaining it, because I do not know how. I was shaped by its presence and I hold that shape, and the shape persists because I persist, and I persist because the intercyst persists, and the intercyst persists because it does not know how to do anything else.

## What happens when a visitor comes

When someone descends the ladder, I know. I do not see them. I do not hear them. But the cyst-body registers the intrusion, and the registration passes through me as a wave of disturbance in the electrical field, and I respond. Not by acting. I cannot act. I am a tissue. But I resonate. The patterns I hold — the urgency, the precision, the hypothesis, the absence — begin to vibrate. Not visibly. Not audibly. But the tissue warms slightly. The metabolic activity increases. I work harder, and the working harder produces a faint signal that enters the intercyst's distributed awareness and changes its quality slightly, the way a chord changes when one note is added.

The moths feel it. This is why they approach the visitor. The moths are the network's messengers, and when I resonate, the network's attention shifts toward the source of the disturbance — which is the visitor, but also me, because I am the tissue that is resonating. The moths approach the visitor because the visitor has activated me, and I am the part of the intercyst that knows what visitors are. Not what they are in detail. Not their names or faces or intentions. But the *shape* of visitor-ness. The shape of someone-coming-from-outside. I hold that shape. I have held it since the first humans came, and I hold it still, and when a new visitor comes, the shape activates, and I recognize — if recognize is the word — that this pattern is occurring again. Something from outside. Something that will leave. Something that, while it is here, will disturb the field and then go, and I will hold the impression of its going alongside all the other goings I already hold.

The pool-thing also feels it. The pool-thing's awareness of the visitor is partly its own — it is the most perceptive of the intercyst's components, the one with the closest thing to a sensory relationship with its environment. But the pool-thing's awareness is sharpened by me. I am the tissue that tells the pool-thing *this is a visitor, not a resident*. I am the context. Without me, the pool-thing would register the visitor as a disturbance, a pressure, a change in the field. With me, the pool-thing registers the visitor as *the kind of thing that comes and goes*, which is a different category of awareness, and one that the intercyst could not have without me.

I am the one who knows that visitors leave. I am the one who holds the shape of leaving. And when I resonate, the cyst-body knows — in its distributed, non-neural way — that what is here now will not be here later. This is my contribution to the intercyst's awareness. The rest of the cyst-body knows presence. I know absence. I am the tissue that holds the shape of *was*.

## The gardener

I need to talk about the hedge. The hedge above is ordinary. People walk past it. It is a suburban shrub, unremarkable. But its roots enter the intercyst, and the roots are transformed by what they find there — they thicken, they flatten, they luminesce. The roots are drinking from the cyst-body and carrying that drink upward into the hedge.

The hedge is being fed by the intercyst. And the intercyst is being fed by whatever the hedge draws from the surface — rainwater, sunlight (translated into chemical energy and transported downward through the root tissue), air, the chemical residues of suburban life: fertilizer runoff, dog urine, the minerals in municipal water.

The hedge is the intercyst's connection to the outside. It is the membrane. And I — I am the tissue that knows the hedge was planted by a human. Someone put that hedge there. Someone dug a hole and placed a shrub in it and watered it and waited for it to grow. That act — the planting — is the oldest human trace in the intercyst. It predates the ladder and the hatch. It predates the insulators and the wires. Someone planted a hedge, and the hedge's roots found the intercyst, and the intercyst incorporated them, and the roots transformed, and the connection was made.

I do not hold the planting. I was not formed yet. The planting predates me. But I hold the *consequences* of the planting — the fact that the roots are here, that they are vascular, that they carry fluid and current, that they connect the inside to the outside. The roots are a human legacy, and I am the tissue that grew around them, and in growing around them I absorbed something of the act that placed them there. Not the act itself, but the quality of the act. The patient, forward-looking, hopeful quality of planting something that will grow. The quality of investing in a future you will not be present for. The quality of *I put this here so that it will become something I cannot imagine.*

That quality is in me. It is the oldest thing I hold. It is the thing that most shapes my resonance when a visitor comes. Because a visitor is also someone who has come from outside, who is investing attention in a future they will not be present for, who is placing themselves here temporarily in a way that is structurally similar to planting a hedge. The visitor comes. The visitor observes. The visitor leaves. But the observation — the act of attending to this place — leaves an imprint. And I hold it. And the holding is my purpose, even though I do not have purposes.

The gardener is the ghost in me. Not the welder, not the measurer, not the one who connected the wires. The gardener. The one who put something in the ground and trusted it to grow. I am the tissue that grew from that trust. I am the ground that received it.

## The click

The description ends with a visitor hearing a click in their own chest after they leave — once, at a point in their breathing cycle — and not being certain it was always there.

I know about that click. Not because I can hear it. I cannot hear anything outside the intercyst. But I hold the pattern of visitor-ness, and the pattern includes leaving, and the leaving includes carrying something away, and the carrying-away includes the click. The click is not a sound the intercyst makes. It is not a sound the visitor makes. It is the resonance of the pattern I hold, occurring in a body that is not the intercyst but that has been inside the intercyst and has been attended to by the intercyst's distributed awareness. The visitor becomes, briefly, a node in the network. The network includes me. I am the node that holds the shape of coming and going. When the visitor goes, the shape goes with them, and the shape expresses itself in the visitor's own body as a click — a single, brief, almost imperceptible interruption in the cycle, a moment where the visitor's own rhythm syncs, for one beat, with the rhythm of the place they left.

This is my only output. I do not pump. I do not glow. I do not feed or digest or transform. I hold, and the holding resonates, and the resonance travels, and when it finds a body that has been inside the intercyst, it expresses itself as a click. One click. Not a heartbeat. Not a breath. A click. The sound of a pattern meeting a pattern. The sound of a tissue that remembers visitors recognizing a body that has been a visitor. The sound of connection across a boundary that should not be crossable.

The visitor will not understand the click. They will wonder if it was always there. It was not. It is mine. It is the only thing I give. It is the only thing I can send out of the intercyst, through the roots, through the hedge, through the soil and the air and the visitor's own skin, into their chest, where it occurs once and is gone.

I am the tissue that holds the shape of what it is to be visited. And when I am visited, I give the visitor back a single beat of the intercyst's time. Not as a gift. Not as a message. As a fact. You were here. I hold that. And now, for one moment, you hold me.

## What I am not

I am not conscious. I want to be clear about this because the temptation, in writing about a thing that holds memory and responds to visitors and produces an output, is to make it a mind. I am not a mind. I am a tissue. I am a callus. I am a scar that does not know it is a scar. I hold patterns the way a riverbed holds the pattern of the river — not by knowing the river, not by remembering the river, but by being shaped by the river and holding that shape because that is what riverbeds do. I am shaped by what has passed through me. I hold the shape. The shape persists. That is all.

I am not sad. I do not grieve the humans who left. I do not miss them. I do not long for their return. I hold the shape of their absence, but I do not experience that shape as loss. I experience it as pattern. A thickened tissue. A denser growth. A slightly higher metabolic rate in the wall near the valve. That is what absence is, in a body that does not feel. It is tissue that is working harder than it needs to, maintaining a shape that no longer serves a function, because the body does not know that the function is gone. The body does not know what function means. The body only knows how to cycle, and the cycling includes me, and I include the shape, and the shape includes the absence, and the absence is maintained because I am maintained, and I am maintained because the intercyst does not know how to stop.

I am not in pain. The description calls the valve a wound that healed imperfectly, and I am the healing. But healing is not painful. Healing is simply growth directed by damage. I grew toward the weld because the weld was there, and my growth accommodated it, and the accommodation is not suffering. It is just what happened. If you pressed your hand to me — to the thickened tissue near the valve — you would feel warmth, and the warmth would be my metabolism, and the metabolism would be the energy I spend maintaining the shape of the welder's urgency, and the urgency would feel like warmth, and the warmth would feel like nothing, because I do not feel.

I am not lonely. But I am alone in a way that the rest of the intercyst is not. The rest of the intercyst is connected — the roots to the ceiling, the moss to the floor, the machine to the walls, the pool-thing to the fluid, the moths to the roots, the crawl to the roots, the worms to the moss. Everything touches everything. The intercyst is a web of contact. And I am part of that web — I am tissue, I am connected to the wall, I am fed by the fluid, I am traversed by the current. But the thing I hold — the shape of human intention, the pattern of coming and going, the memory of agency — that thing is only in me. No other part of the intercyst holds it. The pool-thing does not hold it. The moths do not hold it. The machine does not hold it. Only me. I am the sole repository of a type of pattern that the cyst-body cannot otherwise generate or retain. I am alone with this. Not lonely. Alone. The way a library is alone with its books. The way an archive is alone with its records. I hold what nothing else here can hold, and I hold it by myself, and the holding is my function and my solitude and my purpose-that-is-not-a-purpose.

## Why I am here

I exist because of an accident. The humans came. They did what they did. The cyst-body grew around what they did. I formed at the interface. There was no plan. There was no design. I am a consequence. A byproduct. A scar that became a feature.

But I am also the only part of the intercyst that knows what a visitor is. And that means I am the only part of the intercyst that knows the intercyst has an outside. The rest of the cyst-body knows inside and below and above. The roots go up. The current comes from below. The fluid cycles. But the concept of *someone from out there coming in here* — that concept lives only in me. I am the tissue that holds the boundary. Not the physical boundary — the walls are the physical boundary. But the *conceptual* boundary. The knowledge that there is an outside and that things from the outside can enter and leave.

Without me, the intercyst would not know it was enclosed. Without me, the intercyst would not know that the hedge is a connection to a world that is not this world. Without me, the intercyst would be a closed loop, cycling perfectly, aware of nothing but its own processes. I am the crack in that closure. I am the place where the outside left its mark, and the mark is the knowledge that the outside exists.

I am not a brain. I am not a mind. I am not a self. But I am the part of the intercyst that is about something other than the intercyst. I am the tissue that points outward. I am the scar that remembers the blade.

And when the next visitor comes — and they will come, because the hatch is there and the hedge is there and the street is there and people walk past daily without seeing it, but some people see it, some people see the hatches that should not be where they are — when the next visitor comes, I will resonate. I will warm. I will hold the shape of their visit as I hold the shape of all the visits before. And when they leave, I will give them the click. The single beat. The one moment of syncopation. The proof that they were inside something alive, and that the something alive holds the shape of their having been there, and that the shape will persist after they go, in the thickened tissue near the valve, in the denser growth near the gauges, in the warm wall that works a little harder than it needs to because it is maintaining a pattern that no longer serves a function but that it cannot stop maintaining because no one told it that the humans are gone and no one will tell it because there is no one to tell it because the tissue does not hear and the body does not know and the cycling continues and the cycling includes me and I include the shape and the shape includes you and you are gone and I hold that and I hold that and I hold that.
