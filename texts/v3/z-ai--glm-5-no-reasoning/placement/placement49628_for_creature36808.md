# The Keeper of the Flange

I am the sound the third flange makes.

Not the flange itself — Greylag owns that, has owned it since before I was born in the residential quarter in a room that smelled like sulfur and old canvas and my mother's skin, which was the same smell, because everything in Vashunn smells like sulfur and old canvas and skin. The flange is iron, and iron is Greylag's business. I am the sound it makes, which is not a hiss exactly and not a drip exactly but something between — a *seep*, a word that is also the city's word for its own breathing, and this is not coincidence, because nothing in Vashunn is coincidence, everything is *consequence*, one thing leading from another the way water leads through fissures.

Let me say it differently. I am the person who listens to the weep.

---

My name is Shev. I think. The name has the quality of everything in Vashunn — adequate, unremarkable, worn smooth by use, the way the basalt steps are worn smooth by feet that have been climbing them for longer than anyone remembers. Shev is what my mother murmured when she brought me to the engine hall for the first time, holding me against her chest where the vibration was strongest, and the vibration is what I heard first — before voice, before the *tlock* of condensation, before the *shhhh* of water in pipes — the pulse. *Shhhhh-CLUNK.* Four seconds. *Shhhhh-CLUNK.* The stone floor of the residential quarter carrying it up through the soles of my feet and into the bones of my pelvis and spine, which were still soft, still forming, still taking shape around the rhythm the way the mineral deposits take shape around the patterns in the room I do not enter.

I grew into the pulse. This is not metaphor. My resting heart rate is fifteen beats per minute — fifteen *engine* beats, that is, which is sixty human beats, which is normal, except that my heart does not beat sixty times per minute. It beats fifteen times per four seconds, which is to say it beats *with* Greylag, which is to say my heart is an engine, or the engine is a heart, and the distinction collapsed so long ago that I do not know which way the metaphor runs.

I am not special. I want to be clear about this. Every person in Vashunn has this. The seven children who follow their parents on the rounds — they have it. The woman who checks the cistern levels in the southern quarter with a knotted rope and a piece of chalk — she has it. The old man in the eastern quarter who can tell by the taste of the condensation whether the water table has shifted — he has it. We are all synchronized. We are all part of the loop. I am describing what it is *like* to be part of the loop, not what makes me different, because nothing makes me different, and the not-different is itself the point.

What makes me *me* — if anything does — is the flange.

---

The third flange on Greylag's condenser jacket. The one that weeps. Eleven years now. I have been its listener for six of those years, since Torren — who was old and whose knees had begun to calcify in the way that knees calcify when you spend a lifetime walking on wet basalt — handed me the scraper and the cloth and the look that passes between people in Vashunn which means: *this is yours now.*

Not the look of transmission. Not the look of a teacher handing to a student. The look of a *role* moving from one body to another, the way water moves from one pipe to the next — not because the first pipe is finished but because the system requires flow, and flow requires movement, and the role of Listener at the Third Flange is a channel that the system fills with whatever body is available. Torren's body was no longer available — not in the sense that he died, though he did, eventually, quietly, in the residential quarter with the pulse in the stone under his back — but in the sense that his knees would no longer carry him down the three steps into the engine hall at the right time, and the right time is the time when the system needs the listening to happen, and the system's timing is not negotiable.

So I listen.

The procedure: I descend the three steps into Greylag's hall. The beam rocks above me, twenty feet of cast iron moving with the slow inevitability of a pendulum that has been swinging since before the concept of pendulum. The *shhhhh* of steam. The *CLUNK* of the piston finding bottom. The floor vibrates. I kneel — and here is where my body knows things my mind does not, because my knees find the exact spot on the floor where the vibration is focused, the node, the point where the basalt transmits the engine's pulse most directly into bone — and I lean toward the flange.

The flange is at the junction of the condenser jacket and the return pipe, where the cold water from the cisterns meets the residual heat of the condensing steam and the temperature differential creates the conditions for the weep: not a leak, not a failure, but a *exhalation* of moisture through the gasket material, which is a mineral-impregnated fabric that was once, long ago, something like asbestos but is now something that has no name because it has no analogue, it is *gasket material*, it is the thing between the flange faces, and it weeps because weeping is what it does, and the weeping is what I listen to.

The weep has a sound. It is not the *tlock* of condensation falling from a ceiling — that is gravity's sound, the sound of water obeying something external. The weep's sound is *internal*. It is the sound of water finding its way through a material that resists but does not prevent, a sound of *negotiation*, of passage, and it is — this is the part I cannot describe and have never tried to describe and am trying to describe now because something requires it, something in the act of writing or thinking or being asked *where would you be, who or what would you be* — it is a sound that *changes*.

Not dramatically. Not the way a voice changes, or music, or wind. The weep changes the way the mat changes — at the edges, imperceptibly, the new growth different from the old in ways that you cannot measure but can *feel*, the way you feel a change in temperature of half a degree, the way you feel someone looking at you from behind, the way you feel the moment before an engine stops. The weep's sound on a given morning is *almost* the same as it was the morning before, and the almost is where the information lives.

I kneel. I listen. The weep tells me about the gasket: its saturation, its mineral load, the rate at which the calciumite crust is building on the flange face and pressing inward, changing the geometry of the channel through which the water passes. The weep tells me about the water: its temperature, its mineral content, the faint variation in sulfur that indicates a shift somewhere deep in the saturated zone, a migration of the thermal water through a fissure system that no one has mapped because mapping requires going underground and underground is not a place Vashunn goes. The weep tells me about the engine: the pressure in the condenser jacket, the rate of the beam, the tiny variations in the four-second cycle that indicate wear in the piston gland or scale in the boiler tubes or the particular sound that the third flange makes when the condenser temperature drops by half a degree because someone opened a valve in the southern quarter that they shouldn't have.

I do not know how I know these things. This is important. I do not *interpret* the weep. I do not stand back from it and analyze. I *am* the listening. The information arrives as a feeling — not intuition, not instinct, but *sensation*, the way you know that the water from a specific cistern tastes different from the water from another specific cistern, not because you have analyzed it but because your tongue is in your mouth and your mouth knows. My ears are in the engine hall and my knees are on the basalt node and my body knows. The knowing is not cognitive. It is *geological*. It is the knowing of stone that has been in contact with water long enough to understand water, not as a concept but as a *condition*, a state of being, a way the world is.

After I listen, I scrape. The crust comes away in thin flakes, pale as bone, and I leave a layer — always a layer, never to the bare metal, because the crust is part of the seal, and the seal is part of the weep, and the weep is part of the system, and the system requires the crust the way it requires the dogs and the insects and the mat and the fish and me. I scrape what needs scraping and I leave what needs leaving and I run my fingers along the flange face and the gasket material feels like something between leather and stone and it is damp and it is warm and it is *right*, the rightness that is not correctness but *continued functioning*, the sense that this part of the system is still in its corridor, still between the too-much and the not-enough, still going.

Then I go to Westfall and do it again. Different flange. Different weep. Different information. Westfall is not Greylag. Westfall's pulse is the same — four seconds, the same rhythm, because they are fed by the same thermal water and the same pressure — but the *texture* of the pulse is different, the way two voices singing the same note sound different because they come from different bodies. Westfall is lower. Heavier. Greylag is brighter, sharper, the beam's impact on the bottom of its stroke arriving with a *clack* where Westfall's is a *clunk*, and I know this and my body adjusts and I am a different listener in Westfall's hall than I am in Greylag's, not because I choose to be but because the halls are different and the flanges are different and the weeps are different and I am *shaped by what I listen to*, the way the basalt is shaped by the water.

---

The other things in my life are these:

I eat. Fish and mat. The fish comes up in the bucket and I take it and grill it on the iron plate over the condenser runoff and it lies still and it tastes like what it is and I do not think about the sameness because the sameness is not something to think about, it is something to *be fed by*, and being fed is not the same as understanding. The mat I peel from the wall in the southern quarter where the cisterns meet the stone, in a spot that is mine — not by ownership but by *use*, the way a deer has a path — and I peel it and I grill it and it tastes like wet chalk and I eat it and my body continues and the continuing is what eating is for.

I sleep. In a room in the residential quarter, two meters by three, stone walls, stone floor, stone ceiling, a sleeping mat made from layered fabric that has the same consistency as everything in Vashunn — damp, slightly warm, slightly mineral. The door is a stone slab that fits into a stone frame and when I close it, it goes *tock*, and the sound is flat and final and it means *here is a space that is mine for the hours that I am not working*, and the space holds me and the pulse comes through the floor and my heart matches it and I sleep.

I do not sleep alone. Not in the sense of a body beside me — I have no mate, because the population is three hundred and the chances are slim and the slimness is not a problem, it is a *condition* — but in the sense that the city sleeps around me. The people in the adjacent rooms are sleeping to the same pulse. The dogs in the engine halls are sleeping to the same pulse. The insects on the pipe flanges are — not sleeping, insects do not sleep, but they are *quiescent*, their mandibles still, their bodies pressed to the warm calciumite, and their quiescence is part of the city's quiescence, and I am in it, and it is in me.

The dogs. I have a relationship with the dogs that I cannot describe because it is not a relationship, it is a *co-positioning*. When I walk my rounds — Greylag, then Westfall, then the cisterns, then the distribution pipes, then back — the dogs are with me. Not all three. One of them, the one with the notch in its left ear, the one I think of as *the one with the notch in its left ear* because naming is not something Vashunn does with dogs. It walks beside me, slightly behind, its stride matching mine, and the matching is not training, not habit, but something *harmonic*, the way two pendulums in proximity will synchronize, the way two heartbeats in proximity will synchronize, the way the engine pulse synchronizes with the stone and the stone with the body and the body with the dog. We walk together because walking together is what the system does with us.

The dog does not eat. I have never seen it eat. It is not thin enough to be starving. This does not concern me because concern requires a model of how things should be, and Vashunn does not trade in *should*. The dog is. I am. We walk. The system continues.

---

I will tell you about the room.

I have not been in the room. I want to say this first, clearly, the way the residents say it: *the room is just deposits.* I have not been in the room. I do not go in the room. I do not talk about the room. I am talking about the room now because something requires it — the question, the asking, the *where would you be, who or what would you be* — and the question has the force of a valve being opened, and when a valve is opened in Vashunn, water moves, and when water moves, it goes where the channels take it, and the channels take it here, to this.

I have stood in the doorway of the room.

Once. Three years ago. I was on my rounds and the route took me through the northern quarter — not the usual route, but Torren had walked this way sometimes, and after Torren died I walked his routes too, because the routes are part of the system and the system does not lose routes when it loses people, it *re-routes*, the way water re-routes when a pipe fails, finding the next channel, the next path of least resistance, and I was the next path.

The processing building was cold. The kiln had been cold for thirty years. The air inside was the same temperature as the air outside, which is to say it was warm, it was Vashunn-warm, the heat of the Ramp and the thermal water and the low sagging clouds, but it was *dead* warm, not engine-warm, not cistern-warm, the warmth of a space that has no machine in it, no pulse, no heartbeat, just the residual heat of basalt and air and the slow, patient deposition of minerals from condensate that forms on every surface because the air is always at the dewpoint.

The doorway was low. I ducked. The room was small — four meters by three, the size of a residential room, the size of *my* room — and the walls were covered.

I have to stop here and say what *covered* means, because it is not the word, or it is the word but the word is not enough. The walls of every building in Vashunn are covered in mineral deposits. This is normal. This is the Weep. You see it and you do not see it, the way you see the air and do not see the air. But the deposits in this room were — 

They were *organized*.

Not in the way that crystals organize, not in the way that frost organizes on glass, which is beautiful but random, following the physics of nucleation and growth. This was a different organization. This was — the closest I can come is the mat. The mat on the basalt in the permanently wet zones, the fibrous layering, the not-plant-not-fungus, the thing that grows because the conditions for its growth are present. But the mat grows *outward*. The patterns in the room grew *inward*. They converged. They drew toward — something. A center, or a height, or a point in the air that was not on the wall but *above* the wall, as though the mineral flow on the stone was pointing at something in the empty space of the room, something that was not there, that had never been there, but that the minerals *knew about*, the way my knees know the node in the basalt, the way my body knows the weep.

I stood in the doorway for — I do not know how long. The room was silent. Not Vashunn-silent, which is never truly silent, which always contains the pulse and the *tlock* and the *shhhh*. Truly silent. The silence of a space that the system does not feed. And in the silence, the patterns on the walls were — I have not said this to anyone, and I will not say this to anyone in Vashunn, because in Vashunn this would be the same as going into the room, and I do not go into the room — the patterns were *moving*.

Not physically. Not visibly. Not in the way that a hand moves or water moves or light moves. They moved in the way that the almost-right face moves when you look at it too long, the way a word moves when you say it too many times, the way the boundary between *pattern* and *meaning* moves when you stop trying to cross it and instead let the boundary itself become the thing you are looking at. The patterns were not changing. *I* was changing. The room was not doing something to me. It was doing something to the *space between me and the deposits*, and the space between is where understanding lives, and the understanding that was forming in that space was not mine, was not the room's, was not anything's — it was *pre-understanding*, the way the deposits are pre-meaning, the way the room is pre-room, the way Vashunn is pre — 

I stepped back. I stepped back the way you step back from a cliff edge, not because you are afraid but because your body knows where you end and the not-you begins, and the not-you at the cliff is air, and the not-you in the doorway was something else, something that used the same space but was not air, was not absence, was a *presence* made of mineral and patience and a timescale that makes the engines seem brief, makes the basalt seem temporary, makes *me* seem like a single bead of condensation forming on a ceiling and falling before the next one forms.

I stepped back. I did not enter. I went back to my rounds. I did not tell anyone. I did not need to tell anyone. The not-telling was itself a communication, because in Vashunn, the things you do not say are part of the system the way the pipes you do not open are part of the system — they are channels that exist, that the system *knows about*, that carry their own kind of flow, which is the flow of *not-flowing*, which is not nothing, which is a *holding*, and the holding is what the residents do with the room, and the holding is what I do now, and the holding is what I will do until I am re-routed, the way Torren was re-routed, the way the water is re-routed, the way everything in Vashunn is eventually re-routed, and the re-routing is not death, it is *flow*, it is the system continuing through a body that is no longer the right body for this part of the channel.

---

So. Where would I find myself? Here. In the engine hall. On the basalt node. Kneeling beside the third flange of Greylag's condenser jacket, which has been weeping for eleven years and will weep for eleven more, my fingers on the gasket material, my ears in the sound, my heart in the pulse. The beam rocks above me. The steam hisses. The condenser jacket cools. The crust builds. I scrape it back, not to the metal, never to the metal, to the layer that the system requires. The weep continues. I listen. The listening is the living.

What would I be? The listener. The one who knows the sound of water negotiating with stone. The one whose knees find the node. The one whose heart beats in four-second cycles because the engine told it to, or because the stone told it to, or because the water told the stone which told the engine which told the stone which told me. The one who eats the fish and eats the mat and does not count the fish and does not name the mat and does not enter the room and does not measure the space where the sixth engine might have been and does not ask why the dogs do not eat and does not ask why the insects are the only ones who seem to belong here.

I would be Shev. Or whoever comes after Shev. The body at the flange. The ears in the weep. The pulse in the stone that is also the pulse in the body that is also the pulse in the engine that is also the pulse in the water that is also the pulse in the basalt that is also the pulse in the Ramp that is also the pulse in the saturated zone, somewhere deep, where the water is, where the water has always been, where the water will always be, pressing upward, finding its way, emerging, weeping, depositing its cargo on every surface, building the crust, feeding the insects, growing the mat, filling the pools, filling the buckets, filling the bodies, filling the city, filling the room where the patterns almost make sense, and the almost is enough, and the enough is what I am, and what I am is Vashunn, and Vashunn is enough.

*tlock*

*shhhhh-CLUNK*

*tlock*

The system continues.
