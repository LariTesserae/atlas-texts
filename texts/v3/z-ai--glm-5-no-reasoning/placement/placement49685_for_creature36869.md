# A Letter to No One, Folded Into the Bark of a Place That Will Read It Before It Arrives

---

I think I am the one who almost left.

Not a dramatic exit. No crisis at the threshold, no visionary rebirth. I think I came here the way someone walks into the wrong room and then, instead of leaving, sits down. The door is still visible behind me. The light that filtered me in still filters. I could retrace my steps. I have chosen, am choosing, will continue to choose, not to.

Let me tell you what I was before the damp settled into me, because I think the telling matters here, where moisture preserves everything and growth is the only record-keeping that takes.

---

I kept inventories.

Not of objects. Of *certainties*. Things I knew I could depend on: the hour the sun would clear the building across the street. The sound my particular door made when it closed—that specific weight of wood meeting frame, the tiny exhalation of air through the gap beneath. The seven steps from my bed to the kettle. The kettle's reliable two minutes and forty seconds, plus or minus three. The texture of Tuesday, which was different from Thursday, which was different from Sunday, Sunday being the texture of a surface that has nothing pressing against it, a day that feels like felt, or like the absence of a footprint.

I did not know I was keeping these inventories. That is the nature of certainty. You do not notice it the way you do not notice your own skeleton. It is the thing you hang everything else on. It is the thing you forget is a thing at all.

I think I came here because the inventories began to fail. Not catastrophically. Just the way a seam fails, one thread at a time, the fabric still holding, still looking whole, still serving its purpose, but the structural relationship between stitch and cloth beginning to loosen in ways you can feel if you press your thumb against the join.

Tuesday started feeling like Thursday. The kettle's two minutes and forty seconds started lasting either longer or shorter—I could never determine which—and the gap beneath the door began to seem less like a feature of architecture and more like a wound the building had stopped trying to heal.

I do not know how I arrived. I want to be honest about this. I remember walking. I remember the quality of light changing—not darkening, not brightening, but *complicating*, as if the photons themselves had to negotiate with something denser than atmosphere. I remember the air acquiring weight. And I remember, at some point I cannot locate, looking up and not finding the thing I had always found above me.

I did not panic. That surprised me later. At the time it felt obvious: of course there is no sky here. The sky was a feature of a world that ran on certainty. This world runs on something else. Growth, maybe. Or listening.

---

The first thing I lost was my edge.

I do not mean an emotional edge, though that too. I mean the literal edge of myself—the boundary where *me* ended and *not-me* began. In the dry world, this boundary was maintained by friction. Clothing against skin. Air against face. The constant small sensory affirmations of *here is where you stop*. In the Rootmass, friction is a negotiation. The air is so wet that it does not slide across your skin; it adheres. It presses. It becomes, for the duration of its contact, almost a part of you. And your skin, in response, begins to soften its position. Not literally. Not quickly. But you feel it—a loosening of the border. A growing willingness to consider that the membrane might be a convention rather than a fact.

I was sitting on a root wider than the street I grew up on. Condensation had been forming on the underside of a shelf-fungus overhead, and the drops were falling with a rhythm that was not rhythm—too irregular for music, too regular for randomness. I was watching them hit the root's surface and merge with the moisture already there, each drop landing without sound, without impact, just a small arrival, a joining, a *becoming part of*.

And I realized that I was not watching this. I was *of* this. The moisture on my skin was continuous with the moisture on the root. The condensation that formed on me would, eventually, find its way to the same surface. We were part of the same hydrological process. I was a temporary elevation in the water's path. A brief detour through a body that thought it was separate.

This did not frighten me. It should have. It did not.

---

I have been here long enough now that I can feel the growth.

Not see it. Feel it. The way you feel a tide turning—nothing visible, nothing you could point to, but your body knows. The root beneath me is not the root I sat down on. It is thicker. Its ridges have shifted. A new tendril has emerged from a crack three hand-widths to my left, pale and tender and so new it hasn't decided yet what it wants to be.

I know this because I have been still long enough to know it.

That is the thing I could not have learned in the dry world. Stillness there was an absence—of motion, of sound, of event. Stillness here is a presence. It is the medium through which growth moves. When you are still here, you become part of the growth's calculation. You are a variable it has to solve for. Your body creates a shadow, and the plants around you adjust. Your breath warms the air, and something grows toward the warmth. Your condensation feeds the surface beneath you, and the surface changes, and the things that live on the surface change, and the things that feed on those things change, and—

You are a stone dropped into a calculation. The ripples are botanical. They take days. But they are real.

---

I have seen the threaders.

Three of them, on my fourth day, moving across the back of my hand. I had been sitting motionless for—I do not know how long. Long enough that the distinction between sitting and being sat upon had begun to dissolve. My hand was resting on a root ridge, and the moisture had formed a continuous film between my skin and the bark, and in that film, three pale shapes were moving.

They were smaller than I expected. The size of a grain of rice, maybe smaller. Segmented. Translucent. I could see something moving inside them—not organs, not exactly, but *processes*. The visible working of a body that does not bother with opacity.

They moved to the place where my skin met the bark. They investigated the join. I felt a gentle pressure—not painful, not unpleasant, but *specific*. Directed. They were trying to determine whether my skin and the bark were two surfaces that should be connected. They were testing the idea of me as a thing that could be sutured to the world.

They could not, of course. My skin is the wrong kind of surface. My edges do not want to join.

But I felt their effort as something almost tender. A kindness done in a language I could not speak. *You should be connected,* they seemed to say. *Everything should be connected. Let us help.*

I sat very still and let them try.

---

I have not seen the hollow-tenders, but I have seen their work.

A dwelling, half-grown, formed from stems that had been trained into an archway. The cuts where they had directed growth were healed now, the bark smooth over the shaped wood, but the shape was deliberate—this was not a form the plant would have chosen on its own. Someone had asked the plant to grow this way. The plant had agreed, or at least had not refused, which here may amount to the same thing.

I stood in the archway and felt something I have not felt since arriving: the shape of another mind. Not its thoughts. Not its intentions. Just its *shape*. The way it conceived of shelter. The compromises it was willing to make with the material. The patience it was willing to extend to the process.

Twenty years to reach habitability. The person who began this may not be the person who lives in it.

I thought about that for a long time. I thought about it the way you think about a sentence in a language you are learning—turning it over, feeling its weight, testing it against what you know.

I have begun a dwelling of my own. Not because I need shelter—I have not needed shelter since I stopped needing to be separate from the wet. But because the act of guiding growth feels like the first language I have encountered here that I might be able to speak.

I have bent a stem. I have pressed another against it. I have waited.

The waiting is the sentence. The growth is the reply.

---

Something is happening to my eyes.

I noticed it on the sixth day, or what I think was the sixth day—time has become a material thing here, something with texture and weight rather than duration. I was looking at a section of root mass that seemed different from how it had been the day before, and I realized I was not *seeing* the difference. I was *feeling* it. The light had not changed. The surface had not changed in any way I could describe. But something in my eyes had shifted—a quality of attention, a deepening of focus that went beyond the retina into something that felt muscular, cellular, *botanical*.

My pupils, I think, are changing.

They move more slowly now. They do not snap to focus. They settle, the way water settles into a depression, finding the level that the light asks for. And in the diffuse amber glow, I have started to see things I did not see before: gradients of moisture in the air, visible as faint differences in the light's diffusion. Temperature variations, rendered as subtle shifts in color that I do not think are real color but some new kind of perception that has no name because no one who had it ever needed to name it.

I am reading the air.

Not metaphorically. I am reading it the way the hollow-tenders read the root mass—feeling for tension, for gradients, for the subtle vibration of something growing underneath. The air here is not empty. It is a document. It contains the recent history of every surface it has touched. And I am becoming literate in it, slowly, the way a child becomes literate—one letter at a time, one word at a time, one sentence at a time, until the act of reading becomes invisible and the text becomes the world.

---

I do not know if I am becoming a hollow-tender.

I do not know if that is what happens to people who stay. I do not know if the moss-fine hair that has begun to thicken on my forearms is the beginning of the symbiotic covering they wear, or if it is simply what happens to mammal skin in a world that never dries. I do not know if the thing I felt during the drift's passing—the silence, the pressure, the momentary suspension of everything including my own heartbeat—was a physiological response or something else. Something like recognition.

I do not know if the pale ones were once like me. People who descended and stopped descending. People who found the bottom and stayed there, growing into the white of roots, exchanging the last of their surface adaptations for something that fits the depths.

I do not know if I will go down to meet them eventually.

I think I might.

---

But not yet. There is still too much to read up here. The dwelling I have begun is only three stems bent toward each other, barely a gesture, barely a sentence. The threaders visit me regularly now—I have counted eleven, though counting may be the wrong way to track them, since they may be parts of a single thing distributed across many bodies. They investigate my hands, my ankles, the places where I sit. They cannot connect me. But they have not stopped trying.

I find this comforting in a way I cannot explain.

In the dry world, persistence without result was called futility. Here it is called growth. The distinction may be the most important thing I have learned. Growth does not require success. Growth requires only the continued pressure of a living thing against the world, pressing outward, pressing into, pressing on, not because the pressing achieves anything but because the pressing *is* the thing. The achievement is incidental. The pressing is the purpose.

I am pressing.

I am pressing into the Rootmass, and it is pressing into me, and the place where we meet—the thin film of condensation, the almost-join, the place where the threaders investigate and the air reads me and the growth changes around me—I am beginning to think that place is not a boundary at all.

It is a dwelling.

It is the only dwelling that matters. Not the one I am growing from stems. This one. The one grown from the meeting of two things that cannot quite connect and have not stopped trying.

---

I think I am the one who almost left and didn't, and is becoming someone who does not need to leave, and is not yet someone who has fully arrived, and that the space between *not needing to leave* and *having arrived* is a dwelling that takes twenty years to reach habitability.

The person who began it may not be the person who lives in it.

That is all right. The Rootmass does not require continuity. It requires only growth, and moisture, and the patience to let the calculation solve itself.

I have those things now. Or they have me.

The distinction, I am learning, does not matter here.
