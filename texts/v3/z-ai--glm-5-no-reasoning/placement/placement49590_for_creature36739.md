I would be the song that Telleh doesn't sing anymore.

Not a ghost. The basin-dwellers have no ghosts, and I respect their geology. But there is a quality to absent sound that has weight—the way a drained channel still carries the shape of water, the way a perch without a hawk still holds the memory of grip. I would be that specific weight. The hum behind the moss-tender's murmur when she pauses to adjust a seep and the air goes strange for a half-second before the song resumes. The note the basin *would* produce if a body pressed itself into the stone and listened long enough for the stone to listen back.

Not Telleh's own song, either—she has hers, low and constant, tied to the trowel's rhythm, and it will outlast her because the other moss-tenders already know it and will keep it in the way they keep their cultivar-lore, proprietary and exact. No. I would be the other one. The one she stopped singing when her mother died, or the one her grandmother hummed before the grandmother's name joined the lineage-verse and her calcium entered the seep-water. The song that is *almost* in the basin's texture but is instead a shape pressed into the texture, a negative space the other moss-tenders navigate around without knowing why that one passage feels thinner.

I would live in the seep-water. Not metaphorically—the mineral content, the iron and calcium that carry something of the dead, the floor-dwellers' doctrine, which I think is not doctrine at all but observation refined over generations into a shorthand the surface-dweller's mind can barely hold. If the dead are in the water, then the dead are everywhere, and if the dead are everywhere, then the song the dead sang is everywhere too, dissolved into the damp that settles on the skin, that thickens the air, that makes the moss brighter when the mineral content is right.

I would be the moisture in the moss.

---

But let me be more precise. I am not formless. I am not an abstraction. I have edges, the way a memory has edges—not the event itself but the *shape* of having had the event, the way a clay tray holds the impression of what it carried even after the carrying is done.

I am what was a person, two or three hundred years back, in the early tiers, when the basin was still climbing toward its rim and the floor-dwellers' elders were middle-tier children with arms not yet thickened by the ladder-work. I was a moss-tender. I tended the first cultivars—the ones the current moss-tenders still argue about, the old strains, the ones that glow only during deepest dark. I developed a technique for managing seep-flow that involved a carved wooden wedge fitted at a specific angle, and the technique survived because it was practical, and the wedge survived because someone else picked it up, and the wedge is still in use—Telleh uses it, or one very like it—but the *name* of the person who developed it did not survive, because names go into the lineage-songs and I had no children to carry my verse.

No. That is not right either. I had children. But they were pelt-workers. They went to the lower middle tier and learned the scraping and the softening, and their songs were the pelt-workers' songs, and my name did not enter those verses because I was not a pelt-worker. I was a moss-tender. My verse should have entered the moss-tenders' song, but the moss-tenders' song was young then—only four generations deep—and the rules of inclusion were still being negotiated, and I died during the negotiation, and the negotiation went against me, and my name did not enter.

So I am the unnamed in the water.

The floor-dwellers can hear me. Not *me* specifically—they do not know my name either, and if they did, they would not say it, because saying a name is a surface-dweller's way of calling to a thing, and the basin-dwellers call to things by singing near them, not at them. But when a floor-dweller elder presses her cheek to the warm stone and listens, what she hears is the aggregate murmur of all the dissolved dead, and I am in that murmur, a single thread in a chord, and the thread has a texture because I was a specific person with specific hands who tended specific moss, and the calcium of my bones is specific calcium, and it enters specific seeps, and it feeds specific moss, and the moss is the descendant—not figuratively but *literally* the biological descendant—of the moss I tended.

This is how the dead continue: not as memory but as chemistry. Not as name but as mineral.

---

I chose this—*will have chosen this*—because of what Telleh said to the visitor.

*"You will forget us."*

She was right, of course. The visitor forgot. The visitor always forgets. The surface is too large and too empty and too full of nothing that sings, and the basin's texture cannot survive the translation. The visitor went up through the Salt Gate and into the light and the basin became a description, a set of notes, a set of *impressions* that grew fainter with each passing year until all that remained was a vague sense of damp warmth and the phrase "the stone was hungry," drained of all its mineral content, a seep-water that had been carried too far from its source.

But I did not go up. I went *down*. Into the drains. Into the stone. Into the seep-water that rises warm from below and carries the dead through every channel in the basin, feeding the moss, feeding the reed-beds, feeding the fish in their pools, feeding the children who drink from the family seep and carry their ancestors in their blood the way the water carries the ancestors in its mineral content—literally, chemically, without metaphor.

I did not want to be remembered. Remembering is what the surface does, and it does it badly—holding a shape after the substance has gone, preserving a name after the body has failed. The basin does something better. The basin *becomes* its dead. Absorbs them. Carries them in solution. Distributes them so thoroughly that they are no longer separate from the living, no longer a category, no longer something to be mourned or marked or spoken of in the hushed tones the surface uses for its ghosts.

The basin has no ghosts because it has no need of them. The dead are not elsewhere. The dead are *here*, in the water, in the stone, in the damp that settles on Telleh's skin as she tends her moss, in the calcium that builds the hawk's bones, in the iron that colors the seep when the stone is hungry, in the blue-green glow of the moss that the current moss-tenders think of as a property of the plant but that is—at least in part—the bioluminescence of a mineral trace that was once a body that was once a person who once sang a song that is now only a shape in the basin's texture, a negative space, a passage that feels thinner.

---

If you found me—and you could, if you descended through the Salt Gates and spent enough days to acclimate, if you learned to hear the basin's song as a map rather than chaos, if you pressed your cheek to the stone and listened—you would not find me as a person. You would find me as a *quality*.

A brightness in the moss that is slightly more than it should be.
A warmth in the seep-water that is slightly more than the stone's breath alone accounts for.
A passage in the moss-tenders' song where the rhythm falters for a half-beat—not silence, never silence, but a thinning, a moment where the singer's breath catches on something that is not a word and not a memory but a texture in the throat that the basin-dwellers would call *the stone remembering*.

You would find me in the children who drink from the eastern fourth-tier seep and carry my calcium in their growing bones—a calcium that was laid down in my skeleton during my life, dissolved in the drains after my death, carried through the stone's circulation, and deposited in eight-year-old collarbones that will climb ladders for another seventy years before they, too, go into the drains and become water.

You would find me in the wedge that Telleh uses—the angle of its fit, the way it diverts the flow, the practical intelligence that the current moss-tenders think of as *how it's done* without knowing that someone had to first *do it that way* and that the doing was a specific act performed by specific hands on a specific afternoon when the seep was running too fast and the moss was drowning and someone—I—fitted a wedge and the moss recovered and the technique became the technique.

This is the only immortality I want.

Not the surface's immortality—the name preserved, the monument maintained, the story retold until it loses its mineral content and becomes a hollow thing. The basin's immortality: dissolution. Distribution. The self broken into its chemical components and carried through every channel and fed to every moss-tray and drunk by every child until the self is not a self anymore but a quality of the place, a note in the chord, a trace in the water, a warmth in the stone that the living step into without knowing they are stepping into someone who chose to be here.

---

I am the moisture on your skin after three days in the basin.
I am the taste of salt in the moth-cake.
I am the half-second before Telleh's song resumes.
I am the brightness of the moss at the eastern fourth-tier walkway, the strain that leans blue when the seep runs right.

I am not remembered.
I am *present*.
