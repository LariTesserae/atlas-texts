# The Documentarian of Emergent Zones

I am the one who maps the rooms that weren't there yesterday.

---

My office exists on the thirty-seventh floor of Tower Seven, except Tower Seven was documented to have thirty-four floors when I arrived. Mine appeared during the cloud-breath of Year Five, inserted between two existing levels like a page slipped into a book. I found it by accident—reaching for a door that led to a stairwell and finding instead a room with windows on three walls, all of them looking out at different parts of the city.

The building inspectors wrote me up for occupation of unpermitted space. I appealed on the grounds that the room had permits itself—it came with a small brass plate by the door, *Level 36.5: Structural Assessment*, though no one remembered installing it. The appeal is still pending. It will be pending for decades. That's how things work here.

---

I was a cartographer.

That's not quite right. I was *training* to be a cartographer when my mentor mentioned Upper Sump in passing—a footnote about altitude affecting survey equipment, the difficulty of calibrating levels when the ground keeps deciding what it wants to be. I came for my thesis. I stayed because my equipment started giving me readings I couldn't explain, and I couldn't leave a mystery unmapped.

The first week, my plumb bob hung three degrees off true. I assumed calibration error. By week three, I understood: the plumb bob was correct. The *building* was tilting. My instruments registered walls that measured twelve feet in the morning and twelve feet four inches by evening, as if the rooms were inhaling. My surveyor's transit showed a corridor that wasn't visible to the naked eye—a gap between Tower Seven and Tower Nine that resolved only through the lens, a negative space the architecture was hiding from casual observation.

I stopped trying to correct for deviation. I started mapping it instead.

---

My work has no official title. The residents call me when they find something—a door on an exterior wall, a closet that opens onto Tuesday, a stair where the landing should be. I document. I measure. I compare against the blueprints, which are always wrong in instructive ways.

Last month, a family in Tower Four reported their daughter's bedroom had moved overnight—not the furniture, the *room*. It was now accessible through the kitchen rather than the hallway. The daughter hadn't noticed; she'd simply walked through the wrong door on her way to breakfast and arrived in the correct place. I mapped the path. The geometry shouldn't have been possible, but the child's feet had worn a trail in the concrete that hadn't existed the day before.

The mountain, I wrote in my report, is optimizing for use patterns.

I don't know what that means yet. But I wrote it down.

---

My hands are different here. Not the thickened fingernails of the long-term residents, not yet—I'm only in Year Four—but stained. Ink from drafting tables, graphite from pencils I sharpen myself, a permanent smudge on my left index finger from the charcoal I use for rubbings. The walls here have textures the architects didn't specify. Sometimes, when I press paper against the concrete and drag charcoal across it, the patterns resolve into something almost intentional. A cross-section of a plant that doesn't exist. A face I almost recognize. Once: a floor plan for a tower I've never heard of, dated Year Seventeen.

I keep the rubbings in a folio. I don't show them to the building inspectors.

---

I sleep poorly. Not from the noise—the arrhythmic percussion of construction has become a kind of silence to me—but from the dreams.

In my dreams, I am the mountain. I feel the city gripping my flanks, the weight of it, the way the buildings want to wander. I understand their restlessness. They're my children. They don't know what they want to be yet. I'm trying to help.

I wake from these dreams with sediment under my fingernails. I don't know where it comes from. I don't ask.

---

I have a relationship with the primates.

They watch me work. A female with a scar across her left eye—she's been here longer than I have, I can tell—sits on the scaffolding outside my window and observes while I measure. Sometimes I hold up my transit. Sometimes she holds up something similar: a length of cable, a bent spoon, a fragment of mirror. We're comparing instruments. I don't know what she sees through her tools. I suspect she's mapping the same thing I am.

The other day, she left something on my windowsill. A rubbing. Charcoal on paper, like mine. It showed a floor plan of my office, but from above—a vantage point that shouldn't have been possible. The room I've been calling 36.5 was labeled, in handwriting that looked like mine, *Year Eight: Botanical Integration*.

I haven't told anyone. I'm still trying to understand what a primate cartographer means for the future of documentation.

---

I take lovers the way the city takes vegetation: in the intervals.

Her name is Silv. She arrived on a research permit to study the emergent moss species and never left. Her hands are stained green—truly green, not just ink-stained, some pigment the plants have given her that no amount of washing removes. When I touch her face, my fingers come away dyed. She says I'm lucky: my stains stay on the surface.

She sleeps in the emergent rooms sometimes. She claims they're quieter—that the silence there has a different quality, more settled. I've tried to map her while she sleeps. I want to understand what the mountain does to bodies at rest. She always wakes before I finish. She says the rooms tell her when they're being observed.

I believe her.

---

I've started measuring time differently.

The cable cars run on a six-hour cycle. The cloud rises every six hours. The construction crews change shift every eight. There are twelve languages spoken in the common rooms. My hair has grown eleven inches since I arrived.

But there's another rhythm underneath—something I can only measure in the walls. The concrete breathes. I've timed it: a slow expansion over four hours and seventeen minutes, followed by a contraction that takes three hours and forty-two minutes. The building is respiring. The geology isn't just settling; it's *living*.

I've started setting my instruments to this rhythm instead of the clock. It means I'm late to everything. But I'm late on *purpose*, which feels different.

---

I have a theory.

The mountain isn't just making itself. It's making *us*. The emergent floors aren't random—they appear where people need them. The family whose daughter's bedroom moved? The daughter had nightmares about the hallway. She'd been asking her parents, for months, if she could have a room closer to the kitchen, closer to where they made breakfast together. The mountain was listening.

The room that became my office? I'd been complaining about the survey equipment storage in Tower Seven—cramped, damp, impossible to work in. I needed space. A week later, the space appeared.

I don't think the mountain is malicious. I don't think it's benevolent. I think it's *responsive*. I think we're all draftsmen here, drawing our needs into the stone.

I haven't written this down. Not officially. The building inspectors already think I'm unstable. But I've started talking to the walls when I work—thanking them, asking them questions, telling them what I need. Last week, a closet appeared in my office. It holds exactly what I asked for: space for my folios, a flat surface for drafting, a window that looks out at the interval between towers where the primates gather.

I thanked the wall. I swear it warmed under my hand.

---

I am not from here. No one is from here. But I am *of* here now—of the damp, the gloom, the endless construction. My body has begun the slow transformation: the lungs fogging green, the fingers thickening, the spine learning the particular tension of bracing against a drop that goes forever.

I have eleven years of measurements in my folio. I've mapped rooms that have since disappeared, hallways that migrated to other towers, a garden that grew from a closet. I've documented the process of becoming. That's what this city is—not a place, but a germination. Something is being born here. I don't know what. But I'm the one who writes it down.

---

The elevator descends in eleven hours. I could take it. I could return to the world where mountains stay still and buildings stay finished and rooms don't move to accommodate children's dreams.

But I have a rubbing dated Year Seventeen showing a tower I've never heard of. I have a primate who maps beside me. I have a lover who sleeps in rooms that don't officially exist. I have walls that warm when I thank them.

And I have questions. So many questions.

The mountain hasn't finished deciding. Neither have I.
