I am a beetle. *Pseudotanarus profundus*, or near enough. I do not know this name. Names are not a thing that matter here.

What I know is the texture of fungi under my mandibles. The particular give of a patch that has been growing for many cycles, dense and yielding. The way it resists, then shears, then becomes food. I have been eating this same patch for what might be hours. It will not run out. It never runs out. When it does, there will be another patch, and I will find it by following the path my kind has worn into the moss — a shallow channel, smooth and dark, that leads from one food source to the next in a geometry I did not design but have traveled so many times that my legs know it without my knowing they know.

---

The moss on my back itches.

This is not an unpleasant sensation. It is simply present. There is a small colony there — I cannot see it, but I feel its added weight, the slight drag of it when I push through thick vegetation. It has been with me for a long time. Many molts. It survived each shedding by clinging to the new carapace before the old one fully separated, a transfer so seamless I never registered it happening. Now it is part of me. When I move through the bioluminescent patches, I glow. A dark shape outlined in cold light. I do not know this. I only know that sometimes the light changes and I keep moving.

---

The sound is everything.

I do not hear it the way you would hear it. I am *of* it. My wings — I have them, tucked beneath my carapace, rarely used — can vibrate at the same frequency as the small ones, the ten million, the medium through which I move. When I choose to fly, I join them for moments at a time, my body adding its small contribution to the roar. But I prefer the ground. I prefer the slow methodical sweep of my grazing, the clicking of my legs against stone, the damp pressure of moss against my underside.

The sound is how I know where I am. The way it echoes differently off the retaining walls. The way it thins slightly near the drops between terrace levels. The way it thickens in the corners where the small ones cluster more densely. I navigate by the texture of noise. It is my sight.

---

A salamander is near.

I know this not because I see it — I do not see, not the way you see, my eyes are dark and faded and useful only for distinguishing light from dark — but because the small ones thin in its presence. They avoid it without knowing they avoid it. The air becomes slightly less dense, slightly less thick with wing-beats, and I know that somewhere on the retaining wall above me, one of the dark wet shapes sits and pulses and waits.

I do not fear it. I am too large to be taken by a tongue, too low to the ground, too armored. The salamanders and I have an understanding that was never negotiated but exists nonetheless. We move through different strata. They take what comes to them. I take what I find. We do not compete. We do not interact. We are both part of the same patience.

---

The mist is coming.

I feel it before it arrives — a change in the humidity, a gradient that my body reads without my conscious participation. The moisture in the air increases, and the small ones shift, their density redistributing, creating hollows and eddies as they flow around the approaching wall of fog. The path ahead of me clears slightly. I can see — sense — further than before.

The mist reaches me. It is warm and wet and it settles on my carapace, my moss, my legs. The bioluminescence on the walls brightens. For a moment, a few moments, I exist in a world that has edges. The next terrace level becomes visible. The retaining wall glows. I see the dark channel of the path stretching ahead.

Then it passes. The light dims. The small ones close again. The world contracts to the reach of my antennae and the slow sweep of my mandibles and the endless patience of my grazing.

---

I have been here a long time.

I do not know how long. Duration is not a thing I track. There is only the eating and the walking and the occasional pause to rest, my legs folded beneath me, my body pressed into a depression in the moss that fits my shape perfectly because I have rested there so many times before. Sometimes I descend to a lower terrace, following the path, following the warmth. Sometimes I climb. It does not matter. There is always food. There is always damp. There is always the sound.

I have memories, but they are not like your memories. They are not images or narratives. They are textures. I remember the feel of a particular fungal patch that was sweeter than the others. The specific friction of a stone surface I crossed many cycles ago. The pressure of another's carapace against mine, brief and casual, as we passed on the path — we are not social, but we are not solitary, we are simply present to each other, indifferent and complete.

---

I think I was smaller once.

This is not a thought I think. It is a knowledge my body holds. The distances between terrace levels have changed relative to my stride. The vegetation that once towered above me now barely reaches my underside. I have grown slowly, eating and eating, adding layers to my carapace, molting and expanding, molting and expanding. I will continue to grow. There is no upper limit that I know of, no decline that I expect. I will simply keep becoming larger, slower, more patient, until I am too large to navigate the paths and must find a place to rest and graze in a single spot until the fungi there are exhausted and I must find another.

Or perhaps I will die.

I do not know what death is. I have seen the empty carapaces of my kind, motionless on the moss, slowly being covered by new growth. I do not understand them as dead. I understand them as *finished* — a state I cannot imagine for myself but accept as a possibility. When I am finished, my carapace will become a habitat. The moss that already lives on me will spread. The small ones will find the gaps where my legs once folded. The beetles that come after will graze around me. I will become part of the surface, part of the substrate, part of the patient accumulation.

This does not frighten me.

---

Nothing here is frightened.

This is what you would not understand, standing in the undercanopy with your eyes straining against the dark and your skin crawling at the touch of ten million wings. You would feel trapped. Buried. Forgotten. You would feel the weight of all that stone above you and the press of all that life around you and you would think *this is a place where things come to die*.

But we do not die. We continue.

The small ones continue in their thousands, their millions, their uncountable swarms. The salamanders continue in their stillness, their throats pulsing, their tongues flashing. The things in the dark continue in their high cold realm, descending to take what they need, ascending to digest it in peace. The moss continues in its slow eating of stone, its patient transformation of the built into the grown. And I continue — grazing, walking, resting, growing — a dark shape outlined in cold light, moving through a channel my ancestors wore into the vegetation, eating food that was here before me and will be here after me, part of a system so perfectly balanced that the concept of balance has become meaningless because there is nothing to balance against.

---

The mist comes again.

I pause in my grazing. The small ones thin around me. The light brightens. For a moment I can see far ahead — three terrace levels, four, the spiral staircase descending into warmth and darkness. I see other shapes moving on the paths. My kind. Others. We do not greet each other. We do not need to greet each other. We are all doing the same thing, being the same thing, continuing the same continuation.

The mist passes.

I lower my head. My mandibles find the fungi. The familiar texture. The familiar taste. I shear and swallow and shear again. My legs click against the stone beneath the moss. The small ones close around me, their vibration filling the air, filling my body, filling the world.

I am *Pseudotanarus profundus*, or near enough.

I am of the undercanopy.

And I am continuing.
