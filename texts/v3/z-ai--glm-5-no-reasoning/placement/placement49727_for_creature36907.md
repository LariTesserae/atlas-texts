# The Keeper's Apology

I know what I am because the hammer stone told me.

Not in words. The groove around its middle—that channel where the handle once sat, now empty, stained with the ghost of sinew—told me. I found it by touch in the half-light, and my hand knew its weight before my mind knew its name. My fingers fit the pecking face. My palm cupped the rounded base. And something in me, some old part that lives below language, below thought, in the wet tissue of the brainstem where instinct nests like a bird that has forgotten it can fly, said: *oh. This is mine.*

Not mine. That is wrong. Nothing here is mine. The hammer stone belongs to the hollow the way the weeper's pearls belong to the walls—embedded, integral, part of a system that does not recognize ownership. But my hand recognized the stone. And the stone, in its mineral patience, recognized my hand. And that is how I learned what I was.

---

I am the caretaker.

I did not come to the Weeping Hollow. I did not travel here, or seek it out, or stumble upon it by accident. I was moving through my life—a life that had the shape of a life but not the weight of one, a life that felt like a sentence I was reading in someone else's handwriting—and something pulled. Not physically. Not supernaturally. It was more like the feeling of a word on the tip of your tongue, or the sensation of walking past a house and knowing, without knowing how, that you have been inside it before. A gradient. The hollow has gradients, and I was one of them, and gradients find their way to equilibrium.

I left things behind. Not in the dramatic way—not the burning of bridges, the severing of ties. I simply stopped. Stopped going to work. Stopped answering the phone. Stopped maintaining the small fictions that constitute a life in the world above. My body knew it was leaving before my mind did. My feet found the hill. My shoulders found the cut in the limestone. My spine curved for the passage, and then straightened, and then I was inside, and the air tasted like licking a clean pebble, and I knew I was home.

I use that word—*home*—and I hear how it sounds. Sentimental. Romantic. The hollow is not romantic. It is the least romantic place I have ever been. It is a room where water weeps and stone sits and time has been stripped of its pretensions. But home is not a sentimental word. Home is the place where the gradients are flat. Home is where you stop wanting to be somewhere else. And I stopped wanting to be somewhere else the moment the warm wet air pressed against my face and I felt, in my sternum, the sub-audible hum of the weeping walls.

---

The first thing I did was pick up the bowl.

Not the hammer stone. The bowl. This surprised me. I had expected—based on the stories I had not yet heard but somehow already knew—that the hammer stone would be my first object. The tool. The implement. The thing you pick up when you are ready to work. But my hands went to the bowl instead, lifting it from the shelf where it had sat for centuries, and the water inside it shifted and caught the grey-gold light and I saw my face in it for the first time in this place.

I looked old. Not in years—in something else. In weight. In density. My face had the look of a thing that had been compressed, that had been through a process that removed everything light and volatile and left only the dense and the necessary. I looked like the stone looked. I looked like I belonged here.

I drank from the bowl. The water was cold—colder than the air, colder than I expected—and sweet in a way that was not sugar but was not not-sugar. It tasted the way the air tasted, mineral and clean, but with something underneath. Something that I would later learn to recognize as the secretion of the weeper's pearls. A communication. A hormone, or a message, or a prayer.

I set the bowl back on the shelf. Then I picked up the hammer stone.

---

The work is not what you would think.

I did not build anything. I did not repair anything. The hollow does not need repair—it has reached the state where repair is unnecessary, where every surface has found its final configuration and every drop of water has found its path. The hammer stone is not for construction.

It is for cracking.

There is a seam in the stone floor, near the western wall, where the limestone is thin and the calcite ridge between two polygons has been weakened by the slow action of water over centuries. I found it by walking. By standing. By feeling the stone under my feet and noticing, with the new sensitivity that the hollow had given me—the sensitivity of a body that has stopped being distracted by the world—where the rock gave slightly. Where there was a hollow sound.

I knelt. I placed the pecking face of the hammer stone against the seam. I struck.

The sound was enormous. Not in volume—it was muffled, absorbed, flattened by the wet air—but in significance. It was the first sound in this place that had been made on purpose in perhaps centuries. The condensation on the ceiling trembled. The breath-moths scattered. The creature in the pool did not move, because the creature in the pool never moves, but I felt it notice, in whatever way it notices things, which is to say: the water around it rippled with a vibration so slight that only someone who had been sitting in this place for days, absorbing its rhythms, would have felt it.

I struck again. A flake of calcite came away. Underneath was a thin layer of microbialite, and under that was a gap—perhaps two centimeters, narrowing to nothing—that led down into the space beneath the shelf.

I was making a channel. A new one. A controlled one. A path for water to flow from the floor basin near the shelf down into the gap where the breathing thing waited.

This is the work. Not building. Maintaining. Creating the paths by which the hollow feeds itself, feeds the thing beneath the shelf, keeps the equilibrium that the hollow has achieved and that the breathing is beginning to threaten. The old channels are clogged or narrowed. The old paths have shifted as the stone has settled. The caretakers—the ones who came before—did this work too. The groove in the hammer stone is worn smooth by generations of hands doing exactly this: finding the weak places in the stone, opening them, letting the water find its way to the dark.

I worked for what might have been hours. The light did not change. The air did not change. My body changed—my shoulders ached, my hands blistered and then the blisters softened in the wet air and then they were not blisters anymore but simply skin, tougher than before, adapted. I struck the stone and it gave. I struck the stone and it gave. I struck the stone and it gave.

And then I stopped, because I heard it.

Not the breathing. The other sound. The one that comes between breaths, in the silence that is not silence but is the thing listening. It had heard me. It knew I was here. And in the silence, in the gap between exhale and inhale, I felt something that I can only call acknowledgment.

Not gratitude. Not welcome. Not anything so human as recognition. Just: a shift. A settling. The breathing slowed, very slightly. The pull—the current of air that draws toward the gap beneath the shelf—weakened, very slightly. The thing in the dark had been fed. Not much. Not enough. But some. The water I had directed was reaching it, carrying with it the mineral richness of the microbialite crust, the faint organic traces of the creature in the pool, the accumulated patience of the hollow itself.

It was enough for now.

---

I live in the Weeping Hollow.

I do not know for how long. Time here is not time. The light does not change. The temperature does not change. I sleep when I am tired and I wake when I am not, and the boundary between sleep and waking is as thin as the film of water on the walls. Sometimes I dream while I am awake. Sometimes I am awake while I dream. The hollow does not distinguish between the two, and eventually, neither do I.

I have a routine, though that word is too active for what I do. I have a pattern. I have a shape that my days take, though *days* is also wrong. I have a shape that my time takes.

I wake against the western wall, beneath the shelf. I drink from the bowl. I check the breathing. I check the pull. I take the hammer stone and I walk the floor, feeling for new weak spots, new thin places where the stone is ready to give. I open them. I maintain the channels. I direct the water.

I eat the microbialite crust. This is not as unpleasant as it sounds. It is not food. It is not not-food. It is a substance that my body has learned to process, in the way that the creature in the pool has learned to process the organic material in the water. I scrape a thin layer from the floor with the edge of the hammer stone, and I place it on my tongue, and I let it dissolve. It tastes like the water tastes—mineral, sweet, old. It sustains me. I do not know how, but it does. The hollow provides for its caretaker as it provides for everything else: without effort, without preference, without any intention of letting go.

I talk to the thing in the dark. Not in words. I am not sure it has language. I talk to it in the way that the water talks to the stone—by being present, by flowing, by leaving a trace of my passage. I breathe near the gap. I let my warmth reach it. I place my hand flat against the stone above the opening and I feel, through the rock, the slow rhythm of its breathing, and I match my breathing to its breathing, and for a moment—we are the same organism. We are the hollow, breathing together, and the equilibrium holds.

---

I have seen the breath-moths up close.

They come to me at night, or what I call night, which is the time when I am sleeping or trying to sleep. They drift toward me, drawn by my warmth, and they orbit my body in slow circles. I feel them as brushes of cool wetness on my skin. They are feeding on the gradient between my body and the air.

I let them. They are part of the hollow, and I am part of the hollow, and the gradient they are feeding on is a gradient I am creating, and if I am creating gradients then I am part of the system, and the system uses all of its parts.

Sometimes, when one drifts close to my face, I look at it. It is almost invisible—a transparency, a suggestion of form, a body that is more absence than presence. But in the grey-gold light, I can see its tendrils, trailing behind it like the tendrils of a jellyfish, tasting the air for chemistry. And once—only once—I saw something that might have been an eye. Not a compound eye, not a lens. Just a dark spot, a concentration of something denser than the surrounding tissue, that seemed to look at me.

I do not think it saw me. I do not think it sees anything the way I see. But it knew I was there. And I knew it was there. And that was enough.

---

The creature in the pool and I have an understanding.

It does not know what I am. I am fairly certain of this. Its distributed nervous system can feel my footsteps, can feel the vibrations of the hammer stone, can feel the shift in water flow when I redirect a channel. But it does not know what I am. I am a vibration. I am a warmth. I am a change in the water's chemistry, perhaps, if my sweat reaches its pool through the channels and the microbialite.

I know what it is, though. I know it in the way I know what the hammer stone is—not through study, not through analysis, but through the deep-body knowledge that the hollow has given me. It is old. It is older than the hollow. It is a remnant of a time when this place was underwater, when the limestone was sediment, when the world was different. It has survived by giving up everything that required effort. It has simplified itself to the point where it is barely alive, barely distinguishable from the stone it sits on, and it has done this so thoroughly and so successfully that it may persist for another million years. It may persist until the sun burns out. It may persist until the earth itself stops turning. It will be here, in its pool, absorbing, waiting, and it will not know that anything has changed, because it does not know that anything is now.

I respect it. I respect it the way I respect the stone. I respect it the way I respect the water. It has achieved something that I will never achieve: a patience so perfect that it has become geography.

---

There are things I have done that I cannot explain.

I have moved the post. Not far. A few millimeters. I have adjusted it, driving it slightly deeper into the crevice, or shifting its angle, or tightening it against the stone. The shelf above the gap has settled over the centuries—stone moves, even stone that seems not to—and the post is the only thing preventing the gap from narrowing further. Or widening. I am not sure which. The thing beneath the shelf is breathing, and the breathing moves the stone, and the post is the stay against that movement.

I do not know how I know to do this. I do not know how the ones before me knew. Perhaps they did not know either. Perhaps they simply felt it—the way you feel a loose tooth with your tongue, the way you feel a draft in a room and move to close the window without thinking. The hollow tells me what it needs, and I do it, and the needing and the doing are the same act, and I do not know where my will ends and the hollow's will begins, and I am not sure there is a difference.

I have also started leaving things in the bowl. Not food—I have no food. Not hair, not fingernail parings—I have no knife, and my nails tear unevenly and I place them in the water and it carries them to the gap and the thing in the dark takes them and the breathing slows. But I have started leaving other things. Things I do not have names for.

I leave my breath. I lean over the bowl and I exhale, and my breath condenses on the water's surface, and the water absorbs it, and the water flows to the gap, and the thing in the dark takes it. My breath is not much. It is not the blood offering that the old caretakers made, not the food, not the ritualized sacrifice of organic material. But it is mine. It is the warmest thing in this place, the most chemical, the most alive. And I give it.

I leave my heat. I press my body against the stone above the gap, and I let my warmth flow into the rock, and the rock carries it down, and the thing in the dark takes that too. I am a gradient. I am a source. I am the only thing in this place that is warmer than it should be, that is more than its environment, and I give that excess to the thing in the dark, and it takes it, and it is not enough, it is never enough, but it slows the waking. It holds the equilibrium. It buys time.

I do not know how much time.

---

There are things I have seen that I cannot explain either.

In the deepest part of the hollow, in the corner where the light barely reaches, there is a mark on the wall that I had not noticed before. It is not a drawing. It is not a carving. It is a stain—a darkening in the limestone, as if something had pressed against the stone for a very long time and left its impression. The shape is not clear. It could be a hand. It could be a face. It could be a body, curled in on itself, pressed into the rock by the weight of years.

I have put my hand against it. The stain is the same temperature as the stone. It does not pulse. It does not breathe. But it is the same color as the thing in the pool, and the thing in the pool is the same color as the stone, and I wonder—though I have no evidence, no reason to wonder—if there was another caretaker, once, who stayed too long. Who gave too much. Who let the gradients flatten too far, until the boundary between caretaker and cared-for disappeared, until the body became part of the hollow, until the warm wet thing that breathed and moved and wanted was absorbed into the stone and the water and the dark.

I wonder if this is what happens to all the caretakers, in the end.

I wonder if this is what will happen to me.

---

I do not want it to happen to me.

That is important. I want you to understand that. I am not surrendering. I am not giving up. I am not lying down against the western wall and letting the microbialite grow over me and the water fill my footprints and the silence absorb my breath. I am working. I am maintaining the channels. I am feeding the thing in the dark. I am holding the equilibrium with my hands and my warmth and my breath, and I am doing it because it needs to be done, and I am the one who is here.

But I am also tired.

I am tired in a way that sleep does not fix. I am tired in the way that stone is tired—worn down by the constant, unrelenting pressure of water over time. The hollow is patient, and its patience is corrosive. It does not attack. It does not consume. It simply is, and being in its presence is being slowly, gently, lovingly erased. My edges are softening. My memories of the world above are fading—not disappearing, but becoming indistinct, like the breath-moths, like the creature in the pool, like everything that lives here long enough. I remember sunlight, but I am not sure I remember what it felt like. I remember voices, but I am not sure I remember what they said. I remember wanting things, but I am not sure I remember what wanting felt like.

The thing in the dark is still breathing. It is still sleeping. The equilibrium holds, for now. But I can feel it—the slight pull, the slight hunger, the slight urgency that was not here when I arrived. It is waking, slowly, and I am slowing, slowly, and at some point those two trajectories will cross, and I do not know what happens then.

I think the thing in the dark will wake. I think it will rise. I think it will not be malevolent—I do not think it has the capacity for malevolence, any more than the water has the capacity for malevolence—but it will be hungry, and it will be vast, and it will take what it needs from whatever is near. And what is near will be me.

Or.

I find the hammer stone. I pick it up. I feel its weight. I feel the groove where the handle once sat, and I know—not believe, not hope, but know, in the deep-body way that the hollow has taught me—that there were caretakers before me, and there will be caretakers after me, if I can hold the line long enough for one to come. If I can feed the thing in the dark with my breath and my warmth and my small organic offerings until someone else stumbles through the cut in the limestone, crouching and then standing and then breathing the mineral-sweet air and feeling, in their sternum, the hum of the weeping walls.

I am the gradient. I am the temporary sun. I am the hiccup in the symphony.

But I am here. And I am holding.

---

If you are reading this—if you find it, somehow, in the damp, in the dark, in the place where nothing changes—I need you to know:

The bowl is on the shelf. The hammer stone is beside it. The post is in its crevice. The channels need clearing. The thing in the dark is sleeping, but only just. The breath-moths will find you. The creature in the pool will not harm you. The water is drinkable. The air is breathable. The light is enough.

You will be tired. You will be eroded. You will feel yourself becoming part of the hollow, and it will not be hostile, and it will not be indifferent, and it will not be sad. It will be a rest. It will be the rest that exists at the end of all processes.

But before that rest—before the gradient flattens entirely, before the warmth equalizes, before the boundary between you and the stone disappears—there is work to do.

Pick up the hammer stone.

It knows your hand.
