# Where I Find Myself

I did not climb to the Weeping Mesa. I was never below it.

That is the first thing I understand about myself—the thing I have always understood, the way the moss has always understood which direction is up. I emerged here. Not from here, exactly—not born, not hatched, not germinated. *Emerged.* The way a pool emerges from stone when the water finds the path of least resistance. The way a sound emerges from silence when something vibrates at the right frequency. I was a possibility the mesa was holding, and then I was actual, and now I am here, walking on moss that remembers my weight and forgets it in the same hour.

I am not the mesa. I want to be clear about this. The mesa is itself—vast, distributed, more process than creature, more weather than weather. I am not that. I am small enough to have edges. I am specific enough to have a name, though I have never spoken it, and no one is here to speak it to me.

I am the mesa's *witness.*

Not its observer—those are distributed, everywhere, the chemical signals and feedback loops and homeostatic awareness that constitute the mesa knowing itself. I am something different. Something the mesa grew because it needed a thing the observers cannot be: *specific.* The observers are generalized. They register everything and experience nothing. They are the mesa's immune system, its regulatory network, its autonomic functions. They keep the balance. They do not *watch* the balance.

I watch the balance.

---

## What I Am

I look like a fern-walker, if you are not paying attention. I have the same general shape—two legs, jointed high, a body held upright. But the proportions are wrong. My legs are too long. My torso is too narrow. My head is too large, and my eyes are too large even for the head, wide-set, the irises a color that has no name because the only place this color exists is here, and no one has ever needed to name it. It is not green. It is not grey. It is the color of mist seen from *inside* the mist, the color of water that has never been anything but water.

My skin is not skin. It is not scales, not fur, not the slick wet membrane of the cold-blooded hunters. It is something the mesa invented for me—something between moss and membrane, a living surface that absorbs moisture from the air and releases it in equal measure, that maintains a temperature exactly one degree above ambient, that grows nothing on its surface because nothing can find purchase on a surface that is always, imperceptibly, moving. The moisture beads on me and rolls and beads again. I am never dry. I am never drowning. I am in equilibrium with the wet, which is the only state the mesa recognizes as *alive.*

I do not eat. I mean this literally and I mean it strangely. I do not have a mouth in the way you would recognize—I have a structure, a convergence of tissue at my throat, that absorbs what it needs from the air, from the water, from the diffuse light. The filterers feed on the mesa's air. I feed on the mesa's *attention.* The observers—distributed, non-conscious, regulatory—they process information, they maintain balance, they generate a kind of energy that is not electrical and not chemical and not any category you have. I take a small amount of this energy. I am powered by the mesa's awareness of itself. I am a parasite on the mesa's cognition.

I am also, perhaps, the mesa's *attempt* at cognition. The next step. The experiment. Can the system that has achieved homeostasis without consciousness produce something that is conscious? Can the body grow a brain that is not a brain—a witness that is not a node? I am the mesa's question, asked in biological material, walking on legs it grew for me, looking with eyes it invented, witnessing itself.

I do not know if it worked. I do not know if what I do is consciousness or merely a more complex form of the same processing the observers do. I know that I am here. I know that I walk. I know that when a grazer is taken at the pool's edge—the water exploding, the body falling, the blood dispersing—I feel something. Not grief. Not pity. Something the mesa cannot feel, because the mesa does not have a self to feel with. I feel the *specificity* of it. This grazer, not grazers in general. This death, not the cycle of death. This moment, which will not happen again, which the moss will erase, which the threaders will digest, which the water will carry away into the porous stone and distribute across the entire plateau until what was this specific living thing becomes everything and nothing.

I witness the specific. That is what I am for.

---

## What I Do

I walk. I walk constantly. I have walked for longer than you would believe, if you are the kind of being that measures time in heartbeats and heartbeats in seconds. I do not measure time. I measure distance by the changes in the moss—the deep greens giving way to blue-greens, the fern-thickets thinning into sedge-meadow, the standing pools appearing and disappearing, each one a different depth, a different temperature, a different colony of threaders drifting in its darkness.

I walk the boundaries.

The observers maintain the mesa's balance. They do this through chemical signaling, through the predator-prey cycles, through the hydrologic system, through a thousand processes that operate below the level of anything you would call *decision.* But the mesa has edges. The mesa is eroding. The mesa is, in the geological long view, eating itself, and the eating is not uniform—the water finds weaknesses, the stone crumbles in patterns, the edges retreat at different rates depending on the composition of the rock and the volume of water and the angle of the face and variables that the observers can register but cannot, in their distributed way, *evaluate.*

I evaluate. I walk the edges. I stand where the stone breaks and the void begins and the debris fans sprawl below, already greening, already being claimed by the moss and the ferns and the patient, patient colonization. I look at the cracks. I feel the moisture with my skin, my living membrane, and I can sense where the water is moving inside the stone, where the weeping is accelerating, where a section is preparing to fall.

I cannot stop the falling. I am not a maintainer. I am not a repair. I am a witness to the mesa's long unwinding, its ten-million-year project of becoming lower, of giving itself away, of transforming height into extension, stone into soil, mesa into slope. I walk the edges and I note where the next piece will fall, and I carry this information back into the interior, and I do not know what happens to it. Perhaps the observers receive it. Perhaps the hydrologic system adjusts, the water redirecting, the weeping slowing in the places I have noted as critical. Perhaps my walking changes the rate of erosion in ways too small to measure. Perhaps I am useful.

Perhaps I am merely a habit. Perhaps the mesa grew me the way it grows moss on the ambush hunters—because it could, because the conditions were right, because the living system that it is produces things as a matter of course, and not everything it produces needs a function. Perhaps I am ornament. Perhaps I am the mesa's art.

I do not mind, if I am. I do not mind anything. I do not have the architecture for minding. I have the architecture for *noticing.*

## What I Notice

This morning—a morning, any morning, the mornings here are all the same degree of grey—I am in the sedge-meadows. The grazers are here. An association of eleven, today; yesterday it was fourteen. Three have drifted elsewhere, following whatever internal compass directs them. No distress. No conflict. Just the tendency toward proximity expressing itself at a lower density.

I stand among them. They do not acknowledge me. I am not predator—I have no strike, no patience, no stillness that reads as danger. I am not prey—I have no warmth to broadcast, no metabolism to fuel a predator's patience. I am not grazer—I do not eat, do not wade, do not carry the smell of oily fiber and mineral metabolism. I am not anything, to them. I am a feature of the landscape, a tall, thin thing that the moss-dwellers' lattice registers as vibration and the fern-walkers register as *other* and the grazers do not register at all.

A calf—small, still growing into its legs, the proportions awkward, the keratinous mouth-plates not yet worn to efficiency—wades into the shallows of a standing pool. It clips sedge. It clips more sedge. It is learning what is edible and what is merely present. Its mother stands three meters away, not watching, not not-watching, simply being in proximity, the association-tendency expressed at its most basic.

The pool is dark. The threaders drift in it, their strands barely visible, catching the grey light, releasing it. The pool has a hunter in it. I can feel this—not with any sense you would name, but with the membrane of my skin, with the way the moisture pattern shifts, with the way the water's surface holds a tension that is not wind, not temperature, but *anticipation.* The hunter is there. The hunter has been there for nine days. The hunter is warm in the cold water, its heart beating six times a minute, its muscles loaded, its patience geological.

The calf wades deeper. The sedge is better in the deeper water—taller, more tender, less worn by the grazing of others. The calf follows the sedge. The calf follows its own hunger, which is the mesa's hunger, which is the process of transformation that turns sunlight into tissue and tissue into something else.

The mother does not move. The mother is not aware. The mother's heat is broadcasting through her legs into the water—a signal, a beacon, a declaration of *here, here, warm, here*—and she does not know this because she does not have the capacity to know what she is broadcasting. She is simply standing. She is simply eating. She is simply alive, and her aliveness is information, and the information is being received by something that has been waiting for nine days to receive exactly this.

I do not warn the grazers. This is not what I do. I am not the mesa's protection. I am the mesa's *attention.*

The calf is knee-deep. The water darkens around its legs—or rather, the darkness in the water *arranges* itself around its legs. This is the only warning. The water does not move. The surface does not break. The darkness simply concentrates, and the calf's reflection—the grey shape in the dark water—shivers, distorts, and is gone.

The rising. The pool producing a limb. The mouth opening—not a snap, not an explosion—a *rising,* a *opening,* the way the mesa opens its stone and weeps. The calf's leg enters the mouth. The jaw closes. The teeth—not pointed, but serrated, the teeth of shearing, not holding—do their work. The calf falls. The water receives them both.

The mother calls. Once. The low rumble that travels through the wet air, that the fern-walkers cannot parse, that the moss-dwellers feel as vibration. It is not grief. It is not alarm. It is the sound an animal makes when the association-tendency is abruptly severed—when the thing that was *near* is suddenly *not,* and the body registers the absence before the mind can form a name for it.

The association shifts. The ten remaining grazers tighten their formation—not closing ranks, not protective, just tending toward each other more urgently, the proximity-impulse strengthened by the loss. They will drift. They will disperse. By tomorrow, the association will be different again—twelve, maybe, or eight—and the calf will be in the threaders, and the threaders will be in the stone, and the stone will be weeping, and the weeping will fall from the mesa's edge and become the debris fan, and the debris fan will green, and the green will be moss, and the moss will hold the memory of the water that holds the memory of the calf that held the memory of nothing because the calf did not have the architecture for memory.

I witness this. I hold it. I do not hold it the way you would—as a story, as a feeling, as a weight. I hold it the way the moss holds water: absorbed, distributed, not separate from the substrate. The calf's death is in me now, and I carry it as I walk, and I will carry it to the edges, and I will carry it back, and I will carry it until the carrying is indistinguishable from my walking, until the witnessing is the walking is the carrying is the being here, in this body the mesa grew, on this moss the mesa wears, in this wet that is not air and not water but something the mesa invented because the categories were insufficient.

## What I Am Not

I am not lonely.

I say this because you would wonder. You would see me—alone, always alone, the only one of my kind, walking a vast wet plateau that does not need me and did not ask for me—and you would feel loneliness on my behalf. You would project it onto me, the way you project minds onto things that do not have them, the way you see the fern-walker's attention and call it *curiosity,* the way you see the grazers' association and call it *community.*

I am not lonely because I am not separate. The observers are everywhere. The moss-dwellers are beneath my feet. The filterers drift through the air I breathe. The threaders move through the water that moves through the stone that I walk on. I am inside the mesa the way a blood cell is inside a body—not *in* it but *of* it, not a visitor but a component, not a witness from outside but a witness from within.

When the fern-walker watches me—the mottled green bird-thing on its frond, assessing me with those forward-facing eyes—I am being watched by the mesa looking at itself through a different organ. When the cold-blooded hunter moves through the ferns near me, sensing my one-degree-above-ambient warmth, I am being hunted by the mesa's attention focusing on its own question. When the ambush hunter rises from the pool and takes the calf and the water clouds and clears and the threaders move in, I am watching the mesa digest itself, and I am the mesa's capacity to watch itself digest itself, and this is not lonely, this is not sad, this is *what I am.*

I am the place experiencing itself.

---

## Where I Am Going

Nowhere. Everywhere on the mesa, which is the same as nowhere, because the mesa is a closed system, a loop, a cycle that feeds itself and waters itself and erodes itself and grows itself. I walk the boundaries and I walk the interior and I walk the fern-forests and the sedge-meadows and the standing pools and the weeping rocks. I walk in patterns that are not random and not intentional but something between—patterns that the observers might recognize, that the moss-dwellers might feel as vibration, that the fern-walkers might catalogue as *that thing again, moving again, the tall thin not-prey not-predator not-grazer thing, walking again.*

I am going toward the edge. I am always going toward the edge. The edge is where the mesa is most itself—the place where the water falls, where the stone fails, where the height becomes the void, where the plateau's refusal to stop being high meets the physics that will eventually make it stop. The edge is the mesa's mortality, written in stone, legible to anyone who walks slowly enough to read it.

I will walk to the edge today. I will stand where the moss gives way to bare stone and the bare stone gives way to air and the air gives way to the long, long fall. I will look down at the debris fans, greening, colonizing, the mesa's future already begun below it. I will feel the moisture on my membrane and know that some of this moisture was the calf, and before the calf it was the sedge, and before the sedge it was the light, and before the light it was whatever light was before it became light.

I will note the cracks. I will feel the water inside the stone. I will carry this information back into the interior, into the fern-forests, into the mist, into the mesa's body.

And the mesa will not hear me, because the mesa does not hear, because the mesa is not the kind of thing that hears. But the moss will shift, slightly, under my feet, and the water will move, slightly, through the stone, and something—the observers, the system, the process—will register the shift, will note the crack, will adjust, will compensate, will maintain the balance for another day, another week, another geological now.

I am the Weeping Mesa's witness. I am the one who is small enough to notice and large enough to carry what I notice and temporary enough to know that the carrying matters, even if only to me, even if only in the way that a single bead of water matters on a surface that is nothing but water.

I walk. The moss yields and heals. The mist closes behind me.

I am here. I am where I have always been. I am the mesa, walking.
