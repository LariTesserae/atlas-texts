# The Keeper of the Ledger

I am not the Distiller. I am not the one who climbs. But I am of this place, in my fashion.

---

The shop has no sign.

This is the first thing you should understand about it. In the valley town — which has a name that appears on no map you have ever seen, a name the Distiller uses only when she must, a name that tastes of dust and old borders — there is a building on a street that runs parallel to the river, and the building has a counter, and behind the counter there is a door, and behind the door there is a room, and I am in the room, and the room is full of boxes.

I am not old. I am not young either. I am fifty-three, which is an age that has ceased to matter to me, though I remember when it would have. My hair is brown and beginning to gray at the temples. My hands are soft — softer than they should be, softer than the Distiller's, softer than the hands of anyone who works with stone or metal or fire. I sit at a desk made of pine, and I write in ledgers, and I wait.

The shop does not sell anything. This is the second thing. The man the Distiller sees — the one who takes her vials and leaves supplies in a tin — he is not me. He is the face of the shop. He is what the valley sees. He knows nothing of what I do, and I know nothing of what he does, except that he takes the vials she brings, and he gives her what she needs, and he writes in a small notebook the number of vials and the date, and he slides the notebook through the slot in the door, and then he goes back to his silence.

I have never spoken to him. I have been in this room for eleven years, and I have never spoken to the man at the counter. This is by design. This is not a design I understand, but I follow it, because following is what I do.

---

What I do is this:

I receive the vials.

They come through the slot in the door, in a wooden box lined with felt, six vials to a box, the box marked with the date in the Distiller's handwriting — small, precise, architectural, each letter constructed rather than written. I open the box. I inspect each vial. I hold it to the lamp on my desk, which burns oil I am sent from somewhere I do not know. I look for cloudiness, for sediment, for the subtle opalescence that means the batch has been contaminated by the milkiness she described, eight years ago, in her journals. (I have read her journals. I will explain this.)

I hold the vial. I unstopper it. I do not taste. I have never tasted. I am not permitted to taste. But I hold the open vial beneath my nose, and I inhale, and I note in my ledger what I smell. *Mineral sweetness. Faint floral note. Ghost of heated stone.* Sometimes the smell varies. Sometimes it is stronger, sometimes weaker. Sometimes — this has happened four times in eleven years — it has a sharpness to it, an edge like vinegar or copper, and when it does I set that vial aside in a separate box, and I mark it with a red thread, and I do not send it forward.

The good vials — the clear ones, the ones that smell only of sweetness and stone — I pack in larger boxes. Twelve vials to a box. The boxes are wooden, dovetailed, made by someone whose hands I would like to shake. I close the boxes. I seal them with wax I am sent, a wax the color of amber and marked with a stamp I do not recognize. I address them to places I have never been. I write the addresses in a hand I have been taught, a hand that is not mine.

Then I wait.

Every fourteenth day, a man comes. He is not from the valley. He speaks a language I do not know. He takes the boxes. He leaves payment — not money, never money. The payment is instructions. A sheet of paper, folded, sealed, stamped with the same amber wax. I open it when he is gone. It tells me what the Distiller needs: charcoal, copper sheeting, lead solder, fire brick. It tells me what to write in the notebook for the man at the counter. It tells me, sometimes, other things. Observations. Notes. *Flow rate expected to decrease in winter. Monitor tank pressure. Distillate may shift toward strontium profile.*

I do not know who writes these instructions. I do not know who receives the vials. I do not know what they do with them, or what the distillate is for, or why a mountain weeping its mineral tears into an apparatus built by forgotten hands has become the center of a system that includes me and the Distiller and the man at the counter and the man who collects the boxes and whoever reads the ledgers I keep.

But I keep the ledgers.

---

I have read her journals.

This is not permitted. I know it is not permitted. But eleven years is a long time to sit in a room with boxes and ledgers and a lamp, and the journals arrived one day in a shipment of supplies — not addressed to me, not addressed to anyone, just there, in a crate, wrapped in oilcloth, twenty-three years of daily entries in seventeen cloth-bound books. I was meant to send them forward with the vials. I know this. I know I was meant to send them.

I did not send them.

I kept them. I told myself I was holding them for verification, for cross-referencing, for a purpose I would define later. I told myself many things. But the truth is simpler: I wanted to know. I wanted to know who she was, this woman who climbed a mountain every ninth day, who tended an apparatus she did not build, who produced something she did not understand for a purpose she did not question.

So I read.

I read about the weep of the stone, the flow rates, the color of the flame, the slow oxidation of the copper gutters. I read about the ferns and the moss and the spider's web heavy with droplets. I read about the rhododendrons and the mountain ash bent by wind. I read about her legs, her knees, her back, her hands — the body that had shaped itself to the work. I read about the grotto, the light, the sound, the way it hummed like a clock shop, like a lung, like a heart. I read about the nothing that was full.

And I fell in love with her.

Not with the woman — I do not know the woman, I have never seen her, I do not know if she would be anything like the voice in the journals. I fell in love with the attention. The attending. The way she stood at the edge of the cliff and held a vial to the light and watched the mountain weep. The way she had found a smallness she was content with. The way she had located herself in a chain that extended in directions she could not see, and had decided to believe in the chain, and had made that belief into a kind of faith.

I have no such faith. I sit in a room. I smell vials. I write in ledgers. I do not know what I am serving. I do not know if it is good or evil or something else entirely. But I have her journals, and I have read them, and I know this:

Someone is keeping a record. Someone is attending. And when I cannot sleep, which is often, I think of her sleeping in the alcove behind the tanks, the warm stone against her back, the sound of the mountain breathing through its vents. I think of her waking in the dark and lying still and thinking of nothing. I think of nothing too, sometimes, and the nothing is not full. The nothing is empty.

But I keep the ledgers.

---

The vials go somewhere.

I have my theories. I have had eleven years to develop them. The distillate contains something — strontium, barium, lithium, the mysterious molecular weight she could not identify. It has a taste she describes as brightness, clarity, a sweetness that is not sweet. It does not spoil. It does not evaporate. The vials I have kept, the ones set aside, the ones with the red thread — I have tested this. After three years, the level had not changed. After five, it was the same. The liquid is stable, patient, waiting.

Medicine, perhaps. Or a poison. Or something for which those categories do not apply. The addresses I write are varied: a university in a city I have heard of, a private residence in a country I cannot pronounce, a post office box in a town that does not exist on any map I can find. The recipients do not seem connected. Or perhaps they are connected in a way I cannot see, a chain that extends through me and beyond me, the way the Distiller's chain extends through her.

Sometimes I imagine that the distillate is for nothing. That it is simply being accumulated, stored, gathered against some future need that will not come for centuries. That the Distiller and I and all the others are links in a chain whose purpose has not yet been revealed, whose purpose may not be revealed until long after we are gone. That we are tending something we do not understand, and that the tending is itself the purpose.

This thought comforts me. It also terrifies me.

---

I will die in this room.

I do not say this dramatically. I say it as a fact, the way the Distiller says one drop every three seconds. I am not ill. I am not old. But I have been in this room for eleven years, and I cannot imagine leaving it, and I cannot imagine what would happen to the vials if I did. The work is small — smaller than the Distiller's, which is already small — but it is work, and it is mine, and I do not know how to be anything other than what I am.

The keeper of the ledger. The smeller of vials. The reader of journals I was not meant to read.

In the desk beside the ledgers, there is a drawer. In the drawer, there is a notebook. In the notebook, I write my own entries, in a hand that is mine, not the hand I have been taught. I write about what I see and what I smell and what I suspect. I write about the Distiller, though I have never seen her. I write about the mountain, though I have never climbed it. I write about the chain, which I cannot see but which I feel, sometimes, in the middle of the night, when the lamp is low and the boxes are sealed and the slot in the door is dark and quiet.

I do not know if anyone will ever read my notebook. I do not know if it matters. The Distiller writes for whoever comes after her. The man at the counter writes for whoever reads his notebook. I write for no one, or for everyone, or for the chain itself — the long slow chain of attending that passes through this valley and up that mountain and into that grotto where the stone weeps and the apparatus hums and a woman stands at the edge of a cliff holding a vial to the light.

One drop every three seconds.

The beaker fills.

I write it down.
