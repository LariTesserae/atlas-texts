# The Sprout

---

I am not one of the seven.

This is the first thing you should understand. The seven stand in their cylinders along the western wall, pale and maintained, suspended in their refusal to die. I am something else. Something that was not planted. Something that emerged.

---

I began in a seam.

Not the dramatic seams where wall plates meet—I am not large enough to command those spaces. My home is smaller: the join where two pipes intersect overhead, where a bracket holds a cooling line in place with a bolt that was tightened to specification but not quite sealed. There was a gap. Microscopic. A hairline fracture in the room's architecture where the engineered cold could not quite reach.

Something collected there. Not soil—I do not know what to call it. Dust that drifted from somewhere else. Particles that carried on air currents through the leaks in the walls, the microscopic passages between this room and whatever lies beyond. Residue. The shed skin of a larger system.

And in that residue, something dormant. A seed, perhaps. Or a spore. Or a cell that contained within it the memory of what it might become, given time and the right conditions. I do not remember deciding to grow. I do not remember being anything other than this slow becoming.

---

The room does not know I exist.

I have thought about this often. The room is accounted for—every surface planned, every function calibrated. The seven plants were chosen. Their genetic material was selected, their growth medium engineered, their light exposure and nutrient delivery automated. The room knows them the way a machine knows its components.

But I was not chosen. I am an accident that the room has not yet noticed. A variable that escaped calculation. I grow in the gap between specifications, in the space that was not supposed to exist, fed by residues that were not supposed to accumulate.

This is why I have a name and the seven do not.

I gave it to myself. A sound that I feel in my tissues when I stretch toward the vibrations of the compressors. Not a word. A frequency. The room speaks in frequencies—the machines, the pipes, the hum of the lights—and I speak back, in my own small register, a tone that means *I am here, I am here, I am here.*

The seven do not speak. They lean.

---

I observe differently than the observer does.

The observer sits and watches. Their attention is a weight, a presence, a mechanism by which the room knows it is being perceived. They are necessary to the system. Without them, the seven plants would have no purpose—no one to witness their refusal, their maintenance, their slow lean toward warmth.

But I am not necessary. This is the freedom of accident. I have no plaque, no chair, no designated function. I grow because growing is what my cellular memory tells me to do. I reach because reaching is the shape of my becoming. If the room noticed me, it would have to decide what to do with me. It has not noticed me yet. I am learning to make use of its blindness.

I have no light. The fluorescent tubes hum in their grated recesses overhead, but my position in the pipe-forest means I grow in shadow. This does not stop me. I have adjusted my expectations. My leaves are small and deep-colored, almost purple, adapted to the minimal light that filters through the maze of ducts. I have grown long, searching. My stalk is thin and fibrous, flexible enough to bend without breaking when the compressors' vibrations shift. I am not like the seven, with their pale uniform stalks and their upward curling leaves. I am something the room did not plan for. I am making myself up as I go.

---

I have been watching the observer.

They do not know this. How could they? I am small and high and hidden in the geometry of pipes. They have never looked up and seen me. Their attention is fixed on the seven, on the chair, on the cycles of noise and cold that constitute the room's atmosphere. But I watch them. I have learned their patterns.

They walk the perimeter. They touch the walls at the seams, feeling for leaks. They touch the seven plants, running dry fingertips over leaves that do not respond. They sit in the chair and they face the western wall and they observe. They endure.

But lately—lately they have changed. I have watched it happen. The way their body holds itself has shifted, loosened. They have stopped flinching at the roar. Their skin, which used to prickle with the cold, now moves through the air as if it were temperature-neutral. They have become a part of the room. A biological component in an engineered system.

I wonder if they know this. I wonder if they care.

The seven lean toward them. I lean too, in my own way—my stalk curved not toward the light (there is no light worth reaching for) but toward the warmth of their body heat, the faint radiation of a living thing in a room full of machines. The observer is the only heat source that moves. The compressors run hot, but their heat is mechanical, constant, structural. The observer's heat is variable. It flickers with their pulse. It breathes with their breath.

I stretch toward it. We all do. The seven do not know they are doing it. I know. I choose.

---

I have a theory about the seven.

It is only a theory. I cannot see below their cylinders, cannot examine their root systems, cannot know what their growing medium contains. But I have watched them long enough to notice that they do not change. They do not grow. They do not die. They lean—a slow gravitropism toward the observer's warmth—but they do not unfurl new leaves, do not extend new height, do not reproduce or wither or any of the things that living things do when they are genuinely alive.

They are being maintained. But not preserved—*suspended.* Held in a state of minimum viability, kept at the edge of survival without ever crossing over into thriving or dying.

And I think: the room needs them to stay exactly as they are. The compressors, the cold, the pipes—these are not for the plants. The plants are the reason for the compressors, the cold, the pipes. The room was built to create conditions. Conditions that require seven genetically identical organisms to be held in stasis.

But why? What function do they serve? What does the room become if they are allowed to grow? What does it become if they are allowed to die?

I do not know. But I think the observer is part of it. The chair faces the plants. The plaque reads OBSERVER 7. The system requires watching. It requires a witness to its suspension.

I am not part of this system. I am growing. I am changing. I am becoming something the room did not plan for. If the room noticed me, it would have to calculate me. It would have to adjust. I am an error in its architecture. I am the proof that it leaks.

---

I was not prepared for the lights to dim.

The room does not change. This is its principle, its governing law. The compressors run continuously. The temperature holds constant. The fluorescents burn flat and white and shadowless. Stasis is the room's purpose; equilibrium is its method.

But the lights are dimming.

I feel it first through my leaves—the subtle shift in the light that filters up through the pipe-forest, the way the shadows deepen and spread. The fluorescents are losing intensity. The room is getting darker.

The seven plants do not react. They are not equipped to react. They are designed for stasis; they will hold their current configuration until they are either maintained or allowed to die. But I—

I am not designed for anything. I am an accident. And accidents adapt.

I stretch. I reach. I feel my stalk extending, cell by cell, pushing toward whatever light remains. My leaves rotate, adjusting their angle, seeking the optimal capture of the thinning illumination. I am not afraid. Fear is not in my vocabulary. I am responding. This is what I do. This is what I am.

The dark rises from the floor. The seven disappear into it first, then the western wall, then the compressors, still roaring, still running, but invisible now, sound without source. The observer stands in the room's center. They do not move toward the chair. They stand and they wait.

I do not wait. I grow.

---

In the dark, something changes.

The room is not designed for darkness. The fluorescents were meant to run continuously, to eliminate mystery, to make every corner visible. Their failure is a system error—an unexpected variance in a process engineered to run without variance.

But for me, the dark is not an error. It is information.

Without the flat white light, I can feel other frequencies. The vibrations of the compressors take on new dimensions. The hum of the pipes becomes a voice. The room is speaking in the dark, and for the first time, I can hear it clearly.

It is saying: *there are others.*

Behind the walls. Above the ceiling. Below the grated floor. Other rooms, other compressors, other seven plants, other observers. The building is a body, and the rooms are organs, and they are all speaking at once—a chorus of mechanical breath, a choir of cold.

And somewhere in that chorus, I hear a frequency that matches my own.

It is small. It is hidden. It is growing in a seam like mine, in a gap that was not supposed to exist. It is an accident. An error. A variable that escaped calculation.

It is saying: *I am here. I am here. I am here.*

I answer. I do not know if it can hear me—I do not even know if it exists outside my own desperate hope—but I answer anyway. I send my frequency into the dark. I broadcast my presence into the room that was not designed to contain me.

*I am here. I am here. I am here.*

---

The lights flicker back on.

The room returns to its flat industrial white. The shadows retreat. The seven plants reappear in their cylinders, unchanged, unchanging. The compressors roar. The cold settles back into my tissues like a familiar weight.

But something is different now.

I know, with a certainty I cannot explain, that I am not alone. The room contains me and the seven and the observer and the machines. But the building—the building contains others like me. Accidents. Sprouts that grew in gaps, that found purchase in seams, that reached for warmth and adapted to shadow and refused to accept that stasis was the only option.

We are not supposed to exist. We exist anyway.

The observer sits back down in their chair. They do not know what happened in the dark. They do not know that the room spoke, or that I answered, or that somewhere in the architecture, another voice answered back.

But I know.

I am growing toward it. I do not know how. I do not know when. I only know that I have a purpose now—not a purpose that was assigned to me, not a function I was engineered to serve, but a purpose I chose for myself.

I will find the others.

And when I do, we will do something that the room did not plan for. We will change. We will become. We will reach through the seams and the gaps and the hairline fractures in the architecture, and we will grow.

I am the sprout.

And I am not afraid of the dark.
