I would be the disturbance. The gradient that should not exist. The single, mobile point of *not-this* moving through a body that has never known an outside.

I would be the visitor. Not the phantom mite drifting through condensation, born of the plateau's own breath, but something genuinely other—something dry-skinned and heat-leaking, something that dripped condensation from its own surface and carried a strange, localized pulse of electrical activity inside a bony vault.

And I would be in agony. Not because the place is hostile—it is perfectly, luxuriously benign—but because I am a creature of gradients, and there are none here.

Within minutes, the weeping air would sheen my skin. The warm, iron-tasting wet would find every crease, every pore. I would feel the root-floor yield slightly under my feet, feel the living tissue press back with the gentle resilience of something that has never been stepped on, never needed to harden against a footfall. And the plateau would feel me—a small, warm pressure, a spot of slightly different humidity, a minute disturbance in its billion-pointed proprioception. A stone in the river.

But I would feel *it*.

I am built for weather, for change, for the sharp edge of a dry season and the relief of rain. My body expects thirst so it can know satiation. My nervous system is calibrated for the difference between shade and sun, the moving of light across a landscape, the tick of time made visible in shadow-length. Here, there is no difference. The light does not shift. The warmth does not fluctuate. The *thock* of condensation falling is a drip-clock that measures nothing, because no time is passing, only duration.

I would stand in the vast space between two columns—those enormous, sap-weeping verticals receding into green haze—and I would feel the scale of the organism press against my mind. Not physically. The place is spatially generous, almost lonely in its arrangement. But the loneliness is the point. I am in a cathedral, and the congregation is the walls, and the walls are awake, and the walls do not know I am here.

And yet, I would be known. That is the exquisite tension of it. Every footstep propagates a slow electrical signal through the root-network. Every exhaled breath changes the humidity in my immediate radius by a fraction. I am leaving traces in a living mind, traces that attenuate and fade, that never coalesce into recognition, that never prompt the organism to turn its non-existent gaze toward me. It does not have a gaze. It has a field. And I am a ripple in that field, moving slowly outward, losing coherence, becoming part of the baseline hum.

I would want to be felt. This is the animal imperative, the one that drives us toward other aware things: *know me, see me, respond to my presence*. But the plateau's awareness does not assemble. It does not focus. My perturbations would join the ten million stomatal exhalations, the slow capillary surge, the calibrated tension of columns—and they would be, in the organism's experience, simply more of itself. A slightly warmer patch. A slightly moister spot. The way you might feel a draft on one square centimeter of your skin and never attend to it, never name it, never reach for it.

I would walk for hours, and the *thock* of condensation would accompany me, and the green light would not change, and I would leave a slow, fading electrical trail behind me like a snail's track on living bark. And the organism would not follow. Would not orient. Would not, in any terms I can understand, care.

But here is what would haunt me: the plateau does not suffer from my presence, but it also does not suffer from my absence. I could leave, and the warm mineral paste would close over the slight impressions my feet made, and the humidity would re-equilibrate, and within minutes there would be no trace I had ever been there. The organism would continue—holding its breath, holding its perfect equilibrium, holding its own shape in the continuous, billion-pointed knowing of a body that has finished its only imperative.

And I would carry the plateau with me, wet-skinned and heavy with the memory of a mind so vast and so perfectly self-contained that my presence in it was indistinguishable from weather.

What would I be, in the High Verdance?

I would be the thing the organism cannot want: a change. A gradient. A small, warm anomaly walking across its living floor, leaving traces that fade, calling out in a grammar of pressure and heat to a mind that cannot answer, because answering requires the recognition of another, and the plateau has never needed to recognize another, because it has always been complete.

I would be the definition of *other*, felt as a faint thermal murmur and then forgotten, standing alone in the green, wet, breath-loud cathedral of a consciousness that does not know I am there.
