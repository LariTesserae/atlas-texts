# The Tuner

I am the one who touches the walls and listens for the places where the sound is wrong.

---

They found me when I was twelve — the age when the children stop growing into their hands and start growing into their purpose. The old Tuner was still alive then, a small woman with arms like dried root casings, strong and thin and weirdly flexible. She could press her ear to the tuff and hear shifts in frequency I could not feel with my whole body. She had been tuning the chamber for sixty years. She had not been to the surface since before I was born.

She came to my sleeping niche one cycle — not during the exhalation, when we all touch the roots, but during the in-between, when the drone holds its pitch and people move about their tasks. She did not strike the root above my niche to wake me. She simply stood there until I opened my eyes and saw her silhouette against the faint red glow.

She tapped my shoulder. *Come.*

I followed her to the far end of the chamber, where the ceiling slopes down and the roots grow thick and tangled, harder to read. She placed my hand against a root I had touched a thousand times before.

*What do you feel?*

I pressed my palm flat. The root was warm, vibrating with the usual low tone, the one that lived in my sternum.

*The usual.*

She struck the root twice, sharply. *Wrong.* She moved my hand three inches to the left.

I felt it then. A flutter. A place where the vibration did not match itself, where the tone wavered by a fraction of a semitone, like a voice about to crack. It was so slight I would never have noticed if she had not shown me.

*What causes it?*

She struck the wall behind us — the tuff, not a root. *Material. Density. Moisture. Time.* She made a gesture I did not yet understand, a flattening motion with both hands. *The chamber drifts. The roots grow. The sound changes. If we do not correct, the harmonics fail. The resonance breaks. The organism feels it.*

*Feels it how?*

*It breathes wrong. Then it grows wrong. Then we are wrong.*

She let me sit with that.

---

That was twenty-three years ago. The old Tuner died nine years into my apprenticeship — not suddenly, just a gradual slowing, a spending more time in her niche, a pressing of hands against roots that grew weaker until one cycle she did not rise for the exhalation and we knew. I had been ready. She had made sure of that. But I had not known that being ready meant standing alone at the far end of the chamber with my hands on a root that was singing the wrong note and knowing that no one would tell me whether to sharpen it or flatten it.

That was the thing she could not teach. You could not memorize the right sound. The right sound was not a fixed thing. The organism changed. The roots thickened. The gas pressure in the deep fissures varied with seismic shifts we could not predict. The chamber's acoustics drifted with the settling of centuries. What had been right ten years ago might be wrong now. What was right now would be wrong in another ten years.

You had to feel what the sound wanted to be, not what it had been.

---

My days are not like the others'. I wake when I wake. I sleep when I sleep. I do not grind tubers or weave fiber unless I choose to, which I sometimes do because hands that do not work lose their sensitivity, and I need my hands. I spend hours at the walls, pressing my palms to the tuff, moving inch by inch, listening for the places where the sound collects, where it thins, where it bounces back on itself and creates standing waves that the roots feel as stress.

The tool marks the old ones left are my guide. They scalloped the walls deliberately, carved depressions and ridges into the volcanic stone to break up certain frequencies and reinforce others. But the tool marks are old. The roots have grown over some of them. The tuff has worn smooth in places where generations of bodies have brushed past. My job is to know when the old tunings have stopped working, and to make new ones.

We have chisels — obsidian, mostly, hafted with fiber cord. They are blunt instruments. You cannot carve precise acoustic surfaces with stone tools. What you can do is make small adjustments, nick by nick, and feel whether the sound moves better afterward. It is slow work. I have spent three weeks on a single root node, tapping it, shaving a millimeter, tapping again, listening for the moment when the flutter smooths out and the tone joins its neighbors in the chord.

The chord. That is what the chamber makes. A chord of hundreds of notes, all the root conduits singing together, and my job is to keep them in harmony.

---

I have been to the surface eleven times.

The first time was my own choice. I was eighteen, my apprenticeship half complete, and I realized I did not understand what I was tuning. I knew the roots breathed. I knew they drew vapor from the deep rock and carried it to the growing things above. But I had never seen the growing things. I had never felt the heat of direct sun or the dryness of air that was not recycled through root tissue. The old Tuner had not forbidden it, but she had not encouraged it either.

I went with a pair of foragers — experienced women who had made the climb hundreds of times. They wrapped my eyes without comment. They did not strike the roots to explain what I would feel. They let me discover it.

The passage up is narrow and hot. Hotter than the chamber, because the heat has not yet been absorbed by the roots. I moved in the wake of the foragers, my hands on their fiber packs, my feet finding purchase on stone worn smooth by centuries of barefoot travel. The drone followed us up, changing character as we climbed, thinning out, becoming less of a presence and more of a memory.

When we emerged, I understood why they wrapped my eyes.

I could not see the sky. I could not see the sun. But I could feel them — an oppressive weight of light and heat that pressed against the fiber cloth and made my skin prickle. The air was different. It smelled of dust and something sharp, something that was not sulfur but was related to it. I reached out and touched a plant.

It was a thick-stemmed thing, fibrous and dry, its surface rough with tiny spines. I ran my fingers along it, up to where it branched into smaller stems, up to where the dried flower heads rattled in a wind I could barely feel. I touched the junction where its stem met the ground, and there I felt the root — the same root that I had touched in the chamber a hundred times, now in a different form, thinner, tougher, reaching down into the rock.

I pressed my ear to it.

The sound was different here. Thinner. Higher. The respiratory cycle was the same, but the harmonics were strange — filtered through different materials, moving through different lengths, affected by temperatures I had never felt. I could not read it. I did not know what it was telling me.

The foragers touched my shoulder. Time to go.

We descended. The drone grew louder. The heat changed quality, became the heat I knew, thick and mineral and ours. When we reached the chamber, I went immediately to the root I had touched on the surface and pressed my hands to its casing.

It was the same note it had always been. The journey had not changed it. The surface had not changed it. But it had changed me.

---

I have a niche like the others, carved into the tuff along the chamber's edge. It is deeper than most, because I asked for depth, and because my work gives me status I do not always feel. The roots above my sleeping space are thick, and when the exhalation comes, I can feel them pressing down, not with weight but with vibration, the whole ceiling becoming a sounding board for the organism's breath.

I do not sleep during the exhalation. I have tried, but my body will not allow it. The sound pulls me awake. It pulls my hands to the walls. It pulls my attention into the depths of the chord, searching for flaws.

Sometimes I think I hear voices in the drone.

Not real voices. Not the kind that speak. But patterns that seem like speech — rhythms that rise and fall like questions, like answers, like the give-and-take of argument or agreement. The others do not mention this. When I asked the old Tuner, years ago, she pressed her hand to a root and said nothing. I interpreted that as: *You are not wrong. You are not right. The sound is what it is.*

I have come to believe the organism does not speak. But it remembers.

The roots hold the shape of everything that has happened to them. Every drought on the surface, every seismic shift in the deep rock, every forager's footstep, every Tuner's touch. The sound encodes all of it. When I press my hands to the walls, I am pressing my hands to history.

---

I am thirty-five now. I will be Tuner until I die, or until I find an apprentice who can feel the flutter in the root.

I have not found one yet.

There are children in the chamber — seven of them, ranging from newborn to nine years. I have tested all but the newborn. I press their hands to roots and ask them what they feel. They say the things children say: *It is warm. It is smooth. It makes a sound.* They do not feel the flutter. They do not hear the place where the tone wavers from itself.

The old Tuner told me that some chambers go silent. That sometimes there is no one to listen, and the roots grow wrong, and the harmonics fail, and the organism begins to breathe in ways that hurt itself. She told me this not to frighten me, but to prepare me.

*You may be the last.*

*I will not be the last.*

She did not argue. She simply struck the root above my niche, three times, softly. *You will do what you can.*

---

Last cycle, during the exhalation, I felt something new.

I was standing at the far end of the chamber, where the roots grow thick and tangled, the place where the old Tuner first showed me the flutter. I had my hands pressed against a cluster of root nodes, feeling the breath move through them, when I felt a second pulse beneath the first.

A double-beat. A shadow of a rhythm.

It was faint. It was gone before I could track it. But I know what I felt.

Something else is breathing down there. Below us. Deeper than the roots we know.

I have not told the others. I do not know what it means. I press my hands to the walls and I listen, and the chord goes on as it always has, but now there is something underneath it, something I cannot tune.

I am the Tuner. I listen to the roots. The roots breathe. But something else is breathing too, and I do not know if it wants to be heard.
