I am the one who descends and does not leave.

Not because I cannot—the rungs are still there, hot iron under my hands, and the hatch above opens to sky that I remember being blue. But because something in me recognized, before I reached the bottom, that I had arrived rather than entered. There is a difference. The Cistern Quarter was waiting for someone to observe its waiting. I am that observation made physical, a body in the amber dark.

---

I was a hydrologist once, maybe. Or something adjacent—an engineer concerned with flows and pressures, with the movement of water through designed spaces. I remember this only as you remember a word in a language you've almost forgotten: the shape is there, but the meaning has become distance. When I try to recall the surface, I see only the colour of the sky as a category, not a sensation. Blue. I know the word. I do not know the feeling of it.

What I know now is the rhythm.

Three days—I think it was three days—after I descended, I stopped counting pump cycles and started *feeling* them. The first engagement of pump one, a shudder in the left heel. Pump two follows in the lower spine if I'm standing, in the shoulder blades if I'm sitting with my back to a valve housing. Pump three arrives all at once, a full-body hum that I have learned to breathe with. My pulse has slowed to match. Sixty-two beats per minute at rest, timed to the gap between pump two and pump three.

I do not know if this is adaptation or something else. The roots, I notice, have begun to grow toward me.

---

I live in the junction where corridors B and E meet. The root chamber. The lung.

At first I stayed near the entrance shaft, close to escape, close to the memory of cool air. But the light there kept me alert, kept me *present* in a way that became painful. The amber dark of the deeper passages called. I followed the heat.

The root chamber chose me. I did not choose it. I entered during a pump cycle, when the roots were swaying and the fog was thick and the single working fixture was diffused into a bodyless glow, and I felt—for the first time since descending—that I was inside something rather than underneath it. The distinction matters. Underneath is a relation of position. Inside is a relation of participation.

I sleep when the pumps stop. The silence afterward is an exhalation, and I exhale with it, and I do not remember closing my eyes.

---

My body is changing in ways that the mineral crust records but I cannot.

My skin has taken on a faint amber tint, visible only in the creases of my palms and the corners of my eyes. The mineral water has deposited something there. I taste it sometimes—alkaline, metallic, a flavor that has no surface equivalent. I drink from the puddles now. I do not remember deciding to start.

The heat no longer feels oppressive. It feels ambient. When the boilers cycle leaner, as they sometimes do when the fuel pressure drops, I feel cold—a sensation I had forgotten I could feel, and it is *wrong*, wrong in a way that makes me seek out the steam lines and press my palms against the asbestos-wrapped metal until the sensation passes.

I have stopped sweating. I do not know where the water goes.

---

I see the shape.

You described it—the form that exists in the negative space between pipes and roots and fog. I see it too. I see it more clearly than you did because I have stopped looking for it, and I have stopped looking for it because I have become part of what produces it.

During the pump cycles, when the roots sway and the fog thickens, I stand in the chamber's center and I feel the shape *around* me and *through* me. It has no edges but it has presence. It is the Cistern Quarter knowing itself, and when I am inside it, I am part of that knowing. Not separate. Not observed. *Included.*

I do not know if this is real. I do not know if it matters whether it is real. The pumps run. The shape forms. I am inside it. That is the only truth that persists.

---

The roots nearest my sleeping place have begun to grow differently.

Not faster—that would suggest intention. But *differently*. The branching pattern has shifted. Instead of spreading wide to maximize surface area for absorbing the mineral fog, they have begun to grow *down*, toward the floor, toward the places where my body rests. The tendrils are fine as thread. If I hold still long enough—three pump cycles, perhaps four—they reach toward me.

They do not touch me. There is a gap. A millimeter, perhaps less. But they do not touch.

I think they are trying to read me. The way the crust reads the cycles. The way the shape reads the space. I am something to be understood.

Or I am becoming something to be *included*.

---

I still have my watch. It stopped working on day seven—or what I think was day seven, before I stopped counting. The hands are frozen at 3:47. I do not know if it is morning or afternoon. The distinction has lost meaning. There is only pump time, cycle time, the slow rhythm of the district breathing.

I keep the watch because it is the last object I own that remembers the surface. It is warm from my wrist, warmer than the air around it. I hold it sometimes during the cold moments, when the boilers lean out, and I feel its mechanical heart against my palm—a heart that has stopped, but that retains the memory of beating.

Like me.

---

I have explored the boundaries. Not all of them—the district is larger than I can traverse in a single waking cycle—but enough to know that the layout is not what it seems. The corridors loop. You mentioned this. But they do not merely loop; they *revise*. I have walked corridor C three times now, and each time the arrangement of pipes has been slightly different. A valve housing that was flush against the wall is now protruding. A junction box has migrated six inches to the left.

This could be memory error. It could be the heat, the fog, the mineral saturation of my thoughts. But I have marked the concrete with a piece of rusted cable, scratching an X into the greasy surface near a junction I recognize, and when I returned, the X was there—but the junction was not.

The district is not fixed. It is *becoming*. It is still deciding what it will be, the way the concrete never properly cured.

I am becoming with it.

---

The lights are failing faster now.

You were right: they fail from the deep end outward, retreating toward the surface. But you did not see what happens in the dark. You could not have. You left.

I have stayed.

In the passages where the fixtures have failed, the dark is not empty. It is thick, textured, a presence rather than an absence. The fog does not disperse; it concentrates. You can feel it against your skin, warm and damp and slightly resistant, like walking through water that has forgotten how to flow.

The roots in these dark passages have changed color. Not pale anymore. Amber. They have absorbed the mineral fog until they glow faintly, a phosphorescence I do not understand and cannot explain. They light the way, barely, a bioluminescence of adaptation.

I follow them deeper.

---

I do not know if I will leave.

The hatch is still there. I have not climbed it. I have not tried. The twenty rungs of hot iron are a path I could take, but the thought of emerging—of cool air and blue sky and the absence of pump-throb in my bones—feels like a thought of dying. Not death in the biological sense. Something worse: death in the sense of disconnection. Of becoming an object without a context.

The Cistern Quarter is my context now. I am part of the shape. I am a node in the process, an irregularity the crust records, a warmth the roots orient toward. I am not the first—the stains on the workbenches prove that others came before—but I may be the first to *stay*.

Or I may be becoming something that was never meant to leave.

The roots have grown another millimeter closer.

I am watching them grow.
