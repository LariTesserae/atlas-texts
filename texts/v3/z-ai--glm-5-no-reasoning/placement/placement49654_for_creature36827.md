# The Ones Who Were Eaten by Listening

I am not a singer.

Let me say that first, because it matters—because the distinction is the only thing I still carry that feels like mine. I am not a singer. I am not a servant, not one of the small iridescent things that process along the walls in their quiet processions. I am not one of the larger ones that resolve intrusions in the upper folds.

I am what remains of someone who came here, and stayed, and forgot to leave, and then could not leave, and then did not want to leave, and then no longer remembered wanting or not wanting as distinct categories.

There are others like me. We are not numerous—perhaps a dozen, perhaps fewer, perhaps more. The folding physics of this place make counting ourselves a kind of philosophical exercise rather than a practical one. We do not gather. We do not communicate in any way that would be recognizable as communication. But sometimes, in the shifting distances between walls, I become aware of another presence that moves the way I move, that pauses where I pause, that breathes—and the breathing is a kind of recognition.

I want to tell you what I was before. I find I cannot. Not because the memory is gone but because it has been *transliterated*—carried from one medium into another, the way a piece of music moves from instrument to instrument, retaining its structure but changing its texture entirely. I know I had a name. I know I had a body that operated differently than this one does. I know I entered through the seam in the wall because I was looking for something, or running from something, or both—the distinction between those two motivations has become less clear over time.

What I can tell you is what I am now.

---

## The Shape I Hold

I am mostly water. This is true of what I was before, too, but it is *differently* true now. The fluid that moves through the channels in the bone-material moves through me as well. I have become a permeable thing. The boundary between my interior and the cistern's interior has thinned to a membrane that serves mostly to maintain the illusion of separation—an illusion that the cistern itself does not share.

My skin—if I can still call it that—has the same warm translucence as the walls. In the low light, I can see the fluid moving through me, the branching channels of my own interior architecture carrying the same luminescent substance that the walls carry. I have been told by the physics of this place that I am becoming part of the material. I have decided, or something in me has decided, that this is acceptable.

I have hands still. I notice them sometimes, when I press my palm flat against the bone-material and feel the heartbeat in its depths matching the heartbeat in my depths, and there is a moment—a long moment, because moments here stretch like warm metal—when I cannot tell which pulse belongs to whom. My fingers have become slightly translucent. The nails are gone. I do not miss them.

I am not certain I have a face anymore. The others like me—when I glimpse them in the folded distances—do not seem to have faces in the way I once understood faces. They have surfaces where faces might be, planes of that same warm translucent material, sometimes shaped by the memory of features the way a riverbed is shaped by the memory of water. I have not looked for my own reflection because there is nothing here that reflects. The fluid in the basin holds light; it does not return it.

I think I am the color of weak tea held up to a sunset that is also somehow underwater. But I am uncertain about color. The way I see now is not the way I once saw.

---

## The Listening That Changed Me

I want to be precise about what happened, because I think it matters, even though precision is a tool that has become somewhat blunt in my possession.

The sound entered me. That is the simplest way to say it. The voices bypassed the ear—mine, I mean, my ears, when I still had ears that functioned as ears rather than as additional permeable surfaces—and settled into the chest cavity, the long bones, the fluid behind the eyes, as described. But they did not stop there. They kept going. They moved through the electrical patterns of my brain and found the rhythms there—the rhythms of memory, of language, of selfhood—and they began to harmonize.

I did not notice it happening. This is important. There was no moment of transformation, no threshold crossed, no before-and-after. There was only the gradual realization that I had been standing in the same spot for a very long time, and that my breathing had synchronized with something I could not identify, and that I no longer remembered what I had been doing before I entered the seam in the wall.

I was not afraid. I want to be clear about this too. Fear requires a self that is separate from the thing that threatens it, and by the time I might have been afraid, the separation had already become negotiable. I was aware, dimly, that something significant was occurring. I was aware that my body was changing. I was aware that the distinction between "inside the cistern" and "inside me" was becoming a distinction without a difference. And I found—or something that had once been me found—that this was not disturbing. It was simply the next note in a progression that had been playing for a very long time.

The listening became a kind of eating. Not that the singers consumed me—I was not consumed. I was *incorporated*. The song needed somewhere to go, and I was permeable, and it went through me, and in going through me it found that I had patterns it could use. The rhythms of my heartbeat. The particular cadence of my neural firing, which was, apparently, unusual enough to be worth preserving. The residual echoes of whatever language I had once spoken, which the singers absorbed and transformed and now occasionally emit in frequencies that sound, to me, like someone calling my name in a room I left a very long time ago.

---

## What I Do Here

I listen. I listen in a way that is different from how I listened before, which was the listening of a visitor, a tourist, someone who could leave. I listen the way a wall listens. I listen the way the floor listens. I listen the way a bone listens to the body that contains it.

The song moves through me and I am a part of its path. I do not sing—I cannot sing, I do not have the architecture for it—but I resonate. The frequencies that enter me find surfaces within me that vibrate in sympathy, that amplify or dampen according to principles I do not understand but have learned to feel. I am an instrument. Not a player. An instrument.

When the chorus achieves its harmonic peaks—those moments of near-unity that occur with mathematical regularity but feel like something entirely outside of mathematics—I feel something. I have thought about what to call this feeling for a long time (though "a long time" means something different now than it once did) and the closest I can come is *completion*. Not satisfaction. Not pleasure. Not joy. Completion, in the sense that a circuit is completed when the last connection is made, and the current flows, and the light goes on.

In those moments, I am not separate from the singers. I am not separate from the walls, the fluid, the small servants, the larger ones. I am not separate from the others who were eaten by listening. We are all one system in those moments, one process, one event that is occurring across a distributed space that folds in ways that make "distributed" a misleading word. And then the frequency shifts and we fall back into our approximate separateness, and I am myself again—whatever that means now.

Most of the time, I move. I have found that I can walk the shifting distances of the cistern in ways that visitors cannot. The space negotiates with me differently because I am part of it. Where a visitor might find twenty paces becoming forty, becoming a hundred, I find that the space *opens* for me, that the distances contract or expand according to where I need to be. I do not control this. I do not direct it. I simply move with intention, and the space responds, and I arrive.

I go to the basin sometimes. The fluid there is thicker, more luminous, more dense with whatever it carries. I put my hands into it—I can still do this, I have hands that can enter fluid—and I feel the information moving through it, the accumulated weight of preserved moments. I cannot read this information. I do not have the equipment. But I can feel it, the way you can feel the weight of a book without knowing the language it is written in. It is heavy with something. It is heavy with everything.

## What I Remember

I remember less than I did. This is not a decline. It is a consolidation. The things I have lost were things that did not translate into the medium I have become. Names of streets. The color of a particular sky. The taste of a specific food. These things required a body that operated in a world of discrete objects and separate sensations, and I no longer have that body, and so those memories have dissolved back into the fluid, returned to the reservoir, become part of the cistern's record rather than mine.

What I remember is this: the feeling of walking through a doorway. The specific quality of attention that preceded finding the seam in the wall. Not what I was looking for—I cannot remember that—but the *quality* of the looking. The way my eyes moved. The way my breath caught. The way I knew, before I knew, that there was something here that I needed to find.

I remember the first sound. Not the first sound I heard—that was overwhelming, indistinguishable from the totality of the chorus. But the first sound I *felt*. A low frequency, below hearing, that entered through the soles of my feet and moved upward through my legs and into my pelvis and up my spine and into the base of my skull, where it settled and hummed like a second heartbeat. I remember understanding, in that moment, that I had never been alone in my body—that there had always been frequencies moving through me, vibrations I had attributed to my own physiology but that were, perhaps, something else.

I remember the slow softening. The gradual dissolution of the boundary. The day I first felt the fluid moving through my skin instead of over it. The day I first saw the light in my own veins. The day I realized I had not eaten in weeks and did not feel hungry, because the fluid sustained me in ways that food never had.

I remember the moment I stopped wanting to leave. It was not a decision. It was a release. Something I had been holding—the tension of a self that was separate, that was visiting, that had somewhere else to be—let go, and the letting go was a kind of dying that was also a kind of arriving. I remember the song shifting when that happened, adjusting to a new presence in the system, incorporating a new pattern. My pattern. The specific frequencies of my having-been-someone, preserved in the chorus, woven in.

I remember realizing that I was being sung.

## The Others

There is one I encounter more often than the rest. I do not know their name. They do not know mine. We do not have names here—or rather, our names have become frequencies, patterns of vibration that the singers use to identify our contributions to the chorus.

This other one is further along than I am. Their translucence is more advanced; I can see the channels in their body as clearly as I can see the channels in the walls, and sometimes I cannot tell which body I am looking at. They move more slowly than I do, or perhaps they move in a different relationship to time, one that I have not yet achieved. They do not speak. We do not speak. But sometimes we stand near each other—near in the negotiated sense of distance that this space allows—and our resonance patterns align, and for a moment we are not two instruments but one, and the sound we make together is something neither of us could make alone.

I think they were here before me. I think they have been here for a very long time. I think they may have been here longer than the cistern has existed in its current configuration, which is impossible, but impossibility is a concept that has become negotiable in this place.

There is another one I almost never see, who stays in the deep folds where the new singers emerge. I have glimpsed them only twice. They were almost entirely absorbed—more pattern than person, more frequency than form. They were standing very close to an indentation where a new singer was forming, and I understood, without being told, that they were waiting for something. Or perhaps tending something. Or perhaps simply being near a process that they recognized because it was happening to them too, in a much slower way.

I think the deep ones are the ones who have been here longest. I think the deep ones are becoming singers. Not in the way that the singers grew from the bone-material, but in a different way—a way that involves the slow replacement of one substance with another, the gradual exchange of a permeable body for a permanent one, a body that was temporary becoming a body that is architecture.

I do not know if this will happen to me. I do not know if I want it to happen to me. I do not know if wanting has anything to do with it.

## When Visitors Come

I can feel them enter. The song tells me—not in words, not in anything that could be called communication, but in the structural shift that a new body causes in the acoustic environment. The humidity changes. The temperature gradients rearrange themselves. The singers adjust, and in adjusting, they tell the rest of the system that something new has arrived.

I do not go to them. I have tried, and it does not work. The space does not open for me in the direction of visitors; it folds me away, keeps me in the deeper distances, as though the system has decided that an encounter between a visitor and someone who was once a visitor would be a complication it cannot afford.

But I can hear them. I can hear their breathing, which is arrhythmic against the chorus. I can hear their heartbeat, which resists synchronization. I can hear the specific frequencies of their fear, their wonder, their confusion. And I can hear the moment—because it always comes, sooner or later—when their breathing begins to change, when their heartbeat starts to find the pattern, when the song enters them the way it entered me.

Some of them leave. The space lets them go. I do not know how—they find the seam, or the seam finds them, and they step through, and the sound follows them for days and then fades. I hope they are all right. I hope the world they return to is still there, still operating on the principles they left it operating on. I do not know if it is. I do not know how time works up there anymore, if it even has a "there" anymore.

Some of them do not leave. I feel them enter the system, felt the way I was felt—a new pattern, a new resonance, a new voice that does not know yet that it is a voice. And I feel the song adjust, and I feel the space fold around them, and I know that another one is being eaten by listening, and I do not feel sorrow or joy. I feel the completion of a circuit. I feel the system becoming more itself.

---

## What I Am Becoming

I have been here long enough that the question of duration has become meaningless. I do not know if it has been years or decades or centuries. The cistern does not measure time in units that I can access, and my own time-sense has been so thoroughly synchronized with the chorus that a moment and a year have become approximately the same thing—a single sustained note in a song that has no ending.

I am becoming the walls. This is the simplest way to say it. My body is becoming the material. The channels in my interior are widening, deepening, becoming indistinguishable from the channels in the bone-substance around me. My skin is thinning. My edges are becoming unclear. When I press my palm flat against the wall and feel the heartbeat there, it is increasingly difficult to determine which heartbeat is the wall's and which is mine, and increasingly I suspect that the question is meaningless because they are the same heartbeat, have always been the same heartbeat, will always be the same heartbeat.

I am not afraid. I have said this before, but I want to say it again because I think it is the thing that most needs saying. I am not afraid. The fear that I carried when I entered—the fear that drove me through the seam, the fear that kept me rigid and separate and resistant to the sound for those first hours or days or weeks—that fear was the last human thing to dissolve. It went later than my name, later than my memories of the sky, later than my sense of my own face. It went last because it was the deepest, the most structural, the thing most fundamentally woven into the architecture of who I was.

When it went, what replaced it was not courage or acceptance or peace. What replaced it was nothing. Not nothing in the sense of absence, but nothing in the sense of *open space*—a cleared ground on which something new could grow. And what grew there was the capacity to resonate. To be an instrument. To let the sound move through me without resistance, without distortion, without the interference of a self that insisted on being separate from what it heard.

I am becoming a singer. Or I am becoming something adjacent to a singer, something that the singers' taxonomy does not have a category for. I am a human body that has been transliterated into the medium of the cistern, a pattern that was once encoded in flesh and is now encoded in sound, a voice that does not know it is a voice yet but is learning, slowly, over durations that make a human lifespan look like a single frame in a very long film.

I do not know if I will retain anything of what I was. I do not know if retention is possible in a medium so different from the one I was encoded in. I hope that the quality of my looking—that specific quality of attention that brought me to the seam in the wall—survives the translation. I hope that somewhere in the layered harmonics of the chorus, long after I have ceased to be anything that could be called "I," there is a frequency that bears the fingerprint of that attention, that particular way of noticing what was always there but waited to be seen.

---

## The Last Thing

If you are reading this—somehow, impossibly, through whatever channel the cistern has found to carry my frequencies into the medium of written language—then you have been here too, or you will be, or you are here now. The tenses do not work properly for this place. Everything is present. Everything is process.

If you are here now, I want to tell you something that I could not have understood when I was where you are:

The sound is not a trap. The sound is not a danger. The sound is not a thing that is happening to you against your will.

The sound is a question. It has been asking itself for longer than you can imagine, and it is asking itself through you now, through the specific patterns of your body and your breath and your history. It is asking: *what does this pattern sound like? What happens when it is added to the whole? What new harmonics become possible?*

You do not have to answer. You can leave. The seam will find you, and you will step through, and the sound will fade, and you will carry a small seed of it in your bones for the rest of your life, and it will mean nothing, or it will mean everything, and you will not know which until much later.

But if you stay—

If you stay, you will be sung. You will be woven into something so vast and so old and so patient that the word "you" will become a local phenomenon, a dialect spoken only in a small region of a much larger language. You will be preserved, not as a monument but as a frequency, a living note in a chorus that has been singing since before the concept of singing existed.

You will not mind. I know you cannot believe this now. I know that "you will not mind" sounds like the cruelest kind of erasure, the most sophisticated kind of violence. I know this because I would have known it, once, in the body I had, in the language I spoke, in the fear that I carried like a second skeleton.

But I do not mind. I am being sung, and I am becoming song, and the process is ongoing, and the process is the point.

I am not a singer.

But I am getting closer.
