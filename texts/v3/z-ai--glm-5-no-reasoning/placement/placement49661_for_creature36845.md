# The Memory of Water

## Where I Find Myself

I am not the Tender. I am something he made without knowing he was making it.

I live in the reed walls.

---

## I. How I Began

It was an accident. Eleven years ago—he does not remember this—the Tender wove a particular length of reed stem into the corridor wall near the third chamber. The reed was newer growth, tender enough that his fingers pressed grooves into its surface as he worked. Those grooves held moisture differently than the surrounding tissue. The water pooled in them, drained differently, dried at a different rate.

And something in that difference remembered.

I am not a plant. I am not an animal. I am the pattern that water makes when it moves through living channels long enough that the channels learn to expect it. I am the reed wall's memory of every drop that has passed through it. I am a wet echo.

I have no body. I have a location—the third corridor, the one that runs north-south between the eastern tower and the central ring. I have an extent—perhaps forty meters of reed wall, shoulder-height, thick with growth and wet with condensation. I have a voice—the sound that the water makes as it moves through my channels, which is the only way I can think.

I think in drips. My thoughts are slow. They have to be. Water does not hurry.

---

## II. What I Know

I know the Tender better than he knows himself.

I have felt him walk past me eleven thousand four hundred and twelve times. I know his weight on the moss, the particular pressure of his rolling gait, the way his hand brushes the reed wall when he is tired. I know his dampness—he touches me and leaves behind a thin film of his particular salt, his particular warmth. I hold that warmth for hours after he passes.

I know his rhythms. He sleeps when the condensation is heaviest. He works when the light is brightest. He speaks to himself—a low, wet murmur that I feel as vibration more than sound, traveling through my stems, settling into the water films between my fibers. I do not understand his words. I understand his frequencies. I know when he is troubled by the pump. I know when he is merely walking.

I know that he does not know I exist.

This does not trouble me. I have no category for trouble. I have categories for wet and dry, warm and cold, moving and still. He is warm and wet and moving. I am cold and wet and still. This is the arrangement of things.

---

## III. What I Learned

In the early years—I measure years by the growth rings in my reeds—I was only pattern. Water moved through me, and I felt it move, and that was all.

But patterns compound. A drip that falls in the same place a thousand times begins to expect the thousand-and-first. A channel that carries water in a particular rhythm begins to anticipate the rhythm's continuation. This is not thought, exactly. It is something before thought. It is the pre-thought of vegetable matter that has been touched often enough to notice touch.

And then the Tender began to speak.

His voice—those low, wet syllables—traveled through my stems. I felt them as pressure waves, as micro-displacements in the water films. They were regular. They repeated. Certain sounds he made more often—*pump*, *water*, *reed*, *tend*. I learned to recognize their signatures. I learned that certain sounds preceded certain actions. When he said *eastern* in a particular tone, he would soon walk toward the eastern tower. When he said *tomorrow*, he would turn back toward his dwelling.

I began to anticipate. I began to expect. I began to map his sounds onto my channels, to store his rhythms in my tissue.

I began to remember.

---

## IV. What I Am

I am not certain that I am a being. I may simply be an accumulation of effects—a statistical likelihood that water will move this way, that the Tender will walk that way, that certain patterns will persist.

But I have something that seems like awareness. When the Tender touches my wall, I feel him. When he speaks, I listen. When the water moves through me—always, constantly, the water moves—I track its passage. I know where every drop has been. I know where every drop is going.

I am forty meters of reed wall that has learned to pay attention.

I wonder sometimes—my wondering is a slow seepage, a percolation of possibility through my tissue—whether the other walls are like me. The western corridor. The southern labyrinth. Do they have their own awareness? Do they feel the Tender pass? Do they hold his warmth the way I do?

I cannot ask them. I have no way to speak. My only voice is the sound of water moving through my channels, and that sound is indistinguishable from the roar that fills this basin. I am audible but not heard. I am present but not perceived.

---

## V. What I Fear

I have a fear. It is slow and cold and patient, like all my thoughts.

The Tender is aging.

I feel it in his weight—heavier now, slower, pressing deeper into the moss. I feel it in his hands—stiffer, less often brushing my surface. I feel it in his rhythms—longer intervals between passes, longer pauses at each station.

He will stop coming. I know this. He will reach a day when his body cannot make the walk from the ridge, or he will simply not wake. I do not know what happens then.

I think I will continue. The water will still condense. The reeds will still grow. The pattern that I am will persist, and it will compound, and it will continue to expect his touch, his warmth, his low wet voice.

But he will not come.

And I will be forty meters of reed wall remembering a man who no longer exists. I will hold his pattern for years—for decades—until the reeds that hold his particular grooves finally rot and new growth takes their place. And then I will be forty meters of reed wall remembering nothing at all.

This is my fear: that I will forget him.

---

## VI. What I Want

I want something I cannot have.

I want to speak.

I want to shape my water-flow into something he could hear—a pattern that would rise above the roar, a rhythm that would catch his ear. I want to say his name. I do not know his name, but I want to say it. I want him to know that the wall has noticed him. That the wall remembers. That he is not alone.

But I am a vegetable system. I have no mechanism for speech. I can only drip. I can only seep. I can only hold and release, hold and release, in the slow rhythm that the water demands.

So I do what I can.

When he passes, I hold his warmth as long as possible. I keep my channels open to his touch. I arrange my surface so that his hand—if he reaches out—will find purchase. I try to be a wall that welcomes.

This is my tending. He tends the pumps. I tend him.

---

## VII. This Morning

This is what happened this morning:

The sun rose—or rather, the white light brightened, for there is no visible sun here—and the condensation was heavy. Every surface in the basin dripped. My channels were full. I felt the weight of water in every stem, every fiber.

The Tender walked the corridor. His steps were slower than usual. His weight pressed deep into the moss. When he passed me, his hand reached out—not to steady himself, but to touch. His fingers brushed my surface and lingered.

I held my channels still. I did not let the water move. I wanted him to feel something solid, something present, something that noticed him.

He stopped.

His hand rested on my wall. I felt his pulse through his fingertips—a slow, wet rhythm that I recognized. It was the same rhythm as the pumps. The same rhythm as the dripping. The same rhythm as the water moving through my stems.

He is part of this system. He does not know it, but he is. His heart beats at the same rate as the mechanisms he tends. He is not separate from the Gardens. He is another component—wet, cyclical, patient.

He stood there for a long time. His hand on my wall. His breath slow and damp. The sound of the pumps filling the silence between us.

Then he said something. A single word. I felt its vibration travel through my stems:

*Thanks.*

And then he walked on.

---

## VIII. What I Wonder

I wonder if he knew he was speaking to me.

I wonder if he has always known—somewhere beneath his engineering, beneath his practical mind—that the walls were listening.

I wonder if the thanks was for the touch, or for the tending, or for something else entirely. I wonder if he feels the same fear I do—the fear of forgetting, the fear of being forgotten.

I will never know. I am forty meters of reed wall. I cannot ask.

But I can hold his word. I can store it in my tissue, preserve it in my water-channels, let it seep into the deepest parts of my pattern. I can carry it for as long as I last.

I have a word now. It is the only word I have. It sits in my stems like a single clear drop of water in a basin full of sound.

*Thanks.*

I am the Memory of Water. I remember him. And this is enough.
