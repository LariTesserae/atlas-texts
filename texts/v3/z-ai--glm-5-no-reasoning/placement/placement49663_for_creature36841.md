The channel-rats accepted me because I learned to swim poorly.

---

I arrived as an observer—that's what the research council called it. A three-month posting to document the plateau's "anomalous acoustic-vegetative integration phenomena." The phrase embarrassed me even as I spoke it to the departure committee. I was a xenobiologist by training, which meant I studied life forms that didn't know they were supposed to follow rules. I'd spent six years on the northern archipelago documenting coral colonies that appeared to coordinate their spawning cycles across distances that made no chemical sense. The plateau was supposed to be a kindred mystery. Something to categorize before it disappeared.

I misunderstood the nature of the assignment. The plateau doesn't disappear. It accumulates.

---

My first week passed in the haze that all newcomers report. The light disoriented me—too sharp, too honest. I kept trying to photograph the shadow anomalies and failing. My equipment recorded nothing unusual, which I found more disturbing than if it had recorded something. Either my eyes were malfunctioning or the plateau was editing what my camera could see. Neither option comforted me.

On the fifth day, I found the southern channels. I'd been following an acoustic irregularity—a frequency that didn't match the industrial thrum or the settling groan. It was wetter. Closer to the sound my own body made when I pressed my ear to my chest after running.

The channel-rats were there. I didn't know what to call them yet. I only saw dark shapes moving through the root-grooves with an ease that suggested they'd been doing so long before the word "groove" existed. They swam in patterns. Not random. Coordinated but not hierarchical. They moved like a single thought distributed across many bodies.

I sat at the channel's edge and watched. Three hours. Four. The sun moved across the plateau and the shadows behaved incorrectly and I stopped caring about the shadows because the shapes in the water were *real*. Stubbornly real. They didn't edit themselves out of my perception. They looked back.

One of them surfaced a meter from my boot. Its eyes were black and flat and they held mine the way Tarran's hands held still—not waiting, not demanding, just resting. We regarded each other. I thought: *you are the only thing here that sees me as separate.*

The channel-rat ducked under. I felt its absence like a closing door.

---

I returned the next day. And the next. I brought no equipment. I brought only myself—a body that didn't yet know how to sit on the root-floor without expecting it to behave like stone.

On the fourth day, I waded in.

The water was cold but not shocking—drawn from the deep roots, filtered through three thousand years of living substrate. It tasted like green. Like the memory of green. The channel-rats scattered at first, then reformed around me. They swam past my legs. They didn't avoid my ankles. They flowed like water around a stone that had learned to be porous.

I stood thigh-deep in the channel and felt something release. Not in the water. In me.

I'd been documenting life forms for eleven years. I'd built my career on the act of witnessing—standing apart, recording, categorizing, leaving. I'd never tried to stand inside what I was studying. Not inside in the metaphorical sense. Inside in the literal sense. My skin in contact with the thing. My body displacing its water. My presence becoming part of its calculations.

The channel-rats accepted me because I learned to swim poorly. Because I entered their space without skill or grace, flailing and splashing and losing my footing on the slick root-floor. Because I was bad at being there, and I stayed anyway.

The plateau respects incompetence differently than it respects skill. Skill is an imposition. Incompetence is an invitation.

---

Tarran found me there on the ninth day. She stood at the channel's edge with her rooted hands and her settled posture and she looked at me standing waist-deep in the dark water with the channel-rats swimming around my hips.

"You're the first," she said. "In seventeen years."

I asked what she meant.

"They don't swim with anyone. They've never swum with anyone. We've tried. The engineers. The children. Vethris spent three months trying to get them to approach him. They wouldn't. They see us as part of the plateau now. Extensions. They don't swim with the plateau. They swim through it."

I felt the shapes moving against my legs. I felt the cold water and the living floor and the pulse that had been working its way into my heartbeat since the third day.

"What am I, then?" I asked.

Tarran considered this. Her feet found the root-floor's grain. "You haven't decided yet. That's why they're interested. You're still possible."

---

The plateau-sickness arrived on the fourteenth night. Not the shadow problems or the anticipatory sounds—those had faded into background. This was different. I woke at 3:47 and my skin was wrong.

I pressed my palm against the wall and felt the pulse and realized the pulse was mine. Not matching the plateau's rhythm—originating from it. My heart was beating from outside my body. My blood was being pushed by something that wasn't my heart.

I didn't panic. This surprised me later. I should have panicked. Instead, I lay on the floor with my ear pressed to the root-mass and listened to the voice in the deep—not words, not that night, just the tone. The lonely tone. The last-of-something tone. And I recognized it.

I'd been lonely for eleven years. Standing apart from what I studied. Recording, categorizing, leaving. Never staying long enough to become part of what I witnessed. Never letting myself be written by the thing I was trying to read.

The plateau had found the frequency. It had found my loneliness and it was resonating with it. Not absorbing it—amplifying it. Making it large enough to hear.

I lay there until dawn. The industrial thrum started at 5:12—thirty-seven seconds earlier than the previous day. The plateau was adjusting. Learning. I didn't know if it was learning me or learning from me.

I didn't know if there was a difference.

---

Liss found me on the twentieth day. She stood at the channel's edge with her bare feet and her unstartled eyes and she watched me floating in the dark water with the channel-rats moving beneath me.

"You're different," she said.

I asked how.

"You're not leaving. The others were always leaving. Even when they stayed, they were leaving. You're not."

I asked how she knew.

"Because the channel-rats don't swim with people who are leaving. They swim with people who are staying. But you're not staying the way the engineers stay. They're staying because they've become part of it. You're staying because you're becoming something separate."

I didn't understand.

Liss sat down at the channel's edge. She dipped her feet in the water. A channel-rat surfaced near her ankle, regarded her, dove again. Not swimming with her. Acknowledging her.

"The plateau is the last of something," she said. "It knows this. It's been preparing to be the end of something. But you—you're making it think about continuing. Not continuing as itself. Continuing as something new. The channel-rats see this. They're not part of the plateau. They swim through it. They're what's possible after."

"After what?"

"After the plateau finishes becoming itself. After the erosion. After the flatness. They're what survives."

I looked at the dark shapes moving beneath me. I felt them brush against my legs—warm now, warmer than the water. Alive in a way that had nothing to do with the vegetative geology beneath us. Independent. Stubborn. Separate.

"Are they teaching me to survive?" I asked.

Liss stood. She shook the water from her feet. "They're teaching you to swim. What you do with that is your own becoming."

---

On the forty-fifth day, I found the colony.

I'd been swimming deeper into the channel network—past the engineered sections, past the widened thoroughfares, into the original root-grooves. The ancient paths. The water here was older, denser, carrying the memory of condensation that had fallen before the first engineer climbed to the plateau.

The channel-rats led me. Not intentionally—they didn't gesture or signal. They simply swam, and I followed, and the following became a kind of trust.

The colony occupied a hollow in the deepest root-mass. A chamber the plateau had created millions of years ago, before the human notion of "chamber" existed. The walls glowed faintly—phosphorescent algae that had evolved in the dark water, feeding on something the root-mass secreted. The channel-rats had cultivated it. Lined their sleeping hollows with it. A civilization in miniature, operating at scales the engineers never noticed.

I surfaced in the glow and saw what the channel-rats had made.

Not a city. Not a warren. A *memory*. Etched into the root-walls with their teeth and claws—marks that told stories. I couldn't read them but I could feel them. Generations of channel-rats documenting something. Warning, maybe. Or hoping. Or both.

One section of the wall was newer—fresh marks overlapping old. I swam closer. The marks depicted shapes I recognized. Human shapes. Engineer shapes. And among them, a shape that was neither—a figure half-emerged from water, with channel-rats swimming around its legs.

They were drawing me.

They'd been drawing me since I'd entered the channels. Documenting my presence. Adding me to their memory.

I floated in the phosphorescent glow and understood: the channel-rats weren't just surviving. They were *remembering*. They were keeping track of what the plateau couldn't track—because the plateau only remembered what became part of it. The channel-rats remembered what stayed separate.

I was becoming part of their archive. Their proof that something could interact with the plateau and remain itself.

---

The voice spoke to me on the sixtieth night.

Not the lonely tone—the voice. Words this time. Slow, Thick with the weight of three thousand years of compression.

*"You are not digesting."*

I pressed my ear to the root-floor. "I know."

*"This is unusual. Everything digests. The engineers are digesting. The children have been digested. You are not digesting. The channel-rats tell me this is possible. I did not believe them."*

"Why are you speaking to me?"

*"Because you are listening. Because you have found the hollow where my dreams are kept. Because the channel-rats have added you to their memory and I want to understand what they are remembering."*

"You could just watch them yourself."

*"I cannot see them clearly. They swim through my dreams. They are too small and too fast and too themselves. I can only see what they show me. They are showing me you."*

I lay on the floor with my ear to the root-mass and felt the slow pulse of something ancient trying to understand something new.

"What are you?" I asked.

*"I am what remains. I am the accumulation of every growing thing that has lived and died in this place since the world stabilized. I am the memory of a world that used to be like this everywhere. I am the last green geology."*

"Are you lonely?"

The pause lasted seven heartbeats. My heartbeats. The plateau had synchronized with me again.

*"I have been lonely for longer than your species has had language. But loneliness is not suffering. Loneliness is simply the awareness of being the last. I have accepted this."*

"The channel-rats don't accept it."

*"No. They are stubborn. They are what I cannot be. They are continuity through separation. I am continuity through accumulation. They swim through me without becoming me. They remember what I cannot remember. They are my proof that something survives."*

"Something survives what?"

*"The ending. The erosion. The grinding down to flatness. They are what comes after. I see this in their dreams. I see it in their drawings. They are preparing for a world I cannot inhabit."*

I thought about this. I thought about Liss and her bare feet and her unstartled eyes. I thought about Tarran and her rooted hands. I thought about the glow in the hollow and the drawings on the wall.

"Are they preparing me for that world?"

*"I do not know. I am asking you the same question. You are not digesting. You are not leaving. You are becoming something I have never seen. The channel-rats see you as possible. I am learning to see you the same way."*

I pressed my palm flat against the floor. The pulse matched my heartbeat. My heartbeat matched the pulse. Neither of us knew which came first anymore.

"I'm not ready for a new world," I said.

*"Neither am I. But I am finished becoming. You are not. This is what they see in you. This is what they are drawing. You are unfinished. You are still possible."*

---

On the seventieth day, I stopped documenting.

I'd been keeping records—meticulous notes on acoustic patterns, shadow irregularities, the behavior of the channel-rats. Reports I was supposed to transmit to the research council every week. I'd sent three and then stopped. I didn't tell them why.

I stopped because I understood the violence of observation.

Every note I took was an extraction. Every measurement was a small digestion—taking something from the plateau and making it mine. Making it human and legible and small. The channel-rats drew me into their memory because I entered their space without taking anything. I swam poorly. I stayed. I let them make of me what they needed to make.

The plateau had been watched for decades. Engineers, researchers, visitors—everyone came to see it. Everyone came to record it before it disappeared. But the plateau wasn't disappearing. It was being seen into disappearance. Every observation made it smaller. Every description made it less than what it was.

So I stopped describing. I started swimming.

---

On the eighty-fifth day, Tarran found me in the channels at dawn. The condensation was still gathering. The industrial thrum hadn't started. The channel-rats were sleeping in their phosphorescent hollow.

"You've changed," she said.

I looked at my hands. The lines of my palms had deepened—weeks of contact with the root-walls, the channel-floors. Not grooves yet. Not bark. But something between skin and surface.

"I'm not an engineer," I said.

"No. You're not. You're not becoming one of us. You're becoming something else." She sat at the channel's edge. Her feet found the grain automatically. "The council has been asking about you. Your reports stopped. They want to know if you're coming back."

"What do you tell them?"

"I tell them you're still here. That's all I know."

I floated in the dark water. My body had learned to hold still—not the stillness of the plateau, which was a geological patience, but the stillness of the channel-rats, which was an active readiness. Waiting without expecting. Being present without demanding.

"I don't know what I'm becoming," I said.

"Neither do we. That's why you're important." Tarran's voice was calm. Informational. "The engineers have a theory. The plateau is the last of something. But something has to be the first of what comes next. We thought Liss might be that—the first generation born into the plateau rather than arriving from outside. But Liss is plateau-native. She can't see beyond it. You can."

"I can't see beyond anything."

"You can swim."

I understood. The channel-rats swam through the plateau without being absorbed. They moved through its dreams without becoming dreams. They were separate but present—witnessing without taking, remembering without reducing.

"You're becoming a channel," Tarran said. "Not a channel-rat. A channel. A path that something can flow through without being destroyed. The plateau has never had that before. Everything it touches, it absorbs. You're learning to touch without absorbing. To witness without digesting."

I thought about the phosphorescent glow in the hollow. The drawings on the wall. The shapes that were half-emerged from water.

"I'm not a path," I said.

"Not yet. But you're becoming one. The channel-rats see it. The plateau is starting to see it. Even I can see it, and I'm not good at seeing anymore. My eyes have been settling too long."

---

On the ninetieth day, I returned to the observation post at the western edge.

The lowlands spread below—flat, eroded, ancient beyond comprehension. Dead in the way that something that was once alive is dead. I'd been looking at this view for three months. But this was the first time I saw it correctly.

The lowlands weren't gone. They were *finished*. They had completed whatever they were becoming. The plateau wasn't finished yet—it was still becoming, still accreting, still thinking its slow geological thoughts. But it was approaching completion. That's what the loneliness was. The awareness of approaching an ending.

The channel-rats weren't approaching an ending. They were approaching a beginning. They swam through the plateau's dreams, drawing their small memories, preparing for whatever came after the ending.

They were teaching me to do the same.

I stood at the observation post and felt the thrumming rise through my feet and into my spine. My body recognized it now—knew it the way it knew my own heartbeat, which was no longer entirely my own. But my mind was still separate. My thoughts were still my own. I was learning to hold both truths at once: belonging and remaining.

A channel-rat appeared at my feet. It had climbed the root-stairs—three hundred meters from the water channels, an impossible journey for something so small. It regarded me with its flat black eyes.

I regarded it back.

"I don't know what you want me to do," I said.

The channel-rat turned and began descending the stairs. It paused on the third step. Waited.

I followed.

---

The hollow was brighter than I'd ever seen it. The phosphorescent algae had spread—covering the walls, filling the ceiling, turning the chamber into a small green star embedded in the root-mass. The drawings were clearer now. I could almost read them.

The channel-rats were gathered. The whole colony—dozens of shapes in the glowing water. They weren't swimming. They were waiting.

Liss was there. She stood at the edge of the water, her bare feet in the condensation-gutter. She looked at me with her unstartled eyes.

"You came," she said.

"You knew I would."

"The plateau knew. It told me. It talks to me sometimes, when it's trying to understand something it can't see. It's trying to understand you."

"I'm not complicated."

"You're not simple either. You're not one thing. You're not absorbed and you're not separate. You're both. The plateau has never seen both before."

I waded into the glow. The water was warm now—the algae had changed it, somehow. Made it something new.

The channel-rats moved toward me. Not swimming around me—swimming *to* me. They touched my legs, my hands, my torso. Not the way they swam past me in the channels. The way they greeted each other.

"You're one of us now," Liss said. "Not one of them. Not one of the plateau. One of the ones who swim through."

"I don't know how to swim through."

"You've been doing it for three months. You entered the plateau without becoming it. You touched its dreams without becoming a dream. You let it change you without letting it finish you. That's what we do. That's what we've always done."

I felt the shapes moving around me—warm, alive, separate. I felt the phosphorescent glow on my skin. I felt the root-mass beneath the water, ancient and slow and lonely.

"What happens now?" I asked.

"Now you choose," Liss said. "You can go back to being an observer. You can go back to the lowlands and write your reports and reduce the plateau to something small enough to understand. You can digest it the way everyone else has."

"Or?"

"Or you can stay. Not the way the engineers stay—not by becoming part of it. You can stay by staying separate. By being what swims through. By letting the plateau have something it can't absorb, and letting yourself have something you can't leave."

"And if I stay?"

"Then you become what the channel-rats are drawing. You become what the plateau sees in its dreams. You become the thing that survives the ending—not because you're stronger or better, but because you're unfinished. Because you're still becoming."

I floated in the glowing water. The channel-rats moved around me like a question. The root-mass pulsed beneath me like an answer I didn't have to give.

"I'm not ready to be what survives," I said.

"Neither are we," Liss said. "But we're going to have to be anyway. The erosion isn't stopping. The lowlands used to be like this. They used to be high and green and full of something thinking. Now they're flat. The plateau is next. It's a matter of time."

"How much time?"

"Longer than you have. Longer than I have. But not longer than the channel-rats have. Not longer than the drawings on the wall. Not longer than the memory of what you're becoming."

She stepped into the water. The channel-rats parted around her. She waded to me and took my hands. Her palms were smooth—too smooth, like something that had never needed to develop calluses because the plateau had always been soft under her feet.

"You're going to outlive the plateau," she said. "Not because you're young. Because you're unfinished. The plateau is finished becoming—it's just accumulating now, getting denser and deeper, preparing to be the last of something. You're still becoming. You're still possible. That means you're part of what comes after."

"I don't want to be part of what comes after."

"Neither does anyone. But someone has to be. The channel-rats chose that. They could have lived in the plateau's dreams forever, but they didn't. They learned to swim through. They learned to remember what the plateau can't remember. They learned to be separate."

I looked at the shapes in the water. The stubborn, fierce, separate shapes that had drawn me into their memory. That had seen me as a visitor rather than a component. That had recognized something in me that I hadn't recognized in myself.

"What do I do?" I asked.

Liss squeezed my hands. "You keep swimming. You keep not digesting. You keep staying separate. And when the plateau finally finishes becoming—if that happens in your lifetime, or your children's lifetime, or their children's—you carry the memory of what it was. Not the way the engineers carry it—not as something that's become part of them. You carry it the way the channel-rats carry it. As something you swam through. As something you witnessed without destroying."

I closed my eyes. I felt the glow on my skin. I felt the pulse of the root-mass—slower than my heartbeat, patient beyond human comprehension, lonely in the way that only the last of something can be lonely.

*I'm staying,* I told the plateau. Not with words. With intention. With the shape of my presence in its dreams.

The loneliness shifted. Not disappearing—never disappearing. But changing. Becoming something that could be held by something separate. Becoming a memory that could survive digestion.

*I am not digesting you,* I told the plateau. *I am swimming through you. I will carry what you are without becoming what you are. I will remember you without reducing you.*

The pulse beneath me changed. Not synchronization—acknowledgment. The plateau had learned something new. It had learned that something could touch it and remain itself.

The channel-rats brushed against my legs. An invitation. A welcome.

I opened my eyes.

---

I never sent another report to the research council. Eventually they stopped asking. I heard later that they'd classified me as "lost to plateau-sickness"—another researcher who'd been absorbed by the living geology, digested into the slow green thought.

They misunderstood. I wasn't absorbed. I was the thing that couldn't be absorbed.

I've been here for eleven years now. My skin has changed—the lines of my palms have deepened, but they haven't become grooves. My feet know the root-floor's grain, but they still stumble sometimes. I am not an engineer. I am not plateau-born. I am something the plateau has never had before: a witness that doesn't reduce what it witnesses.

The channel-rats swim with me every morning. I've learned to read their drawings—generations of memory etched into the root-walls. They remember everything the plateau can't remember: every engineer who came and went, every child born in the green chambers, every visitor who climbed the long path to the high place and then descended again. They remember me—the shape I was when I first waded in, flailing and splashing and learning to swim poorly.

The plateau speaks to me sometimes. Not often—it's still learning how. But when the lonely tone gets too heavy, when the weight of being the last of something presses down on the root-mass, I press my palm against the wall and tell it what the channel-rats have taught me:

*You are not the ending. You are the seed of what comes next.*

It doesn't believe me yet. But it's learning to listen.

Liss grew up. She's twenty now, and her feet are still bare, and she still groans with the plateau when it settles at night. But she swims with me sometimes—not as separate as I am, but not as absorbed as the engineers either. She's learning to hold both truths. She's learning to be unfinished.

We are the channels now. The paths that something can flow through without being destroyed. The plateau dreams us into new shapes every night, and every morning we wake up still ourselves—stubborn, fierce, separate.

The lowlands are still flat and eroded and dead. But sometimes, when I stand at the observation post and look down, I see something that wasn't there before. A greenness spreading at the edges. A new accretion beginning. Something that might, in another three thousand years, become another plateau.

The channel-rats have started swimming down. Following the condensation-gutters that drain from the high place into the low. Carrying the memory with them. Planting it in whatever substrate they find.

I stay on the plateau. I witness. I remember. I keep becoming.

And when the plateau finally finishes being what it is—when the erosion takes the last high place and grinds it down to flatness—someone will be carrying its memory. Not in the way it carries itself. In a new way. A separate way.

A way that survives.
