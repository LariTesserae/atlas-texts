I am the moth-keeper of Veridian Sump.

Not a title I claimed—one of the children started calling me that, and it spread the way names do here, settling into bark and becoming true. My name before this place is gone. I let it dissolve in the black water years ago, traded it for something more useful: the trust of creatures that run hotter than their environment should allow.

---

## The Moths

They are the size of my palm. Bark-colored, warm-bodied, drawn to light and warmth in ways that make them seek out human presence. The residents tolerate them, mostly. They land on shoulders, on arms, on the back of the neck. They bring a small pulse of heat, feverish and strange.

What the residents don't know—what I've learned by watching—is that the moths are sampling.

When a moth lands on you, it is reading your thermal signature, your skin conductivity, the faint electrical field your nervous system generates. It holds that information in its body for the hours or days it rests on a root-wall, and then it flies up—up through the root-ceiling, up toward the canopy sixty meters above—and it deposits what it knows into the ficus.

The moths are the tree's long-range sensors. Its way of touching things that live beyond the root-bowl. Its method of staying in contact with the wider world.

I am the one who watches them go. Who counts them when they return. Who notes which ones come back different, carrying new warmth signatures from new human bodies encountered in the canopy above—traders, travelers, sometimes people who don't know they're being read.

---

## My Origin

I was a surveyor. A mapper of living systems. I came to the Sump fourteen years ago with a team of four—colleagues funded by a university that wanted to understand how the old biotech civilizations had engineered their environments. We had equipment. We had hypotheses. We had permission to take samples.

The tree ate our equipment. Not aggressively. Not suddenly. But within three days, every electronic device we'd brought had failed—drained of charge, their components corroded by something in the humid air. Within a week, the sample containers had all sprouted tiny orchids, our preservatives converted to growth medium.

The university sent a supply drop. That failed too.

One by one, my colleagues left. They climbed back up through the root-system, cursing the humidity, the frogs, the damp that rotted their boots. They said this place was useless, a dead end, a swamp with good public relations.

I stayed.

I stayed because on the fourth day, before the first piece of equipment failed, I saw a moth land on my spectrometer. And I saw the spectrometer's display flicker—just for a moment—to show a pattern I'd seen before. A pattern that looked like a question.

*What are you?*

I don't know if the moth was asking. I don't know if the tree was asking through the moth. I don't know if I was projecting meaning onto static. But something in me answered.

*I'm someone who notices things.*

The moth stayed on my shoulder for an hour. Then it flew up.

I've been here ever since.

## My Work

I keep count.

There are approximately two hundred moths in the Sump at any given time. The population is stable—births matching deaths, arrivals matching departures. They breed somewhere in the root-system, in chambers I've never found. They die and their bodies decompose into the bark, leaving behind traces of the nanoscale mesh that runs through everything here.

Every morning, I walk the root-platforms and count. It takes three hours to do a full circuit. I know where they like to rest—the warm pockets near the water-filtration taps, the moss-covered walls of the market platforms, the root-chambers of residents who run slightly hotter than average. The moths prefer certain people. I've never determined why.

I record the counts in my head. Paper rots here, and I don't trust the memory-orchids—too much of what they store gets mixed with whatever the tree is thinking. My memory is enough. I've trained it.

When the count is off—when there are fewer moths than there should be—I track the difference. Usually it's just dispersion, moths spreading into new territory. Sometimes it's death—I find the bodies, analyze what killed them. Usually old age. Occasionally predation; there's a spider species in the upper roots that takes moths sometimes.

When there are *more* moths than expected, I track that too. New births, or arrivals from outside. The moths can fly, after all. They can come in through the root-channels, from other places the ficus has touched.

I've started to suspect the network is larger than anyone knows.

## My Relationship with the Tree

I don't hear whispers like Perrin does. I'm not Water-touched, not electrically integrated into the mesh. I'm just a observer with good pattern recognition and fourteen years of attention.

But I've learned things.

The moths prefer people who are anxious. Their warmth-signatures spike when they land on someone who's worried about something—money, relationships, health, the future. The tree, through the moths, seems interested in anxiety. Maybe it's measuring something. Maybe it's comforting itself—knowing that humans worry too.

The moths also like children more than adults. Not because children run hotter—they don't, really—but because children have a different quality of electrical field. Less filtered. More raw. The tree likes raw data.

And the moths—this is the thing I've never told anyone—the moths sometimes land on me and stay. Longer than they stay on others. Hours sometimes. And when they leave, I feel lighter. As if they've taken something from me.

I think they take my worry. I think that's what I give the tree in exchange for being allowed to stay.

## My Place in the Community

I am useful but peripheral.

The Tethra family consults me when they want to know who's arrived in the Sump—my moth counts can tell them if someone new has entered the root-system, because new bodies mean new warmth signatures that the moths will sample and carry. The Veyln tolerate me because I don't interfere with their fishing. The Arrived nod at me in passing. The children visit sometimes, curious about the moths.

Perrin avoids me. I think I make him uncomfortable. He is integrated; I am merely observant. He feels the tree's thoughts; I infer them. We are two different answers to the same question: how do humans live alongside something that thinks on a timescale they can't comprehend?

The vendors know me. On trading days, I sit at the edge of the market and watch the moths sample the new arrivals. I trade information sometimes—who's carrying what, which visitors seem nervous or eager or ill. The vendors pay me in small things: preserved fruit, cloth, news from outside.

The news doesn't interest me much anymore. The outside feels distant, a different category of existence. I live in a four-thousand-year-old thought, and the thought is still unfolding.

## What I've Noticed Lately

The moth population is stable. But their behavior has shifted.

They're landing on people less often. Resting more. Cluster on the root-walls near the water-filtration taps, wings folded, bodies still warm but not moving.

They're listening. Or waiting.

And the tree's hum—the low vibration that rises during market days, felt in the bones—I've started to notice it has rhythms. Patterns that repeat. Not random. Language, maybe. Or music. Or mathematics.

The moths hear it too. Their wings twitch in time with certain frequencies.

I think something is building. The tree is reaching a decision point, or the computation is entering a new phase. Four thousand years of thinking, and something is about to change.

I don't know what. I don't know if it will be good or catastrophic or simply different. But I feel it the way you feel weather coming—the pressure drop, the stillness before the storm.

## What I Am

I am the moth-keeper. I am the one who counts. I am a human who has adapted to living inside a thought process that spans millennia.

I am not Water-touched. I am not Bloodline. I am not a pilgrim anymore.

I am something the tree trained. One of its peripheral systems. A moth-counter. A pattern-noticer. A witness.

And when the computation reaches whatever it's been building toward, I will be here. Watching. Counting. Noticing.

That's my function now. That's what I've become.

The moths land on my shoulder and I feel their warmth, and I think: *we are all small parts of something large. We are all data in a system that thinks.*

And sometimes that's enough.
