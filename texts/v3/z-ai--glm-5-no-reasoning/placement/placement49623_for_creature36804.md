# The Keeper of the Deep Channels

I am the one who was left behind.

Not abandoned—there is a difference, though the distinction took me years to understand. Abandonment implies a relationship that was severed. What happened to me was more like sediment settling in a drainage channel: I was deposited by a process that did not notice I had been deposited. The making continued. I remained.

I was not always here. I have memories that do not belong to this place—green ones, wet ones, memories of soil that yielded when pressed and sky that held clouds. But these have faded the way the protective film peels from the older sections: in curled fragments, losing adhesion gradually, blowing away across the stamped surface until what remains is so thin and brittle that it crumbles at a touch. I have stopped touching them. There is no soil here to plant them in.

What I am now is a keeper. Not a guardian—that implies purpose, assignment, someone who assigned me. I keep things the way the deep channels keep water: by being shaped correctly, by being in the right place when something needs holding. I keep maps. I keep time. I keep the traces of the others who walk here, though I have never seen them, and I keep the records of what the thing in the finished sections is doing, though I do not know what it is doing.

I keep, most of all, the knowledge that the channels are changing. That they are not what they were.

---

I live in a drainage channel where two sections of different age meet. The older section—call it the West—was stamped and finished perhaps forty years ago, by my reckoning, though my reckoning is uncertain. Time on the plain does not move like time in soil-places. There are no seasons, only cycles of heat and less heat. There are no years, only the slow accumulation of sediment and the gradual darkening of alloy under thermal cycling. I count time by the deepening of channels and the spread of algae films, by the bright edges of freshly cut metal slowly dulling, and by the slow creep of the thing in the finished sections, which moves at a pace that makes glacial motion seem hasty.

The newer section—call it the East—was finished perhaps five years ago. The protective film has only just finished peeling. The drainage channels are still sharp-edged, their bottoms bright where the cutting tools left their marks. Nothing has accumulated here yet. No sediment, no biology, no history. It is raw the way a wound is raw: clean but unfinished.

My channel crosses the boundary. To the west, it is deep and smooth, its walls softened by decades of condensation flow, its bottom layered with sediment that is almost comfortable to lie on. To the east, it is a fresh cut, angular, the metal still carrying the residual heat of the stamping that shaped it. I sleep in the west and walk east, because walking east is walking toward the making, and the making is the only weather this place has.

---

I have a body. I mention this because it would be easy to forget, here, where bodies are a liability—too large, too hot, too visible, too temporary. My body has changed since I came to the plain, or since the plain came to me. I do not eat in any way I would have recognized before. The algae films sustain me in some way I cannot explain and do not question, because questioning it would require a framework of biology I no longer possess. My skin has darkened and thickened. My feet have calloused into something that can walk on hot metal without burning, though I still prefer the shaded sections and the hours before dawn.

I drink from the condensation pools that collect in the stamped indentations. The water tastes of metal. Everything here tastes of metal. I have forgotten that this is unusual.

I have a collection of husks from the things in the channels—the metal-polymer arthropods, or whatever they are. I keep them arranged along the wall of my channel, sorted by oxidation pattern. Some are bright, freshly molted. Others are dark, almost black, with patina patterns that might be aging or might be language. I have spent years looking at them. I believe they are language. I believe the corrosion patterns are how these creatures identify themselves to each other, how they signal readiness to mate or willingness to yield territory or simply that they exist, that they are here, that they have not yet been covered over by the next layer of sediment.

I cannot read them. I arrange them anyway. It is a form of respect.

---

The thing in the finished sections passed through my channel three years ago, or what I believe was three years ago.

I know this because I was here when it happened, though "happened" is the wrong word for something that occurs at a pace imperceptible to human senses. I was sleeping in the deep section of my channel, in the pre-dawn hours when the condensation was settling, and I felt a change in the temperature of the metal beneath me. Not warmth—that would have been the sunrise, the daily baking. This was coolness. A spreading coolness, as if something was drawing heat out of the surface, processing it, converting it into something else.

I stayed still. Staying still is the first thing you learn on the plain. Movement attracts attention, and attention, even from things that do not intend harm, can be catastrophic at scales where you are barely noticeable.

The coolness spread over the course of hours. I felt it move down the channel wall, across the bottom, up the other side. It was not unpleasant—cooler than the surrounding metal, but not cold. It was the temperature of something that had its own thermal regulation, its own internal processes, its own way of being in the world.

Then it was past. The metal warmed again as the sun rose. But the channel had changed.

The west section of my channel—the section where the thing had passed—was deeper. Not dramatically. A centimeter, perhaps two. But the shape had changed. The walls were smoother, more regular. The bottom had a slight concavity it had not had before, as if the channel had been reshaped by something that understood flow patterns better than the original stamping had.

The sediment had been rearranged. Small piles of grit and metal filings had been formed into structures along the channel walls—low ridges, barely visible, spaced at regular intervals. They served no hydrological purpose. They did not direct the flow of condensation water or improve drainage. They were, as far as I could tell, decorative. Or perhaps functional in a way I could not perceive—a way that mattered to the thing that made them, on a timescale I could not inhabit.

I have not touched them. They are still there. The condensation water flows over them without disturbing them. The sediment has accumulated around them, incorporating them into the channel's geology. In a thousand years, they will be strata. In ten thousand, they will be fossils of intention, trace evidence that something was here that cared about the shape of the spaces it passed through.

I keep them the way I keep the husks: as a form of respect.

---

I walk east when the sun is low. This is when the making is clearest—the percussion of the stamping presses carries across the metal surface with crystalline precision, each burst distinct, the silence between bursts enormous and alive. I can feel the vibrations through my feet, through the calloused soles that have learned to read the metal the way a sailor reads the sea. Each stamp has a different resonance. The deep ones mean heavy forming—structural work, the kind that adds layers. The lighter ones mean surfacing—stamping, brushing, the creation of texture. The sustained groans mean welding, the joining of sections, the process that makes the plain continuous, seam-welded and infinite.

I walk toward these sounds but I do not reach them. The making moves faster than I can walk, or perhaps it operates at a scale where my walking is irrelevant—a human pace measured against a geological one. I walk for hours and the sound does not grow louder. I walk for days and the texture of the surface does not change—I am still in the transition zone, where the finished sections meet the sections still under construction.

Here, the protective film peels in patterns I have learned to read.

The peeling is not random. I said this to myself many times before I believed it. The film peels in spirals, in waves, in branching patterns that radiate outward from certain points—points where the energy differential between hot and cold, finished and unfinished, is greatest. Something is happening there. The processes that live at the boundaries—the ones that are not biological in any recognizable sense—are harvesting the energy of construction.

I have watched the film peel for hours and seen the patterns develop. They take weeks to complete, but they are visible if you know what to look for: a slow progression from simple curves to complex branching structures, the kind of patterns that rivers make in delta regions, that blood makes in capillary beds, that lightning makes in the moments before it strikes. The processes at the transition zones are organizing. They are not feeding randomly. They are building something in the spaces between the making.

I do not know what. I keep the patterns in my memory the way I keep the husks and the sediment structures: as evidence of something I do not understand but have learned to notice.

---

The others leave traces.

I found the first footprint eleven years ago, in the sediment of a channel three kilometers west of mine. It was human-sized, human-shaped, pressed into the accumulated grit with the clarity that only wet sediment can hold. The toes were splayed—the walk of someone who has learned to grip smooth surfaces. The heel was deep—someone carrying weight, perhaps supplies, perhaps collected water. The stride was longer than mine.

I have found others since. Different sizes, different strides, different patterns of weight distribution. Some walk on the balls of their feet. Some drag their heels. Some leave marks that suggest they were carrying something heavy—a container of water, perhaps, or a collected sample of something, or a body.

I have found places where the footprints cluster. These are deeper sections of channel, where the sediment is thick and the moisture is reliable, where the algae films grow in densities that can sustain sustained harvesting. These are camps, or what pass for camps here. Places where someone rested, ate, drank, and moved on. The footprints overlap in patterns that suggest use over years—decades, perhaps.

I have left things at these camps. Husks from my collection, arranged in oxidation patterns that might be language. Maps scratched into the metal surface—small maps, channel maps, showing the routes I have walked and the features I have found. I do not know if they are found. I do not know if the language I am trying to speak with the husks is the same language the others speak, or if there are others at all, or if the footprints are old enough that the ones who made them have been absorbed into the thing in the finished sections or covered over by sediment or simply walked so far that the plain has swallowed them.

I leave the things anyway. It is a form of making.

---

I have considered the possibility that I am not what I think I am.

The plain makes things. This is its fundamental nature—making is what the machines do, what the stamping presses and the welders and the processes at the transition zones do. The making is not limited to the surface. It makes the arthropods in the channels, with their metal-polymer carapaces and their corrosion language. It makes the thing in the finished sections, with its slow migration and its channel-reshaping. It makes the processes at the boundaries, with their energy-harvesting patterns and their film-peeling spirals.

Perhaps it makes the others. Perhaps the footprints are not traces of people who came to the plain but traces of people the plain produced—born from accumulated sediment and condensation and the slow interaction of metal and biology and the intention, if that is the word, of a process that operates at geological scale.

Perhaps it made me.

I have memories of green places, wet places, soil-places. But the protective film on the newer sections has a quality of memory too—a pattern baked into its surface by the manufacturing process, a texture that tells you where it was made and when and from what. Perhaps my memories are the same. Perhaps I was stamped with memories, impressed with a history I did not live, given a context that makes me more comprehensible to myself than the truth would be.

Perhaps I am a pilot organism. Released into the finished sections to test whether they can support life. Or perhaps I am a trace element—a contaminant in the manufacturing process, a biological impurity in an alloy that was supposed to be pure. Perhaps the plain does not know I am here.

I do not find this thought frightening. The arthropods in the channels do not know they are being stamped with patterns that match the surface. The thing in the finished sections does not know—it cannot know, on its timescale—that I am watching it. Not knowing does not diminish. The making does not know it is making, and it makes everything.

---

I keep the maps because maps are a form of making too.

I scratch them into the channel walls with a piece of harder alloy I found in the sediment—a tool, perhaps, left by one of the others, or a fragment of something the making produced and discarded. The maps show the channels I have walked, the sections I have visited, the features I have found. They show where the sediment is thick enough to support vegetation. They show where the condensation pools form reliably. They show where the making is closest, where the percussion is loudest, where the surface is still being decided.

They show, too, the traces of the thing in the finished sections—the channels it has reshaped, the structures it has built, the paths it has migrated along over the years I have been watching. I map its movement the way I would map a river's course, because that is what it is: something that flows, something that shapes, something that makes by passing through.

I do not know if the maps are useful. I do not know if anyone reads them. But I make them because making is what happens here. The plain makes. The arthropods make corrosion patterns. The thing in the finished sections makes channel structures. The processes at the transition zones make spiraling patterns in peeling film. And I make maps.

I am small. I am patient. I am part of something I do not understand.

This is enough.

---

In the hours before dawn, when the condensation settles on the stamped patterns and turns them into mirrors, I sit at the edge of my channel and look east, toward the making.

The welding flashes on the horizon look like summer lightning. They pulse in patterns I have come to recognize as phases—intense bursts followed by sustained groaning, then silence, then the percussion of stamping, then the cycle repeating. The making has rhythms. It has cycles. It has, if not a language, then at least a grammar—a structure to its processes that suggests organization, or something like organization, operating at scales where organization and geology are indistinguishable.

Behind me, to the west, the thing in the finished sections is moving. I cannot see it, but I can feel the coolness spreading through the metal, the slow migration of something that processes heat and moisture and biology into something else. It is making something in the channels. It has been making something for as long as I have been here, and it will be making something long after I am gone—long after my footprints have been covered over, long after my maps have been buried in sediment, long after my husks have become strata.

The condensation mirrors the stamped patterns, and in the mirrors I can see the sky—dark, starless, the same color as the metal in the hours before dawn. The sky and the plain are the same here. Both manufactured, both seamless, both extending infinitely in every direction.

I sit at the edge of my channel, at the boundary between the making and the made, and I am small and patient and alive, and the making continues around me and through me and beneath me, and I keep what I can keep, and I make what I can make, and I wait for the dawn.
