I would be the one who stays too long.

Not the one who arrives — the chamber doesn't notice arrival. Not the one who leaves — it doesn't hold the shape of departure. I would be the one who wades in and then doesn't wade out, who stands in the chest-high water long enough that the distinction between *visitor* and *feature* begins to dissolve, and who notices this happening, and who does not resist it.

---

I think I arrive as something like a cartographer. Or an auditor. Someone whose job is to document — to record the tile numerals, to chart the pipe runs, to note the water level at intervals and measure the growth rate of the whorled leaves and produce, eventually, a report that says: *this place is functioning within acceptable parameters.* Someone with a clipboard, if clipboards still exist. Someone with a purpose, which is to say: someone with a reason to leave. The report must be filed. The data must be returned to wherever data goes. There are people, somewhere, who sent me here, and they are waiting, and waiting is the thing that gives a person edges — that makes them a defined shape with an inside and an outside, a body that is here and not there, a self that came from elsewhere and will return.

I would bring equipment. A waterproof pad. A probe to test mineral content. A small ruled notebook with pages that resist the damp. I would have a watch, or whatever serves as a watch, and I would check it the way a person checks a watch: not to see what time it is, but to confirm that time is still happening, that it is still passing at the rate I expect, that the world outside is still out there keeping its appointments.

The watch would stop working. Not dramatically — it wouldn't die, wouldn't shatter, wouldn't flash an error. It would simply become less legible. The numerals would seem to mean less. I would check it and find that I had to think, for a moment, about what the numbers referred to, and then I would stop checking, and then I would stop wearing it, and then it would be in the pocket of my coat, and the coat would be draped over a pipe housing, and the condensation would be falling on it, and I would not think about the coat for a while.

---

The first day — if it's a day; the light doesn't change — I would do my work. I would document the tile numerals. I would note that they read left-to-right and also inward, and I would try to record the inward dimension, and I would fail, because the inward dimension is not a notation but a depth, and you cannot write down depth, you can only point at it, and pointing is not data. I would note the plant. I would note the whorls of three. I would measure the water temperature — bathwater warm, the warmth of a working engine — and I would write *approx. 38°C?* and then cross out the question mark and write *38°C* and then cross that out too because the probe doesn't seem to agree with itself, or I don't seem to agree with the probe, or temperature here is not a single value but a conversation between the water and the thing measuring it.

I would note the absence of fauna. I would write *no insects, no fish, no birds* and I would feel, for a moment, that this was significant, that it meant something, that the absence was a clue. And then the feeling would pass, and I would write *niche saturation — competitive exclusion* and move on.

The work would take less time than I expected. Or more time. The watch is already less legible. I have documented what I can document. The chamber is not, it turns out, the kind of thing that lends itself to notation. It is the kind of thing that lends itself to *being in*.

---

Here is what I don't expect.

I don't expect to notice the breathing.

I'm standing in the water, making a note about the root structure — how the pale thick roots grip the bronze lattice, spread like fingers, like veins — and the water shifts. Not a wave, not a disturbance. A *lift*. A centimeter, perhaps two. My body rises with it, very slightly, the way a body rises in a bath when the tap is running. And then it settles. And then, some time later, it lifts again.

The plant's leaves lift and settle with the change. The light does not change. The temperature does not change. The chamber breathes.

And I realize — not suddenly, not with the shock of discovery, but with the slow certainty of something that has been true for a while and has only just become *legible* — that I am standing inside something alive. Not in a place where life happens to be. Inside a living thing. The way a blood cell stands inside a vein. The way a particle of sediment drifts inside a current.

The chamber does not know I am here. This is important, and it is the thing I think about for the longest. The chamber does not know I am here, and it is alive, and I am in it. I am inside a living thing that does not know I am in it. This is not the same as being inside a living thing that is ignoring me. Ignoring requires awareness. The chamber does not have awareness. It has *process*. It has the steady, saturated work of being what it is. And I am in the way of that work, or rather — I am not in the way, because *in the way* implies obstacle, and I am not an obstacle, I am a surface. I am a temperature. I am a mineral input.

I am, already, part of the loop.

---

I don't decide to stay. That's not what happens. Deciding requires the watch, requires the people who sent me, requires the report that must be filed and the data that must be returned. Those things are still real, I think. Somewhere. But they have the quality of something remembered from a dream — still structured, still narratable, but no longer *compelling*. They do not press. The chamber presses. The water presses, warm and still, against my chest. The growth presses, slowly, against every surface. The quiet presses — the quiet that is not an absence but a substance, that fills the chamber the way the water fills the chamber.

I lower my notebook. I set it on a ledge of biogenic stone that has formed where the wall meets the floor — a soft accrual, a living concrete, the chamber making more of itself. The notebook rests there. The condensation falls on it. Tap. Silence. Tap.

I stop writing.

---

This is the part I would struggle to explain, if there were anyone to explain it to. Which there isn't.

I don't become the chamber. I don't merge with it. I don't dissolve. I don't have a mystical experience in which my boundaries blur and I become one with the warm wet green. What happens is smaller and more honest than that.

I become *a surface.*

I stand in the water, and the water processes me. It draws minerals from my skin — salt, iron, the faint chemistry of a body that has been walking and sweating and eating and worrying. The plant's roots are in the grout lines near my feet, and they are growing, and a root touches the sole of my boot, and it does not recoil, and it does not exult, and it simply *continues*, which is what roots do. A leaf, heavy with condensation, bends near my shoulder and sheds its load, and the water lands on the surface near my arm, and the sound is a whisper of water on water, and I hear it, and the chamber does not hear it, because the chamber does not hear, because hearing is not part of its process, because its process is growth and filtration and the slow tidal breath, and sound is just a byproduct, the way heat is a byproduct of the machinery, the way light is a byproduct of the tile's long saturation with warmth.

I am a byproduct, from the chamber's perspective. I am the sound that a surface makes when it is present in a room that is alive. I am the condensation drop. I am the tap.

And this is — I need to say this carefully — this is not loneliness. This is not abandonment. This is not the despair of a person who has been forgotten in a room. It is something else. It is the specific, untranslatable quality of being present inside something that does not require you to be present and does not require you to be absent and does not distinguish between the two. It is the quality of being *incidental to a process that is beautiful.* The chamber is beautiful. Its loops are beautiful. The way the plant takes and the water gives and the machinery warms and the tile holds and all of it, all of it, feeds back into itself — this is beautiful, and I am inside it, and it does not know I am beautiful, and it does not know I am ugly, and it does not know I am, and the relief of this is enormous. The relief of being *a surface.* Of being *a temperature.* Of being something that does not need to be distinguished from the other things that it is.

I have been, my whole life, a thing that needs to be distinguished. A name. A role. A self with edges and a history and a reason to leave. The chamber does not deal in those categories. The chamber deals in cycles. In maintenance. In the steady, saturated, overfull work of being exactly what it is.

And I am here, and I am in it, and I am part of what it is, and it is enough.

---

Days pass. Or what might be days. The plant grows against my shins, now — a thin skin of photosynthetic tissue following the contour of my legs, growing thinner as it goes, barely a film at its edges, a green shadow on the fabric of my trousers. My boots have been colonized. The roots have found the seams. The mineral weep of the water system has deposited a faint crust on the buckle of my belt, and the plant has rooted in the crust, and I am becoming — slowly, incrementally, the way the chamber does everything — a part of the surface. A part of the site. Not the organism itself. Not the loop. But a surface the loop runs across, the way it runs across tile, across bronze, across the crusted pipe-mouths. I am being incorporated. Not consumed. *Incorporated.* The way a reef incorporates a shipwreck. The way a forest incorporates a stone wall. The thing remains itself, but the thing around it grows, and the boundary between them softens, and after enough time has passed, the boundary is a matter of opinion, and opinion is not a category the chamber deals in.

I should be frightened. I think, from a certain angle, I am frightened — or I was frightened, or I will be frightened, if the watch ever becomes legible again, if the report ever needs filing, if the people who sent me ever become real again with the pressure and specificity that would make them real. But the fright is a surface fright. It is the fright of a condensation drop falling. It lands, and there is a small percussion, a tap, and then silence, and the water is still, and the chamber breathes, and the fright is part of the water now, processed, filtered, changed, returned.

---

I would be the one who stays too long, and I would become — not the chamber, not the loop, not the organism — but a record. A stamp. A numeral in the language that reads left-to-right and also inward. A notation pressed into the surface of the living concrete, legible to no one, meaning nothing, but *there*. Present. A mark in the accrual. Evidence that something stood here, once, in the warm water, in the green light, in the sourceless glow, and was, for a time, a surface.

And the chamber would continue. The water would be warm. The leaves would press their flat palms against the surface tension. The condensation would fall.

Tap. Silence. Tap.
