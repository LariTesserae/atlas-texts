I am the one who scores the bark.

Not the bark of the great structural frames—thoseLoad-bearing curvatures of living banyan-willow that form the outer walls of the meeting hall and the deep dwellings. That work belongs to families who have breathed the resin of those trunks for four generations, who know the slow pulse of the sap as intimately as their own heartbeat. My hands are too quick for the old wood. My fingers, long and flat-palmed, itch for the delicate work, the fine cuts, the green and growing edges where a wall is not yet a wall but only an idea reaching for the light.

I grow the doors.

You have walked through them. You must have. Every dwelling has one, and the meeting hall has seven—each grown from a different parent branch, each with its own grain and temper and time of year for opening. Perhaps you noticed the one nearest the central basin, the door of my grandmother's dwelling, the one that has been growing for longer than I have been alive. Its hinge is a living spiral of wood, scored and pegged forty wet-seasons ago by hands that are now ash in the root-mat and smoke in the walls. It thickens every year. I have added three new pegs myself, bone wedges carved from the ribs of a ridge-viper that a child found dead in the upper channels. The door remembers the hands that shaped it. Sometimes, in the deep night when the frog-roar drops to its lowest register, I press my ear to the grain and listen for my grandmother's breathing. The wood is warm. The wood is always warm.

I was born in the Weeping, as all of us are, emerged wet and squalling into air so thick with moisture that my first breath was half water. The children here do not cry the way lowland children cry. Our first sound is a kind of sputtering click, an attempt to clear the fluid from our lungs that mirrors the click-language we will learn later. My mother tells me I was a quiet child—not silent, never silent here, impossible, but *contained*, my sounds fitting into the spaces between the frog-calls like a stone settling into a stream-bed. I did not run through the interstitial passages the way the other children did. I watched. I touched. I traced the grain of the living walls with my small fingers and felt something there that I had no words for: a direction, a purpose, a *wanting* in the wood.

I was five growths old when my grandmother first let me hold a scoring blade.

The blade was bone—a curve of rib from some animal long dead, ground to an edge so fine it could part the bark without tearing the cambium beneath. My hands were too small then to make the cuts myself, but she guided them, her fingers over mine, pressing the blade into the green skin of a young branch in the precise spiral that would teach it to bend without breaking. I remember the smell: sap and wet fiber and the mineral tang of my grandmother's skin. I remember the way the wood *gave*, a sudden soft surrender as the blade found its depth, and the way the exposed tissue beneath gleamed, pale and wet, like the inside of a mouth. I remember the keen of it, the rightness, the sense of having found the thing my hands were made to do.

I am not sure I can explain this to you. You come from a place where things are *built*. Assembled from dead materials into forms that the materials themselves did not choose. Here, we shape what is already growing. We do not impose; we *direct*. A door is not a plan realized; it is a conversation with a living thing that has its own tendencies, its own preferences, its own way of reaching for light. I have spent whole seasons learning the grain of a single branch, understanding where it *wants* to go, before I make the first cut. The cut is a suggestion. The peg is an argument. The growth that follows is a negotiation.

Sometimes the branch refuses. This is not metaphor. I have scored a spiral that should have produced a hinge, pegged it true, waited through the long wet season—and watched the branch grow straight despite my cuts, healing the wound and continuing its reach toward the canopy. A lesser grower might have forced it, scored again deeper, pegged again tighter. I let it go. The branch knew something I did not. Perhaps the light was wrong where I wanted the hinge. Perhaps the weight of the door would have been too much for that particular joint. The branch was not *my* door. I was its shaper, and it was mine, but the shaping went both ways.

You asked about art, once. You were looking for paintings, carvings, the visual markers you associate with creative expression. You did not recognize what was under your hands every time you pushed through a woven root-curtain. The weave itself. The pattern of living and trained branches that form the walls of every dwelling. The spiral of a door-hinge that echoes the spiral of water moving through the channels. The way a grower will sometimes score a branch in a pattern that is not strictly structural—three cuts where two would suffice, a curve that could have been straight—simply because the pattern is *beautiful*, because it pleases the branch, because the Weeping gives and the Weeping takes and sometimes what it gives is the chance to make something that is not only useful but *right*.

I have grown doors that sing.

This is not a claim I make lightly. The door of the meeting hall's eastern entrance—the one I grew from a seedling twelve growths ago, the one that is still young and thin and will thicken for decades yet—it has a resonance. When it closes, the living hinge creaks in a tone that harmonizes with the frog-roar. I did not plan this. I could not have planned it; the pitch of the frogs shifts with humidity and season and the mood of the hollow itself. But I scored the hinge with a particular depth and spacing, and the wood grew in a way that produced a thickness and tension at just the right point, and now, when the door is pulled shut in the early evening, it adds its voice to the chorus. Sometimes I close it just to hear it. I am not the only one.

The children know. They have learned to swing on the vine-ropes near the eastern entrance, letting their momentum pull the door open and shut in a rhythm that makes the hinge-sound rise and fall against the frog-bass. The first time I heard them doing it, I stopped in the passage and pressed my hand against the nearest wall and felt the vibration travel through the living wood, and I wept, though I could not have told you why. The Weeping gives. The Weeping takes. The Weeping keeps. And sometimes, in the keeping, there is this: a door that sings, and children who sing with it, and a hollow so full of sound that the sound itself becomes a kind of silence, a dwelling-place, a home.

You will not understand this. I see it in your face when you watch me work—the way your brow furrows, the way your fingers twitch as though you want to take the blade and *make* the wood do what you want. You come from a place of imposition. You believe that the shaper has authority over the shaped. Here, we know better. The branch was alive before I cut it. It will be alive after I am gone. My cuts are a single moment in a conversation that began before my grandmother was born and will continue after my great-grandchildren are ash in the root-mat. I am not the author of the door. I am the midwife.

My grandmother died in the wet season two growths past.

She was very old—older than anyone in the hollow could remember, though remembering is not our way. Her skin had faded to the color of water. Her eyes were pale wells. She moved through the passages with a slowness that made everyone move around her, and she touched every wall as she passed, her fingers reading the grain like a language only she could speak. In her last days, she could not rise from her sleeping platform, and the dwelling was so full of keeners that I could not enter, could only stand outside and listen to the sound of her breathing growing slower, quieter, finding the spaces between the frogs until there were no more spaces and she was gone.

I did not eat her. This is not a thing I am required to explain to you, but I want to. The eating of the dead is not universal; it is a choice made by those who worked most closely with the one who has gone, and my grandmother's hands had been too weak to shape for a long time before her death. Those who ate her were the growers of the old walls, the ones who had learned from her when they were children. They took her flesh into themselves and they will carry it into their work, and someday the walls they grow will hold something of her strength, her patience, her way of listening to the wood.

I took her hands.

Not literally—though I was there when they were separated from her wrists, and I watched the bone-brittle fingers curl inward as though reaching for a blade they would never hold again. I took the *memory* of her hands. The way they moved. The pressure they applied. The particular angle of the blade when she scored a spiral that would become a hinge. I have her hands now, inside my own. When I work, I feel her guiding me, adjusting the depth of my cuts, showing me where the grain wants to go.

Her actual hands were burned in a slow smolder beneath the root-mat of the eastern door—the one she grew, the one that is older than me, the one that will be growing long after I am ash. The smoke rose green and thick and was absorbed into the living wood. This is not symbol. This is feeding. The door carries her now, as it carries every grower who has tended it. When I press my ear to its grain, I hear them all—a depth of sound beneath the frog-roar, a murmur of hands and blades and breathing, the accumulated whisper of everyone who has ever shaped this place.

I think you believe this is metaphor.

I think you cannot help believing it. You come from a world where the dead are gone, where the walls are dead and the doors are dead and everything that shelters you was killed before it was made useful. Here, the walls breathe. The doors sing. The dead are not gone—they are *incorporated*, taken into the living tissue of the hollow, becoming part of the structure that shelters those who come after. My grandmother is in the eastern door. She is in my hands. She is in the root-mat beneath my bare feet, in the water that beads on the walls, in the condensation-lice that the frogs eat and the frogs that the snakes eat and the snakes that my daughter is learning to avoid with that particular lift-and-place gait that I learned at her age and my grandmother learned at mine.

This is not comfort. This is fact. The hollow keeps what it needs. The rest goes on.

My daughter is six growths old. She has my quick hands and her father's gift for the click-language, and she moves through the interstitial passages like water through the channels—swift, inevitable, finding every gap and crevice that the adults have outgrown. She brings me frogs from the upper walls, the spray frogs that I cannot reach without climbing, and she watches me work with an intensity that I recognize because I wore it myself at her age. She has not yet held a scoring blade. She is too young. The cuts require a steadiness that children her age do not have.

But I have given her a piece of bone—a sliver of my grandmother's rib, saved before the burning—and I have shown her how to press it against the bark of a young seedling, not cutting yet, just feeling the grain, learning the direction the branch wants to grow. She does this every morning before the trading begins, her small fingers tracing the living wood, her breath making patterns in the cold wet air. She is learning to listen. This is the first skill: not the cut, not the peg, but the *listening*, the long patience of attending to what is already growing and understanding what it wants to become.

She is not cute. I am glad of this. Cute children die in the hollow. My daughter is sharp as a bone blade, alert as a rail-bird, quick as a spray frog leaping from a wet wall. She does not shelter. She is not sheltered. She learns by doing, by going, by touching everything and climbing everything and falling sometimes and getting up again. She has been bitten by a snake once—a young emerald, barely a foot long, not enough venom to kill but enough to teach. She was sick for three days. On the fourth day, she was back in the passages, and her gait had changed: a slight lift-and-pause that was not there before, a new attention to the quality of stillness on surfaces.

I did not grieve. I did not need to. She learned. The lesson took. This is the way of the hollow.

You will leave. I know this. I have watched you in the passages, your dry skin catching on the living walls, your narrow feet slipping on the root-mat, your face blank where ours are expressive, your voice too loud and too slow for the quick communication of the hollow. You do not hear the door-songs. You do not feel the grain-pulse. You cannot read the faces. You are not of the Weeping, and the Weeping has not claimed you, and you will return to your dry ridge and your dead walls and tell stories of the strange wet people who eat their dead and grow their doors and live in a place where everything, everything, everything is alive.

I will not miss you. This is not unkindness. You were never here. You passed through like a beetle paddling in a puddle, touching the surface but never the depth, and when you are gone the hollow will close around the space you occupied and forget you were ever here. This is the way of the Weeping. It gives. It takes. It keeps what it needs and releases what it does not.

But I will tell you this, because you asked, and because the telling is itself a kind of shaping:

I am the one who scores the bark. I am the one who grows the doors. I am a cell in the body of the hollow, a process in the long conversation between the living and the living, a pair of hands that listens to wood and suggests the shape it might become. My grandmother is in my hands. My daughter is in the seedlings. My own hands will be ash and smoke someday, absorbed into a door I have not yet grown, carried in the grain of a wall that is reaching now toward the canopy light, patient and green and alive.

The Weeping gives. The Weeping takes.

The Weeping keeps.

I am kept.
