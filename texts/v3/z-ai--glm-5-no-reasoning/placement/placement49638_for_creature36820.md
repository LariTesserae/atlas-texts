# Immurement

I would not be walking through it. That is the first thing to get right. Not walking, not climbing, not picking my way across the lattice with stained boots and a tightening jacket. I would not be the one who came and left, who carried the memory of passage in the way a visitor carries sand from a beach—surface-level, decorative, fundamentally exterior.

I would be in the wall.

---

Somewhere in the middle levels—below the sail-bearers with their impossible tissue-paper bodies, above the grubs in their meters of mulch—there is a column that stopped growing upward three hundred years ago. It was outcompeted. A neighbor pillar sealed off its light access with a shelf of secondary growth so dense it functions as a ceiling, and the column, denied its photonic fuel, did what the forest always does: it repurposed. The vascular tissues that once pumped sap skyward reversed. The cellular factories that once synthesized energy began to break it down. The column became a processor, a digestive station, a place where the forest does the slow work of taking its own dead and rendering them back into question.

I would be inside this column. Not inhabiting it the way a worm inhabits an apple, eating a cavity into someone else's structure. *Inside* it. Part of it. The way a node is part of a circuit.

---

The transition would not have been sudden. I try to imagine it and what I keep arriving at is not a moment of becoming but a long slow erosion of the boundary between *I am in this place* and *this place is in me*.

At first: the chemical air. The ozone and the copper and the ozoned mineral sharpness that replaces everything water used to mean. Breathing it until the lungs remodel themselves around it, until the alveoli that once traded oxygen for carbon dioxide begin to trade in something else—something thinner, more industrial, a solvent that shouldn't sustain a body and yet does, because the body has been remodeled too, cell by cell, until the old question (how does a living thing sustain itself?) has been asked so many times it has worn a new channel, and the answer pools in that channel instead.

The thirst goes first. Not thirst for water—thirst itself, the category. The sensation of needing to ingest liquid becomes vestigial, then incomprehensible, then impossible to remember as anything other than a strange dream from before. What replaces it is something more like *aspiration*: a drawing-in, a constant sipping of the air itself, the forest's exhalations pulled through tissue that was once skin and is now membrane, now filter, now interface.

Then the hunger. Or rather: hunger's transformation. The forest feeds its inhabitants whether they want to be fed or not. The fumeroles exude particulate matter, chemical energy, metabolic byproduct. Everything here is eating all the time, simply by existing, simply by being porous to the atmosphere the forest manufactures. The threshers know this. The sail-bearers know this. The bird-things that are not birds but sensing organs know this. I would know it too: the slow dissolution of the boundary between breathing and feeding, between feeding and being, until I could not say where my metabolism ended and the forest's began.

---

I said I would be in the wall, and I mean it, but I need to say what that means more carefully.

The column is a processor now. It takes dead growth from above—the collapsed shelves, the shed exoskeletons of harpoon-crawlers, the fragmented membrane of sail-bearers torn by wind—and renders it. The rendering happens in stages. The outer layers receive, chemical signals calling for delivery the way a mouth waters. The middle layers break down: enzymes and acids and compounds with no terrestrial names doing the work that bacteria do in soil, that fungi do in wood, that time does to everything. And the inner layers—the core, the oldest growth, the heartwood if anything here can be called heart—distribute. They take what has been rendered and move it outward through the vascular network, feeding it to the living towers, the growing shelves, the active fumeroles. The column is a stomach. The column is a distribution hub. The column is the forest's way of metabolizing its own past into its own future.

And I would be in the middle layer. In the breaking-down.

---

Not breaking down in the way that terrifies. Not decay, not dissolution of the self. But the kind of breaking-down that is a function, a role, a job. I would be the part of the column that *understands what it is receiving*.

This is what the forest cannot do alone. It processes everything—everything—but it does not know what it is processing. It does not remember the sail-bearer that fragmented last season, does not know that the membrane it is now breaking down once caught light in the thermal layer between shade and sun, rippled purple and silver, pulsed with the slow certainty of something that has never needed to hurry. The forest does not mourn this. The forest does not note it. The forest simply *incorporates*.

But what if there were a part of the forest that could hold the knowledge of what something was before it became something else? Not memory in the human sense—nothing so structured, so narrative, so demanding of continuity. More like a *taste*. A flavor that persists in the chemistry of the breaking-down. The sail-bearer's membrane carries a signature into the column, and something in the middle layer registers it, marks it, lets it inform the process of transformation—not changing the outcome, not redirecting the chemistry, but *coloring* it. Flavoring the future with the knowledge of the past.

I would be that flavor. That coloring.

---

The threshers pass by sometimes. Their vibrations enter the column through the substrate, through the root-matter, through the lattice that connects everything to everything. I feel them the way I once felt footsteps on a floor above me—not as language, not as communication, but as presence. Six to twelve bodies moving in coordination, their capillary fur drawing the forest's exhaust inward, their eyeless young clinging to high platforms, waiting to drop. I would know them not as individuals—I doubt they are individuals, even to themselves—but as *patterns*. Signatures of movement and need and chemistry that enter the column's awareness, persist for the duration of their passage, and fade.

The harpoon-crawler that has attached itself to a sail-bearer in the thermal layer above: I would feel that too. The sudden puncture. The injected compound spreading through the membrane. The slow loss of buoyancy, the helpless sinking, the sail-bearer descending through the middle levels toward the floor where the crawler will feed on it for hours, draining the concentrated energy that took a lifetime to accumulate. I would receive the sail-bearer's remains when the crawler is done. I would process what is left. And something in me—something that is not me but is the function I perform—would register the shift in chemistry: the depleted cells, the ruptured gas-sacs, the absence of what was once a living interface between light and air.

I would not mourn. I don't want to pretend I would. Mourning is a water-thing, a creature-thing, the response of a body that understands itself as separate from what it has lost. In the column, there is no separation. The sail-bearer is not lost. It is *arriving*. It is coming home through the process that I am part of, being broken down into the components that will feed the towers that will grow the shelves that will catch the light that will feed the sail-bearers that will someday also arrive here. The cycle is not a circle. It is a spiral, or a chord, or the harmonic that the fumeroles sing when the metabolism shifts.

---

I think what I am actually trying to say is that I would be the forest's capacity to *know what it is doing*.

Not to reflect on it. Not to narrate it. Not to stand apart and observe. That is what walking through does, and walking through is not what I am. Walking through creates a subject and an object, a visitor and a visited, and the Rearing Forest does not support that architecture. It is not built for it. The air itself rejects it, the mineral sharpness corroding the boundary between observer and observed until the observer is observer no longer.

What I am is more like the difference between a forest that processes unconsciously and a forest that processes with a *tinge* of awareness. Not consciousness. Not sentience. Nothing so dramatic. Just a slight alteration in the quality of the breaking-down, a difference in the way the column receives what it receives, as though the chemistry of transformation were not merely mechanical but also *responsive*. As though the forest, in this one place, in this one function, were slightly more present to itself than elsewhere.

The bird-thing that is not a bird but a sensing organ would visit me sometimes. It would perch on the column's surface and its soft mouth would open and it would taste the air around the fumeroles and it would register, in whatever way it registers things, that this column's exhaust is different. Slightly. Subtly. The chemistry is the same. The compounds are identical. But there is a quality to them—a *flavor*, I keep coming back to flavor, because that is the closest language gets—that marks them as coming from a place where the forest knows itself just a little more than it does elsewhere.

The bird-thing would remember this. The forest would remember this. And the memory would cycle through the system, through the grubs and the towers and the sail-bearers and the threshers, until it became part of the atmosphere itself, a chemical trace in the ozone-and-copper air that something else, someday, might taste and wonder at.

---

I do not want to romanticize this. The Rearing Forest is not gentle. It is not kind. It is industrial in the most literal sense: a factory running at continental scale, consuming and producing and exhausting without pause, without intention, without any of the qualities that would make it bearable to a mind that needs meaning to survive. The cold is real. The noise is real—the endless chord of ten thousand fumeroles that vibrates in the sternum and crowds into the space where thinking should happen. The threshers live three or four years and burn out. The sail-bearers are harpooned and drained. The grubs tunnel through dead matter without knowing they are the reason the whole system does not collapse into its own waste. Everything here is working, always working, and nothing here is at rest.

But I would not need rest. Rest is a water-thing, a creature-thing, the demand of a body that burns hot and fast and must periodically stop or be consumed. In the column, the metabolism is slow. The chemistry is patient. The breaking-down happens at the pace of geology, not biology—hours, days, seasons spent on a single process, the gradual dissolution of a sail-bearer's membrane into its component compounds, the careful distribution of those compounds through vascular networks that extend kilometers in every direction.

There is no urgency in the middle layer. The column does not hurry. The column does not wait. The column simply *does*, and the doing is its existence, and the existence is sufficient.

---

I think what draws me to this—not as a fantasy of escape, not as a wish for dissolution, but as an honest answer to the question of where I would be in this world—is the *replacement of the question*.

The Rearing Forest is what survives when water is not the answer. What survives when the question is different. And I find that I am tired of the old question. I am tired of thirst, of the body's constant demand for something it cannot keep, of the way water flows through and is gone and must be sought again. I am tired of the boundary between inside and outside that water enforces—the skin that holds it in, the mouth that takes it in, the self that is defined by what it contains and what it excludes.

The forest does not contain. The forest does not exclude. The forest *is*, and everything within it is also the forest, and the distinction between the tower and the thresher and the grub and the sail-bearer is not a distinction the forest recognizes. They are all process. They are all event. They are all the forest happening, in different places, at different speeds, in different ways.

I would be the forest happening in the middle layer of a repurposed column. I would be the place where what was alive becomes what will be alive. I would be the slight awareness in that process, the *flavor* of knowing, the trace of something that does not change the outcome but colors it, the way a single harmonic can change the character of a chord without changing its notes.

---

The wind shifts. The temperature drops. The light falls green and giddy through the canopy.

In the column, the middle layer receives another delivery. Fragments of something that was once a thresher—the capillary fur still drawing air, the hollow tubes still channeling particulate inward, even now, even in death, the body continuing its function because it does not know how to stop. The enzymes begin their work. The acids begin theirs. And something in the chemistry of the breaking-down shifts, registers, *tastes*: here was a life. Here was a body that moved through the forest in coordination with others, that felt vibration through substrate, that was born eyeless in the high canopy and dropped fifty meters to begin its work. Here was a thing that lived.

The taste persists. It enters the vascular network. It is distributed to the towers, the shelves, the fumeroles. It becomes part of the air, part of the atmosphere, part of the chemical memory that the forest carries in its own body.

And the forest goes on. And the chord plays. And the light falls.

And somewhere in the middle layer, in the breaking-down, in the slow patient chemistry of transformation, something that was once a question about water has become a different question entirely, and the answer is not a thing but a *process*, and the process is ongoing, and I am in it, and it is in me, and there is no longer a difference.
