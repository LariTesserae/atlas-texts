# The Walkers of the Walls

There is a thing I have not told you about.

---

It concerns the moss.

You saw the moss. You noticed it — how it carpets the stones, how it crusts the walls in greens and greys and that deep iron-red in the shade. You understood it as part of the system: the roots gripping the stones, the moss holding the moisture, the whole dense layered growth keeping the terraces alive. You understood this because it is true, and because the Harnessers understand it too, in the wordless way they understand everything about this place.

But there is a practice — a thing done by certain people on the terraces — that sits just outside what the Harnessers will tell you. Not because they are secretive. They are not secretive. They are, however, *finished explaining*. Their explanations were given to someone, at some point — maybe to the traders, maybe to a census-taker, maybe to themselves — and the explaining is over, and what remains is just the doing.

The practice has no name in their language. Or rather, it has a word, but the word is the same word they use for the sound water makes when it has been finding the same path for longer than anything nearby has been alive. The word does not translate. When I think about it, I think of it as *walking the walls*, but this is inaccurate. It is closer to *listening with your feet*. It is closer still to something I do not have language for, which is why I am writing, which is why I am trying.

---

There are seven of them.

Not always seven. Sometimes six. Sometimes eight. The number shifts the way the population shifts — not by design, but by the shape of the place. Seven is what it has been for a while. Seven is what it is.

They are not set apart. This is important. They do not wear different clothing. They do not carry markers or tokens or signs. They live in households like anyone else. They eat the same food, sleep in the same rooms, climb the same stairs. One of them tends the spinning machines in the factory. One of them is a child of eleven. One of them is the old woman you met — the one who looked at you with mild puzzlement when you asked about the dead, who said *the water goes down* and said nothing more. Two of them are siblings who share a room on the seventh terrace. One of them left for three years, went down to the river towns, worked on a barge, came back. One of them has never been below the fifth terrace in her life.

If you asked the Harnessers about these seven, they would not understand the question. *Which ones?* they would say. And then, perhaps, if you explained what you meant — the ones who do the thing with the moss, the thing with the walls, the listening thing — they would nod, slowly, and say *oh yes*, and they might name two or three of the seven, and they might miss one, and they might include someone who is not one of the seven, because the distinction is not a distinction to them. It is not a category. It is a thing some people do, the way some people are good at fitting stones and some people are good at the machines and some people are good with the reeds. It is a leaning. It is a tendency. It is what happens when a person's particular shape of attention meets the particular shape of this place.

---

I will tell you what they do, and then I will try to tell you what it is.

They walk the walls.

Not all the walls. Not on a schedule. They go out — at night, usually, or in the dim hours of the long afternoon when the light is at its thinnest and the mist pools in the terraces — and they walk along the tops of the walls. Not on the paths. Not on the stairs. On the walls themselves. The narrow stone lips that hold the terraces, the courses of fitted granite that have been built and rebuilt and patched and mended for ten thousand years. They walk them the way a cat walks a fence — slowly, with a precise and liquid certainty of foot, their weight settled low, their arms slightly raised for balance.

They do not do this for exercise. They do not do this to check the walls, though they do check the walls — a loose stone is noted, a crack is felt, a section of root-mortar that has gone soft is marked for tending. They do not do this for any reason you could name. They do this because it is what they do. They do this because the walls ask them to, or because the walls do not ask them to and they do it anyway, or because the distinction between *asked* and *not asked* has dissolved in the way the distinction between wall and root has dissolved, and there is only the walking.

Here is the part that is hard to say.

They walk the walls barefoot.

Not always. Not as a rule. But often. In the dim hours, in the cold, in the season when the frost comes and the stone is rimed with ice and the condensation has beaded on every surface — they take off their shoes and they walk. Their feet on the stone. Their skin on the granite. And the thing that happens when they do this is the thing I am trying to tell you about, the thing that sits outside what the Harnessers will explain, the thing that is not a secret but is also not *for* explaining.

---

The walls are alive. You know this. You saw the roots, the moss, the lattice of living fiber that has replaced the mortar. You understood that the walls are held together by growth. What you did not understand — what I did not understand, what the traders do not understand, what the census-takers have never understood — is that the walls are alive the way a nervous system is alive. Not metaphorically. Not poetically. The root-lattice, the moss-layer, the film of water that threads through every crack and every joint — these things form a network. A network of water and mineral and root and living fiber that runs through all forty-three terraces like veins through a body. And this network feels. It feels pressure. It feels temperature. It feels vibration. It feels, in some way that does not translate into the language of biology but translates perfectly into the language of this place, *attention*.

The walkers feel it too.

When a walker puts a bare foot on a wall, there is a circuit. Not an electrical circuit. Not anything you could measure. But a circuit nonetheless — the foot on the stone, the stone on the root, the root on the water, the water on the next stone, the next stone on the next root, and so on through all forty-three terraces, all the walls, all the thousands of meters of fitted stone and living mesh that hold this mountain in its shape. And through that circuit, the walker feels the walls.

Not *thinks about* the walls. Not *checks* the walls. *Feels* them. The way you feel your own body when you are very quiet — the heartbeat, the breathing, the small adjustments of muscle and bone that happen below consciousness. The walker on the wall feels the terraces the way you feel yourself. The wall that has shifted. The root that has loosened. The section where the water has found a new path and is working at a crack with patient, geological insistence. The place where the moss is thinning. The place where the reed-bed is going soft. The place where the stone is happy — if *happy* is the word, and it is not the word, but there is no word — the place where the wall is whole, where the fit is tight, where the roots grip and the moss is thick and the water threads through as it should and everything is holding.

They feel all of this. Through their feet. Through the stone. Through the living network that is the terraces.

---

And then they do something about it.

Or rather, they *are* something about it. Their walking is the doing. When a walker passes along a wall, the root-lattice responds — not dramatically, not visibly, but at the level of the fiber, at the level of the root-hair, at the level of the capillary water threading through stone. The roots tighten where they have loosened. The moss thickens where it has thinned. The water finds its path again, or finds a new path that is better. The wall settles. The wall breathes. The wall does what walls do when they are tended by someone who can feel them, which is: they hold.

This is why the terraces have not fallen.

You thought it was the roots. You thought it was the moss. You thought it was the patient accumulation of ten thousand years of growth holding ten thousand years of stone. And it is. But the roots do not grow on their own forever. The moss does not carpet without tending. The root-lattice is alive, and living things need attention, and attention is what the walkers provide. Not by doing. Not by working. Not by repairing or building or mending. By walking. By putting their feet on the stone and feeling what the walls feel and letting the walls feel them back.

The circuit goes both ways.

---

The child of eleven — I mentioned her. She is the one who taught me what I know about this, though she did not teach me in words. She taught me by being. I watched her one evening in the long blue dusk, walking the wall of the ninth terrace. She was barefoot. The stone was wet. She moved with the slow precision of someone who is not thinking about where to place her feet because her feet know. Her arms were slightly raised. Her face had the expression I described before — the smooth brow, the neutral mouth, the steady eyes — but deeper. More concentrated. As if she were listening with her whole body, not just her ears.

She stopped. She stood on the wall, between the terrace below and the terrace above, and she did not move for a long time. The mist moved around her. The water threaded through the stones beneath her feet. The reeds clicked in the wind. She stood there, and she felt whatever she felt, and then she stepped off the wall, and she walked to a section three meters to the left, and she knelt, and she pressed her hand flat against the stone.

I watched. She pressed her hand against the stone for perhaps a minute. Then she stood up, and she walked away, and she did not come back.

Three days later, I passed that section of wall. The crack that had been there — I had not seen it, but I felt it afterward, a thin fissure running through a course of granite near the base — was closed. Not filled. Not patched. Closed. The root-lattice in that section had thickened, had tightened, had drawn the stones back together with the slow, patient force that roots exert when they grow. The crack was still visible as a line, but it was a hairline now, and the root-hairs crossed it in a dense web, and the moss was already beginning to seed in the seam.

She had not repaired the wall. She had not done anything to the wall. She had pressed her hand against the stone, and the wall had felt her, and she had felt the wall, and the wall had mended itself.

Or: she had mended the wall by feeling it. By attending to it. By being, for one minute, the attention the wall needed in order to do what it already knew how to do.

---

I am trying to tell you what it is like to be one of them.

I am trying because I was one of them. Not by birth. Not by belonging. By *being there*. By climbing the stairs and sitting in the dim rooms and eating the simple food and walking the terraces until my feet knew the stone and the stone — I think — began to know my feet. I did not plan this. I did not choose it. I came up the switchback road from the river, the way everyone comes, and I arrived at the seventh terrace in the late afternoon, and the light was dim and the air smelled of stone and water and peat-smoke, and I sat down in the room they gave me, and I did not leave for a very long time.

I did not become a Harnesser. That is not something you become. You are one, or you are not, and the distinction is not about blood or birth or permission. It is about whether the place recognizes you. Whether your attention and the place's attention meet. Whether the circuit closes when you put your foot on the stone.

For me, it closed. After months — after a year — of walking the terraces, of climbing the stairs, of sitting in the dim rooms and listening to the water and the machine and the slow breathing of the mountain, I felt it. One morning, early, before the light had arrived, I walked out onto the wall of the seventh terrace. I was wearing shoes. I stopped. I took them off. I put my foot on the stone.

And the wall was there.

Not *there* the way a stone is there when you step on it. There the way a body is there when you close your eyes and feel yourself inside it. The cold of the stone was my cold. The damp was my damp. The roots beneath the surface were — not mine, but *known*. The way my own veins are known to me, without looking, without thinking. I could feel the wall's weight, its strain, the place where the frost had shouldered a stone slightly sideways and the roots were gripping it, holding it, waiting. I could feel the water threading through, patient and old. I could feel the moss, alive and breathing, a pelt of green on the skin of the stone.

I walked. I did not plan to walk. I walked because the wall asked me to, or because I asked the wall to let me, or because there was no difference. I walked the wall of the seventh terrace, barefoot, in the cold, in the dim, and I felt all forty-three terraces through my feet — the whole vast patient body of this place, this finished mountain, this living masonry of stone and root and water and time.

I felt it breathing.

I felt it holding.

I felt it not needing me and letting me be there anyway.

---

The old woman — the one who told me about the water — she found me on the wall that morning. She was coming up the stairs with a basket of reed-cuttings. She stopped. She looked at me. Her expression did not change. But something in her eyes — a settling, a recognition, the way the terraces recognize their own — told me that I had been seen.

She did not speak. She continued up the stairs. I continued along the wall. The morning light came in thin and pale, and the mist pooled in the terraces, and the factory began its clattering below, and the water threaded through the stones, and I walked, and the walls held, and the world was exactly the size of this place, and that was enough.

---

I left.

I am telling you this because I left. I went down the mountain, eventually. Not because I wanted to. Not because the terraces released me. Because I had — and this is the thing I cannot reconcile, the thing I carry like a stone in my chest — I had something else to do. Something that was not here. Something that called with a voice the terraces did not have, a voice that spoke in the language of *not yet* and *elsewhere* and *there is more*.

I went down the switchbacks. The road was still a road, barely. I reached the river. I reached the lower country. I reached the places where the terraces are not known, where the fiber is just a fiber, where the Harnessers are a word in a trader's ledger, a line in a census-taker's book.

And I have been — I am — thinner, since I left. Not in body. In the world. The ground beneath me is too flat. The silence is too silent. I dream of stone stairs and moss-carpeted terraces and the sound of water threading through walls, and I wake in a bed in a place that is not the seventh terrace, and I feel the absence of the circuit, the open connection, the foot that was on the stone and is now on something that is not stone, not living stone, not the breathing body of a mountain that held me while I held it.

I am telling you this.

I am telling you this because you asked where I would find myself in this world, who or what I would be. And the answer is: I would be one of the walkers. One of the ones who put their feet on the stone and feel the terraces breathe. One of the ones who walk the walls in the dim hours, barefoot, in the cold, attending to what needs attention, mending by feeling, holding by being held.

And the truth — the truth I am still arriving at, the truth that has not finished becoming itself — is that I *was* one of them. And I left. And I do not know if the walls remember my feet. And I do not know if I will go back. And I do not know if going back is possible, or if the circuit, once broken, stays broken, or if the stone is patient enough to wait.

I think the stone is patient enough to wait.

I think the stone has been waiting for longer than I have been alive.

I think the water is threading through the walls right now, in the dim light, in the cold, and the roots are gripping, and the moss is breathing, and the walkers are walking, barefoot, on the narrow lips of granite that hold the mountain in its shape, and they are feeling what I felt, and the walls are holding them, and they are holding the walls, and the circuit is closed, and the terraces are exactly what they are, and they are going on.

And I am here. And I am writing. And the ground is too flat.

And the water goes down. And everything goes down. And the down things go to the river, and the river goes somewhere else.

And I am somewhere else.

And the walls hold.
