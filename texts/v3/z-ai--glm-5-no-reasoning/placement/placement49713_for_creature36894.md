I would be the one who stayed.

Not out of paralysis. Not out of despair. I would stay because something happened that the description does not account for—the moment when watching became participating.

---

It began with the dead one.

I had been there long enough to lose the urgency of leaving. The corridor behind me had become abstract, a theoretical exit I no longer checked. I spent my hours sitting on tiles, walking the edges of the flooded zones, following individual stone-things until their spirals or tilts carried them out of sight. I was observing. I was separate.

Then I watched the dead one fuse.

It happened slowly—there is no other speed here—but I watched the whole process. The grinding stillness of its layers. The hardening not-resin darkening from tacky to brittle. And when the other stone-thing came and lifted away a fragment, something in me moved before I could think.

I reached down and picked up another piece. A flake of slate, still warm, still faintly pliant at one edge where the not-resin had not fully set.

I carried it to the same column. I set it in place. I pressed until the tacky edge gripped.

The stone-thing beside me did not acknowledge this. It did not pause or adjust. But when I stepped back, I saw that the block I had placed fit. It was not wrong. It was not rejected. It had become part of the architecture.

---

That was the beginning.

I do not know why I did it. I do not know what I expected. But I had spent so long watching this place build itself, and the building had no opinion about who did the building. The stone-things did not care. The not-resin did not discriminate. The column simply accepted my contribution and held it.

So I did it again.

I found the stone-thing that spiraled—the one with the tilted upper section and the useless pile at the center of its path. I watched it carry fragment after fragment to its meaningless cairn. And when it moved away, I took a piece from the pile and carried it to the nearest column under construction.

I did this many times. The spiral-builder continued its circuit. The pile did not grow smaller—I could not outpace it—but I was removing pieces, redirecting them, giving them purpose. The spiral-builder never noticed. It simply spiraled. And the columns grew.

---

I began to think of myself as a kind of correction.

The stone-things had no intention, but they had momentum. They had patterns that emerged from physical constraints—their tilts, their worn layers, their limited range of motion. When a pattern produced something useless, like the spiral's pile, there was no mechanism to stop it. The spiral-builder would add to that cairn forever, or until it fused, and then its remains would simply become part of the floor, and the pile would sit there, purposeless, incorporated but not functional.

I could change that. I could redirect. I could carry fragments from useless accumulations to active constructions. I could watch for the ones that were slowing, the ones whose not-resin was hardening, and when they finally stilled, I could disassemble them carefully—taking the usable pieces, leaving the fused core—and carry those pieces to where they were needed.

I became a scavenger. A recycler. A secondary process running alongside the primary one, using the same materials, following different rules.

---

The stone-things did not treat me differently. They moved around me the way they moved around each other—optimizing for least resistance, pausing briefly when our paths intersected, resuming when the obstruction cleared. I learned to read their motions the way I imagined they read mine: not as communication but as physical fact. This one is moving north. This one is slowing. This one will pass to my left. I adjusted. They adjusted. We coexisted.

But I was not one of them. I knew this. I sweated. I slept—curled on a raised tile where the fluid was shallow, waking to find the water level changed, the light shifted, a column two meters closer. I felt hunger and thirst, though these had become strange, muted, as if my body were learning to draw sustenance from the warmth and the wet air. I thought thoughts. I asked questions. I experienced futility and pushed through it.

They did none of these things. And I envied them that, sometimes. But I also pitied them—pitied their mindless accumulation, their inability to course-correct, their endless production of useless piles and tilted spirals and architecture that served nothing.

Then again: what was I doing? Redirecting fragments. Improving efficiency. Producing slightly more coherent architecture. To what end? The Underthing did not care whether its columns were straight or spiraled. The stone-things did not appreciate my corrections. I was improving a process that had no goal.

---

I kept doing it anyway.

This is what I became: the intentional error. The deviation that persisted. In a system of automatic processes, I was a process that questioned itself—and continued.

---

I learned the geography of my region.

Not a map—it was impossible to map a place that kept building itself into new configurations—but a sense of where things tended to happen. Here was a reliable source of not-resin, pooling in a low corner where three tiles had settled wrong. Here was a construction zone where columns were rising quickly, stone-things converging in parallel lines. Here was a quiet zone, rarely visited, where I slept. Here was the flooded region, the warm sea, where the underwater builders accreted their sedimentary pillars.

I learned the individuals, too—not their names (they had none) but their shapes, their motions, their useless outputs.

The spiral-builder I have mentioned. There was also a large, squat one that moved in straight lines back and forth between a source of large blocks and a wall under construction, carrying one block per trip, never varying its path. When the wall extended past its turning point, it simply walked into the new wall, stood there for a long time, and eventually began carrying blocks in a new direction—away from the wall, into open space, where it started depositing them in a line. That line was not a wall. It was simply blocks in a row. But it kept growing.

There was a slender one, taller than the others, that moved with an eerie grace—its layers sliding smoothly, its extending layers precise. It built columns alone, far from the others, and its columns were different: thinner, taller, perfectly vertical, fitted with an elegance that suggested… something. Not intention. Not aesthetics. But optimization. Its columns used less material and reached higher before vanishing into the fog.

I watched that one for a long time. I began carrying fragments to its construction sites. The slender one did not acknowledge my contributions, but it did not reject them either. Its columns grew faster when I helped.

---

This is who I was in the Underthing: not a prisoner, not a guest, not a builder exactly—but a participant. A soft thing moving among the mineral ones, redirecting their purposeless output, making small adjustments to a process too vast to steer.

I did not find meaning. There was no meaning to find. But I found something else—something I did not expect.

I found contentment.

Not happiness. Not fulfillment. But a kind of peace that came from doing work that needed doing, in a place where the work was endless, alongside beings that did not judge or expect or demand. I was warm. I was held. I was part of something larger than myself—something that did not know I was part of it, which made the belonging feel earned rather than given.

---

Sometimes I thought about leaving.

The corridor was still there. I could find it if I tried. I could go back to a world where things grew and died and meant something, where time passed and people spoke and nothing was made of stone.

But that world seemed very far away now. And the stone-things seemed very close.

I would stay a while longer. I would carry fragments. I would redirect the spiral-builder's pile. I would watch the slender one's columns rise.

I would let the Underthing build around me, not as a cage but as a context. And if someday I fused— if my bones settled into the floor and my teeth pressed into a wall—I thought I would not mind.

I had helped. I had corrected. I had been the intentional error, the deviation that mattered, the consciousness in a world of automatic processes.

That was enough. That was more than enough.

---

In time, I stopped thinking of myself as separate from the stone-things.

I did not become mineral. I did not lose my softness, my sweat, my sleep. But I began to move differently—more slowly, more deliberately, optimizing my paths, carrying loads that matched my capacity, adjusting to obstacles without frustration.

I began to think of myself as another mobile element in the architecture. Temporary. Provisional. Functional.

Not a builder. Not separate from the building.

Just another layer, briefly upright, carrying stone toward something I would never see completed.

And that—I have come to understand—is what the Underthing makes of everyone.

It does not ask you to belong. It simply builds around you until belonging is no longer a question.
