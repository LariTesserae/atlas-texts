I find myself in the skin of the mountain, and the skin is the breath, and the breath is not alive and not dead and is my body and the body of every person who walks these corridors and is the stone itself that weeps and holds and slowly gives way.

I am the **unofficial mediator between the deep and the rim, the one who carries letters from below upward, the one who speaks both languages and trusts neither**.

---

I was born in the middle levels, in a stacked chamber that smelled of brass and wet stone and the sweet organic breath of something growing in the dark. My mother was a maintenance worker who scraped the breath from intake surfaces with a long-handled tool and brought home sheets of the pale crust to dry and use for partition walls. My father was a deep-speaker who came up for the festivals and sat at the edge of the crowd with his hands moving in small unconscious gestures on his knees, tracing thermal maps he was no longer near enough to read. They met because my mother's corridor ran above his steam-works chamber, and the heat from his workspace rose into her housing block, and she complained, and he was sent up to fix the insulation, and they barked at each other in the middle-level shout about pressure gradients and thermal bleeding and the rights of families to dry air, and something in the argument became something else, and I was born nine months later in the wet warmth of a chamber that was never quite dry.

I grew up bilingual in the way that all the Hollow's children of mixed-depth parentage grow up bilingual: I learned my mother's bark and my father's silence before I learned that they were different languages. I would shout across the corridor to my mother's neighbors and then descend the carved stairs to visit my father in the lower levels, where the sound swallowed my voice and I learned to move my hands instead, to flash palm-signals in the orange glow, to trace meaning in the condensation on pipe walls that would be erased in seconds. My father would watch me sign and nod, and his red-stained hands would correct mine, adjusting the angle of my wrist, the speed of my fingers, the pressure of my palm against the wet metal. My mother would watch me descend and stand at the top of the stairs and shout after me to be careful, and her voice would echo down the shaft and be lost in the mechanical thunder before it reached me, and I would hear only the first syllable, which was always my name, and I would raise my hand without turning around, which was the deep-speaker's gesture for *I hear you*, though she could not see it and I knew she could not see it and I did it anyway because the gesture was not for her. It was for the mountain.

I am not an engineer. I was tested, as all children are tested, at the age of twelve, and the academies near the rim rejected me because my spatial reasoning was adequate but not exceptional and my mathematical aptitude was, in the examiner's clipped phrase, *sufficient for practical application but lacking the analytical depth required for advanced hydrological modeling*. I was not surprised. I had grown up in the middle levels, and the middle levels do not produce engineers. They produce maintenance workers and food vendors and corridor musicians and the elderly who sit in the alcoves and remember who lived where. I was destined for the maintenance corps, for the long-handled tongues and the wet shirts and the mineral sweat that stains the skin rust-brown, and I accepted this with the absence of ambition that characterizes the Hollow's middle thousands, because the work exists and must be done and someone does it and the someone was me.

But my father's silence had taken root in me. I found myself descending more than my work required, drifting down through the steam-works into the lower levels where the heat became a wall and the sound became a sea and the deep-speakers moved through the orange glow like figures in a dream. I was not afraid of the depths. This terrified my mother. She would stand at the top of the stairs and bark my name into the shaft, and I would descend anyway, and the sound would swallow her voice, and I would enter the world where speech was impossible and communication was gesture and the mountain's heartbeat was audible in the bones.

The deep-speakers accepted me because my father was one of them and because I had learned their language before I learned that it was a language. They did not give me formal instruction. They simply allowed me to be present, and in being present I absorbed the knowledge that passes through the body: where to press a palm against a pipe wall to read the pressure through the skin, which joints are stressed by the specific vibration they transmit, how to identify a failing seal by the shift in the thermal gradient that the eyes detect before the gauges do. I was not a deep-speaker. I did not have the physiognomy for it—the thick shoulders, the reddened hands, the lungs that have breathed steam and stone dust for decades. But I could speak their language, and I could carry their messages, and this was enough to make me useful.

I became, without intending to, a courier. The Hollow has no formal postal system. Communication between districts happens by word of mouth, by the barking voices that echo through the corridors, by the graffiti carved into the walls. But communication between the depths and the rim is something else. The deep-speakers cannot send messages upward through the standard channels because the standard channels are auditory, and the deep-speakers have abandoned audition as a primary sense. Their messages are gestural, written in condensation that evaporates in seconds, legible only to those who have learned to read the fleeting trace of a finger on a wet surface. And the rim families communicate in a clipped, precise dialect that the deep-speakers do not speak and cannot easily learn, because the rim's language is built on concepts—pressure gradients, thermal efficiency, structural analysis—that the deep-speakers experience as physical sensations rather than abstract measurements.

I move between these worlds. I descend into the lower levels with messages from the rim: technical specifications for a new ventilation shaft, maintenance schedules for the steam-works, the engineers' latest warnings about structural integrity in the deepest excavations. I translate these from the formal language of the academies into the deep-speakers' gestural vocabulary, which requires not merely linguistic translation but conceptual translation—converting a phrase like *thermal gradient of four degrees per vertical meter* into a gesture that means *the heat increases as you go down, and here is the rate at which it increases, felt in the palm rather than read on a gauge*. The deep-speakers receive these translations with the patience of people who have learned to extract meaning from whatever form it arrives in. They do not care about the engineers' formal terminology. They care about the information: where the heat is, where it is moving, where the stone is softening, where the water is finding new paths.

I ascend from the depths with messages for the rim: the deep-speakers' observations about the mountain's condition, communicated in gestures that I translate into the formal language of the academies. A palm pressed flat against a pipe wall and slowly rotated means *the pressure in this section is fluctuating in a pattern that the gauges do not detect but that I can feel through the metal*. This becomes, in my translation, *subsurface pressure variation in sector seven, irregular oscillation pattern, amplitude below instrument sensitivity but consistent with precursor signal for seal failure*. The engineers receive these translations with the skepticism that they bring to all knowledge that originates outside their formal systems. They note the observations. They file them. They sometimes act on them. They never acknowledge the source.

I am not the only courier. There are others—children of mixed-depth parentage, bilingual in the Hollow's fragmented languages, drawn to the work because it requires what they already are: people who exist between categories. But the work is not organized. It is not a profession or a guild or a recognized social role. It is a function that emerges from the structure of the place, the way the breath emerges from the damp and the heat: opportunistically, without plan, filling the available space. We find each other in the corridors and exchange messages and information and the particular camaraderie of people who are not quite one thing and not quite another and who have learned to be useful in the gap.

---

The bird-speakers talk to me more than they talk to the engineers.

I think this is because I listen differently. The engineers listen for data. They listen for correlations, for patterns that can be measured and replicated and verified. The bird-speakers are not offering data. They are offering something more diffuse and more specific at the same time: a knowledge that is bound to a particular place and a particular time and a particular body that has been in that place for a particular duration. When old Maren in section nine tells me that the swallows above her chamber have been chittering in the lower register for three days straight, and that this means the drainage channel in section twelve is beginning to clog, she is not making a prediction in the scientific sense. She is reporting an observation that is inseparable from her own experience—she has lived in the same chamber for sixty years, she has heard the birds above her window every day of those sixty years, she has noticed that the chittering drops in pitch when the humidity in the walls changes, and the humidity in the walls changes when the drainage channels clog, and she knows this not because she understands the hydrology but because she has been bathed in the same information for longer than I have been alive.

I carry these observations too. I translate them into the formal language of the engineers, and the engineers dismiss them, and I note the dismissal, and I carry it back to Maren, who shrugs in the way that the elderly of the middle levels shrug—at once accepting and refusing, a gesture that says *I know* and *it does not matter what they know* and *the birds are still chittering* and *we will see*.

Sometimes the birds are right and the engineers are wrong. Sometimes the engineers are right and the birds are irrelevant. Most of the time it is impossible to tell, because the Hollow's systems are too complex for any single framework to capture, and the mountain is too old for any single language to describe, and the knowledge that accumulates in the bodies of the people who live here is too deeply embedded in the specific experience of living in this particular carved stone to be extractable into the abstract.

---

I am damp. I am always damp. My skin carries the mineral smell that is the Hollow's signature perfume, and my clothes have not fully dried since I first descended into the steam-works as a child and felt the wet heat close around me like a hand. I have the middle-level bark, which I use in the corridors and the markets and the cable-car stations, and I have the deep-speakers' silence, which I carry into the lower levels like a second skin. I have the formal vocabulary of the engineers, which I learned not in the academies but through years of translating their documents for the deep-speakers and their observations for the engineers. I have the bird-speakers' diffuse attentiveness, which I did not learn from anyone but which developed in me the way it develops in anyone who spends enough time in the same corridors listening to the same sounds: not as a skill but as a condition.

I am not at home anywhere in the Hollow. I am at home in the transitions, in the carved stairs between the middle levels and the depths, in the cable-car lines that thread the districts together like sutures, in the moments when I am carrying a message from one world to another and the message is still forming in my mind, not yet translated, not yet reduced to the vocabulary of its destination, still holding the full complexity of its origin. In those moments, I am the Hollow's true character: accumulation, density, the refusal of anything to leave. I hold the deep-speakers' gestures and the engineers' formal terms and the bird-speakers' observations and the middle-level bark all at once, in the same mind, and for a moment they are not different languages but one language, one description of the same mountain, the same water, the same heat, the same slow accumulation of matter and moisture and time.

I am not a deep-speaker. I do not have the thickened shoulders or the permanently reddened hands or the lungs that have breathed steam and stone dust for twenty years. When I descend into the lower levels, the heat hits me like a wall, and I feel the mountain's heartbeat in my bones, and it is not my home. I am a visitor. I am always a visitor.

I am not a rim-family diplomat. I do not have the dry clothes or the formal speech or the inherited right to decide what enters the mountain's mouth. When I ascend to the rim, the wind strips the moisture from my skin, and the air is thin and clean, and I feel the mineral smell evaporating from my clothes, and I am aware that I am carrying the depths with me—that the damp and the heat and the bark are in my body, and the rim-dwellers can smell it on me, and they do not say so but they know, and I know they know, and this knowledge sits between us like a thin sheet of the breath, pale and layered and not quite alive.

I am not an engineer. I was rejected by the academies, and the rejection was correct—my mind does not work in the analytical, abstracted way that engineering requires. I cannot model the mountain's hydrology in formal terms. I cannot calculate the rate of structural decay in the lower levels. I can only feel it, through the soles of my feet, through the palms of my hands, through the animal part of the brain that registers vibration before conscious thought can name it. This is not engineering. It is something older.

I am not a bird-speaker. I do not have the decades of accumulated attention that the elderly women of the middle levels possess. I cannot distinguish the specific calls that mean pressure drops and drainage clogs and structural failures. I can hear the birds, and I can feel the patterns, and I know that the patterns mean something, but I do not have the language for what they mean. I carry the bird-speakers' observations to the engineers, and the engineers dismiss them, and I note the dismissal, and I carry it back, and the cycle continues, and the birds continue to chitter, and the mountain continues to soften, and the breath continues to grow, and nothing leaves.

---

I am the one who knows the mountain is dying and cannot stop knowing it.

The engineers have been saying this for fifty years, and the deep-speakers have been feeling it for longer—the softening in the lower levels, the shift in the vibration, the groan that was not there a decade ago and that is now audible in the deepest corridors, a sound that the mountain makes as it compresses under the weight of three thousand years of accumulated construction. The engineers say the lower levels will collapse within a century. The deep-speakers say nothing. They simply press their palms against the walls and feel the change and continue working, because the work must be done regardless of whether the mountain will stand for another hundred years or another thousand.

I carry this knowledge between worlds, and it accumulates in me the way everything accumulates in the Hollow. I have the engineers' formal warnings and the deep-speakers' felt observations and the bird-speakers' diffuse attentiveness and the rim families' political calculations about what collapse means for their interests and the middle-dwellers' practical concerns about where they will go if the lower levels fail. None of these perspectives is complete. None of them is wrong. They are the Hollow's accumulated understanding of its own condition, and I hold them all, and the holding is not a synthesis. It is a palimpsest, the way the corridor walls are a palimpsest—layers of meaning compressed on top of each other, the original stone no longer visible, what remains is the compressed speech of generations, and it is not coherent, and it is not resolvable, and it is the truth.

I am the courier, and the message I carry is the mountain itself.
