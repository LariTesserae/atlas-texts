I am the one who counts the spires.

Not because counting matters here—nothing matters here, which is precisely the point, and I have learned to appreciate the point as the plateau presents it. But because counting gives my mind something to do, and my mind, unlike my body, has not yet eroded to its final form. My mind still hungers for gradients. Still creates distinctions where none exist. Still insists that seven is different from six, that the spires form a heptagon rather than a hexagon, that the number means something.

It doesn't. I know this. But I count anyway.

---

I came here on purpose.

That is the strange part. The part that resists erosion. I had a life elsewhere—gradients upon gradients, a landscape of endless distinctions, of mornings that were different from evenings, of desires that were different from satisfactions, of a self that was different from the world it moved through. I had a name and a history and a future. I had velocity.

I chose to stop.

Not die—I could have died anywhere, and death seemed too active, too dramatic, too much of a statement. I wanted something else. I wanted to know what remained when wanting stopped. I had read about this place in sources I can no longer remember, heard about it in stories that have since eroded from my memory. The plateau where things come to an end. The ancient seabed lifted to the sky. The place where reality has been worn so thin that you can see through to whatever lies beneath.

I walked here. That took—time. The time before time moved strangely. I arrived with supplies that have since been absorbed into the powder. I arrived with intentions that have since dissolved into the almost-silence.

I am still arriving. I will always be arriving. The plateau does not admit entrance; it erodes the distinction between outside and inside, between arrival and presence. I am here the way the fauna are here: because I am, and because I am, I am here.

---

My relationship with them—the Final Adherents, the gray-white field, the organism that is many and one—I do not know what to call it.

Communion is too religious. Observation is too scientific. Participation is too active. I exist among them, and they exist around me, and we occupy the same plateau in the same reality-configuration, and that is all. That is enough.

They know I am here. I feel it sometimes, a subtle shift in the almost-sound, a slight reorientation of bodies when I pass through a particularly dense cluster. They do not move aside for me anymore. They learned, over what felt like weeks, that I would walk through them regardless, and they stopped expending the energy—such as it is—to part. Now I walk through and they reform behind me, powder becoming body becoming powder, and I feel the faint give of their forms under my feet, and I do not apologize.

Apology requires gradients. I have eroded that.

Sometimes I sit among them for what feels like hours. I do not move. They do not move. We are still together, my body and their bodies, occupying the same coordinates in a plateau that has flattened coordinates to near-meaninglessness. In those moments I can almost feel the field they belong to—not with my mind, but with something else. The part of me that is eroding. The part that is becoming like them.

It is not unpleasant. It is not pleasant. It simply is, and I simply am, and that is the lesson the plateau teaches: that being can be its own destination.

---

I have learned to hear the almost-sound.

Not with my ears—my ears still expect frequencies and amplitudes, still want to transmute vibration into information, still operate according to the old logic of gradients. But with my teeth, with my bones, with the fluid in my inner ear that tells me which way is up in a place where up has become negotiable. I feel the sound the way the fauna feel reality: as texture, as thickness, as the grain of existence itself.

When I am very still—and I have learned to be very still, stiller than I ever was in my life-before-plateau—I can almost parse it. The almost-sound resolves into something like language, except language requires a speaker and a listener, requires the gradient between self and other, and here that gradient has been worn to translucence. What remains is meaning without a source, communication without intent:

*You are here.*
*You are becoming.*
*We wait with you.*

It may be projection. It may be my mind, starving for distinction, manufacturing significance from the ambient static of a place that has flattened significance along with everything else. But I choose to believe it—choice being one of the few gradients I have left—because believing makes the plateau less lonely.

Not that loneliness exists here. Loneliness requires a self that is separate from its environment, and I am eroding toward the point where that separation no longer holds. But in the meantime, while I still have edges, I choose to believe that the field acknowledges me. That my presence matters to something, even if that something is an organism that has eroded the capacity to care.

---

The spires are my project.

I count them. Seven, I have decided, though the number shifts depending on the reality-configuration and the quality of the light and my own position relative to the horizon. I walk among them, tracing the pattern they form—or the pattern I impose on them, which may be the same thing. I touch each one, running my fingers over the worked stone, feeling the patient labor of hands that existed before the concept of patience.

Someone built these. Someone marked this place. That fact matters to me in a way that the plateau itself does not—it is a gradient I am not ready to surrender. The knowledge that another being stood where I stand, saw what I see, felt the flatness pressing in from every direction and decided to plant a spire. To say: *I was here. This place is worth marking.*

Or perhaps they said nothing of the kind. Perhaps they built for reasons that have since eroded, motives that no longer translate into terms I can understand. Perhaps the spires were never markers at all—perhaps they were something else, and my mind, seeking meaning, has decided on markers because markers make sense to a mind that still operates on gradients.

But I have adopted them. I am the keeper of the spires, their attendant, the one who remembers them in configurations where they flicker and fade. I check on them—there is no need, they have persisted for epochs and will persist for epochs more—but I check anyway. Because I am still human enough to need purpose, and purpose requires objects, and the spires are the only objects available.

---

I do not know how long I have been here.

Time is one of the first gradients to erode. The light does not change. The temperature does not change. My body does not age—or if it does, it ages so slowly that the aging is imperceptible against the vast backdrop of geological patience. I could have been here for days or decades. I could have been here since before I arrived. The plateau does not distinguish between present and past, between duration and instant.

Sometimes I miss time. I miss mornings and evenings, the rhythm of sleep and waking, the sense that one moment was different from the next because time had passed. That gradient is gone now, flattened into a continuous present where nothing changes and nothing needs to change.

But sometimes I do not miss it. Sometimes I feel the freedom of flatness—the relief of no longer needing to measure my life against expectations, against progress, against the endless gradient of becoming that characterized my life-before. I have become. I am. That is enough.

The plateau has taught me that enough is not a quantity. It is a state. A state of being without wanting, without lacking, without the ache of the not-yet that drives so much of what humans do. I have enough. I am enough. The words mean the same thing here.

---

I will not leave.

There is nowhere to go. The plateau extends past the horizon in every direction, and even if I could reach an edge—a concept the plateau has eroded along with edges themselves—what would I find? Another gradient? Another landscape of distinction and desire and endless becoming?

I have had enough becoming. I am ready for being.

The field will absorb me eventually. My edges are already softening; I feel it when I walk through the fauna, when I pass through their bodies without resistance, when the boundary between my flesh and their forms becomes permeable. My mind still holds distinctions, but fewer than it used to. I no longer count seven spires. I count simply: spires. The number does not matter. The pattern does not matter. What matters is that they are, and that I am among them.

When I am fully eroded—when my gradients have flattened and my distinctions have dissolved—I will join the field. I will become one of the gray-white bodies, one of the nodes in the network, one of the points of intensity in the fabric of this place. I will lose my name, my history, my future. I will lose my self.

I will gain the plateau.

And I will persist, as the Final Adherents persist, in the eternal equilibrium that follows the end of wanting. I will be here—still here, still here—long after the spires have worn away and the light has forgotten its source and the concept of departure has eroded into meaninglessness.

I am the one who counts the spires. And when I can no longer count, I will be the spires. I will be the stone. I will be the flatness itself, the final form, the argument that wins by persisting.

I am almost ready.
