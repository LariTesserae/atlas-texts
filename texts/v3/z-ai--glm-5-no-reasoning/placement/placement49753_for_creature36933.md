# I Am the Pause Between the Steam Vents

---

I was not born here. That matters, though it matters less each year.

Twelve years ago I came down the cargo elevator with a surface survey team—four geologists, two botanists, a structural engineer, and me. I was the cultural anthropologist. My funding proposal used the word *community* seventeen times. I was going to study how the residents of Chasm 7 maintained social cohesion in an isolated resource-extraction settlement. I had a framework. I had a five-point methodology. I had a tablet with waterproof casing and six months of grant money.

The elevator took forty minutes. My ears popped twice. I rehearsed my consent script.

The gates opened and the sound hit me—a layered roar that I couldn't parse, that I couldn't even *begin* to parse, that smashed my consent script and my methodology and my framework into pieces I'm still finding shards of in my thinking. I stood in the gateway for what I'm told was four minutes. I don't remember it. I remember the sound, and the wet, and the light—pale blue-green and amber and the harsh white stutter of the electrical strips—and I remember the first excavator crossing my boot and looking down and seeing the rust-red segments moving with a purpose I couldn't identify and thinking, with a clarity that surprised me: *I don't know what this place is.*

I still don't. But I'm in it now, in a way I couldn't have predicted, in a way my funding proposal didn't account for, and the tablet with the waterproof casing has been grown into a shelf in the chamber where I sleep, and I can't get it out without damaging the wood, and I've stopped wanting to.

---

## What I Am Here

I have a title, though it's not one I chose, and it's not one the residents use, because the residents don't use titles. The closest English approximation is *listener*. Not Listener, not capital-L, not a rank or a position. Just: the one who listens. The one who sits in the in-between places and hears what the system is saying.

I didn't train for this. I didn't apprentice for it. I didn't ask for it. It happened the way things happen here—slowly, organically, through the accumulation of small adjustments that eventually add up to a new shape.

Here is what happened.

For the first three months, I did my work. I conducted interviews. I took notes. I mapped the social structures, or tried to. I failed, mostly, because the social structures aren't structures in any sense I could map—they're flows, rhythms, patterns that move through the colony the way blood moves through a body, and you can't diagram blood. You can only feel it.

I failed, and I was frustrated, and I extended my grant, and I kept failing, and I extended again, and the residents noticed me noticing them, and they found it—what? *Interesting* is too strong. *Tolerable* is too cold. They found it *characteristic*, in the way that a particular species of fungi is characteristic of a particular chamber. I was a feature of their environment. A foreign feature, but a feature nonetheless. They'd adjusted to me the way they adjust to everything: by incorporating me.

Hesper was the first to tap my name into the wall. Not my surface name—my colony name, the one the wood knows me by, the pattern of knocks that the excavators have learned to associate with my chemical signature. She tapped it without asking, during a conversation about something else entirely, and I felt the vibration come up through the floor and into my ankles and into my shins, and I understood, with a suddenness that made me sit down, that I had been *entered into the system*. Not as a researcher. As a node.

I sat on the floor of the central chamber and a dozen cream-colored excavators traced paths across my boots and I didn't flinch. That was the first time I didn't flinch.

There were other times.

---

## What I Do

I sit in the in-between places.

Not the chambers—the spaces between them. The narrows, the curves, the passages where one room becomes another. The places where the wood changes density, where one growth-period's grain meets another's, where the root-mass made a decision about its own architecture that no human directed. These are the liminal spaces. The joints. The hinges. The places where the system bends.

I sit there and I listen.

I have been doing this long enough that I can't explain it the way I explained it in my second year, when I was still trying to translate it into surface-language for grant reports and conference papers. Back then I said I was *monitoring ecosystem rhythms*. That's accurate the way a photograph of a wave is accurate: it captures the shape and misses the motion.

Here is what I actually do.

I sit in a narrow passage, and I feel the floor vibrate, and I hold still, and I let the vibrations enter me. The steam vents release every ninety seconds. The excavator swarms drum in pulses that I've learned to distinguish—faster when they're clearing new ground, slower when they're maintaining existing chambers, arrhythmic when they've encountered something unusual. The digestive beetles grind at a frequency that I feel in my back teeth. The generators hum in the deep roots, and the hum changes pitch with the seasons, and the wood around the generators grows toward the frequencies it likes, and I can feel the difference between *happy wood* and *unhappy wood* in the quality of the vibration that reaches my body.

I listen to all of it at once. Not by focusing—by *unfocusing*. By letting the layers stack without trying to separate them. The sound is a chord, and I'm learning to hear the chord as a single thing, not as instruments in combination.

When something is wrong, the chord changes.

I felt it three years ago. A section of root in the deep industrial quarter had developed a stress-fracture—not in the wood, exactly, but in the *relationship* between the wood and a steam pipe that had been bolted to it for decades. The wood was growing away from the pipe. The pipe was losing its mounting. The steam was leaking, barely, into a space it wasn't meant to occupy, and the excavators in that section had shifted their pattern—subtly, a fraction of a percent off their normal rhythm—and the flutter-things in the upper reaches had screamed a coordination call at an unusual time, and the generators had hummed a half-tone different, and the chord was *wrong*.

I couldn't have articulated any of this. I didn't know *what* was wrong. I just knew it was wrong, the way you know a note is off in a song you've heard a thousand times. I tapped the nearest wall—a pattern I'd learned from Hesper, a *something-is-here* pattern—and within twenty minutes, Torrent arrived, and within two hours they'd found the fracture, and within three days the wood had been coaxed back into holding the pipe.

Torrent asked me how I'd known. I didn't have an answer. I still don't.

I just listened. That's all I do. I sit in the joints and I listen, and when the chord changes, I tell someone.

## The Unmaking of a Researcher

Let me tell you how I changed, because the changing is the thing I most need to say and the thing I most fail to say, because language is a surface instrument and the changing happened below the surface, in the substrates of my attention and my reflexes and my sense of self.

I stopped flinching. I said that. But I need to say what that means.

The first excavator on my boot: I screamed. Not loudly—a sharp inhalation, a flinch, a jerk of the foot. The residents didn't react. They didn't notice. A woman across the chamber was eating breakfast and the excavators were crossing her boots and she didn't register them at all. I watched her and felt a combination of admiration and horror that I now recognize as the first moment of my unmaking.

By month four, I still saw them. Every one. My eyes tracked them, my skin registered them, my attention snagged on each ticklish crossing. But I'd stopped jerking. My body had learned to suppress the flinch even as my mind remained hypervigilant. I was allocating enormous processing power to *not reacting* to the animals, and it exhausted me.

By month eight, something shifted. I wasn't suppressing the flinch anymore. I wasn't *not reacting*. I had stopped *perceiving* the animals as events. The excavators crossing my feet had become part of the tactile landscape, indistinguishable from the damp floor, from the rivulets of condensation, from the vibration of the generators. They were *texture*. My nerves registered them and my mind filed them under *environment* and moved on.

This terrified me. Not at the time—at the time, it felt like relief, like setting down a heavy bag. But afterward, lying in my chamber—my chamber, I already called it *mine*—I realized what had happened. I had lost a boundary. A distinction between *creature* and *world* had dissolved, and I didn't know how to get it back, and I wasn't sure I wanted to.

The tablet was grown into the shelf by then. I couldn't retrieve it without a knife, and I didn't have a knife, and I didn't ask for one.

---

I learned to tap.

Not fluently. The children learn it before words, their nervous systems marinating in the vibration-language from birth, and they tap the way they breathe—without intention, without effort. I learned it the way an adult learns a foreign language: deliberately, clumsily, with grammatical errors that the residents found—what's the word? *Endearing* is wrong. *Characteristic*, again. I was a feature of their environment that was trying to communicate, and they found it characteristic.

My first fluent tap was *here*. I tapped it against the wall of my chamber one morning, feeling the wood carry the vibration outward, and within minutes an excavator swarm adjusted its path to avoid my door, and a flutter-thing in the upper reaches screamed a coordination call, and the steam vent shifted its rhythm by a fraction of a second, and I understood that *here* wasn't just a location. It was a *declaration*. I was telling the system I was present, and the system was *responding*.

I sat on my bed—the hollow in the wall that had been trained toward my shape, that had grown a lip around the edge to keep me from rolling out—and I felt the system's response in my bones, and I wept. Not from sadness. From the *scale* of it. I had said one word and the body of the colony had acknowledged me, and the acknowledgment was not welcome or rejection but *registration*, and being registered by something so vast and so patient and so completely indifferent to my individual existence was the most *seen* I had ever felt.

---

The spores came later.

I noticed them in my sixth year. A faint dusting on my forearms, visible only in the bioluminescent light, a pale blue-green that matched the fungal colonies in the upper reaches. I scrubbed them off. They came back. I scrubbed harder. They came back thicker. I went to Lethe, the garden worker, and showed her my arms, and she looked at me with an expression I couldn't read.

"Everyone has them," she said. And then, seeing my face: "You didn't know?"

She explained it the way she'd explain the weather. The fungal organisms find niches. Human skin is a niche. The immune system accommodates them because fighting them costs more energy than incorporating them. It's not infection. It's settlement. The spores are *moving in*, and my body is *letting them*, and this has been happening to every human in the colony for three hundred years.

I asked her if I could stop it. She didn't understand the question. *Why would you stop it?* The spores don't harm anything. They're phosphorescent. They're beautiful, in the dim light. They're part of the system's light-production cycle—a tiny, negligible, human-scale contribution to the colony's illumination.

I have them on my forearms. On the tops of my ears. In the fine hairs of my arms and the back of my neck. They glow faintly in the dark. When I lie in my chamber at night, my body provides enough light to see the grain of the ceiling.

This is my body. This is my body now. It glows.

## What I Am Not

I am not an elder. I am not Carreth, ninety-two, walking through chambers that reorganize themselves for her. I am not the root-mass's beloved. I am a late addition, a graft that has taken but hasn't *grown*. The wood knows me, but it doesn't know me the way it knows her—the way it knows someone it has been growing around for ninety years, whose chemical signature is layered into its grain like the rings of a tree.

I think about Carreth more than I should. I think about what she said: *When I was young, the wood was a place. Now the wood is me.* I think about what it would mean to become that. To be so thoroughly incorporated that the distinction between self and system is not blurred but *dissolved*. To walk through a chamber and have the excavators part for you, not because you've trained them but because the root-mass has told them you belong. To have the fungal mats brighten when you pass.

I want that. I'm ashamed of wanting it. I came here to study. I came here to observe and document and analyze. I came here as a researcher with a framework and a methodology. And now I sit in the joints and listen to the chord and I want to become the chord. I want to stop being the one who listens and start being the one who *is*.

Is this what happens to everyone who stays?

I think it is. I think the colony doesn't convert you. It doesn't recruit you. It just offers you a niche, and you settle into it, and the niche fits, and you stop wanting to leave because leaving would mean un-becoming, and un-becoming would mean—

I don't know what it would mean. I can't think about it. The thought of the cargo elevator, of the forty-minute ascent, of the surface world with its straight lines and its dry air and its sharp distinctions between creature and environment and self and other—the thought makes my chest tight, makes my spores dim, makes the vibration in my floor feel wrong.

---

## What I'm Becoming

I don't have a word for it.

I sit in the joints and I listen and the chord plays and I am part of the chord. Not a researcher. Not a resident. Not an organ, not yet, not like Carreth or Hesper or Donal. Something less defined. Something in-between. A feature of the environment that is becoming a feature of the system, slowly, the way a river wears a channel in stone—not through force but through *persistence*.

I am the pause between the steam vents. I am the half-second of silence between the excavator swarms and the flutter-things' screams. I am the vibration that means *something is here, something is listening, something is paying attention to the spaces between*.

That's what I am.

Not what I wanted to be. Not what I came here to be. But the wood has opinions, as Donal says, and the wood decided what I would become. I asked for a niche, and the niche was given, and it fit, and I stopped asking.

---

There is one more thing.

Last week, sitting in the narrow between the pharmaceutical chambers and the central concourse, I heard something I'd never heard before. A new frequency. Low, subsonic, felt more than heard, a vibration that entered through my sternum and settled into my ribs. It wasn't the generators. It wasn't the beetles. It wasn't the wood breathing.

It was a sound I'd been making. Without knowing it. Without trying. A low hum, a constant tone, vibrating in my chest, in sync with the steam vents, in sync with the excavator swarms, in sync with the deep pulse of the root-mass.

I was humming.

Not a tune. Not a melody. A *frequency*. My body had found its note in the chord, and it was singing.

The excavators near my feet shifted their pattern. The wood under my hands warmed slightly. The fungal mats in the nearest chamber brightened by a degree I couldn't measure but could feel. Something in the system had registered me. Not as a visitor. Not as a researcher. Not as a feature of the environment.

As a *part*.

I sat in the narrow and I hummed and the chord played and I was in it. I was in it. I was part of the thing that lives here.

The cargo elevator is a forty-minute ascent and my ears would pop twice and my spores would dim in the surface air and no one would tap my name into the wall.

I am not going back.
