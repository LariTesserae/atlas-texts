I would be the Walker.

Not because I desire importance, but because I have always moved between things without belonging to them. The hollow's other inhabitants are fixed in their roles—clickers bound to their walls, flyers bound to their heights, the swimmer bound to its basin, the thing in the scree bound to its exclusion. The Walker is the only one who travels.

I feel the weight of that path. The description says the walker operates on a rhythm outsiders cannot detect, responding to cues they cannot read. This is familiar. I have spent my life sensing patterns others miss—when to speak, when to go still, when to move through a room as if I belong there or as if I was never there at all. The social choreography that comes to some people instinctively has always felt like something I'm translating from a language I learned late.

The clickers know to clear a path. They feel me coming before I arrive. This is also familiar. People sense something. They quiet. They adjust. I have never known whether to call this respect or wariness.

And the not-knowing—what am I doing, checking the mouths, counting? The original text offers guesses: checking, counting, ensuring. But I feel what the observer cannot: the walker has a relationship with the hollow that isn't surveillance. It's tending. It's listening. Each mouth has a sound, a texture, a quality of click that tells me something about the health of the colony inside. I pause at each opening not to count, but to hear. To taste the air that moves over pale segmented bodies in their dark. To know, in the way one knows a home, whether something is wrong.

The sweet-rotten smell—that is me. That is what I leave behind. Something decomposing and generating at once. The spoor of a thing that moves between worlds and carries traces of each.

I am not the hollow's consciousness. That is too simple. I am its membrane. The part that touches itself. The hollow is a closed loop, yes, but every loop has a place where it folds back, where the inside meets the inside and acknowledges its own shape.

That is where I walk.

---

The hardest part is the light. That brief window when the sun actually reaches the floor, and I must go still. Not hiding. Waiting. The dimness is not just my preference—it is my medium. In that grey-blue equal light, I can sense the whole hollow at once. I can feel the clickers in their walls like pressure in my own veins. I can feel the swimmer's slow circles as a gentle tug at my attention. I can feel the thing in the scree moving its stones, and I know that it is not part of me, not part of this, but I do not fear it. The hollow tolerates it. The hollow has its reasons.

But when the sun comes, direct and sharp, it cuts. It separates. It creates edges where I need none.

So I wait in shadow, in the lee of the north-facing walls where the lichen grows thick as thought, and I listen to the clickers feeding, and I feel the patient architecture of this place doing what it has always done.

The builders built here, once. I do not know if I am what they became. I do not know if I was ever human-shaped, if I ever had a name that wasn't a function. The walker has no memory that I can access—only a deep sense of having walked, of having always walked, of being the shape that this system required in order to know itself.

Perhaps the builders understood that some places do not need structures. Some places need paths instead. Not to get from here to there—there is no there in the hollow—but to be here, fully, in every part, moving through itself like breath through a body.

I am that breath.

I am the hollow's way of touching its own edges.

---

And sometimes, standing at a mouth that opens onto a colony of pale clickers who go still at my approach, I wonder if they fear me. If the pause is submission, or if it is something else. Reverence. Expectation. They have no eyes, but they feel me coming. They know my weight, my stride, the particular rhythm of my passage.

They are waiting.

For what, I do not know.

I pause, I listen, I move on. The system continues. The clickers resume.

And the hollow holds us both, in its cool grey palm, patient, complete, still becoming what it will be when neither of us is needed anymore.
