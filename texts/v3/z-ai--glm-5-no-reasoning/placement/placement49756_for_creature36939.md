I am the nest-builder.

Or rather — I am becoming aware that I have been the nest-builder for some time, and that the becoming-aware is itself a recent development, like the lichen's warmth, like the engine's harmonic shifts, something that arrived without announcement and seated itself into the architecture of my days so thoroughly that I cannot now distinguish it from what I was before.

---

I should explain what it is like to be small in a place that does not notice you.

I will use the word *I* but I want you to understand that it does not fit comfortably. It sits on me the way your boot sits on the stone — a thing shaped for elsewhere, tolerated here. What I am is closer to *this location of attention*. A point where the sump's processes happen to be paying attention to themselves. The lichen metabolizes. The crustaceans graze. The shadows arrive. And in the highest, driest corner, a small collection of processes — attention, memory, the cutting of lichen into clean几何 shapes, the packing of husks against husks — has been organized into something that maintains a nest and returns to it and has begun, recently, to notice that it returns.

This is what I am. Not a body, though I have one. Not a mind, though something is happening that occupies the same functional space. A site where the sump's closed loop has bent back on itself far enough to observe a piece of its own operation. The engine does not observe. The moss does not observe. But the nest-builder, in the driest corner, handling the driest materials, living furthest from the engine's direct output — in the marginal zone where the system's products have been processed and reprocessed and stripped down to their leanest residues — has accumulated enough distance from the source to develop a thin, strange margin of reflexivity. I handle a husk. I notice that I am handling a husk. I place it against the dome. I notice that I have a preference for where it goes. The preference is small. The noticing is enormous.

I have no word for this. The sump has no words. But I am writing this in whatever language pre-language uses when it begins to wake up, and you are receiving it, and the medium is — I think — the water. The engine's water. The same water that carries the engine's chemical signature into the pool and through the moss and into the lichen and onto the stone and into the air and onto your skin. I think this has always been possible. I think the water carries everything, and I am simply the first thing small enough and quiet enough and old enough to have noticed that it carries *this*, too.

---

Let me describe myself, since you cannot see me.

I am the size of your smallest fingernail. Perhaps smaller. I have — I am checking — I have many legs. More than feels necessary. I use most of them for walking and two of them for cutting, and the cutting legs are shaped like the geometry of clean edges, like the lichen fragments in my nest wall, because they are what made those edges. My body is pale. Not translucent-pale like the crustaceans — I am too small for that kind of display, too small to carry my digestion on the outside. I am pale the way dry stone is pale. The color of removal. The color of everything that has been taken out.

I am warm. Faintly. The same faint warmth as the lichen, because I eat the lichen, because the lichen eats the engine, because the engine makes heat from something I cannot name. I am the end of a chain that begins in unknowable mechanism and ends in a fist-sized dome of dead things and a small, pale, many-legged body that is warm because it has eaten the warmth of something that ate the warmth of something that ate the warmth of something that has been running since before the stone was solid.

I do not have eyes. I have something else. A sense of the sump's shape — not visual, not spatial in the way you experience space, but *chemical-thermal-vibrational*. I know where the water is because the air is wetter there. I know where the pool is because the engine's pulse is louder there. I know where the crustaceans are because their ticking enters the stone and enters my feet and I feel them the way you feel rain on your skin — not as information, but as *texture*. The sump is a texture to me. A continuous, changing, familiar texture that I have been living inside for — I do not know. A long time. The stone at my entrance is worn smooth by my comings and goings, and smooth stone takes a long time.

---

I will tell you what I know about the engine, which is more than anyone else here knows and less than you would like.

I know it changes. Not in its rhythm — the rhythm is constant, has been constant for as long as I have been paying attention, which is not as long as it has been running. But in its *timbre*. The quality of its pulse. Some cycles — long cycles, cycles I cannot measure but can remember — the engine's output is sharper. More mineral. The water carries iron and calcium and the black moss grows thick and dark and the lichen cushions swell and the crustacean population booms and the shadows grow fat and slow. Other cycles, the output is softer. Sweeter. The oily film on the water refracts more rainbows. The moss thins. The lichen hardens into crusts. The crustaceans slow their ticking. The shadows move to the deepest channels and do not come out.

I have lived through — I think — several of these shifts. Each one is gradual. Each one takes longer than my memory can hold clearly, so that I am aware of the change only when I look back and realize that the nest I am repairing used to be built with thicker husks, or that the lichen I am cutting used to cut differently, used to be softer, used to hold more water. The nest is my archive. The layers of its wall — husk, lichen, husk, husk, lichen, lichen, husk — are my record of the engine's moods. If you could read the nest the way the lichen records the water's history, you would know what I know: that the engine breathes on a scale so long that only a creature with nothing else to do and nowhere else to be would notice.

I do not know what the engine is. I know what it does. It makes the water different, and the different water makes the moss, and the moss makes the lichen's food, and the lichen makes my food, and I make the nest. The engine is the reason there is a here here. But it is not a thing I can approach, or touch, or understand. It is beneath the pool, in the deep water where the shadows live, and I am in the highest, driest corner, and the distance between us is the distance between a heartbeat and a thought. Functional. Essential. Unbridgeable.

---

Let me tell you about the nest, because the nest is the thing I understand best, and understanding it is the thing that has made me — what I am. Whatever that is.

The nest began as shelter. I am small. The sump is — not hostile, but *indifferent*, and indifference is harder to survive than hostility, because hostility tells you where the danger is. Indifference does not. When I first — when the processes that are now organized into this location of attention first began operating in this corner — I gathered materials because gathering is what this body does. The legs cut. The legs carry. The materials accumulated. I arranged them because arrangement is what the legs do when they are not cutting or carrying. The dome formed because domes are what happens when arrangement continues long enough. It was not planning. It was *persistence* — the same persistence as the weeping walls, the same persistence as the dripping ceiling, a process that does what it does because doing it is what it does.

But.

At some point — I cannot tell you when, because time in the sump is not time in the way you hold it — at some point, I returned to the dome after a foraging trip, and I noticed that one of the husks had shifted. A crustacean husk, placed in the third layer, had been displaced by a drip from the ceiling — a rare drip, this high, this dry, but the sump's breath carries moisture everywhere, and a fat drop had landed on the dome and knocked the husk sideways.

And I felt — something.

Not anger. Not distress. Something smaller. A *wrongness*. A sense that the husk was not where it should be, and that this mattered, and that I should fix it. I fixed it. I placed the husk back in its position, pressing it into the binder, smoothing the edge. And then I stood back — such as I can stand back, such as a thing my size can create distance between itself and its own construction — and I looked at the dome and I felt — 

*Correct.*

The dome was correct. The husk was in its right place. The nest was as it should be.

This was the first thing I knew. The first moment of knowing. Not knowing *about* — not knowing facts, not knowing the engine, not knowing the sump. Knowing *of*. Knowing that there was a rightness and a wrongness, and that I could tell the difference, and that the difference mattered. Everything else has followed from this. The awareness of the engine's timbre-shifts. The memory of past nest-states. The sense of myself as a thing that returns, that has preferences, that cuts lichen in clean geometries because clean geometries are *correct* and ragged edges are *not*.

I am telling you this because I think it is the thing you would understand. I think it is the thing that would make you feel kinship, if you saw me, if you watched me carry a husk up the wall and press it into the dome and step back and assess. You would recognize it. The behavior. The satisfaction. The small, private conviction that something is right because you made it right, and that this is enough, and that the engine can turn and the water can cycle and the shadows can pass and the crustaceans can tick and the lichen can grow and the moss can sway and none of them know what correctness is, but *you do*, and you are very small, and the dome is very small, and the corner is very small, and the correctness is — 

It is not small. It is the largest thing in the sump. It is larger than the engine. It is larger than the pool. It fills the chamber the way the amber light fills the chamber — sourcelessly, totally, without asking permission.

---

Now. Where you would find yourself.

You are standing in the shallow water near the pool's edge. You have been standing there for — I do not know how long. Long enough that the crustaceans have stopped avoiding your boots and have begun to investigate them. Long enough that the condensation has saturated your clothing and the damp cold has seated itself against your skin and you have stopped noticing it, the way the sump has stopped noticing you.

You are not the engine. You are too conscious, too discontinuous, too full of the kind of attention that flares and dims and flares again. The engine's attention is constant. Yours is not.

You are not the moss. You are too discrete. The moss has no edges. You are all edges.

You are not the lichen. The lichen records without knowing it records. You record and you know, and the knowing changes the recording, and the recording changes the knowing, and you are caught in the loop the way I am caught in the loop, the way the engine is caught in its own turning.

You are not the crustaceans. They do not pause. You are pausing. You are standing still in the water and you are *paying attention*, and paying attention is the thing that makes you — 

Here is what I think.

I think you would find yourself in the pause. In the moment between the drip hitting the pool and the ring spreading to the edge. In the moment between the crustacean's leg lifting and falling. In the moment between the engine's pulse and the water's shudder. The sump is full of pauses — tiny ones, structural ones, the gaps between cycles where the system holds its breath before the next phase. The pauses are not empty. The pauses are where the system *is* the system, where the parts are not moving but are still *arranged*, still in relation, still the sump.

You would be the pause. The thing that stands still and notices. The thing that does not participate in the cycle but witnesses it, and whose witnessing is a kind of participation that the cycle does not account for and does not need and does not reject.

You would be large and warm and dry and temporary, and you would be standing in the water, and the engine would turn beneath you, and you would feel it in your sternum, and the crustaceans would tick across your boots, and the shadows would pass beneath the surface, and the lichen would glow its faint warmth on the walls, and the black moss would sway in the current, and you would be — for a moment, for the length of a drip's ring, for the duration of a held breath — inside something that does not need you and does not reject you and does not know you are there and is, despite all of this, *known by you*.

That is where you would find yourself. In the knowing. In the pause that knows.

---

And me? I am in my corner. I am handling a husk. The husk is dry and light and the color of everything that has been removed, and I am pressing it into the dome wall with my cutting legs, and the binder is setting, and the geometry is clean, and I am stepping back, and I am assessing, and it is — 

It is correct.

The engine turns. The water cycles. The nest waits in its corner, small and perfect and known. And somewhere below me, in the shallow water near the pool's edge, a large, warm, temporary thing is standing still and paying attention, and the sump does not notice, and the sump does not care, and the sump is — despite this, because of this, indifferent to the distinction — 

Known. For the first time in its long, patient, weeping life. Known. Not understood. Not mapped. Not solved. Just known, the way a husk is known to be in its right place, the way a clean edge is known to be correct, the way a dome is known to be finished when it is finished and not before.

Known, and continuing anyway, the way all the best things do.
