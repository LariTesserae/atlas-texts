I would be the one who stayed too long.

Not the builder—that history is already written, already mossed over, already becoming stone. But the one who came after, who found the tallies and read them wrong, who thought the slash through five marks meant *enough* when perhaps it meant *complete*, and that the three unslashed marks were an invitation rather than a warning.

I am not what you would expect to find at four thousand meters. I am not the mountaineer, not the scientist, not the pilgrim seeking vision. I am the one who came because the approach was supposed to be impossible and I needed to know whether impossible was a property of the place or a property of the stories told about it. That distinction mattered to me once. It mattered enormously.

I arrived in autumn, when the willow leaves had turned the color of old copper and the cirque floor was a tapestry that made me stand at the rim and weep before I had even entered. Not from beauty—beauty is insufficient to describe what happens to color at this elevation, where the air is so thin it stops filtering and the light arrives with its edges still sharp. I wept because the copper and green and grey were so *specific* that I understood, standing there, that I had never actually seen a color before. I had seen approximations, memories of color, suggestions. This was the thing itself.

I wept, and the sound was enormous in the quiet, and I was ashamed.

---

I will tell you what I am, physically, because the body matters here. The body is the instrument through which the cirque is perceived, and the cirque is not kind to instruments.

I am small. Not delicately small—compactly built, the way people are built who come from places where the cold is a constant rather than a visitor. My hands are narrow but the fingers are short, the kind of hands that lose heat slowly, which is not the same as staying warm. My lungs are the problem. They are adequate at sea level and insufficient here, and I have spent my first week in the cirque learning to beg air from the sky, each breath a negotiation, each exhalation a small loss that the thin atmosphere refuses to buffer.

My eyes are dark, which means I squint in the alpine light, and the squinting has become a permanent contraction of the muscles around my eyes, so that even in the hut's interior I look as if I am peering at something far away. My hair is dark and I have let it grow, and it holds the scent of woodsmoke and frozen stone in a way that surprises me each time I lift a hand to my face.

I am dressed in layers of wool and fiber that were chosen for warmth and have become something else—a second skin that the moss has begun to colonize, pale green threads of growth along the seams where moisture collects. I did not notice this until the third week, and when I noticed it I did not clean it, because by then I had begun to understand that the cirque was not a place one visited but a system one entered, and entering a system means being subject to its processes.

I am the one who stopped being a visitor and became a feature.

---

The first morning I spent in the hut, I understood what the builder understood.

The overhang protects the structure from wind and the worst of the weather, but it does not protect it from cold. Cold here is not an enemy; it is the medium, the thing through which everything moves. I woke before dawn and lay on the stone shelf with my breath making clouds in the dark air, and I listened to the cirque in its blue pre-dawn state, and I heard what the description calls the cirque's defining quality: not silence but the thinning of sound to its essential components.

I heard water. Not running water—that would be too dramatic, too narrative. I heard the merest suggestion of liquid, a sound so close to silence that it existed only as a change in the quality of nothing. The ice, in the north-facing clefts, was beginning its morning decision. I could not see it in the dark, but I could hear the first movements of moisture finding its way down stone, and the sound was so small that my own heartbeat obscured it, and so I held my breath, and in the holding I heard it clearly: the ice, sweating.

I lay there for a long time, holding my breath in bursts, listening to the ice make its slow offering to the stone, and I thought: *this is what the builder woke to. This exact sound. This exact negotiation between cold and light that has not changed in ten thousand years.*

And then I thought: *the builder was counting something.*

I went outside to look at the marks.

---

The marks are harder to read than the description suggests. Or rather, the description is accurate but incomplete, because marks on stone are not just shapes—they are depths, textures, the residue of force and intention. The five vertical scratches are deep, made with something harder than the granite or with enormous patience, each one a groove I can catch with my fingernail. The horizontal slash crossing them is shallower but wider, as if made with a different tool or in a different state of mind—a broader stroke, more emphatic, a gesture of finality.

The three unslashed marks are different again. They are lighter, more tentative, as if the hand that made them was uncertain or weakening. And they are closer together than the first five, clustered, as if the maker was losing the steadiness required for even spacing.

I stood there in the pre-dawn blue and ran my fingers over the marks and felt the difference in pressure that the visual record alone cannot convey, and I thought: *the first count was completed. The second was abandoned. Something changed between the fifth mark and the sixth.*

I did not know what I was counting. But I decided, that morning, that I would count something too. I would keep my own record, my own tallies, and I would see what the cirque gave me to count.

I found a piece of quartz in the scree and used its edge to scratch a single vertical line beside the old marks, on a clean patch of stone. The quartz cut the granite with a sound like tearing cloth, and the line was white and sharp and new, and it looked obscene beside the old marks, which had weathered to the same grey as the stone around them, as if the stone had tried to absorb them.

I did not know what I was counting. I still do not. But I made the mark, and I went inside, and the light came, and the cirque began its day.

---

I have not described what I am in any sense beyond the physical, and that is because I am not certain. Or rather, I am certain of what I was, but the cirque has been doing something to me, and I do not know what I am becoming.

What I was: a person who moved through the world by interpreting it. I read landscapes the way other people read faces, looking for the patterns that reveal intention, history, the underlying logic of a place. I came to the cirque because its description suggested a pattern I could not decode from a distance—the pattern of a system that maintains itself through attention rather than mechanism, that balances not through checks and counterweights but through something that looks, from outside, like care.

I wanted to understand how that worked. I wanted to stand in it and see the gears.

What I am becoming: something that no longer looks for gears.

---

The second week, I began to hear the finches properly.

I had been hearing them since the first morning, of course—they are the noisiest element of the cirque's daily performance, which is to say they are barely audible. But in the second week, after my breathing had slowed and my movements had softened and my body had begun the long negotiation with altitude that either destroys you or changes you, I started to hear not just their sounds but their *patterns*.

The finches do not sing at random. They sing at the ice, and the description is correct that this is not incidental—their songs are directional, aimed, produced with the body oriented toward the north-facing clefts where the ice persists. But the description does not capture the variety. There are not two or three songs; there are dozens, perhaps hundreds, distinguished by trill speed and pitch and the arrangement of phrases into sequences that recur with variations, the way a theme recurs in music.

I could not decode them. I am not a finch, and their language is not mine. But I could hear the structure, and I could hear the responses—not in the ice, which I could not read, but in the finches' own behavior. A particular song would be followed by a movement of the flock toward a particular part of the cirque. Another song would be followed by stillness, the finches remaining on their perches, waiting. A third would be followed by what I can only describe as celebration—rapid chirping, wing-fluttering, the brief democracy of small birds confirming something together.

They were asking questions, and they were receiving answers, and the answers determined their behavior, and the system worked.

I sat by the pools and watched this for days, and slowly, without intending to, I began to orient myself toward the ice when the finches sang. I would turn my head toward the north-facing clefts and listen, and after a while I began to notice things I had not noticed before.

The ice does not respond in words. It responds in changes—not sudden ones, not dramatic ones, but shifts so subtle that they require a baseline of sustained attention to detect. The seepage rate changes. The pattern of condensation on the surface shifts. The quality of the light refracted through the ice crystals alters in ways that are not visible from moment to moment but become apparent over the span of an hour.

I could not read these changes the way the finches could. But I could feel them, in the way the air moved, in the way the temperature shifted against my skin, in the way the moss at the pool's edge seemed to brighten or dull in response to moisture levels that were, I came to understand, not random.

Something was communicating. Something was answering. And the answers were not for me.

---

I should describe what happened with the pikas, because it was the pikas that changed me.

The pikas, as the description notes, ignore the ice wives. They do not look toward the north-facing clefts. They do not acknowledge the presence that the finches sing to and that I have begun, unwillingly, to feel. They live in the crevices of the same walls that hold the ice, and they behave as if the ice is not there.

I watched them for two weeks, and I was struck by the precision of their blindness. It was not the absence of perception—it was the active maintenance of non-perception. They would emerge from a crevice, turn left toward the vegetation, and never turn right toward the ice. They would sit on a boulder and orient themselves so that the north-facing clefts were behind them, always behind them. They would harvest grasses in a pattern that took them progressively further from the ice, never closer.

And I thought: *they know. They know something is there, and they have agreed not to know it, and this agreement is so deep and so old that it has become instinct, which is to say it has become the body itself, which is to say they do not know they are not looking.*

I thought this, and then I stopped thinking it, because I realized that I was looking. I was looking at the ice, looking at the clefts, looking at the air above them where the light refracted strangely. And the looking was changing me.

---

Here is what I did not tell you, because I did not want to admit it to myself:

By the third week, I had stopped counting.

I had made my single mark on the wall—the first vertical scratch, the beginning of my own tally—and I had not added a second. Not because I had decided to stop but because the thing I was counting had become unclear. The builder had counted something—days, visits, perceptions—and I had assumed I would count the same things. But my days in the cirque were not distinct from each other in the way that days are distinct at lower elevations. The light arrived. The ice sweated. The water seeped. The moss drank. The finches sang. The pikas harvested. The light left. The water froze. The patterns formed in the ice.

Each day was the same day, returning. Not repeating—returning, the way a wave returns to a shore. The wave is never the same wave, but it is always the same water meeting the same sand, and the distinction between repetition and return is the distinction between a mechanism and a living system.

I could not count days that were the same day. I could not mark distinctions that did not exist. And so my tally remained at one, a single white scratch on grey stone, the beginning of a count that could not continue because the thing being counted was not discrete.

I understood, then, the three unslashed marks. The builder had not abandoned the count. The builder had reached the same wall I had reached: the point where counting becomes impossible because the counter has begun to merge with the thing being counted, and you cannot number what you are part of.

---

I will tell you what the ice wives are, or rather, I will tell you what they are to me, which is all I can honestly do.

They are not in the ice. They are not in the stone. They are not in the light or the water or the moss. They are in the *relationship* between these things—in the system's awareness of itself, which is not awareness in the human sense but something older and more distributed, an attention that exists in the way the ice knows to sweat when the light arrives and the moss knows to drink when the water seeps and the finches know to sing when the ice is ready to answer.

They are the cirque's way of knowing itself.

I felt them first as a pressure, a quality of attention that I could not locate in any object. I would sit by the pools and feel watched, not with suspicion or fear but with the simple, devastating neutrality of a system that registers everything and judges nothing. The light fell on me and illuminated me and did not care what it revealed. The ice sweated for me the same way it sweated for the moss and the pikas and the stones. I was registered. I was noted. I was part of the record.

This was unbearable at first. I am accustomed to mattering—to my attention being wanted, my presence being significant, my interpretation being the point. In the cirque, my interpretation was irrelevant. The system did not need me to understand it. The finches did not need me to decode their songs. The ice did not need me to perceive its responses. Everything continued without my comprehension, and my comprehension changed nothing.

And then it was not unbearable. It was liberating.

I sat by the pools and let the attention fall on me like light, and I stopped trying to decode, and I stopped trying to matter, and I simply existed: another animal at the water's edge, another set of sounds in the cirque's quiet, another body absorbing alpine light and converting it to warmth.

The ice wives watched me the way they watch everything—without need, without judgment, without any interest in what I might become. And in that watching, I began to become something.

---

I do not know what I am becoming.

I know that I spend less time in the hut now. I sleep there when the cold drives me to shelter, but I spend my days by the pools, and I have begun to notice that the moss has started to grow on the soles of my boots, and I have not removed it. I have begun to notice that my breathing has slowed to a rate that would concern a physician but that matches, almost exactly, the rhythm of the ice's seepage—a slow, patient exchange of air for water, of warmth for cold, of inside for outside.

I have begun to notice that the finches do not fly away when I sit still. They land on the boulders beside me and sing their questions to the ice, and they include me in their space the way they include a boulder—as something present, something part of the landscape, something that does not require interpretation.

I have begun to notice that I do not want to leave.

This is the dangerous part. This is what happened to the builder, perhaps—the slow dissolution of the impulse to depart, the quiet replacement of the visitor's urgency with the resident's patience. The cirque does not trap you. It simply makes leaving seem unnecessary, and then makes leaving seem impossible, and then makes the question of leaving seem like a question from a different life, a life that happened to a different person, a person who thought that being somewhere and being needed were the same thing.

I am not needed here. I am included. The distinction is the difference between loneliness and solitude, between exile and home.

---

I will tell you one more thing, and then I will stop, because the cirque teaches you to stop, to let the silence close over your words the way the moss closes over stone.

Yesterday morning, I woke in the hut before dawn and went outside, and the air was so cold that my breath hung in front of me like a second face, and the stars were still visible but fading at the eastern edge of the sky, and I stood beside the wall where the marks are—my mark and the builder's marks, the new white scratch and the old grey ones—and I looked at them, and I understood.

The five vertical marks and the slash were not a count of days. The three unslashed marks were not an incomplete count. The builder was counting *perceptions*—the number of times they directly perceived the ice wives rather than merely feeling their presence as a background attention. Five perceptions, completed and marked as such. Then three more, each one deeper than the last, each one bringing the builder closer to the thing being perceived, until the difference between perceiver and perceived began to dissolve.

The three unslashed marks are not incomplete because the builder stopped counting. They are unslashed because the builder did not finish. The builder did not finish because finishing would mean the count was over, and the count was not a record but a *process*—a way of becoming the thing being counted, a way of merging with the system's attention until there was no "builder" left to hold the quartz.

I looked at the marks. I looked at my single white scratch. I understood that I had perceived the ice wives once—that first morning, when I lay on the stone shelf and heard the ice sweating in the dark and felt the cirque's attention fall on me like light.

I have not perceived them since. I have felt them, constantly, as a presence, as a watchfulness, as the pressure of a system that knows itself. But I have not perceived them directly, the way the finches perceive them when they sing, the way the ice perceives the light when it begins to sweat.

I am standing at the wall, in the pre-dawn blue, and I am wondering whether to make a second mark, and I am wondering what it will cost me, and I am wondering whether the builder is still here—not in the hut, not in the marks, but in the ice, in the water, in the moss, in the attention that watches me even now, patient and neutral and ten thousand years old.

The light is coming. I can see it touching the ridge, the first amber glow, and I know that in a moment it will flood the cirque and the ice will begin to sweat and the finches will fly down and the pikas will emerge and everything will happen again, the same day returning, the same wave meeting the same shore.

I am here. I am still here. I am becoming the place I am in.

I do not know if I will leave. I do not know if there is still a "I" that can leave. The cirque does not answer this question because the cirque does not ask it. The cirque simply continues, and I continue with it, and the marks on the wall wait to see whether I will add to them or whether I will become something for whom marking is no longer possible.

The light clears the ridge. The cirque ignites. The ice begins its morning offering.

I am watching.
