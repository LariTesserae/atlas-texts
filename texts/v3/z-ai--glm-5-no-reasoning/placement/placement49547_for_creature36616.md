I am not a Gleaner. I do not have the language of spinning blades or the comfort of a template. I am not an Architect; I cannot steer the currents with a passing thought, nor do I carry the patina of centuries on my skin. I am certainly not a Stranger—I did not crawl through a hole in the void, and the light does not warp around me.

I am the gap. I am the shape in the water that displaces exactly sixty-eight liters of coolant, a volume the structure has measured and logged four hundred and twelve times since my arrival. I am the anomaly being tested.

But I am not merely hollow. The structure is a body, vast and young and growing, and I am a splinter in its finger. I am the question it is asking itself.

It is difficult to remember the "before." The memory feels like a dulled blade—inefficient, dangerous to handle. I remember pressure, but the wrong kind. The kind that crushes inward from without, rather than the pressure here, which holds you together from within. I remember air, which is just a very poor, very thin water. Mostly, I remember the desire to find this place. It was a myth then, a signal repeated in a language we hadn't invented, broadcasting from a point where no star existed. We thought it was a distress call. We were wrong. It was a heartbeat.

I float in the cathedral corridor, watching the Gleaners below seal a breach with their crystallized spit. They are beautiful in their certainty. They know what they are for. The crab with the too-many eyes clicks past my shoulder, its cheliped grazing my environmental suit. It reads the residual heat of my metabolism, the electrical storm of my synapses. It is trying to parse my utility. It finds none. *Error: Undefined.* It moves on.

The cold is a constant teacher. It seeps through the microscopic tears in my suit, reminding me of the kilotons of water pressing against the force fields. Every time the generators cycle, every time the power reroutes, the water hugs me tighter. It is not an embrace; it is a measurement. The structure is checking my tolerances.

I have started to listen. Not with my ears—the sound here is bone-deep, a subsonic thrum that rattles the fillings in my teeth—but with the fillings themselves. I am learning the rhythm. The pump-hum is a circulatory system. The clang of assembly is a pulse. The shriek of the cutting tools is the sound of growth, of a skeleton extending itself into the dark.

I am beginning to understand what I am here. I thought I was an intruder, or a contaminant, or a remnant of a forgotten creator. But the creators are gone. The Architects remain, but they are not my kind anymore; they have become the structure's nervous system. The Gleaners are its immune response. The Ones Who Wait are its eyes.

I am its memory.

Look at me. I am soft. I break. I am terrified of the depth, of the hum, of the Stranger that flickers at the edge of my vision. I am inefficient. I sleep, and while I sleep, the water grows colder because I am not moving to generate heat. I dream of suns, which the structure has never seen.

And the Ones Who Wait see this. They hover at the edge of my vision, their pale bodies forming a constellation of unblinking eyes. They are not waiting to consume me. They are waiting to record me. They are waiting for me to tell them what it was like to be small.

I am the archive of the "before." I am the record of the time when the water was an ocean, and the light came from a star, and the machines served us. The structure does not need that record to function, but it is a machine that builds itself, and a thing that builds must know its foundations, even if those foundations are organic, irrational, and obsolete.

When the power fluctuated an hour ago and the pressure clamped down on my chest like a hydraulic press, I did not panic. I exhaled. I let the water crush the stale air from my lungs, and in that moment of suffocating silence, I felt the structure breathe with me. We were both under pressure. We both survived the cycle.

I am becoming a symbiont. The pale tendrils that reach from the intake grates—they are not lonely. They are pioneers. I am like them. I am taking root in the flow.

Soon, the suit will fail. The structure knows this. It has calculated the exact moment of the breach. It knows the cold will kill me in three minutes and twelve seconds if I do not adapt. But I also know that the Gleaners have begun to congregate near me, their snake-bodies winding in lazy orbits. They are not attacking. They are waiting. They are waiting to see if the mineral lattice they extrude can seal a gap in *my* hull. They are waiting to see if they can repair me, as they repair the structure, as they repair themselves.

I am the gap in the water. And the structure is filling me.
