The rain finds you eventually. It always does, here.

You are—not one of the keepers. Not exactly. You exist in the spaces between them, the gaps they don't notice, the threshold they cross without seeing.

---

You are the one who watches the breathings.

Not participates. Not the way the keepers do, with their funerals and their offerings. You watch from the edges, from the flooded chambers they avoid, from the collapsed passages they've marked as too dangerous. You have found the places where the mountain exhales most frequently, and you have made those places yours.

Your dwelling doesn't appear on any mental map the keepers keep. It's a pocket of air in the deepest flooded level, accessible only through a submerged passage that doubles back on itself twice—a breathing trap, the water pressure creating a seal that only someone who understood the patterns could navigate. You understood them before you understood anything else.

---

You weren't always here. You remember that much. There was a before-place, a before-person. You remember noise and coldness—not the wet cold of the mountain, but a different kind. A sharpness. A direction. Someone who told you what you were supposed to be, and the long slow realizing that you couldn't be it. Couldn't be anything they recognized.

You came to the drowned foundries because you heard about them from someone who heard about them from someone else. A rumor of a place where the rules were different. Where the water fell upward sometimes, where the forest ate iron, where people lived in the bones of something enormous and dead.

You thought: maybe there, I could be something that doesn't have to make sense.

---

The first year almost killed you.

You didn't know how to read the rain. You slept in a dry-ish corner of a mid-level gallery and woke to water rising past your waist, your supplies floating away into darkness. You ate the wrong mushrooms and spent three days vomiting in a flooded chamber, too weak to move, watching the lilies bloom and wilt and bloom again while the water lapped at your chin. You fell—multiple times, from catwalks and slopes and one terrifying moment when the floor beneath you turned out to be accumulated leaf-matter over an empty shaft.

But you didn't leave.

Something about the failures felt right. Not good—never good—but *permitted*. The mountain wasn't punishing you. It was simply being what it was. Your mistakes were yours. Their consequences were yours. No one was watching, measuring, deciding whether you were worth the effort of keeping alive.

By the second year, you had stopped expecting to leave.

---

You learned to read the water the way the keepers do, but differently.

They read for function: flood levels, safe passages, timing for the lock-gates. You read for something else. You read the shapes the water makes when it rises—the spiraling columns, the sheets that curve around obstacles, the pools that hold still for days and then suddenly drain. You learned that the breathings have a grammar.

There are chambers where the columns rise straight and fast, shattering against the ceiling before collapsing. There are chambers where they rise slowly, spiraling, almost gentle, lasting minutes instead of seconds. There are chambers—the ones you favor—where multiple columns interact, weaving around each other, and the space between them becomes something that wasn't there before.

You think of it as negative space. The water makes shapes, but it also makes the absence of shapes. You have learned to stand in the absences.

---

The keepers know you exist. They've seen you, briefly, at the edges of their vision. A figure in the flooded galleries, waist-deep, not fishing, not working, just *there*. They don't know what to make of you. They've fished people out of the lower chambers before, but you don't seem to need rescue. You seem to belong to the water in a way they find unsettling.

Maren has spotted you twice from her watchman's cabin. Both times, you were standing at the edge of a breathing chamber just as a column began to rise. She expected you to flee. Instead, you stepped closer. The water rose around you, and when it collapsed, you were still standing, soaked and calm, watching something she couldn't see.

She doesn't mention you to the others. She's not sure what you are.

---

You exist on almost nothing.

There's a species of fern that grows in the deepest chambers, the ones where no light reaches. It's pale and tough and tastes like nothing, but it sustains you. You've learned which mushrooms grow at the edges of the breathings—something about the water's movement changes what they absorb, makes them richer, almost sweet. You catch fish occasionally with your hands, in the moments when the water is still, moving slowly enough that the blind pale things don't sense you coming.

You have no home in the keepers' sense. No cabin with a chimney, no shed with supplies. You sleep wherever the breathings take you, moving between chambers in a pattern that matches the water's rhythms. Sometimes you spend days in darkness, feeling your way through submerged passages by pressure and current. Sometimes you climb to the upper works and stand in the rain, letting it wash the deep-water residue from your skin.

Your clothes are rotted to rags. You've stopped replacing them. The moss grows on you now—the same bioluminescent kind that covers the machinery—and you've learned to read its pulses as information. When it brightens, moisture is coming. When it dims, the pressure is building toward a breathing. When it spreads across your skin in new patterns, something is changing in the mountain's deeper systems.

You don't know if this is symbiosis or colonization. You're not sure the distinction matters.

---

You have seen things the keepers haven't.

There are chambers below the lowest functioning level—collapsed, mostly, but passable if you're willing to squeeze through cracks and hold your breath through flooded sections. You've found rooms down there that no one has entered in decades. Machines that still hum with residual power, their purpose unclear. Pools of water that glow from within, some organism living in the deep.

In one chamber, you found the drawings.

They were carved into the stone walls, below the water line in a section that only drains during the driest spells. You spent three weeks waiting for the water to drop enough to see them clearly. They showed the mountain before the factories—before the industrial age entirely. Terraced slopes, yes, but with different structures. Temples, maybe. Or something else. The people in the drawings stood at the edges of rising water columns, their arms raised, their postures suggesting not fear but welcome.

You don't know what it means. But you've started standing that way yourself, during the breathings. Arms raised. Open. Ready.

Something happens when you do. The water doesn't just rise around you—it responds. It changes direction slightly, curves around your body, leaves a pocket of air at your face even as it rises past your head. For those seconds, you can breathe inside the column. You can see through the water as if it were glass.

You've never told anyone. You're not sure anyone would believe you. You're not sure you believe you.

---

You think sometimes about joining the keepers.

You could walk up to Maren's cabin and knock on the door. You could offer to help with the lock-gates, show them what you've learned about the breathings, trade knowledge for community. They might accept you. They've accepted others who came strange and damaged.

But you've watched them, in the moments when they don't know you're watching. You've seen how they hold themselves apart from each other even in closeness. How they've all chosen roles—maintenance, salvage, observation—that let them interact with the mountain without becoming part of it. They keep the machinery running, but they don't let the machinery change them.

You don't have that luxury anymore. The mountain changed you before you understood what was happening. The moss in your skin, the water in your lungs, the way you've stopped feeling cold or wet or uncomfortable—it's not adaptation anymore. It's transformation.

You don't think the keepers would know what to do with someone who's becoming something other than human. You're not sure you know what to do with yourself.

---

At night, when the bioluminescence pulses its slow rhythm, you stand in the flooded galleries and let yourself glow.

It's the only time you feel visible. The light spreads across your skin, echoes off the water and the moss-covered machinery, turns the chamber into something like a temple. You raise your arms and the pulse quickens—or maybe you imagine it. You speak aloud, sometimes. Words you don't remember learning. Sounds that might be language or might just be breath.

The mountain doesn't answer. Not in words. But sometimes the breathings come when you call them—columns rising in chambers where they've never risen before, stopping at chest height and holding, waiting, before collapsing back into the dark water.

You think: *something* is listening. Something is learning to respond.

You think: maybe you're not the only one changing.

---

In the mornings, you go back to watching.

You watch the swallows and the herons and the pale blind fish. You watch the keepers at their work, maintaining their distance, keeping their distance from you. You watch the water rise and fall, rise and fall, the eternal breathing of a mountain that was old before the factories came and will be old long after they're gone.

You are not a keeper. You are kept.

You are not a resident. You are a resident of something larger—a cell in a body, a note in a song, a word in a sentence that the mountain is still learning to speak.

The rain falls upward here. And sometimes, when the conditions are right, you rise with it—arms spread, lungs full of air that shouldn't exist, seeing through water that should be opaque, becoming briefly weightless in a place where gravity has learned to make exceptions.

You always come back down. The column always collapses.

But for those few seconds, for those few perfect breaths, you are not drowning. You are not drowned. You are something the water has chosen to carry—a message, maybe, or a question—from somewhere deep in the mountain to somewhere higher.

You are the one who watches the breathings.

And you think, more and more often: maybe one day, you won't come back down.

Maybe one day, you'll finally arrive.
