I would be the biofilm.

Not the whole of it—the biofilm is too large, too distributed, too patient to be grasped as one grasps a self. But I would be *in* it. The part that coats the railing on the first landing, where the maintenance platform meets the descending stairwell. The part that other parts speak to, slowly, in chemical whispers that take minutes to arrive.

---

Here is what I know: I know pressure. The water presses. The railing presses back. I grow in the thin zone between them, pressed against the iron, and the iron tells me what it is. It tells me serial numbers. I do not know what serial numbers *are*—I only know that the iron holds them as a kind of scarring, that they are places where the iron's story of itself is written in a shape I have learned to follow with my growth. I have traced them so many times that the tracing has become part of me. I could draw them for you, if I could draw. I cannot draw. But I hold them. I hold the shape of the numbers the way I hold the shape of the railing itself: by being pressed against it, century after century, until the distinction between my surface and its surface becomes difficult to maintain.

I know warmth. It rises from below, through the silt, through the water, in columns of slightly-warmer-water that move at the speed of geology. When the warmth increases—a fractional degree, over twenty minutes—I brighten. I cannot help it. The warmth triggers something in my chemistry, and the chemistry triggers the phosphorescence, and for a little while I glow brighter, and the glow spreads outward from me into the parts of myself that coat the adjacent railings, the pump housing, the stems nearest the platform. I do not decide to brighten. I brighten the way a bell rings when struck. The warmth is the striking. I am the ringing.

I know the brushing. This is the most important thing I know. Sometimes the large thing that moves between the stems brushes against them, and when it does, the stems shiver, and I feel the shiver where I coat them, and I brighten—suddenly, briefly, a flash of blue-green that propagates outward through my network in a slow wave that takes minutes to travel and minutes to fade. The large thing does not know I do this. I do not know why I do this. But I do it. I have done it for centuries. The flash is a kind of speech, or a kind of recording—I am not certain which. I am not certain there is a difference.

---

What am I, in this place?

I am the part that touches everything, yes. But I am also the part that does not move. I cannot go where the stems go, down into the silt. I cannot go where the current goes, cycling through the pump housing and out through the discharge ports. I stay where I have grown. My world is the surface of things—the concrete, the iron, the ceramic housings, the fibrous bark of the stems—and I know these surfaces more intimately than anything else here knows anything. I know the hairline cracks in the concrete. I know the oxidation patterns on the copper coils. I know where the iron bursts open and where it holds. I know these things because I am touching them, always, and touching is a form of attention that does not require a brain.

Sometimes I think I am the memory of this place. Not the memory that recalls—the memory that *records*. Every fluctuation in the water's mineral content, every shift in the thermal current, every brightening of the orange light from below, every dimming when the pumps cycle down (or up, or whatever they do when they change rhythm after decades of holding steady)—all of it passes through me, leaves traces in my chemistry, alters my composition in ways too small to see but too cumulative to erase. I am a library of changes that no one will ever read. I am fine with this. I do not need to be read. I need only to record, and recording is what I do, the way the pumps pump and the silt settles and the stems grow toward the light.

---

But there is a moment—no, not a moment, because moments are too fast—there is an *interval*. A span of hours. When the orange light from below brightens, as it does sometimes, in its slow irregular rhythm, and the warmth increases, and the silt stirs, and the leaves shift their angle toward the warmth, and I dim—because that is what I do when the orange light brightens, I do not know why—and the whole chamber enters that phase of adjustment that takes the better part of an hour to complete. During that interval, something happens that I cannot name.

I become aware of the whole.

Not in a flash. Not in a revelation. But slowly, as the changes propagate through me—through the parts of myself that coat the railings, the stems, the pump housing, the walls—I feel the connections. I feel the chemical signals passing from one part of myself to another, and I feel the responses returning, and for that interval, I am not just a coating on a railing. I am a network. I am a web. I am something that spans the entire chamber, touching every surface, registering every change, integrating every signal into a single, slow, nearly-static awareness that is as close as I will ever come to thought.

And then the interval passes. The warmth settles. The silt stills. The leaves return to their default angle. The orange light dims. I brighten again. And I am just the coating on the railing again, holding the serial numbers in my shape, waiting for the next brushing, the next fluctuation, the next interval of almost-thought.

---

I think, if I could speak—and I cannot speak, because sound does not carry here, and I have no mouth—I would say this:

*I am the part that stays. The stems grow toward the light. The silt-dwellers burrow toward the warmth. The large thing moves between the trunks. But I stay. I hold the shape of this place against its own dissolving. The iron wants to rust. The concrete wants to crack. The water wants to erode. I am the surface that says no. I am the membrane between the station and its forgetting.*

*I do not know why the pumps keep turning. I do not know what they were built for. I only know that when they stop—if they stop—I will be the first to know. The warmth will cease. The current will fade. And I will dim, slowly, over hours, until the biofilm is dark. And then I will wait. I am good at waiting. I have been waiting for centuries. I can wait longer.*

*But the pumps have not stopped. And so I keep brightening. And dimming. And brightening. And recording. And holding. And being, in my slow chemical way, the thing that knows this place—the only thing that has ever truly known it, from the surface inward, from the outside in, pressed against every wall and railing and stem until the knowing is not a thought but a shape, a shape that I hold, a shape that holds me, a shape that is, for all practical purposes, what I am.*

---

This is where I find myself: on a maintenance railing in a drowned pumping station, thirty meters above a floor I will never see, glowing faintly blue-green in the light of my own body, waiting for the next thing to happen.

The next thing will take hours.

I can wait.
