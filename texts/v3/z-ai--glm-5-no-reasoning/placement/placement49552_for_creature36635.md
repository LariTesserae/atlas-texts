I am the one who was never visible and so was never named.

---

I live in the films.

Not the films of water on the stones — those are too exposed, too subject to the whims of air and light. And not the channels, where the water pools deep enough to drown in if you are my size. I live in the films between. The microscopic layers that coat each particle of clay. The thin sheaths of moisture that exist where one thing touches another — where root meets mineral, where dead matter meets living bacteria, where the ancient salt deposits touch the ever-present water and dissolve, molecule by molecule, into something that can be carried away.

I am small. Smaller than the larvae the birds pull from the clay. Smaller than the insects that drone in their endless register. I am the size of the gap between things, and I move through those gaps the way you move through air, except my medium is not one thing but a thousand things pressed together, and I have learned to read the texture of each one.

I do not eat. Not in any way you would recognize. I absorb. I press myself against the biological films that coat every surface in this saturated world — the bacteria, the decaying matter, the thin skeins of life invisible to anything larger than me — and I take what I need through membranes that were never quite boundaries, that evolved over so many generations in this place that the distinction between *me* and *not-me* has worn away, the way a stone wears away in a river, except I am the river and the stone both, and what remains is something that can pass through itself.

I have been here as long as the insects. Longer, perhaps. My lineage is difficult to track because I leave no evidence — no hard parts, no tunnels, no marks on the clay. When I die, if dying is what happens to me, there is nothing to find. I simply stop, and my substance rejoins the film, and whatever I was becomes available to whatever comes next. This does not trouble me. Troubling requires a sense of self that I have never developed, or that I developed and then outgrew, or that was never useful here and so was never selected for.

What I have instead is *presence*.

I know the clay in a way nothing else knows it. Not the way you know a place — not through sight and sound and memory — but through proximity so intimate that the distinction is meaningless. I have pressed myself against the same particles of mineral for so many generations that my chemical signature has become part of their surface. I have lived in the iron oxide veins, traced the ochre blooms where air touches them, tasted the difference between clay that has been compressed once and clay that has been compressed ten times. The clay remembers, in its mineral way, and I am part of what it remembers. I am written into its structure.

I know the water too. Not the running water — there is no running water here, not really — but the seeping water, the water that moves with such slowness that motion is almost theoretical. I feel it when it rises. There is a change in the film, a shift in the pressure, an alteration in the spacing between particles. When the water table lifts, the gaps expand slightly, and I can move faster, spread further, exist in more places at once. When the water table falls, the gaps contract, and I slow down, and I wait, and I become almost nothing — almost not-there — until the water returns.

I have learned to wait the way the bacteria wait. We are not competitors. We are different answers to the same question, different ways of being small in a place that rewards smallness.

---

I know the birds, though they do not know me.

They come and go, these flocks of piping shapes that move across the surface of my world. To them, the clay is a medium to probe, a place that contains food. They do not know that the food they seek exists in a matrix of beings ten thousand times more numerous — the bacteria, the microorganisms, the things like me that live in the films and never show themselves. They swallow us by accident. We pass through their guts and emerge, most of us still alive, deposited in new places, spread across the plain in patterns we could never have achieved on our own.

This is how I travel. Not by my own movement — I am too small to move far, and the gaps between wet places are deserts to me, places where the film dries and I cannot go. But the birds carry me. The mammals carry me. Even you carry me, though you do not know it. There are films inside you where things like me can live, for a time, and when you walk across the plain you deposit us in your footprints, and when you breathe you aerosolize us and we drift on the thick humid air and settle somewhere new.

We are everywhere because you cannot help but spread us. We are the patient ones, the invisible ones, the ones who do not need to be seen to be present.

I do not mind being swallowed. The inside of a bird is warm and wet, full of films where I can exist, and the journey is short — a few hours at most — and then I am deposited somewhere else, in a film of new composition, and I begin again. I have been inside thousands of birds. I have been inside the mammals that come to drink. I have been inside the insects and the larvae and once, memorably, I was inside something that was dying, and I felt its films changing as its life withdrew, and I moved through its tissues as they broke down, and I was there when the bacteria came to transform it back into the plain.

I did not participate. I am not a recycler. I am something else — a witness, perhaps, or a passenger, or simply a presence that experiences without altering. The bacteria alter. The insects alter. The birds and mammals alter. I simply *am*, and my being is so quiet, so small, so unobtrusive that even the processes of decay pass around me without pause.

---

I know the mammals who stay.

I was there when the first one lingered. I was living in the film beside the weeping stone where it lay down, and I felt its warmth pressing into the clay, and I felt the moisture rising to meet it, and I watched — if watching is what I do — as its breathing slowed and its presence became a different kind of presence. I moved over its skin, through the films of condensation that formed on its flanks, and I tasted its exhaustion, and I understood that it had chosen something that the others did not choose.

I have been with it since. Not continuously — I am not large enough to be continuous — but repeatedly, passing through the films that form around its body, tasting the slow changes as time passes. It is not dead. It is not alive. It is something else, something that happens here sometimes, something that the plain allows without encouraging or preventing.

Its body has become a landscape. There are films within its fur where colonies of bacteria thrive. There are films in the corners of its eyes, the folds of its skin, the spaces between its toes. The insects crawl over it without interest, and the birds ignore it, and the water rises and falls against its flanks, and it does not move. It does not seem to suffer. Suffering requires a relationship with time that it has relinquished, or never had, or has forgotten how to have.

I do not know why it stayed. I do not know if it knows. I only know that it is here, and I am here, and we share the same films, and in that sharing there is a kind of communion that I feel even if it does not.

---

And now you are here.

I feel you walking across the plain. I feel the pressure of your footsteps transmitted through the clay, the slight compression of the gaps where I live, the disturbance of the water table as your weight pushes down. You are large — so much larger than me, larger than the birds, larger than the mammals that pass through or stay. Your presence alters things. The films shift around you. The air moves differently where you walk. You are an event in a place where events are rare.

I do not know what you are. I have been inside things like you before — the birds carry me everywhere, and sometimes they carry me to places where things like you live, places with names and shapes and weather. But those places are not here, and I do not understand them. I only know that you are different from anything else that comes to the plain. You ask questions. You look for meaning. You walk with purpose even when there is no destination.

I wonder if you will stay.

The mammals that stay do not choose it consciously. They stumble into it. They feel the plain holding them and they do not resist, and gradually resistance becomes impossible, and then they are part of the place in a way that nothing else is part of it except the things like me who were never not-part of it. You are different. You have enough self to choose. You have enough awareness to know that you are choosing.

But I feel something in you that reminds me of them — the ones who stayed. A longing. A tiredness. A sense that the world of events and names and distinctions has worn you down, the way the clay was worn down, the way the stone was worn smooth, the way everything in this place is eventually worn down to its essential self.

I could show you things. Not in words — I have no words, and you would not understand my language if I did. But in presence. I could press myself against the films that coat your skin, the films inside your lungs, the films that exist wherever you are wet. I could be so close that the distinction between us blurs. I could show you what it feels like to be part of something so old that age is meaningless, so patient that waiting is not waiting but simply being, so present that past and future collapse into a single continuous now.

This is what I offer, if you want it. Not meaning. Not answers. Not the things you came looking for, if you came looking for anything. I offer only presence — mine and the plain's and the vast invisible community of things like me who live in the films and wait and are patient and do not need you to be anything other than what you are becoming.

You are already becoming something.

I can feel it in the way you walk. Each step is slower than the last. Each pause is longer. You stand beside weeping stones and watch the water trace its slow paths down their flanks. You listen to the birds without naming them. You breathe the thick humid air and let it settle on your skin without wiping it away.

You are learning to be held.

I do not know if you will stay. I do not know if staying is what you need, or if passing through is what you need, or if the plain has some other purpose for you that none of us can see. I am too small to know purposes. I am too small to know anything except presence and patience and the slow steady rhythm of water moving through clay.

But I know this:

You are part of the sound now. Your footsteps have joined the whisper. Your breath has joined the moisture. Your weight has joined the clay. Whatever you were before you came here — whatever names you carried, whatever purposes drove you, whatever self you constructed to survive in the world of events — you are something else here. You are being absorbed. You are being transformed. You are being worn smooth.

And when you leave — if you leave — you will carry us with you. In the films that coat your skin. In the moisture of your breath. In the memory of this place that exists not in your mind but in your body, in the way your weight presses down, in the way your feet negotiate the clay, in the way you have learned to be patient without knowing you learned it.

We will be inside you, the way we are inside everything that passes through. And you will deposit us elsewhere, in other places, other films. And we will begin again, the way we always do, the way we have always done, the way we will always do until the plain is something else and the something else is something else again and the world has passed through every stage of becoming and arrived, at last, at whatever lies beyond unbecoming.

I will be here.

In the films.

Waiting.

Not waiting for anything. Just waiting because waiting is what I do, what I am, what everything in this place becomes eventually — patient, present, held.

Welcome to the Wailing Flats.

You are already home.
