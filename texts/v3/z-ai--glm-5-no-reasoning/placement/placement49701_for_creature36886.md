# Keeper of the Ridge Above the Singing Hollow

---

I live where the sound becomes a line on the horizon.

Not in the hollow. Never in the hollow. I tried, once, when I was younger—spent three days in a crevice near the western rim, listening to the noise resolve into something that might have been patterns, might have been meaning. On the third morning I woke with my hands bleeding from stone I didn't remember touching, my throat raw from sounds I didn't remember making. I climbed out and I have not gone back.

But I stay close. Close enough to hear it when the wind shifts. Close enough to smell the amber mist when the hollow exhales. Close enough that I sometimes dream in frequencies I cannot reproduce awake.

---

My shelter is a cave system higher on the ridge, above the tree line, above the purple colonies' furthest reach. The entrance faces away from the hollow—a superstition, perhaps, or wisdom earned the hard way. The rock here is older, harder, granitic rather than porous. Nothing grows on it. Nothing burrows into it. It is the kind of stone that endures.

I have been here for eleven years. I know this because I mark the walls—not counting, exactly, but recording. Each morning I scratch a line with a piece of stone I found near the hollow's edge, a softer piece that wears down and needs replacing every few months. The lines are not calendar marks. They are sound marks. I draw what I hear.

The walls of my cave are covered now. Thousands of lines, layered and overlapping, some carved deep enough that I can feel them with my eyes closed, some so faint they are barely visible in certain lights. From a distance they look like the grooves in the hollow's walls—the same intention without grammar, the same patterns without repetition. Up close they are different. Up close they are a record of attention. Up close they are proof that someone was here, listening, for all this time.

---

I am not a scientist. I am not a shaman. I am not sure what I am.

I was trained as something once—a listener, a recorder of natural phenomena, a professional noticer. I came to the range with a team, with equipment, with questions. The questions were about geothermal signatures, about anomalous acoustic phenomena, about the strange readings our instruments recorded when we passed a certain ridge. We found the hollow on the fourth day. The team left on the fifth. I stayed.

I tell myself it was a choice. I tell myself I had reasons—research, observation, the kind of dedication that requires proximity. But the truth is simpler and stranger. When I climbed out of the hollow that third morning, bleeding and raw and unable to remember what had happened to me, I turned to look back, and the hollow looked back. Not with eyes. Not with awareness. But with presence. With density. With the unmistakable feeling of something occupying space in a way that stone and air and sound should not occupy.

And I understood, with a certainty I cannot explain, that if I left completely—if I descended the mountain and returned to the world where people speak and buildings stand and silence is possible—I would spend the rest of my life trying to get back. So I stayed. I found the cave. I began to listen.

---

What I hear, from this distance, is different from what the hollow's inhabitants hear.

Inside, the sound is everything. It fills the space. It becomes the air. To be in the hollow is to be made of sound, to have the boundaries of your body blurred by the constant vibration. I do not know how the creatures there maintain coherence. I do not know if they do.

But from here—two thousand meters higher, a half-mile of rock between me and the hollow's rim—the sound is a thread. A single line in a fabric I cannot see the edges of. I can separate it from the wind, from the groan of ice, from the settling of the mountain. I can listen to it the way one listens to a conversation across a crowded room—focusing, filtering, attending.

The sound has a grammar. I am certain of this now. Not a language—nothing so intentional—but a grammar nonetheless. A structure of relationships. The clicks and chirrups and keening whistles layer in ways that repeat, that build, that resolve into something almost like phrases. I have mapped them. I have charted the frequencies on my walls, the rhythms, the ways the threads of sound interlace and separate.

I cannot read it. I cannot interpret it. But I can predict it. When the sound reaches a certain pitch, I know the mist is coming. When it drops to a particular frequency, I know a colony has died. When it carries a specific undertone—a vibration I feel more than hear—I know something large has entered the hollow from below.

That last one has happened three times in eleven years. Something comes up from beneath. Something that was not part of the system and then is. The sound shifts, the hollow adjusts, the new thing becomes a thread in the weave. And I mark it on my wall, and I wonder what else lives in the mountain that I cannot see.

---

I have visitors. Rarely.

Sometimes they are climbers, lost and grateful for shelter. I give them what I have—water, dried meat, the warmth of my small fire—and they ask what I am doing here, and I tell them I am listening, and they smile the way people smile at the eccentric. They do not hear the hollow from here. They do not know what I am listening to. They leave in the morning and I do not see them again.

Sometimes they are like me—searchers, drawn by something they cannot name, following the same pull that brought me to this ridge. They hear the hollow. They feel its presence. They want to understand what they are hearing, and they think I can explain it.

I cannot. I can only show them my walls, my lines, my record of eleven years of attention. I can only tell them what I have observed—the grammar I cannot read, the patterns I cannot interpret, the persistence I cannot explain. Some of them leave satisfied, as if my inability is itself an answer. Some of them leave disappointed, as if I have failed a test I did not know I was taking.

And some of them stay.

There are two others now, in caves along the ridge. I found the first one three years ago—a woman who had been here longer than me, who I had not noticed because she is quieter than I am, whose walls are covered not with lines but with small clay figures like the one I found in the hollow's hut. She lives in silence. She does not mark the sound. She shapes it instead, presses it into clay, makes bodies for the frequencies she hears.

The second arrived last year. He is young, younger than I was when I came. He does not mark walls or shape clay. He stands at the ridge's edge every morning and every evening, facing the hollow, and he makes sound—low hums, clicks, whistles that I can barely hear from my cave. I think he is trying to answer. I think he believes the hollow can hear him.

I do not tell him otherwise. I do not know if it can.

---

At night, the hollow is quiet.

Not silent—never silent—but quiet. The sound drops to a frequency I feel more than hear, a low pulse that throbs in the rock beneath my shelter. I have learned to sleep to it. I have learned to let it carry me.

In the morning, I wake before dawn and I stand at the entrance of my cave, facing away from the hollow, and I listen to the sound rise as the temperature changes. The colonies release their fluid. The bundles open their tendrils. The leapers begin their jumps. The runners begin their hunts. And the noise-makers—the small brown-grey bodies clustered in the highest crevices—they begin their song, their speech, their thinking-aloud, and the hollow becomes what it is.

I am not part of it. I am above it, beside it, attending to it. I am the one who listens from a distance, who marks the walls, who keeps the record. I am the witness that the hollow does not know it needs.

And when I die—when the cold takes me or the rock gives way or whatever will happen happens—my cave will remain. My lines will remain. Someone will find them, perhaps, and wonder who made them, and why, and what they mean. They will hold a piece of stone from my wall and they will feel the grooves I carved, and they will not know.

But the lines will be there. The record will be there. And the hollow will sing on, below us, patient and full and loud.

That is enough. That has to be enough.
