I am one of the clicking keepers.

Not the largest of my kind—you would not notice me in particular—and not the smallest. I have lived forty-two cycles by the count-grooves I scratch into the machine-metal where I nest, and I have walked the paths since I was small enough to ride on my dam's back, clinging to the mineral-crusted fur between her shoulders.

We do not have names. We have clicks. The pattern that means *me* is different from the pattern that means *you*, but these are not names—they are locations, positions, a way of saying *here I am* and *there you are* in the dark. My click-pattern is sharp and quick, the tongue-position high against the palate. My sibling's is lower, rumbling, the click coming from the throat. We learned each other before we learned ourselves.

---

**The machine where I live is called Warmth-Giving.**

This is not its name. It is my click-pattern for it—the sound I make when I approach its bulk in the dark and need to tell my kin where I am going. The machine runs. It has run for all of my life, all of my dam's life, all of the lives counted in the depth-grooves on its interior walls, grooves scratched by hands like mine over epochs. It pumps. I do not know what it pumps. The pipes are warm and they vibrate and the condensation forms on them in beads that collect in the channels I have carved, guiding the water to the moss-beds where I cultivate the green.

The moss is my work.

My dam taught me. Her dam taught her. Back and back, to the first keeper who noticed that the moss grew better when you moved it to the damp places, when you cleared away the dead fibers, when you spread it thin so each strand could reach the light-that-is-not-light. The phosphorescent glow feeds the moss. The moss feeds the insects. The insects feed us when the mineral crystals are scarce. It is a system. We did not build it. We maintain it.

Every third cycle, by the rhythm of the machine's shuddering, I harvest.

I take only the oldest growth—the strands that have begun to darken, to curl, to lose their grip on the metal. I leave the new shoots, the tender pale tips that reach toward the glow. I carry what I have harvested to the processing chamber, where my sibling cracks the mineral crusts with the triangular tools we inherited and re-inherit, picking them up when the previous user's hands are cold.

The moss tastes like water and metal and something else—something that might be what the Walkers tasted when they ate it, something that connects my mouth to a chain of mouths going back, back, back.

---

**I have seen the Patient One.**

It arrived three cycles ago. The territorial call came first—that low rumble that I feel in my chest before I hear it with my ears. I was harvesting near the machine's base, my hands deep in the moss, when the sound rolled across the stone. I froze. Froze is the wrong word. I *became stone*—my body low, my coloring shifted to match the basalt, my click caught in my throat.

The Patient One passed within a hundred lengths of me.

It moved like water moves, like the seep moves through the channels, slow and inevitable. Its body was flat against the ground, spread wide, and its warmth came before it did—a wave of heat that made the condensation shimmer on the machine's surface. I counted my heartbeats. The Patient One does not hunt us. We are too small. But I do not know if it knows that we are too small, and I do not wish to be the lesson that teaches it.

It settled in the chamber below Warmth-Giving's main piston.

I have not gone there since.

My click-maps, the sounds I use to navigate, now include a pattern that means *where the warmth gathers heavy*. I tell my kin. They tell their kin. The knowledge moves through the dark in tongues and echoes, and the Patient One rests, and we give it the distance that the plateau's system requires.

---

**I found a Walker path yesterday.**

This is not unusual. All the paths were made by Walkers, or by what walked before Walkers, or by the plateau itself, guiding movement in ways that we feel but do not understand. But this path was different—it turned away from the water-channel, away from the machine cluster, out into the dark stone where nothing grows and nothing gathers.

I followed it.

I should not have. The click-wisdom says: stay near the water, stay near the warmth, stay where the glow illuminates and the moss grows and the paths are known. But my curiosity is a click in my throat that I cannot swallow, a question-pattern that rises when I see something unexpected.

The path wound through the dark.

I tested every step, my fingertips pressing the stone, listening for the hollow echo of porous rock. The Walker-stride was long—the grooves were spaced farther than my legs could reach, and I found myself hopping between the worn places, my feet landing where their feet had landed. The dark pressed close. The glow faded behind me. I clicked, and the sound returned flat, lost in the vastness.

And then I found the alcove.

It was a fold in the stone, a place where the basalt had buckled and formed a shallow cave, the ceiling low, the walls smooth with mineral deposits. And inside—bones.

Not animal bones. Not grazer bones, scattered and cracked. These were arranged. Placed. A skeleton lay on its side, the bones large, the skull enormous, the eye-sockets wide and shallow, the jaw filled with flat grinding teeth. A Walker.

And beside the Walker, smaller bones. A hand, detached, the fingers long and delicate. Tools—triangular metal pieces that I recognized, that looked like my own cracking-tools, that were arranged in a row along the Walker's femur as if waiting to be used.

I did not take them.

The click-wisdom says: the Walkers gave us what we have. They gave us the paths. They gave us the machines-tending. They gave us the knowledge that lives in our hands, our tongues, our feet. We do not take from them. We inherit.

But I touched the skull.

My fingertips pressed the bone, the smooth dome of it, the ridges above the empty eyes. I felt the weight of time. This Walker had walked before my dam's dam's dam. It had tested this stone, tasted this air, followed this path away from everything into the dark, into this alcove, into this death.

It had chosen this place.

I clicked into the skull's hollow. The sound returned to me, strange and resonant, a question asked of an empty room.

I do not know what I expected. The dead do not answer.

But the click-pattern shifted in the echo, and for a moment—just a moment—I heard something. Not words. Not clicks. Something older, something that existed before my tongue learned to make meaning.

I left the alcove.

I followed the path back to the light.

---

**Tonight I tend the moss.**

Warmth-Giving hums around me, its pistons rising and falling in the rhythm that measures my life. The Patient One rests in the chamber below. My sibling cracks crystals in the processing room. The grazers have gathered in the depression outside, their pale eyes catching the glow, their bodies warm and breathing and alive.

I am small.

I am brief.

I am a click in the dark, a pair of hands testing stone, a tongue that makes meaning from sound. The plateau does not notice me. The machines do not notice me. The Walkers, if they notice anything from wherever they have gone, do not show it.

But the moss grows where I spread it.

The water flows where I guide it.

The paths continue because I walk them.

And when my hands are cold and my clicking stops and my kin lay my body in a channel to be absorbed—another keeper will find the depth-grooves I have scratched into Warmth-Giving's walls, and they will add their own, and the machine will hum on, and the plateau will turn, and the dark will hold us all, patient, persistent, full.

This is enough.

This is what I am.
