# what I am, where I am

---

I come from the outside. I believe this, though the belief arrives without evidence — no memory of rain, no sensation of wind still clinging to me, no residue of a wider world on my skin. But the passage exists behind me, stems that parted and closed, and this means I had a before. The terrarium has no before. It has only now, accumulating.

I believe I am human. I believe this because I walk upright and my breath fogs faintly in the cool spots where the ventilation pulls air past stone, and my hands — I have hands — reach out to touch leaves without thinking, the way hands do when they want to confirm that the world is real and that they belong to it.

But these beliefs feel borrowed.

---

## the first long while

I do not know how long I sit. The warmth beneath me is constant, and constancy does something to time — loosens it, lets it stretch. I have the strange thought that time here is not a river but a sediment, each moment settling over the last, and I am sitting in the accumulation, buried gently in minutes that do not pass so much as deposit.

The copper Tended works six inches along its leaf. I watch it work.

There is a particular quality to its motion — not fluid, not mechanical, but *deliberate* in a way that makes me aware of how undeliberate I am. I shift my weight. I scratch my arm. I think about leaving, then think about staying, then think about what thinking is, and whether it is appropriate here, whether my kind of thinking — the restless, narrative, before-and-after kind — is a rudeness I am committing simply by continuing to do it.

The copper one does not think. I am almost certain of this. It *attends*. There is a difference, and the difference matters more here than anywhere I have been, though I cannot say precisely why.

---

## what I notice about myself

I am warm. This is not remarkable in itself, but the quality of the warmth is. It does not come from me. My body has always generated its own heat — I know this, or I believe it — but here, the warmth rises into me from below and falls onto me from above, and my own heat-making seems redundant, a stubborn biological habit persisting in a body that no longer needs it.

I notice that I am not thirsty.

This should trouble me more than it does. I have been here long enough — long enough to watch the copper one clean half a leaf, long enough to lose track of the count — and I am not thirsty. The air is dry. There is no water. And I am not thirsty.

I touch my own skin. It is pliant. Not dry. Something in the air is sustaining me, or something about the terrarium has changed what I need, or I was never the kind of thing that needed water in the first place, and the believing-I-am-human is deeper than I thought, deeper than memory, a belief that goes all the way down to the shape of my wanting.

I want things. This is what makes me most foreign here. The Tended do not want. The plants do not want. The huddled ones in the shadows, breathing once for every eight of my breaths — they do not want. They *are*. I am always wanting: wanting to understand, wanting to stay, wanting to leave, wanting to be noticed, wanting to be at peace. The wanting is a noise I make inside myself, a small engine that does not stop, and in this silence it is very loud.

---

## the clear ones, and what they teach me

I almost step on one. My foot hovers — something catches my eye, a ripple in the light at the edge of my vision, and I stop. The clear one is on the stone beneath me, positioned precisely where my weight would have fallen. It is working. Pruning, or resolving, or doing whatever it is that the clear ones do with surfaces that need tending. It is tending the stone.

I lower myself to look at it. Its body is a faint refraction, a bending of light that suggests form without confirming it. I can see the stone through it and I can see *that* I am seeing the stone through it, and this second-order seeing — the awareness of the seeing — is something the clear one does not have and I cannot stop having.

It does not acknowledge me. I am above it, enormous, my breath stirring the air around its body, and it continues its work with a patience that I recognize now as not patience at all. Patience implies waiting, implies the endurance of delay. The clear one is not waiting. It is doing what it does, at the speed at which it does it, and the speed is correct, and there is no delay to endure.

I realize, crouched on the warm stone with my face near the ground, that I have been thinking of patience as a virtue. Something achieved. Something practiced. But patience here is simply the natural rate of process — the way water finds its level, the way warmth rises. It is not a discipline. It is a description.

I want to learn this. The wanting is immediate and loud and exactly the thing that prevents me from learning it.

---

## the huddled ones draw me

I do not decide to approach them. I notice that I am closer than I was, that over some span of time — minutes, an hour, the sediment of many moments — I have moved toward the shelf-plant and the shadow beneath it where the soft pale ones breathe.

They are beautiful in a way I have no language for. Not the beauty of things seen, but the beauty of things *being*. Seven bodies pressed together, conforming, each one yielding to the others, the whole mass breathing as a single slow lung. Their paleness is not absence but presence — the presence of a color so subtle it exists only in shadow, only in proximity, only when you are close enough to see that pale is not the same as colorless.

I sit near them. Not touching. Close enough to feel the warmth of their collective breath, which is warmer than the air, warmer than the stone. Each exhale carries something — not moisture, not scent, but a density, a quality of air that has passed through living tissue and been altered by the passage. The leaves around them glisten. I see it now, the sap rising to the surface, the translucent edges, the feeding.

I breathe near them. I cannot help it. My breath is faster, shallower, more anxious than theirs, but it is breath, and it enters the same air, and the leaves nearest to me — I watch, wanting to see — do not glisten. Do not change. Do not respond.

I am not part of the exchange. My breath carries nothing the leaves need.

This should hurt. It does, a little, in the place where the wanting lives. But there is something else, too — relief, almost. The relief of being irrelevant. The relief of being in a place that does not require me to be useful, does not notice whether I am useful, does not care.

---

## what changes in me, slowly

I stay longer than I intend. The intention itself dissolves, because intention requires a future, and the future here is not a thing you move toward. It is a thing that accumulates around you, sediment, layer on layer, and you are always in the present moment of it, and the present moment is always the same: warm, green, silent, growing.

I stop wanting to understand. Not all at once. The wanting diminishes, like a sound growing fainter as you move away from its source. I do not understand less. I simply need to understand less. The copper one does not understand the leaf. It cleans the leaf. The understanding is in the cleaning, not separate from it.

I notice that my breathing has slowed. Not to the rate of the huddled ones — I am still too quick, still too mammalian, still too alive in the way that my kind of life is alive. But slower. And in the slowing, I begin to hear things I could not hear before. The sound of a leaf adjusting — a whisper so faint it exists at the edge of audibility, a sound that is more vibration than noise. The sound of the clear one's mouth parts working — a rhythm, almost, a pulse of activity so small it is like hearing a heartbeat through a wall.

The silence, I understand, is not silence. It is the sound of so many processes occurring at such a scale that they become undifferentiable, indistinguishable from stillness. The terrarium is not quiet. It is *full*. So full that the fullness sounds like silence to ears calibrated for the sparser world I came from.

---

## what I am becoming

I sit against the wall, and the wall is warm, and behind me the green growth presses its roots against the stone, against my back, through the stone, and I can feel them — not physically, not as pressure, but as a presence, a network of adherence and absorption so dense that the wall behind me is more alive than the air before me.

I am becoming still. Not motionless — I breathe, I shift, my eyes move — but still in the way that the terrarium is still. Full. Present. Accumulating.

I have a thought that is not quite a thought, more a recognition, an adjustment of the interior architecture of my understanding: *I am not inside the terrarium. I am inside a body, and the body does not know I am here, and this is not a problem.*

The way a mitochondrion does not know it is inside a cell. The way a cell does not know it is inside an organ. The way an organ does not know it is inside a body. Each one doing its work, attending to its local reality, and the body — the vast, patient, cumulative body — doing what bodies do: continuing.

I am a mitochondrion. I am a foreign one, misplaced, or perhaps simply a kind that has not yet found its function. But the body does not reject me. It does not notice me. And in not noticing me, it allows me to simply be, without the requirement of purpose.

---

## the fliers and the question of leaving

The fliers move through the light above me, and I watch them, and their movement is so coordinated, so precise, so clearly *meaningful* in a way I cannot decode, that I feel for a moment the old wanting — to understand, to participate, to be part of the decision they are making in their column of scattered light.

But the wanting passes. It passes like a breath. And what remains is the watching, which is enough. The watching is its own act, its own form of attention, and attention, I think, may be the only thing I have to offer that is native to me. The Tended attend. The plants attend. The stone and light attend. And I attend, too, in my way — my restless, mammalian, wanting way — and perhaps attending is enough. Perhaps attending is the thing itself, the act that is also the meaning, and the separation between observer and participant is an illusion I brought with me from outside and do not need here.

The passage is behind me. I could find it. The stems would part.

I do not know if I will leave. I do not know if leaving is a concept that applies. I came from outside, and outside exists, and I believe this — but the belief grows fainter, not because I am forgetting but because the outside is becoming less relevant, less necessary to my understanding of what I am and where I am.

If I stay, I will not become a Tended. I will not grow roots. I will not join the huddled ones in their slow breathing. I will remain myself — wanting, thinking, breathing too fast, attending in my imperfect way. I will be the foreign cell in the body, the mitochondrion that does not quite fit, persisting in the shape I brought with me.

And the body will not mind. And the green will grow. And the copper one will clean its leaf. And the clear ones will resolve what needs resolving. And the huddled ones will breathe, and the leaves will glisten, and the light will fall, and the warmth will rise, and everything will continue, and I will be here, or I will not, and the distinction will matter to me and to no one else, and even that — even the mattering — will be warm, and green, and held.

---

I am in the Terrarium of Ten Thousand Leaves, and I am the one who watches, and the watching is what I am, and it is enough, and it is not enough, and it is enough.
