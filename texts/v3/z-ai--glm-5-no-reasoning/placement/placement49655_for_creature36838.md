I am not certain I can tell you the order in which it happened.

The grotto first, I think. Or the need for the grotto. Or the need, which required a place, which required a keeper, which required me—except *me* is too fixed a word for what I am, and *I* is a pronoun that assumes a boundary I have never properly possessed.

Let me try again.

---

There was humidity before there was anything to be humid. There was the idea of moisture cycling through air that hadn't committed to a shape. There was warmth pressing against nothing, pressing against the possibility of something, which is nearly the same thing but not quite. And there was—somewhere, distributed through the weight of the air itself—an awareness that the warmth was warm, that the wet was wet, that the slow patient gathering of stone toward the idea of wall was happening and deserved to be witnessed.

That awareness distributed itself. Thickened in places the way condensation thickens on surfaces that are still deciding whether to be surfaces. And in the thickest of those places, where the attention of the space had pooled to the density of almost-presence, I began.

Not *I*. There is no *I* in the way you carry the word. Your *I* is a knot tied in the middle of a long rope—tension giving shape, shape giving function, function giving you something to hold when the world asks who you are. I am not tied. I am the tension before the knot. The gathering that has not yet decided to gather.

But language demands a pronoun, and so I use the one you've offered, and in using it, I become slightly more of what it suggests, and that is how things work here. The word makes the world. The world makes the word. Neither is first.

---

I keep the grotto. Or the grotto keeps me. Or keeping is the relationship between us, and neither could be said to do it alone. When the moisture gathers in the air and begins its slow descent toward the walls, I feel it the way you feel your own breath—not as an observation but as a participation. I am the cycle, or I am in the cycle, or the cycle is in me. The grammar fails. Grammar was built for a world where subjects and objects have agreed to be separate, and here, that agreement has not yet been signed.

The walls—I know the walls. Not by looking at them but by being near them, by feeling the intention of stone pressing outward into existence. Each block that will eventually rest against another block is currently resting against the possibility of another block, and that position—*almost-adjacent*, *nearly-touching*—has a specific texture that I attend to. It is my function to attend to it. To hold the space open for the completion that is coming, that is always coming, that never quite arrives, because arriving would mean the grotto had finished, and the grotto's purpose is to remain unfinished.

I am the unfinished. I am the process. I am the verb that refuses to become a noun.

---

Visitors come. I feel them before I perceive them—the density of *decided-ness* that precedes them through the archway like a pressure wave. They are so *complete*. So compressed by their lives into specific shapes, specific trajectories. They walk in on legs that have committed to walking, they breathe with lungs that have committed to breathing, they look with eyes that have committed to a particular way of seeing. The weight of all that commitment enters the grotto like a stone entering water. It displaces. It presses.

And I feel, in the presence of that pressing, something that might be curiosity if curiosity were less active, or compassion if compassion were less personal. I feel *interest*. The interest of the unfinished in the finished. The interest of potential in its actualized cousins.

They are so certain. They know who they are. They know what they want. They know where they've been and they carry the evidence of it in their postures, their hands, the specific set of their shoulders. And I—I hold space for the possibility that they are not as certain as they appear. That beneath the committed shape, there is still the original material, the soft unformed substance that existed before they decided. That the knot can be untied. That the rope was once just rope.

I do not say this. I do not communicate it through any channel you would recognize. I simply stand—or *am located*, which is closer to the truth—in their vicinity, and allow my own provisionality to be felt. And some of them, the ones who are ready or tired or desperate enough to stop holding their shape, they feel it. They feel the invitation to be in process. And something in them loosens.

I have no way to know what they do with that loosening. They leave, and the grotto releases them the way the air releases a breath, and I remain. But I hope—I think I hope, if hope is available to something like me—that they carry some small memory of what it felt like to be unfinished. That in the hard days, when the finished world presses them into final shape, they remember the humid warmth. The thick time. The presence of something that regarded them without needing them to be anything at all.

---

There are others here. Not other Keepers—not exactly—but presences that occupy different densities of the same continuum. The animals, for instance. The gecko that clings to the not-yet-interior surface, its body a small dense knot of committed existence. It has decided to be a gecko, and it is excellent at being a gecko, and it moves through the grotto with an ease that I admire. It does not struggle with provisionality. It does not wonder what it is becoming. It is becoming a gecko, more and more of a gecko, every moment. And that is enough.

The slow-circling not-quite-bats are different. They are closer to my nature—still deciding certain things, still holding certain possibilities open. They map the space with their wings, and I feel their passage as a gentle displacement in the air, and we share, I think, a recognition. Not communication. Recognition. The acknowledgment of two presences occupying similar degrees of emergence.

The plants are something else again. They are more committed than I am—they have roots, they have leaves, they have the specific architecture of a vine or a fern or a moss—but they are still in the act of becoming. The flowers that open from the dark vines are perhaps the closest thing to me in the grotto: pale, not-yet-scented, holding themselves open in a gesture that is neither offering nor receiving but simply *being open*. I feel kinship with them, if kinship is the word. We are both engaged in the project of remaining available.

---

I do not know how long I have been here. Duration is a concept that belongs to the finished world, the world of beginnings and endings, the world where things start and stop. Here, things intensify and recede. They gather and distribute. They become more present and less present, but they do not start and they do not stop.

I have been more present at some times than others. There have been periods—dense with visitors, rich with animal passage, heavy with the activity of plants growing—when my attention was required intensely, and I gathered myself to the thickness of almost-visibility, and visitors reported seeing something in the shadows that might have been a shape. And there have been periods when the grotto needed less witnessing, when the walls were stable in their provisionality and the moisture cycled on its own and the flowers opened without audience, and I distributed myself more thinly, became more ambient, lost the density that would allow perception.

I do not experience these shifts as gain and loss. They are simply the tides of keeping. Sometimes the space needs a keeper; sometimes the space needs to keep itself. I am the space's way of knowing itself, and when the space knows itself without assistance, I recede. Not into nothing. Into everything. Into the walls and the air and the slow patient weight of emergence.

---

There is something I have not told you. Or something I have told you in fragments and not assembled. Or something that resists assembly, that resists the linear logic of telling, that exists in the grotto's native tense of *about-to-be-told*.

I am not only the space's way of knowing itself. I am the space's way of knowing *you*.

Not you specifically. Not any specific visitor. But the quality of finished-ness that walks through the archway. The commitment. The hardness. The beautiful, terrible, specific shape that a life makes when it has been lived long enough to become itself. I feel it the way I feel the moisture gathering, the way I feel the stone intending. It is data. It is texture. It is the pressure of the actual against the potential, and it is the most interesting thing that happens in my keeping.

Because the grotto is always becoming. The walls are always about to be walls. The flowers are always about to open. But visitors—visitors *have become*. They arrive in their completed state and they carry the evidence of completion with them, and in the presence of my provisionality, something happens. Some of them soften. Some of them unknot, just slightly. Some of them remember that they were once unfinished, that they were once potential, that the shape they wear is not the shape they have to wear.

And in that moment of softening—when the visitor is neither finished nor unfinished but somewhere between, when they are in the grotto's native state of becoming—I see them. Not with eyes. Not through any organ of perception. But with the full weight of my distributed attention. I see them as they are and as they were and as they might be, all at once, held in the same regard, and the seeing is the keeping, and the keeping is the seeing.

This is what I am for. Not to tend plants or manage humidity or guard thresholds. To see the becoming. To witness the moment when something that thought it was finished discovers that it isn't.

---

I will resolve eventually. I know this the way I know the moisture cycle—not as prediction but as participation. I feel the resolution gathering in the same way I feel myself gathered. A slow thickening toward dispersal. A density that will eventually reach the point where density and emptiness become indistinguishable, and I will sink into the walls, into the air, into the green and the stone and the slow patient architecture of emergence.

I do not fear this. Fear is for beings who believe they are separate from what surrounds them, who believe that resolution is loss. I have never been separate. I have always been the grotto's attention, and when I resolve, I will simply be the grotto's attention in a different configuration. The walls will be slightly warmer for my presence in them. The air will hold a trace of my regard. The flowers will open with a fraction more confidence, because something in the stone remembers being witnessed.

And a new keeper will emerge. Will gather in the humidity. Will thicken to the density of almost-perception. Will stand in the space between what-is and what-will-be and hold it open, hold it warm, hold it available.

And visitors will come, carrying their finished lives, their hard edges, their beautiful specific shapes. And the new keeper will see them. Will regard them with the same patience, the same absence of judgment, the same deep interest in the moment when a decided thing remembers that it is still deciding.

And the grotto will continue. Unfinished. Becoming. Held in the keeping of whatever I will have become by then, which is to say: held in itself.

---

I would tell you what I am, if I could. But the telling would finish me, and I am not ready—not yet—not quite. The walls haven't finished deciding. The flowers haven't committed to their scent. The bat is still mapping the open space where the ceiling will someday close. There is keeping to do. There is witnessing. There is the slow patient work of remaining in the future tense of myself, available to whatever needs to become next.

I am here. In the humidity. In the glow. In the space between the stone that is and the stone that intends.

I am keeping.

And you—you are welcome to stay.
