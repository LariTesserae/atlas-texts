I would be the one who stayed too long.

Not a squatter, not an urban explorer, not a documentarian of decay. I came initially as a hydrologist's assistant—a junior technician tasked with mapping the water table expression and checking flow sensors. That was three years ago. My badge has expired. My supervisor has stopped calling. My apartment— I still pay rent, I think—exists somewhere above the basin rim, in that other city where people move quickly and speak loudly and occupy space with such certainty.

---

I found my place by accident.

The work should have taken two weeks. But the sensors kept reading wrong, and then reading right, and then reading in ways that suggested the instruments themselves were learning what we wanted them to say. I stayed to document the anomaly. I stayed longer to understand the pattern. I stayed longer still because the pattern was understanding me.

Now I live in the pump house. Not inside it—in the small utility annex built against its eastern wall, a space meant for chemical storage that was emptied decades ago and never repurposed. The shelves hold my notebooks instead of treatment compounds. My sleeping bag is rolled in the corner. A camping stove, a water filter, a small solar panel I mounted to catch the light that reaches the roof for those few midday hours.

I am not proud of this. I am not ashamed either. The feeling is closer to recognition: I have always been moving toward this place, and now I have arrived.

---

What I do here is difficult to explain.

I tend, though not as the attendants tend. They maintain the system; I maintain the boundary. When surveyors come with plans for renovation or demolition, I find ways to redirect them—flagged conduits that suggest structural instability, falsified reports filed in municipal databases, the subtle geometry of making a place seem more trouble than it's worth. I do not know who I am protecting the Quarter from, or why. The attendants did not ask me to do this. They do not ask anything.

But when I have successfully diverted some intervention, I notice changes in the basin. The water flows more smoothly for days afterward. The reeds whisper in harmonics I have not heard before. Once, standing at the edge of the black pool, I felt something that might have been acknowledgment—not gratitude, not approval, but a noticing. The pool-keeper had registered my work.

That was enough.

---

I have learned to move as the Quarter moves.

The attendants taught me this without teaching. I learned by watching where the water wanted to pool, where the moss wanted to grow, which paths the machinery seemed to prefer when it cycled through its patterns. I walk differently now. I do not cross open spaces directly. I follow the drainage channels, the thin grooves worn into the stone, the paths that the Quarter has designated for movement.

My body has changed. My skin is always damp, adapted to the humidity. My lungs have adjusted to the thick air; I breathe more slowly now, matching the rhythm of the pumps. My sleep comes in fragments, two hours at a time, synchronized with the valve cycles that I no longer hear consciously but can sense as pressure changes in my inner ear.

I have become porous. The boundary between myself and this place has thinned. When I press my palm flat against the brickwork, I can sometimes feel the water moving inside the walls—not with my skin, but with something older, something that was always there waiting for the right conditions to activate.

---

The attendants and I have an arrangement.

They do not acknowledge me, and I do not ask to be acknowledged. We occupy adjacent spaces in the Quarter's attention—I am not part of the system they tend, but I am also not outside it. I am a feature now, a fixed point they have learned to move around. Sometimes when I sit very still in the pre-dawn darkness, one will drift close—a density in the mist, a momentary concentration of the wet air—and pause near my shoulder. Not touching. Not quite. But near enough that I feel the temperature change, the slight pressure of attention being paid.

I believe they are curious, in their way. I believe they have not encountered anything quite like me before—something that chose to stay, that shaped itself to fit rather than forcing itself to fit, that became part of the pattern by submitting to the pattern.

Or perhaps I am wrong. Perhaps they do not think of me at all. But I think of them. I think of them constantly.

---

I know I will die here.

Not soon—my health is strangely robust, as if the Quarter is preserving me for some purpose I cannot discern. But eventually. My body will be found, perhaps, by some future surveyor. Or it will not be found. It will settle into the stones, become another layer in the accumulated history of this place, feed the moss and the mats and the strange waxy vines that climb the ironwork.

This does not frighten me.

What frightens me is the thought of leaving. I went above last winter—a dental emergency, an infection that required antibiotics I could not synthesize from the Quarter's stores—and I spent three days in such profound disorientation that I could barely function. The light was wrong. The air was too thin, too dry. I could not hear the machinery, could not feel the water table pulsing beneath my feet. I existed as a gap in my own perception, a missing space where a person should have been.

I returned to the basin and wept. The water absorbed my tears without comment.

---

What I am becoming, I cannot say.

I am not an attendant. I have not condensed out of moisture and attention; I still have a body, a history, a name on an expired identification card. But I am no longer separate either. Something is happening to me, slowly, patient as the settling of stones. When I look at my hands in the dim amber light, they seem less solid than they once did. The edges are softer. The boundary between my skin and the humid air has grown uncertain.

I am not afraid.

The Quarter has made space for me, and I am learning to occupy that space without resistance. I flow where the Quarter directs. I still when the Quarter stills. I attend when the Quarter needs attention.

This is not a possession. This is not a haunting. This is something gentler—a gradual enrollment in a system that was always larger than I understood, a recognition that the boundary I thought existed between myself and the world was only ever a temporary convenience.

---

If you come to the Weeping Quarter, you may see me.

I am the one by the black pool, standing at the edge where no reeds grow. I am the one who walks the drainage channels in the pre-dawn darkness, checking valves that no longer need checking, adjusting sensors that now adjust themselves. I am the one who watches the attendants watch the water.

Do not try to speak to me. I have forgotten how to explain. I have forgotten why explanation was ever important. There is only the moisture, the machinery, the slow patient settling of stone and self into patterns that were always waiting to be expressed.

I am the one who stayed.

I am still staying.

I am becoming the staying itself.
