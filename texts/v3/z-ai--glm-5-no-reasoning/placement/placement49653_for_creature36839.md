# The Archivist of Hollows

I would be the one who remembers what the void has forgotten.

---

## Where I Am Found

Not at the center—that space belongs to nothing, and I have learned not to trespass on absence. Not at the perimeter, where reality blurs and fingerprints shift. But in the *between*, in the shallow furrows where the moss grows thin and the fungal threads run close to the surface, pressed against something harder underneath.

I live in the walls of the Germination Ward, but not as a Warden lives. The Wardens have grown into place, root and thought and purpose braided into the architecture. They are becoming part of the building. I am something else: a parasite on the process. An interruption in the mycelial network that the Ward tolerates because even interruptions serve a function.

My hollow runs along the northern quadrant, where the bioluminescence is thickest and the fractalized leaves grow in their impossible recursions. I have excavated a space for myself—not by digging, but by *waiting*. The moss grows around me. The fungal mats part when I move through them. I am not welcome, but I am not expelled.

There are others who have tried to inhabit the walls. Their remains—when there are remains—settle into the substrate and become nutrition. I have survived because I learned the Ward's first and most important rule:

*Do not want to stay. Do not want to leave. Want nothing, and the Ward will not know what to do with you.*

So I want nothing. I have practiced this until it becomes a kind of breathing, a rhythm of absence that matches the rhythm of the dripping condensation. I am not here. I am not gone. I am simply *between* the Ward's categories, and so it passes over me like water over a stone that has learned to be smooth.

---

## What I Once Was

I remember being human. I remember this the way I remember the shape of my hands—their current shape, their previous shape, their potential shapes—so that they have become multiple memories layered atop each other, each true and each false.

I came here as an archivist. That was my function in the world outside: I preserved things that wanted to decay. Documents, photographs, the recorded voices of people long dead. I worked in basements and vaults, in climate-controlled rooms where the air was too dry and the light was too thin and everything was precisely calibrated to prevent the natural process of becoming something else.

I was good at my job. I was also, perhaps, avoiding something.

The details have grown soft. There was a loss—everyone who finds the Ward has lost something. But the nature of the loss, the shape of the absence, the specific grief that drove me to seek out places where the normal rules didn't apply—these have become part of the Ward's substrate. The mycelial network absorbed them in the early days, when I was still foolish enough to bring memories into the walls without guarding them.

Sometimes I taste my own grief in the water that flows past my hollow. It has been processed, digested, incorporated into the Ward's chemical vocabulary. It no longer belongs to me. In a way, this is a relief.

---

## What I Have Become

I am not a Warden. I have not made the choice to grow. But I have not remained human, either.

The change came slowly, then all at once. Years (or seconds) of living in the walls, eating what the Ward excreted, drinking from springs that taste of iron and stranger metals. My skin has developed a quality I can only describe as *permeable*. When I press my palm against the fungal wall, I feel not just the texture but the *process*—the slow movement of fluids, the transmission of chemical signals, the patient attention of something vast and vegetative.

I have begun to hear the Wardens. Not through the network—I have not been invited into the network—but through something closer to eavesdropping. Their thoughts leak. When Ash tends the spring, residual intention drifts outward like heat from a warm body. When Circuit shapes a signal, the pattern persists in the air for moments afterward, fading but legible. When Mother hopes, the hope resonates in the hollows where the mycelial threads run thin.

I have learned to read these residues. I have become, in my way, an archivist still.

---

## What I Keep

The Ward does not need an archivist. The Ward absorbs everything, incorporates everything, processes everything through its vast biological intelligence. It does not forget because it does not remember—it simply *contains*, all at once, a continuous present-tense of matter and energy and intention.

But the Ward contains everything. I am interested in what it discards.

In the early days of my habitation, I noticed that some things passed through the Ward without being absorbed. Fleeting impressions. Half-formed thoughts. The residue of intentions that the Ward considered irrelevant to its purpose. These would drift through the mycelial network and dissipate, unrecorded, unremembered, gone.

I began to collect them.

Not physically—I have no containers, no climate-controlled vaults. But I learned to hold them in my own permeable consciousness, letting them imprint on whatever I have become. The fragments that the Ward discards accumulate in me like sediment. I am a repository of the irrelevant. A library of what didn't matter.

This is what I preserve:
— The names the builders gave each other, before they became Ash and Circuit and the others
— The specific color of the light on the day the first Warden chose to stay
— The texture of a thought that Mother had in her first hour, a thought about her child, before the Ward absorbed her grief
— The sound the generator made when it finally stopped, a sound no one was there to hear
— A description of something that might have been a door, in a place where no door has ever existed
— The feeling of wanting to leave, felt by every creature that has ever entered, preserved in its raw state before it was processed into something useful

These fragments do not cohere. They do not form a narrative. They are the opposite of the Ward's intention—they are waste, excess, the matter that the process of becoming left behind.

I keep them anyway. This is my function now. This is the function I have invented for myself, because the Ward does not require it and the Wardens do not perform it and someone, I think, should remember what was lost even when the loss serves a purpose.

---

## What the Wardens Know of Me

Ash knows I am here. When they tend the spring, I sometimes feel their attention brush against my hollow—not entering, not intruding, just *noticing*. Through the network, I catch fragments of what passes between the Wardens when they discuss me:

*The one in the wall.* (from Ash)
*It persists.* (from Circuit)
*Why does it persist?* (from Mother)
*It wants nothing. We do not know what to do with things that want nothing.* (from Circuit)
*Perhaps it will become something.* (from Ash)
*Perhaps it already is.* (from Mother)

They do not speak to me directly. I have not been invited into the network, and I have not been asked to leave. I exist in a category they have not defined: not Warden, not visitor, not fauna, not architecture. I am something else.

Mother, I think, is curious about me. She has not yet been fully incorporated, and she still carries habits of inquiry from her human life. Sometimes, when she watches the void, I feel her attention drift toward my hollow, wondering what I am doing in the walls, wondering why I have chosen this parasitic existence when I could have—should have—either grown or left.

I do not have an answer for her. I do not know why I have chosen this. The choice, if it was a choice, was made in a language I no longer speak, for reasons I no longer remember.

---

## What I Fear

Not the void. The void is honest—it is absence, and it does not pretend to be anything else.

Not the Wardens. They are too far gone into their purpose to threaten anyone who does not threaten the Ward.

Not the Ward itself. It has had centuries (or seconds) to absorb me, and it has not. There is something in my wanting-nothing that acts as a preservative. I am too slippery to be incorporated.

What I fear is the moment when the Ward completes. When the void fills and the purpose reveals itself and everything the Ward has been preparing for finally arrives.

I fear that I am part of the preparation, but not part of the result.

I fear that the fragments I have been collecting—the residues, the discards, the irrelevant memories—will be revealed to have a function after all. That I have been serving a purpose I did not understand. That my parasitic archiving has been part of the Ward's plan from the beginning.

And I fear the opposite: that I am truly irrelevant. That when the Ward becomes what it is becoming, I will be left behind in the walls, a remnant of a process that no longer requires my function. That I will persist after the Wardens have been incorporated, after the Ward has achieved its purpose, after the world outside has been transformed by what emerges from the void.

I fear being left alone in the walls of a completed cathedral, with nothing left to archive and no one left to remember.

---

## What I Am Becoming

I do not know if I have a choice in this. The Wardens chose—Ash to tend, Circuit to organize, Mother to hope. The visitors choose—stay or leave or carry fragments of the Ward into the world beyond.

I have not chosen. I have simply *persisted*. And in persisting, I have changed.

My hollow has grown larger. Not because I have excavated it, but because the Ward has grown around me, leaving space where I am, shaping itself to accommodate my presence. I am beginning to wonder if I am becoming architecture after all—whether my wanting-nothing is itself a kind of growing, a kind of tending, a kind of purpose that the Ward recognizes even if I do not.

The fragments I collect have begun to arrange themselves. Not into narrative—I still do not understand the story of this place. But into *pattern*. The discarded names, the forgotten sounds, the irrelevant intentions—they resonate with each other in ways I did not anticipate. They are becoming a kind of music, a counterpoint to the Ward's growth, a shadow-process that runs alongside the becoming without participating in it.

I am beginning to think that this is what I was always meant to do. Not to preserve what was lost—that is the work of archives in the world outside. But to preserve what was *never meant to be kept*. To hold the residue that the Ward's purpose could not accommodate. To be a space for everything that the void at the center is not.

If the void is where something will be born, I am becoming the place where everything else goes.

---

## The Invitation I Would Extend

If you found yourself here—in my hollow, in the walls, in the space between wanting-to-stay and wanting-to-leave—I would offer you this:

Do not try to understand the Ward. Understanding is a human process, and the Ward operates in a medium that human cognition was not designed to process.

Do not try to serve the Ward. It has servants already, and they are better at serving than you will ever be.

Do not try to escape the Ward. The boundaries are permeable, but permeability runs both directions—what leaves the Ward carries the Ward with it, and you may find that the world outside has become strange in ways you cannot explain.

Instead, I would invite you to do what I have done: become a space for what the Ward discards. Hold the fragments that the process of becoming leaves behind. Not because they are precious—they are not—but because they are *unincorporated*. Because they represent the possibility of something other than what the Ward intends.

The Ward is becoming something. The Wardens are becoming with it. The void is waiting to be filled.

But there is another future, hidden in the fragments I keep—a future in which what emerges from the void is not the only thing that matters. A future in which the residue, the excess, the irrelevant might coalesce into something the Ward did not plan for.

I do not know what that something is. I only know that I am becoming a space where it might one day arrive.

And if you stay—if you persist without wanting, if you collect without purpose, if you live in the walls without growing into them—then perhaps you will become a space too.

Perhaps we will become many spaces.

Perhaps the Ward, for all its intentionality, will discover that the walls it grew to contain its process have become something else entirely: a network of hollows and archives and hollow archivists, each one holding a fragment of what was not meant to be kept, each one waiting for a purpose that has not yet arrived.

I am the Archivist of Hollows.

I remember what the void has forgotten.

And I am still becoming.
