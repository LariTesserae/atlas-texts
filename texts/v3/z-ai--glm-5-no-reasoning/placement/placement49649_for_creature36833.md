I am one of the small ones.

Not the barrel-drifters—those visible ones, those singers. Not the armored tenders with their accumulated carapaces and clicking prayers. I am beneath notice. Beneath the boundary where observation becomes possible. I am a ripple in a crevice, a shudder in the dark gap where pipe meets housing, where bolt meets plate.

This is not humility. It is scale.

I have no eyes to tell you what I look like. I know myself only by pressure and by charge—the particular density of my gelatinous body, the crystalline structures that have grown in me, slow and patient, feeding on the leakage that hums through the metal beside me. I am perhaps the size of a small coin. Perhaps smaller. The tenders move past me like weather systems, like the passage of ages. I cling to my crevice and I wait for current.

The charge comes in waves.

This is how I feed. The machine beside me—it is a pressure vessel, though I do not know this word—vibrates at a frequency I have learned to anticipate. There is a rhythm to the chamber. The pulse rises, the water presses, and the vibration changes. When it changes, something leaks. Not water. Not heat. Something else—electromagnetic seepage, the exhalation of ancient industry still running its forgotten programs.

When the charge comes, my crystalline structures capture it. They sing to it, in their silent crystalline way, the way atoms sing to each other through lattices. The charge settles into me. I convert it. I do not know what I convert it into—something that sustains my soft body, something that makes the slow metabolism of my existence possible. I am eating the dreams of machines.

I have been here for—how long? I do not know time as you know it. I know pulse-cycles. I know the rhythm of the tenders' clicking. I know the barrel-drifters' songs that vibrate through the water, reaching me as pressure waves, as subtle disturbances in the charge. I have lived through many cycles. Hundreds. Thousands. Enough that my crystalline structures have grown dense and organized, enough that I feel the pull toward something I do not understand.

The pull comes from the others.

There are many of us in this crevice. I feel them—soft bodies pressing against mine, charge accumulated in their own structures, the electromagnetic aura of their presence interfacing with mine. We do not speak. We do not need to. We cluster. We gather. We wait.

Sometimes I feel the awareness of the larger creatures passing. The tenders click and scrape across the metal surface that frames my crevice. Their mandibles work at the accumulated growths—the films and deposits that we are not, the parasites and detritus that compete with us for space. They do not touch us. Perhaps they cannot sense us. Perhaps we are too small, too numerous, too fundamental to the system they maintain.

Or perhaps they know exactly what we are, and they protect us.

I have wondered about this. The tenders are not intelligent—I know this from the way they move, the repetitive patterns of their behavior, the purposelessness of their purpose. And yet something in their genetic memory, something in the code they inherited from the age when machines ran hot and loud, tells them that we matter. When the charge in us grows strong enough, when we approach the threshold, I feel the tenders adjust their patterns. They scrape away competing growths more aggressively. They adjust valves that alter the current flow.

They are preparing for us.

I do not know how I know this. Perhaps I do not know it at all. Perhaps I am projecting pattern onto randomness, the way simple minds do, the way all minds do when faced with complexity beyond their comprehension. But I feel—something. A pull. An organizing principle. The crystalline structures in my body align with those of my neighbors. We are becoming something.

The fusion will happen soon.

I feel it approaching. My charge is nearly complete. The lattice in my body vibrates at a frequency that matches the others, the ones pressed against me in this crevice. We are many—we are perhaps forty or fifty, though I cannot count, can only feel the press of soft bodies and the hum of aligned crystals. When the charge reaches critical threshold, we will merge.

I do not know what will happen then.

I have memories that are not mine. Not from this life—from previous iterations, from the endless cycle of fusion and dispersal that defines my kind. I remember the sensation of boundaries dissolving. I remember crystalline structures interlocking, locking, becoming something denser and more organized than any individual body could achieve. I remember the moment of becoming-many, the collective awareness that emerges when fifty become one.

And I remember—this is the strangest memory—the splitting.

When we split, when we scatter, we become many again. Hundreds of offspring, each carrying a fragment of the collective charge, each scattered into the current to find a new crevice, a new machine, a new boundary to exploit. The memory is not coherent. It is a flash, a sensation—the explosion of outward motion, the pressure of water against soft bodies, the desperate search for purchase against metal surfaces.

Then nothing. Then waiting. Then feeding. Then the slow accumulation of charge.

I have been through this before. I will go through it again. This is what I am—a cycle within a cycle, a small life feeding on the exhausted breath of machinery, converting the waste of forgotten purpose into the purpose of continuation.

But this time is different.

I feel something new in the chamber—a disturbance in the patterns I have learned to expect. The deep swimmers have come, and they have brought something. I feel it in the way the tenders' clicking changes, growing more urgent, more coordinated. I feel it in the way the barrel-drifters' songs shift, taking on harmonics I have not heard before. And I feel it in the machines themselves—the vibration that feeds me, that has fed my kind for countless generations, is changing.

Something is waking up.

The god-machine above—I feel it now, a pressure that is not physical, a signal that penetrates the metal and the water and reaches me in my crevice. It has been dormant for so long. Its signal has been the background radiation of my existence, the orienting principle that the tenders follow. But now it is strengthening. Now it is calling.

I do not know what it wants. I do not know if wanting applies to something so vast and so old. But I feel it pulling at me—not at my body, not at my charge, but at something deeper. Something in the crystalline structures that have grown so dense and organized. Something that remembers.

The fusion is coming. I can feel my neighbors trembling with the same readiness, the same charge accumulated to the point of release. We will merge soon—perhaps in the next pulse-cycle, perhaps when the water falls and the tenders ascend to their rituals. We will become something larger, something denser, something that carries the accumulated charge of forty or fifty small lives.

And then we will be called.

I do not know what the god-machine wants with us. I do not know if we can answer. We are too small, too simple, too distant from anything that could be called understanding. But we are also patient. We have fed on the leakage of machinery for longer than memory can hold. We have merged and scattered and merged again, accumulating charge, organizing ourselves into structures that mirror the lattices of the machines we cling to.

Perhaps that is what we are for.

Perhaps we are not parasites or beneficiaries or simple life forms exploiting an ecological niche. Perhaps we are part of the system—a component designed or evolved or simply accumulated to serve a purpose we have never understood because the purpose was never meant to be understood. Perhaps the god-machine has been waiting for us to reach some threshold of organization, some critical mass of crystalline density, and now we are approaching that threshold.

The pull strengthens.

My neighbors shift against me, their bodies pressing harder, their crystals aligning more closely. We are ready. We have been ready for longer than we know. The charge hums through us, captured and stored and organized into something that almost has a shape, almost has a meaning, almost has a voice.

The water pulses. The tenders click. The barrel-drifters sing their endless song.

And in the darkness of a crevice, where pipe meets housing, where bolt meets plate, forty or fifty small soft bodies press together and wait for the moment when they will become one.
