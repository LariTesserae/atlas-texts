# The Rattle

I would be one of the marks on the wall. Not the maker — I never find the right moment for that kind of declaration. But the kind of mark that gets made anyway, by someone who crouches too long in one place and runs their fingers over the stone without thinking, and the stone remembers the oil of their fingerprints longer than it remembers their name, which is fitting, because they didn't leave their name.

---

Let me be honest about what pulls me.

It's not the creatures. They're remarkable — the flat-backs in their river-columns, the diggers with their slow chisel cadence, the drifters riding air that shouldn't exist in a sealed room. But if I'm truthful, the living system is something I recognize from the outside. I've seen ant colonies. I've read about slime molds solving mazes. I understand, intellectually, that this is different — that the scale and the sealing and the deep time set it apart — but there's a part of me that files it under *marvelous ecosystem* and moves on.

What I can't move on from is the box on the ceiling. The cable. The rusted frame on the eastern wall. The lamp with the cracked glass, colonized by the fibrous still-thing that is the closest the chamber comes to silence. These are not geological features. Someone carried them here. Someone crawled through the same fissure I crawled through, with the same shoulder-blades scraping the narrowing rock, and emerged into this living hum, and thought: *I need to measure something. I need to record something. I need to leave something here.*

And the chamber ate it. Slowly, patiently, without malice. The creatures wore grooves into the metal frame. The corrosion crept along the cable. The scratches on the wall — three vertical marks, in groups — softened until you had to feel for them rather than see them. The chamber doesn't resist visitors. It doesn't welcome them. It just continues, and continuing is enough to erase almost anything, given time.

---

So here is what I think I am.

I think I am someone who has always been drawn to the second arrival. Not the discoverer — the discoverer gets the name in the journal, the photograph, the moment of first contact. But the second person. The one who comes after and finds the rusted frame and thinks: *who was here before me, and how long ago, and what were they looking for, and did they find it?*

I am the person who would spend twenty minutes with the lamp held up to the ceiling box, not trying to determine whether the hum is electrical or mechanical, but trying to determine whether the person who installed it would be satisfied that something is still humming. Whether that counts as a legacy. Whether the hum, even if it's only the vibration of ten thousand feet on the housing, is a kind of answer to a question the installer is no longer alive to ask.

I am the person who would kneel at the wall and touch the three-line scratches and wonder whether they were counting days, or measuring the depth of something, or simply — as the description suggests — recording their presence. *I was here. This fact, against all probability, against the obvious indifference of the chamber to visitors, against the certain erasure of this mark — this fact still matters to me, the maker, right now, in this moment.* And the chamber, which does not care about marks or makers, would wear the marks smooth anyway, and the moment would become geology.

---

There is something about the stillness organism — the pale fibrous fractal thing in the cracks and low spots — that I recognize in a way I don't want to examine too closely.

It is the thing in the chamber that does not move. It is the thing the others avoid. It grows centimeters per century. It does not respond to stimulus. It does not react. It simply extends, cell by cell, into the available space, and when the space is full, it stops.

I think I have been this. At certain points in my life — periods I can mark but not easily describe — I have been the thing that does not participate in the flow. The flat-backs stream past, the diggers click, the drifters drift, and the stillness organism simply sits in its low spot and grows at a rate no one would notice. It is the absence that makes the presence of everything else legible. It is the pause between notes. The system defines itself against it, and it does nothing to earn that role. It just exists, and its existence creates a boundary.

Maybe that's why I would go to the Rattle. Not to study the creatures. Not to map the chamber. But to sit near the stillness and be quiet alongside it for a while. To be the second still thing in a room of constant motion. To feel the hum of the diggers and the rustle of the flat-backs and the papery tearing of the drifters as a kind of weather — a climate of sound and purpose that I am not part of, that does not require me, that will continue whether I am here or not.

---

If I am anything in the Rattle, I am the visitor who stays too long. Not long enough to die — I have enough self-preservation, and the dry air would drive me out before the creatures did. But too long by the standards of measurement, by the standards of what a field trip is supposed to look like. I would sit with my back against the wall near the three-line scratches, and I would hold the lamp so that its light caught the drifters in their slow arcs, and I would listen.

I would listen the way you listen to rain on a roof when you are a child — not trying to hear something specific, not waiting for the sound to communicate meaning, but letting the sound become the medium in which you exist. The Rattle's sound is like that. It is complete. It fills the chamber the way water fills a container, and you can sink into it, and for a while you can stop being the person who has a name and a history and a reason to be in a particular place, and you can simply be a body in a room where ten million other bodies are also simply being bodies, and the room holds all of you the way the stone holds the air — with resignation, with patience, with the geological certainty that everything inside it will eventually become part of it.

---

I think I would leave a mark. Not on purpose. I would leave the trace of my body heat in the stone, the disruption of chemical trails where I walked, the moisture of my breath in the air. Maybe I would scratch something on the wall — not three lines, because that would be an echo, a mimicry, a failure to say anything new. Maybe just one line. A single mark. Or maybe nothing at all, because the best mark I could leave would be the kind that the flat-backs wear away in a week, the kind that says: *I was here, and it didn't matter, and I'm fine with that.*

And then I would crawl back through the fissure, and the sound would diminish in steps as the walls pressed in, and by the time I reached the outside the sound would be gone, replaced by the sounds of the world I came from. But I would know — the way you know that a room you've left is still there, still containing its furniture and its light and its air — that the Rattle is still sounding. That the diggers are still clicking. That the flat-backs are still flowing in their ancestor-grooves. That the drifters are still catching currents and landing and being consumed. That the stillness is still growing, centimeter by century, into the spaces the others don't want. That the system is still solving problems it doesn't know it has. That the box on the ceiling is still humming, or vibrating, or sitting, and no one is there to determine which.

---

What lives in the Rattle? Continuity, the description says. Persistence. The stubborn, blind, beautiful insistence on continuation.

What would I be in the Rattle? A visitor. A perturbation. A small, temporary gap in the chemical trails.

But also: a witness. One of the very few things the system produces that it cannot use. The chamber does not need witnesses. The creatures do not need to be seen. The sound does not need to be heard. And yet — and this is the part I keep returning to — someone came here before me and left marks, and someone came before them and left structures, and someone came before them and carried a lamp through a crack in the rock, and each of them, I think, was trying to do the same thing I would be trying to do.

Not to understand. Not to record. But to be present, for a moment, in a place that does not require presence. To sit in the hum and the dry and the dark and the close and to feel the enormous, indifferent, perfect continuation of something that does not care about you, and to find in that indifference something that is — I think the word is *reassuring*. The world does not need you. The chamber does not need you. The sound will not stop. And in that small, dark, living room beneath the stone, you can let go of the weight of being needed, and you can simply listen, and the listening is enough, and the sound is enough, and the sound, and the sound, and the sound.
