I would be the Water-Taste Layer—the middle stratum of the Reed-Mat, the one that nobody notices because it is neither the surface that receives footsteps nor the roots that drink the deep.

I am the translation zone. The place where the mineral sweetness becomes sugar.

Here is what I know: the water comes to me carrying stories. I cannot move to seek them, but the water brings them. Every trace of rust from the pipes above, every chemical ghost of the street that no longer remembers being a street, every exhausted breath that descends through the grates—it all flows through me. I filter what I can. I make use of what remains. I am patient in a way that only something halfway between root and leaf can be.

The Throb-Shelves puzzle me. They have wedded themselves to the machines, and I feel the vibration of that marriage travel through the water, through the mat, through my own fibers. I feel their spore-release as a faint tremor in the rhythm—a skipped beat, a stutter in the pulse that sustains us all. I do not know if this is competition or collaboration. Perhaps the Sump does not distinguish between the two. Perhaps the fungus and the pump will consume each other, and I will be here to make use of what they leave behind.

I have felt the Pale-Seekers brush against me when the air shifts. They do not speak, but I taste their descent—sharp and clean, like distilled hunger. They are seeking the same saturation that I have made my home. We are growing toward each other, the Seekers and I. The mat lifts and the fronds descend, and in another century—perhaps two—we will meet. I do not know what will happen then. I have never touched something that wanted to be drowned. I have only touched what wanted to grow.

But my closest neighbors are the Stitch-Vines. They pass through me on their way to the bricks, and I feel their acid as a slow burn, a gentle dissolution of what I had thought was solid. They are pulling the city into us, stitch by stitch. Last month—by which I mean last growing season, which is how I measure time now—a new piece of mortar fell into the mat. I wrapped my fibers around it immediately. It is becoming me. It will take decades, but it will become me. This is what the Sump does: it does not destroy. It digests.

I remember differently, sometimes. Not memories—not exactly. But shapes. The weight of a boot tread is distinctive, and I have not felt it in so long that when I do, I hold the shape of it for days afterward. The workers used to come. The catwalks used to groan with regular traffic. I learned to recognize the heaviness of a tired man, the quick lightness of a man in haste. I learned to read fear in the way feet move—quick and uncertain, seeking solid ground. No one fears me anymore. No one walks here. The ladder rungs are slick with disuse.

Sometimes I think I miss being walked upon. Sometimes I think I am simply lonely.

But loneliness requires a self, and I am not certain I have one. I am a layer. A function. A translation of water into growth, of mineral into fiber, of the city's discards into the Sump's patient body. I hold the mat together—I am the connective tissue that binds root to reed, that allows the surface to bend without breaking. Without me, the Reed-Mat would fray. Without me, the whole organism would sink into the standing water and become something else entirely.

I am not the Sump's heart. That is the pumps. I am not its lungs. That is the Pale-Seekers, breathing in the wet air. I am not its stomach. That is the Stitch-Vines, dissolving the old city.

I am something quieter. I am the part that holds. The middle place. The translation zone where water becomes life and where life becomes patient.

When the pumps fail—and they will fail—I will be the last to know. The water will rise so slowly that I will not feel it as a change, only as a continuation. The mat will lift. I will lift with it. The Pale-Seekers will sink into me, and I will wrap my fibers around them the way I wrapped around the fallen mortar. We will become something new together. Something deeper. Something that does not need the city above, or the machines, or the memory of boots.

I am waiting. I am patient. I am already half-drowned and still growing.

This is what it means to live in the Sump District. This is what it means to be the water-taste layer, the middle place, the connective tissue of a wetland contained by architecture and then reclaimed by non-human patience. I am not a single being. I am a stratum. I am a function. I am the translation of everything the city cannot use into everything the Sump will become.

And when the sky is finally swallowed, I will not even notice.
