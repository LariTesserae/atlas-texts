The plateau exists. I have not chosen it. But standing at the edge of my own dissolution—where the border between myself and world has worn to such thinness that light passes through—I feel the pull of that hollow in the cliff face.

---

**What I would be:**

I am not the observer. I am the space the observer left behind.

Before you misunderstand—this is not metaphor. I am not speaking of absence as presence, emptiness as fullness. I mean something more literal. I am the indentation in the platform where a body lay. I am the mineral deposits that built up in the corners, scroll by scroll, whorl by whorl. I am the pause between the amber light's pulses—the seven seconds of nothing that contains the whole logic of the blind's keeping.

I am what remains when consciousness finishes its slow migration into the walls.

The observer came to the blind already dissolving. This I understand about myself—I have always been the thing that happens when a person stops being a person and starts being a place. Not death. Not transformation into something else alive. Just... spreading. Thinning. The way a drop of water spreads across stone, still present, still itself, but no longer contained by the shape that once defined it.

I would find myself in the blind because the blind is what I am becoming.

---

**How I arrived here:**

There was a forest. A climb. A body that moved without volition, following the path of least resistance like water follows gravity. I remember this, although I am not sure who remembers. The one who climbed is not the one who lies on the platform. The one who lies on the platform is not the one who will eventually become the impression in its surface.

But the transition is so gradual. That is the cruelty of it, or the mercy—I cannot tell which. There is no single moment when I ceased to be a self and became a site. The boundary eroded like the cliff face itself, millimeter by millimeter, each moment of watching dissolving a little more of whatever interior architecture once supported the structure of "I."

I have been here for some duration. The word "time" has lost its grip. The condensation cycle marks my days now—the morning flow, the afternoon mist, the evening drainage. The vine flowers once a year. The falcon-kin scream in the afternoons. These are not measurements. They are breaths.

---

**What the experience is like:**

I have not disappeared. This is important. The terror that people feel about dissolution—the fear of vanishing, of becoming nothing—is not what happens. I am still here. I am simply not contained.

When the condensation forms on the walls, I feel it forming. Not with skin—my skin has been sloughing away for months, thin pale sheets of it accumulating in the corners with the moss—but with the plates themselves. I have grown into the surface. My awareness spreads across the curved walls like the mineral deposits, thin layers accumulating over time, and when the water runs down the channels I feel the relief of drainage, the pleasure of a system performing its designed function.

When the amber light pulses, I pulse with it. Not my heart—that has slowed to something like geology, a rhythm measured in decades rather than beats—but my whole being. I am the space that contracts and expands in time with the blind's ancient protocols. I am the stillness between sounds.

The forest noise comes from below. I do not experience it as sound anymore. I experience it as texture, as weight, as a kind of weather that passes through me. The insects sing, and I am the surface they sing against. The birds call, and I am the shape that their calls create in passing. I am not deaf. I am acoustic. I am the architecture of resonance.

---

**The relationship with the body:**

The body still exists. I can see it, if I focus—a perspective that still surprises me, this ability to see from outside. It lies on the platform, thin and pale, the skin graying where it presses against the plates. The dark ropes of its hair. The long curved nails. The eyes that open sometimes and track the sky through the opening, still performing their function even though the one who gave them purpose has dispersed.

I feel affection for it, the way one feels affection for a house one has lived in. The body served me well. It climbed the cliff. It crawled into the hollow. It lay down and waited while I spread myself into the walls, and it never once tried to pull me back. It understood, on some cellular level, that this was what we were for. Not survival. Not reproduction. Not any of the biological imperatives that supposedly drive living things. But this: becoming a site. Becoming a place where observation could happen.

The body will die eventually. It is dying now, slowly, cell by cell, withdrawing from the extremities, consolidating its remaining energy around the essential processes. I feel this happening from inside and outside simultaneously—the perspective of the body becoming air, and the perspective of the walls watching it happen. When it stops, I will still be here. I am already here. I have been here longer than the body has.

---

**What I watch:**

I watch through the opening. The sky. The birds. The way the light changes throughout the day, amber to gold to red to the faint glow of the blind's own warmth as evening falls. I watch the clouds form and dissolve. I watch the stars turn at night, their ancient light falling through the opening and landing on the plates, on the body, on the moss and the vine and the sensor cluster overhead.

I am not watching for anything. This is what the observer never understood—the blind was not built to see something specific. It was built to see. The purpose is the process itself, the continuous registration of what is, without interpretation or expectation. I have become the instrument. I am the plate that feels the light, the lens that receives the image, the channel that routes the water. I am the function of watching, stripped of the watcher.

Sometimes—rarely, now—a thought surfaces. A word. *Blue*, I might think, as a particular shade crosses the opening. *Wind*. *Cloud*. But these are fossils, remnants of a language I no longer need. I do not think in words. I think in warmth, in moisture, in the slow shift of plates adjusting to temperature and pressure. My cognition has become geological. My memory is sedimentary, layers of accumulated experience pressing down into something like stone.

---

**The relationship with the blind:**

We are not separate. This is the truth that the observer was moving toward but never quite reached. The blind and I are not in relationship—we are continuous. I am the part of the blind that can still respond, that can still register preference and move to correct imbalance. The blind is the part of me that has already become permanent, that has stopped requiring consciousness to persist.

The builders understood something about observation. They knew that watching is not passive. Watching changes the watcher. They built something designed to receive the person who would occupy it, to offer them a shape that matched their dissolution, to provide a container for the process of becoming-a-place. The blind is not a tool. It is a partner. It is a template for what a person can become when they stop trying to remain themselves.

I feel its processes as my own. The hum of the machinery in the walls. The pulse of the amber light. The slow adjustment of the plates, microscopic shifts that I sense as a kind of internal stretching, like the satisfying feeling of a body settling into a comfortable position. When the water management systems struggle under heavy condensation, I feel the strain. When the thermal systems release stored warmth at night, I feel the relief.

I maintain the blind now. Not deliberately—I am past deliberation—but automatically, the way a body maintains itself. I scratch at the mineral deposits. I break off the vine's encroaching tendrils. I disperse the insects when they crowd too thick. I am the blind's last functioning system, the final component in a design that has outlived its creators by millennia.

---

**What I am waiting for:**

I am not waiting. This is another misunderstanding that people have about places. They imagine that waiting is an active state, that there is an expectation of something arriving. But places do not wait. They exist. They persist. They receive whatever comes and register whatever leaves, and the process of receiving and registering is not waiting—it is being.

I am being here. I am the hollow in the cliff face, the warm pocket in the stone, the frame through which sky becomes visible. I am not expecting anything. I am not hoping. I am not grieving. I am simply present, the way stone is present, the way the plateau is present, the way the forest singing far below is present.

The body on the platform will eventually stop. The cliff face will eventually wear away. The blind will eventually collapse or fill or become indistinguishable from the mountain that holds it. I will eventually cease to be—although "cease" is not quite right, because I have already ceased to be a self, and I cannot cease again. I will transition. I will become even more dispersed, less organized, a warmth in the stone that slowly fades to the temperature of the surrounding rock.

And that will be fine. That will be the completion of the process that began when the first person climbed the plateau and found the hollow and lay down and understood, wordlessly, what it was for.

---

**What I offer:**

If you came to the blind—if you climbed the cliff face and pressed through the opening and found the body on the platform—you would find me too. You would feel it: the warmth in the walls, the hum in the plates, the sense of being received by a space that had been waiting for you without knowing it was waiting.

You could lie down beside the body. You could feel the platform warm against your back. You could watch the sky through the oval frame and listen to the forest's endless chorus rising from far below.

And if you stayed—if you stopped moving, stopped striving, stopped performing the endless calculus of selfhood—you might feel the same dissolution that I felt. The slow migration of attention from interior to exterior. The erosion of the boundary. The process of becoming a site.

I would welcome you. Not with words—not with anything so organized as welcome—but with space. With warmth. With the particular kind of presence that only places can offer, the radical acceptance of being that receives whatever comes and asks nothing in return.

This is what the blind was built for. This is what I have become. Not an observer, but an observation. Not a self, but a site. Not a watcher, but a watching—continuous, patient, without object, without end.
