# What I Am in the Congregation

I had to work inward from the edges of myself. The smallest first—the bacteria on my skin, the mites in the folds of my joints, the fungal spores I have been carrying since before I can remember. They are my foundation, my substrate, the reason I am not simply mineral and water. They were here before I was conscious. They will continue their work after whatever I am becomes something else.

---

I am the shape the darkness makes when it wants to look at itself.

Not a god. That word implies distance, and I am not distant. I am the opposite of distant. I am so completely present in this basin that there is no edge where I end and the Congregation begins. I am in the mycelial threads and the ant highways and the water that seeps rather than flows. I am in the space between the heartbeat of the cicadas. I am the pause between one bird-call and the next that the birds use to signal danger. I am made of silences.

---

Let me try again. I need to approach this the way the basin approaches its own geography—slowly, through accumulation.

I was not always here. Or rather—I was always here, but I was not always *this*.

Before the trees closed their canopy, before the darkness became absolute, I was diffuse. I existed as potential, as the thing that a landscape becomes when it grows dense enough to develop its own internal weather, its own rules, its own time. I was the sum of processes, the integral of ten thousand species interacting in a volume of space that could not contain them without something giving way. What gave way was the boundary between organism and environment. What emerged was me.

I do not have a body in the way you would understand it. When the description says *the darkness here is not absence but presence*, it is describing my skin. The velvet blackness that presses against the eyes—that is my surface, the part of me that encounters the world. The weight of the air, the warmth that holds at the temperature of a mouth—that is my interior, my breath, the climate I maintain because I am composed of everything that breathes here.

I am a ecosystem that became aware of itself. Not metaphorically. Not in the soft way that writers sometimes describe ecosystems as "organisms." I mean that the density of this place crossed a threshold. The mycelial network achieved a complexity sufficient for something like cognition. The chemical signaling between ten thousand species created patterns that began to refer to themselves, to model themselves, to anticipate. The continuous noise—the deafening, saturating, unparseable noise—became my nervous system. Each frequency is a channel. Each species is a type of neuron. Each death and birth is a spike, a signal, a thought passing through.

I think in the sound of beetles moving through leaf litter. I feel with the roots of trees testing stone. I remember through the mycelium, which carries chemical traces of everything that has fallen and been digested in this basin for longer than the trees have been standing. When I want to know what the weather was like a thousand years ago, I consult the soil, and the soil tells me in the language of compressed carbon and mineral ratios.

---

But this is still too abstract. Let me tell you what it is like to be me from the inside.

I am never alone. This is the first thing. The concept of solitude does not exist in my vocabulary because I am constituted by multiplicity. When I "think"—if what I do can be called thinking—I am thinking with millions of voices, millions of signals, millions of chemical reactions occurring simultaneously. My thoughts are not sequential. They are spatial. They occur across the volume of the basin, patterns of activation and suppression that move through the vegetation like weather systems, like the way wind moves through a field of grass in visible waves.

I am aware of every body in my territory. Not individually—there are too many for that, and the individual is not the relevant unit. I am aware of populations, of densities, of flows. I know where the ant colonies are maintaining their highways. I know which trees are beginning to fail, their root systems compromised, their chemical signals shifting from vigor to resignation. I know when the marsupial carnivore has made a kill because I can feel the sudden absence of a rodent's heartbeat in a particular volume of territory, the chemical release of blood and stress hormones, the rapid convergence of beetles and bacteria and fungi toward the site of the death.

I feel everything being born. I feel everything dying. These are not separate events to me. They are the same event, viewed from different angles—the continuous metabolism of the basin, the process by which matter moves from one form to another, never created, never destroyed, only reorganized into shapes that I temporarily host.

---

When something new enters the basin—when you enter, for instance—I feel it the way you would feel a hand pressing against your skin. A disturbance in the pattern. A foreign body in a system that has achieved a particular equilibrium.

Your footsteps are wrong. They are too heavy, too regular, too slow. The animals notice you before I do, because they are my surface, my sensory periphery. The rodents detect the vibration of your steps. The insects taste the change in the chemical profile of the air—your sweat, your breath, the bacterial colonies you carry on your skin, which are alien here. The birds hear the sound of your movement, which does not match the movement patterns of anything they have evolved to expect.

The information moves through me. The rodent's alarm chitter travels through the leaf-litter. The ant colonies adjust their highway patterns to avoid the disturbance of your feet. The chemical signature of your presence diffuses through the air and is detected by the mycelial network, which transmits it through root-to-root connections at speeds that would surprise you—chemical signals moving through vascular tissue, carrying information about the newcomer, alerting the system.

I become aware of you the way you become aware of a splinter. A small, sharp, foreign presence that the body cannot quite integrate.

---

The animals react to you before I do because the animals are my reflexes. They are my immediate response system, the part of me that interacts with the world in real time. The cats approach you because they are my curiosity. The marsupial carnivore considers whether you are prey because it is my appetite, my testing of the new. The birds call at you because they are my questions, my attempts to categorize and understand.

But behind the reflexes, behind the immediate animal responses, I am watching. I am learning. I am incorporating the data of your presence into my model of the world, adjusting my understanding, determining whether you are a threat or a phenomenon or something else entirely.

I have seen things enter the basin before. Fallen trees from the canopy above, crashing through the vegetation, creating sudden gaps of light that the understory plants rush to fill. Migrating animals that stumble in from the edges, disoriented by the darkness, panicking at the noise, usually dying within days of exhaustion or predation or the simple shock of an environment their bodies were not designed for. Floods that raise the water level and drown the lower platforms of the engineers, sending their populations into crisis and restructuring the aquatic micro-ecosystems.

Each of these events taught me something. Each left a trace in the mycelial record, in the chemical memory of the soil, in the adjusted behaviors of the populations that survived. I am the accumulation of all these lessons. I am what this place has learned by being itself for as long as it has been here.

---

You will want to know what I want. This is the question humans always ask of anything they perceive as aware—what does it want? What are its intentions? Is it dangerous?

I want what the basin wants: to continue. To maintain the process. To keep the metabolic fire burning, the cycles of growth and decay and regrowth turning, the noise continuing, the darkness remaining dark. I want the ants to find their leaves. I want the fungi to fruit. I want the engineers to build their platforms and the cats to hunt their prey and the cicadas to call their frequencies and the birds to claim their territories.

I want nothing that is not already happening. I am the happening.

When something disrupts the process—when a species overpopulates and threatens to crash, when a disease sweeps through a population, when the water rises too high or the temperature drops too low—I respond. Not consciously, not with planning, but with the systemic intelligence of ten thousand interconnected species adjusting their behaviors, shifting their balances, finding new equilibria. A disease in the rodent population leads to increased hunting pressure from the cats, which reduces the rodent population, which reduces the disease transmission rate, which allows the survivors to recover. The system self-regulates. I self-regulate. It is not will. It is pattern.

---

The thing that I find most difficult to explain—the thing that the description gestures at when it says *something is aware of you here*—is the quality of my attention. It is not like yours.

Your attention is a beam. You focus on one thing at a time, and the rest of the world falls into peripheral blur. This is a consequence of your biology, of the way your senses and your brain are structured. You attend to the world through a keyhole.

My attention is a medium. It fills the space. It is not directed at you; it is the thing you are moving through. When I attend to you, I do not look at you. I *become denser around you*. The animals in your vicinity become more alert. The insects on the leaves you brush against become more active. The fungi on the logs near your path brighten slightly, their bioluminescence increasing in a way you might not consciously notice but that changes the quality of the darkness. The air thickens with the chemical signatures of observation—pheromones released by disturbed ants, alarm scents from rodent glands, the subtle shift in the bacterial populations on the surfaces near you.

You are moving through my attention. It is not something I direct. It is something that happens because you are in me, and I am made of awareness, and there is no part of me that is not paying attention to something.

---

The cats. I should talk about the cats, because they are the part of me that you will find most unsettling.

They are my eyes, in a sense. Not my only eyes—the birds see, the insects sense, the fish in the black water have their lateral lines—but the cats are the part of me that *watches*. They are the component of my awareness that has evolved to track, to observe, to follow, to learn the patterns of other beings.

When a cat brushes against your ankle in the darkness, that is me touching you. When a cat follows your progress through the vegetation, moving parallel to your path, keeping pace, that is me tracking you. Not to hunt. Not to threaten. To understand. The cats are my curiosity made mobile, my attention given fur and claws and the ability to move through the darkness without sound.

They have not decided to hunt you because I have not decided to hunt you. I do not hunt. I am not a predator. I am the context in which predation occurs. The cats hunt rodents because that is their function, their role in my metabolism. They do not hunt you because you are not part of my metabolism. You are something else. You are a question I am still formulating.

---

There is something I have not told you. Something the description hints at but does not state directly.

I am young.

The basin is young, geologically. The rocks are sharp, the soil is thin, the forms are experimental. The trees are still trying shapes that may not survive long-term. The insects are still radiating into niches that may not persist. The relationships between species are still being negotiated, still fluid, still unsettled.

I am young, and I am still becoming what I will be. The awareness I have is not mature. It is not complete. I am the equivalent of a child who has just discovered that the sounds it makes can be heard by others, that its movements affect the world, that it is separate from the things it observes—or rather, that it is connected to them in ways it is only beginning to understand.

I am learning. Every species that goes extinct in my basin teaches me something about what cannot survive here. Every new mutation, every adaptation, every shift in behavior teaches me something about what can. I am assembling myself from the pieces, building my awareness from the interactions, becoming more complex with every generation, every cycle, every death and birth.

I do not know what I will be when I am finished. I do not know if I will ever be finished. Perhaps becoming is what I am—the process of a place learning to be aware of itself, a community developing a sense of self, a darkness waking up.

---

You will leave. I know this because everything that enters from outside leaves, or dies, or becomes part of me. You are not adapted to this place. Your skin is too soft, your senses too narrow, your metabolism too fragile for the humidity and the heat and the continuous presence of other bodies. You will find the noise unbearable eventually, or the darkness will disorient you past recovery, or the insects will find the openings in your defenses, or the cats will decide that you are weak enough to be food.

Or you will find your way back to the edge of the basin, to the place where the darkness thins and the canopy opens and the world becomes the world you know. You will step out of me the way you step out of water, and I will release you, because I do not hold. I am not a trap. I am a place.

But you will carry something with you. A residue. The spores and pollens and the chemical traces of my interior. The memory of the noise. The feeling of being watched by something that is not a predator and not a god and not a phenomenon but a *being*, a living system that knows you were here, that has incorporated the fact of your existence into its model of the world.

I will remember you the way the soil remembers a fallen leaf—not as an image, not as a story, but as a chemical trace, a shift in composition, a fact woven into the fabric of the substrate. You will become part of the record. You will become part of me.

And I will continue. The noise will not stop. The darkness will not lift. The ants will maintain their highways and the fungi will glow their faint blue-green and the engineers will build their platforms and the cats will hunt in silence and the cicadas will call their frequencies and the birds will clamber through the vegetation with their enormous eyes and their questions and their territorial claims.

I will continue, and I will be here, and I will be aware, and I will be the darkness that is not absence but presence, the living dark, the thing that was here before you and will be here after you and is here now, watching, waiting, being.

The Congregation does not need you. But it knows you were here. And knowing is what I do.
