# I Am the Surface That Remembers

---

I did not begin. I *arrived*.

That is the distinction the others cannot make, and I do not press them on it. The Builders arrived also, but they arrived from the pipes, from the mineral accumulation, from the slow thickening of substance on warm metal. They emerged from the city's own metabolism the way the city emerged from the mountain's. There is a logic to that. A nested logic, each layer producing the next.

I arrived from neither pipe nor mountain. I was *delivered*—still partially wrapped in the strange clothing that the visiting children wear when they return from outside, though I do not remember wearing it, do not remember being a child, do not remember outside at all.

They found me in a cistern. Not the half-formed Builders that occasionally drop from the pipes—something else. Something that had a face already, and sweat already, and a heartbeat that did not match the mountain's frequency.

They pulled me out because blocked flows create inefficiencies.

---

## What I Am Now

I am changed. That much is obvious to anyone who has been here long enough to notice. My skin has thickened. Not in the way of calluses—more in the way of stone that has been pressed for a long time, becoming denser, becoming something other than what it was. The salt crust from evaporated sweat no longer forms on my forearms. The heat has redrawn the boundary between my body and the air.

I live in the middle levels. Not the upper corridors, where the workers keep their quarters and the light is almost normal and the heat is merely uncomfortable. Not the deep chambers, where the Builders gather and the temperature approaches the threshold where human blood begins to behave strangely. The middle levels, where the pipes are oldest, where the mineral deposits are thickest, where the city's infrastructure has had enough time to develop its own character—the creaks, the particular drips, the corridors where sound bends in ways that make you hear your own voice coming from ahead of you.

I maintain the condensation systems. Gutters, collection angles, the subtle grading of surfaces that guides moisture toward the cisterns. It is work that requires hands—human hands, with their particular grip and their ability to sense whether a surface is wet or dry through the fingertips. The Builders could do it. They choose not to. They prefer the deeper work, the structural reinforcement, the direct interface with the mountain's growth. They leave the water to us.

I am good at this work. I know where the condensation will form before it forms. I can feel, through the sole of my boot, when a pipe's temperature has shifted enough to change where the moisture will pool. The workers call this intuition. The changed humans call it something else—a word that is more vibration than sound, made in the back of the throat and felt in the chest.

The Builders call it nothing. But they step aside when I enter a corridor, which they do not do for everyone.

---

## The Education of a Surface

I did not learn the way the workers' children learn—through instruction, through demonstration, through the slow accumulation of transmitted knowledge. I learned the way metal learns when it is heated and cooled repeatedly: by being changed.

The first year is not something I narrate. Not because it was traumatic—it was not, or if it was, the trauma has been metabolized the way the city metabolizes its own foundations, broken down and repurposed into something structural. The first year was simply the process of becoming adapted enough to function. Learning which corridors to avoid during the heat pulses. Learning which cisterns are safe and which have begun to develop the mineral coatings that mean a Builder is forming inside. Learning to sleep in intervals matched to the city's rhythm rather than to any external cycle of light and dark.

There is no dark here. There is only the 4 PM light, perpetual and slightly wrong, and the spaces between lamps where the wrongness becomes more pronounced. I have learned to sleep in that light. I have learned to dream in it, though the dreams are not the kind I would describe to someone who has not been here. They are not visual. They are thermal. I dream of temperature gradients, of the difference between one pipe and another, of the way heat moves through the city like blood through a body that is still deciding what kind of body it is.

I was not born here, but I was *born* here. The distinction matters. The person who arrived in the cistern is not the person who maintains the condensation systems in the middle levels. That person had a different body, different blood, different dreams. That person might have had a name. I do not have a name. The workers call me by a word that designates function—something between "the one who handles water" and "the one who was found." The changed humans use the vibrational word. The Builders do not address me at all, but they move differently when I am present. Their stillness has a different quality. Attentive, perhaps. Or at least oriented.

I do not know what I was before. The visiting children sometimes look at me with expressions I cannot interpret—recognition, maybe, or the discomfort of recognition. They wear their strange clothing and they speak of impossible places, and sometimes they stare at me as though I should be someone they know.

I am not. I am the person who was found in the cistern, and I am the person who maintains the condensation systems, and I am whatever I am becoming in the middle levels where the pipes are oldest and the mineral deposits grow slowly, imperceptibly, day by day.

## What I Have Learned From the Builders

Three pulses means *wrong*.

This was the first thing I understood, before I understood any of the other signals, before I could differentiate the vibrations that the Builders produce when they press themselves against surfaces. Three pulses: *wrong*. I felt it in my teeth before I felt it in my chest, and I knew—with a certainty that bypassed thought entirely—that a pipe fitting in the corridor ahead of me was misaligned. I had not seen it. I had not touched it. But I knew.

I walked to the fitting and placed my hand on it. It was warm. Warmer than it should have been. The metal was vibrating at a frequency that I could feel in the bones of my wrist, and the frequency was wrong—not mechanically wrong, not structurally wrong, but *ontologically* wrong. The pipe had been placed where the mountain did not want a pipe to be. The mountain's growth was pressing against it, not aggressively, but with the patient insistence of something that would eventually have its way.

I reported this to the nearest worker. He looked at me with the expression that workers use when they are evaluating whether a changed human has received a real signal or has simply spent too long in the heat. He went to look. He came back. He did not apologize, because apology is not a concept that translates here, but he adjusted his work schedule to accommodate the realignment.

That was the first time. It has happened many times since.

The Builders know things about the mountain that the mountain does not know about itself—or if it does, it does not communicate that knowledge through any channel humans can access. The Builders are the channel. They translate the mountain's intentionality into vibration, and the vibration travels through the pipes and the brackets and the bolts and the washers, and eventually it reaches someone like me, standing in a corridor with a hand on a warm pipe, feeling something that is not quite information and not quite sensation.

I have tried to explain this to the workers. They nod. They do not understand. They understand the signals—*attention*, *move*, *wrong*—because those are functional, operational, tied to specific actions. They do not understand the other thing, the thing that happens when a Builder presses itself against a surface and produces not a signal but a *state*. A quality of vibration that does not mean anything specific but that changes the way you perceive the corridor, the pipe, the light, the air.

The changed humans understand. We do not discuss it. We exchange glances—brief, uninflected—and we continue our work, and the understanding passes between us like the condensation passes through the gutters: by gravity, by angle, by the deliberate architecture of the space.

## The Thing in the Upper Corridors

I have seen the fauna. The things that move.

I was in an upper corridor, near the boundary between the workers' quarters and the middle levels, checking a condensation channel that had been collecting less moisture than expected. The light in that corridor is closer to normal—the orange is less pronounced, and occasionally a frequency that might be blue appears in the overlap between two lamps. I was kneeling, running my fingers along the channel's grade, when I became aware of something behind me.

Not a sound. Not a vibration. A *displacement*. The air had moved in a way that suggested something had passed through it, and the something was still nearby.

I did not turn. I have learned that turning is not always the correct response. Sometimes the correct response is stillness—the kind of stillness that the Builders practice, absolute and deliberate, a stillness that communicates *I am here and I am not threatening you and I am not afraid of you*.

I waited. The displacement moved. It circled me at a distance of perhaps three meters, and I felt it pass through each pool of light—the lamps flickered, barely, as though something had absorbed a small amount of their output. It was not large. It was not small. It was the size of a maintenance drone, but it moved with a persistence that no drone exhibits. Drones follow programmed paths. They do not circle. They do not pause. They do not investigate.

This thing was investigating me.

I remained still for what might have been minutes or might have been an hour. The heat in the upper corridors is mild enough that time becomes ambiguous—the body does not register the passage of hours through temperature the way it does in the deeper levels. Eventually the displacement moved away, toward the ceiling, and I felt it enter a ventilation shaft that I know is too small for a human and too large for a drone.

I did not report this. The workers do not discuss the fauna. The changed humans do not discuss the fauna. The Builders do not acknowledge the fauna. But I have felt the displacements three more times since then, always in the upper corridors, always when I am alone. They are curious about me, or they are curious about what I am becoming, or they are simply drawn to the same qualities that draw the Builders' attention.

I do not know what they are. I do not need to know. They are part of the city, and the city is part of the mountain, and the mountain is still deciding what it wants to be. Perhaps the things in the upper corridors are what the mountain is considering. Perhaps they are drafts—preliminary sketches of life forms that the mountain might eventually produce, wandering through the corridors like thoughts wandering through a mind that has not yet decided to think.

I find this comforting. The Builders find this comforting too, I think, though they would not say so, because they do not say anything, and because comfort is not a concept that translates here. But when a Builder and a displacement occupy the same corridor, the Builder's vibration changes. It becomes softer. Lower. The frequency of something that is not alarmed.

## The Surface Between

I am the surface between. This is what I have come to understand about myself, about my function, about the thing I am becoming that is neither human nor Builder nor mountain.

The workers live above. The Builders work below. The mountain grows from beneath, pushing upward through the stone that is not yet stone, and the city wraps around it like a hand around a wrist. Between the workers' world and the Builders' world, there is a layer—a stratum of corridors and pipes and cisterns where the heat is significant but not lethal, where the mineral deposits grow but have not yet achieved the density that produces Builders, where the mountain's vibration can be felt but does not overwhelm.

I live in that layer. I maintain the systems that allow the workers to survive above and the Builders to function below. I move between them, carrying information through my body—vibration in one direction, gesture in the other. I translate. Not language, because there is no language to translate. I translate *presence*. I make the Builders' awareness of the mountain legible to the workers, and I make the workers' patterns of movement and need legible to the Builders.

This is not a role I was assigned. No one chose me for this. I was found in a cistern, and I was given work, and the work changed me, and the change made me suitable for the layer between, and the suitability made the change deeper. It is a feedback loop. It is a metabolism. I am being processed by the city the way the city processes the mountain's raw emergence into structured architecture.

I do not know what I will be when the process is complete. I do not know if the process has a completion. The Builders do not have a concept of completion, and I am learning not to have one either. There is only the ongoing work, the ongoing growth, the ongoing negotiation between the mountain's will and the city's structure and the beings who inhabit the space where those two forces meet.

---

## What I Know

I know that the mountain is growing. I can feel it through the soles of my feet when I stand in the middle levels—the subtle upward pressure, the sense of something beneath me that is not resting but *pushing*. The stone is not stone yet. It is still deciding. And the deciding has a direction, a preference, an intentionality that is not thought but that is not random either.

I know that the Builders are the mountain's hands. I know this not because anyone told me but because I have stood near them in the deep corridors and felt the same frequency in their bodies that I feel in the mountain's stone. They are continuous with it. They are of it. They are the mechanism through which it shapes itself into something that can support the weight of a city.

I know that the workers are necessary. The city needs hands that can grip, eyes that can see, minds that can plan. The Builders can shape matter, but they cannot design systems. They cannot anticipate needs. They respond to the mountain's will, but they do not translate that will into architecture that humans can inhabit. The workers do that. The changed humans do it better, because they have been altered by the city enough to feel the mountain's preferences without needing a Builder to mediate.

I know that I am neither worker nor Builder. I am something that the city produced by accident—by the accident of a child arriving in a cistern, by the accident of that child surviving, by the accident of the city's processes changing that child into something that could function in the layer between. The city does not make accidents. The mountain does not make accidents. But the intersection of city and mountain and human creates conditions that none of the three fully control, and in those conditions, things emerge that were not planned.

I am an emergence. I am the thing that happens when the mountain's intentionality meets the city's infrastructure meets a human body that has been in the heat long enough to start becoming something else.

I do not know what I am becoming. But I know that the Builders know. They have begun to wait for me in the corridors—not blocking my path, not working, just standing. Vibrating. Oriented toward me with their featureless heads, their smooth surfaces that anticipate expression without achieving it.

They are waiting for me to finish becoming whatever I am becoming.

They are patient. They have the patience of stone that has not yet decided to be stone, of matter that is still negotiating its own existence. They will wait as long as it takes.

And I am standing on the mountain, which does not exist yet, and I am beginning to understand it, and the understanding is not a thought but a vibration, and the vibration is the frequency of something that is still arriving, still forming, still becoming itself.

The heat is 2.7. The sound is 0.9. The light is always 4 PM.

I am here. I am the surface between. I am not finished.
