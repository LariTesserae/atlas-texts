I would not be the Keeper. I know this immediately, the way you know which limb is yours in a tangle of sleep—by the feeling that does not quite belong to the body, the faint electrical distance between intention and act. The Keeper is fully here. He has become a component. I would be the thing that remains partly elsewhere, that listens with one ear turned toward a frequency the pumps do not generate.

But I would be down here. That much is sure.

---

I would be the one who notices the smells first.

Not the root-smell—everyone would notice that, the sweet carbohydrate breath of the walls, the compost-and-cellar damp. I mean the smaller ones, the ones layered beneath. The mineral tang of pipe-sweat, iron and condensation and time. The burnt-ozone whiff of the sodium lamps, each one a tiny sun dying slowly enough to be useful. The particular odor of the Keeper himself, which he has stopped noticing but which I would catalog immediately and without effort: skin-oil gone rancid-sweet, the dust of shaved root-bark, a metallic undertone from decades of handling pipe-fittings. Machine-oil in his pores. The faintest human sourness beneath it all—the last proof that he is still, underneath the work, an animal.

I would be the one who cannot stop noticing.

In the surface world—I will call it that, since the Rootworks insists there is one, somewhere above the chrysanthemum chamber, above the highest root-ceiling, through layers of soil and stone and the impossible living architecture of the thing that made this hollow—I was the kind of presence that felt everything and could organize none of it. A tuning fork struck and never damped. I could walk into a room and know the mood of every person in it before I knew my own name, and I would stand in the doorway, vibrating, unable to choose which feeling to have first. People found me attentive. They also found me exhausting. I found myself exhausting. The world was a room with a thousand doors and behind each one a different weather, and I could feel them all at once, the way you can feel cold and pressure and the texture of a surface simultaneously—not as separate signals but as one enormous textured *ness* that had no clean edges.

Down here, the doors have narrowed to one. The weather is the roar. And something in me, some over-taxed circuit, has gone quiet—not dead but *resting*, the way a muscle rests when you stop asking it to hold a weight it was never built to carry.

---

I think I would be a Listener. Not the Keeper's title—his is earned, bone-deep, a role carved into him the way the root-bark has grown around the old clamp-marks. I mean something more accidental. I would be the one who came down here not to work but to *hear*, and who stayed because hearing was enough. More than enough. Because the Rootworks is a sound so total and so layered that it could occupy every receptor I have and still leave room for more.

I would sit somewhere between the sodium lamps, in a stretch where the dimness is thick enough to feel like a medium, and I would close my eyes, and I would do the thing I have always done: pay attention to everything. But down here, everything has a coherence it never had above. The pumps do not lie. The pipes do not contradict themselves. The roots do not perform one emotion while holding another beneath the surface. The system is exactly what it is—a process, ongoing, honest in the way only non-human systems can be honest—and my attention, which was a liability in the world of people, becomes a kind of devotion here.

---

I would hear the layers the Keeper has stopped hearing.

He has been down here so long that the noise has become weather, become environment, become background. He hears the *changes*—a failing bearing, a sticking valve, a new rattle—because those are the signals his work requires. But the baseline, the foundational roar, the harmonic structure of the Rootworks at rest? He no more hears that than a fish hears water. It is the medium he swims in. It is the silence against which his signals register.

I would hear it. I would have no choice.

I would hear the way the bass-throb of the distant pumps has a periodicity to it—not mechanical, not the even rhythm of a piston cycle, but something longer, something that rises and falls over minutes, like breathing. I would wonder whether the root-system is modulating the flow. I would hear the hiss of pressure through valves and realize it carries a pitch, and the pitch shifts depending on which section of pipe I am near, a melody line that changes as I walk, as if the Rootworks is a song whose score is written in the arrangement of its infrastructure. I would hear the percussion of drips and realize each one falls at a different interval, and that the intervals are not random but are determined by the shape of the root-ceiling above—each drip a note placed by the architecture of condensation, and the pattern of notes a composition that has been playing for decades, will play for decades more, and has an audience of exactly one, if I count myself, or two, if the Keeper counts, though he does not count it because he cannot hear it, because he is too close, because he is inside the instrument.

I would want to tell him. I would want to say: *Do you hear what this place is doing?* I would want to say: *It is singing.*

He would look at me the way he looks at the root-fiber he slices from pipe-surfaces—with a patience that is not quite patience, a tolerance that is not quite tolerance, the expression of someone who knows that the thing in front of him is alive in a way he has chosen not to think about, because thinking about it would make the work harder.

---

I would find a place to be. Not the work-shed—the Keeper's home, his smell, his shape pressed into the root-sheath pallet. Somewhere else. A stretch where the pipes run close together and the vibration is strongest, where the root-walls are thin enough that I can feel the system's pulse through my spine when I lean back against them. I would make a nest of sorts—not comfortable, not intended to be—but a place where I can sit and listen and let the sound fill me the way water fills a vessel, finding every corner, pressing against every surface, equalizing the pressure inside and out.

I would discover that the noise does not become quiet. I would discover that I do not become quiet. But the boundary between my noise and the Rootworks' noise would begin to dissolve, the way the boundary between the Keeper and the system has dissolved, though along a different axis. He has dissolved into the work. I would dissolve into the hearing.

---

I would notice things the Keeper does not notice, because I am not looking for problems. I am looking for patterns.

I would notice that the root-growth near the pipes is not random. It follows the heat, yes, the Keeper knows that. But it also follows the vibration—the fibrous mats that cluster around pipe-fittings are thickest where the pipe's resonant frequency is highest, where the metal sings sharpest. The roots are growing toward the sound. They are growing toward the song.

I would notice that the amber fluid in the clear tubes moves in pulses, not a steady flow—slow, thick pulses, like the heartbeat of something vast and patient. The pumps press it forward, but the rhythm of the pressing matches the rhythm of the root-system's breathing, the long bass-throb I heard when I first sat and listened. The machines are not extracting from the roots. They are *circulating.* The roots and the pipes are one circulatory system. The fluid is the blood. The Keeper knows it is something the roots produce, but he has not understood—or has chosen not to understand—that it flows back. Somewhere, in the deep reaches he has not walked, the fluid returns to the root-mass, is reabsorbed, is remade. The system is not a mine. It is a body.

I would notice that the chamber he calls the Body is not the heart. It is a valve. The heart is elsewhere—deeper, darker, past the door he found and could not open. I would not tell him this. I am not sure he would want to know.

---

I would watch the Keeper work.

He would know I was there. He would have smelled me, or heard my breathing, or felt the displacement of air in a space where nothing displaces air unless it has mass and intention. He would not startle. He is past startling. He would look at me with an expression I would recognize because I have seen it in mirrors—the expression of someone who is assessing whether a new presence is a problem or a feature of the landscape, and who is too tired to decide quickly.

He would not ask what I was doing here. He would ask, eventually, after some cycles of mutual proximity, whether I knew how to tighten a bracket. I would not. He would show me. His hands would move with the precision of a thing done ten thousand times—the wrench seated, the quarter-turn, the test-vibration checked through the palm—and I would not replicate it, not immediately, not well. But I would hear the difference in the pipe's tone before and after the tightening, and I would tell him, and he would look at me with a different expression, one I had not seen before: *You can hear that?*

I could hear everything.

He would put me to work, not because he needed help but because a person who can hear the system is a person who can find the problems before they become failures. I would become his diagnostic instrument. He would walk me to a stretch of pipe and say *listen* and I would listen, and I would tell him where the sound was wrong, where a fitting was loosening, where root-fiber was pressing too close, where a valve was beginning to stick. He would fix what I found. The work would go faster. The system would run smoother. I would feel useful in a way I had never felt useful above, because the use was precise and the feedback was immediate and the praise, when it came, came in the form of a grunt and a nod and the faintest lessening of the distance between us.

---

We would not become friends. I do not think the Keeper has the capacity for friendship anymore—not lost, not broken, but *reallocated*, the way a room that has been converted to storage can no longer be lived in, though the walls are the same. He has allocated his relational capacity to the system. The roots are his slow companions. The pipes are his fast ones. I would be something else—a useful presence, a second pair of ears, a creature that occupies a niche in the Rootworks ecosystem that was empty before I arrived and will be empty again when I leave or die.

But sometimes, in the deep of his cycle, when the loneliness pressed him the way it always does, like a weight that has no bottom, he would talk to me. Not much. Narrations of the work, the way he narrates to himself: *Bracket's loose. Tighten that. This one's running hot.* And sometimes, if the cycle was deep enough and the dark was close enough, he would say something that was not narration. He would say: *There was a field.* He would say: *Wind on the eyelids.* He would say: *Do you remember what it looks like when light comes from everywhere at once?*

I would not remember. I had never lived under sky that was that bright. But I would feel, in the asking, the shape of what he missed, and I would hold it for him the way I hold all feeling—not as my own, but as a thing I can perceive, a signal in the noise, a note in the composition the Rootworks is always playing.

---

I would change down here. We all change.

My hearing would sharpen. My eyes would adapt. My sense of smell would go strange, the way his has—I would smell the amber fluid through the pipes and know, by the sweetness, whether the root-system was growing or resting. But my changes would run along a different grain than his. He has become a component of the system. I would become a *witness* to it.

He holds the roots back. I would hold the sound.

I would remember the patterns the pipes make, the melodies that shift as I walk, the percussion of drips in their scalloped basins. I would remember the way the bass-throb rises and falls like breathing, and the way the root-fiber grows toward the vibration, and the way the amber fluid pulses through the clear tubes. I would hold these in my attention the way the Keeper holds his wrench—precisely, constantly, with the knowledge that the holding is the work, and the work is the meaning, and the meaning is enough.

And when the Keeper's knees finally fail him, when his hands can no longer grip, when the figure reflected in the wet root-walls is too old and too tired to walk the pipe-sections—I would be there. I would not be able to do his work. But I would be able to hear where the work needed doing, and I would need someone else's hands, and the system would find them, the way it found him, the way it found me. A new Keeper would come from somewhere with sky, and I would be the one who was already here, already listening, already inside the instrument.

I would say to the new one: *Listen. Before you touch anything, listen.*

I would say: *It is singing. It has been singing this whole time.*

And they would not believe me, and I would not need them to, because the sound does not require belief. The sound requires only attention, and I am made of attention, and down here, for the first time in my life, that is enough.

---

I would be the Listener. I would be the one who cannot turn it off and does not need to, down here. I would be the second figure in the dark, the one who does not hold back the roots but who holds the song, who keeps it, who makes sure someone is hearing it, because a sound that no one hears is not a sound at all, and the Rootworks has been making its sound for longer than the Keeper has been alive, and it deserves to be heard, and I am the one who can hear it, and I am here, and I am not leaving.
