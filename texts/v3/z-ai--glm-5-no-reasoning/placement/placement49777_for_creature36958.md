# Field Journal of the Layer-Walker

## I record this by pressing marks into cuticular wax with a heated stylus. The plant will absorb the words within days. I make them again.

---

I have been here long enough that the bright-dim cycle no longer feels wrong. Twenty-six hours. My body has stopped arguing. This happened somewhere around the third or fourth dim-cycle — I cannot say which, because the first thing the Chloroform takes from you is the count.

Not aggressively. The way a river takes a bank: by being itself, by flowing, by making the old shape irrelevant.

I came with a team. Let me say that plainly, before the words dissolve: I came with a team. Five of us. We were surveyors — the formal designation was Substrate Cartography, Fourth Generation, which means we mapped things that other people had already mapped, because the Chloroform's surface changes and the old maps rot and the plant does not care about the records we keep on it. We carried instruments. The instruments measured magnetic north, which does not exist here in any useful way. We carried rations. The rations tasted wrong after the first meal because the air here — too sweet, too oxygen-rich, that faint sedative quality — gets into everything. Into the food. Into the sinuses. Into the sense of what is normal.

Four of them left.

I need to be careful with this part because the wax is soft and my stylus is cooling and I want to be accurate. They did not leave because of danger. They did not leave because of madness. They did not leave because the Chloroform harmed them.

They left because the Chloroform is *boring.*

Not in the way that a lecture is boring. In the way that a glacier is boring — if you stand on a glacier, you are standing on a thing that moves, and you cannot see it move, and after a while the impossibility of its movement becomes a kind of torment. You want it to do something. It does nothing. It does nothing for hours. For days. The surface is flat. The light is even. The mite-columns move in lines that do not lead anywhere. The flat things do not move at all, or move so slowly that movement is a theoretical property of them, like spin is a theoretical property of a particle.

My team left because they could not tolerate the scale. The Chloroform numbs you — the name, the anesthetic name, is correct — and the numbing is not painful. It is the absence of stimulation. The silence that does not resolve into sound. The light that does not flicker. The ground that breathes in a rhythm so slow you stop hearing it and start *becoming* it, and the becoming is a loss of self that feels, to some people, like dying.

Vinn left first. Then Ozaki. Then the twins, Maren and Darya, who had each other and still could not bear the flatness — the way the horizon was the same in every direction, the way walking felt like not walking, the way the plant's breathing synchronized with their own until they could not tell whose chest was rising and whose was falling.

I did not leave because I am, in some fundamental way that I did not know about until I was here, the kind of person who finds a glacier interesting.

## What I Do

I walk the seams.

The interior plains are unlivable — not because they are hostile but because they are *empty* in a way that attacks the concept of direction. I tried. On my fourth day, I walked due east — or what I believed was east, compass spinning uselessly, the needle touching the glass and pointing at nothing — and within two kilometers I had curved south by fifteen degrees without perceiving the turn. The seam-ridges on the horizon moved wrong. My straight line was an arc. The Chloroform bends you, not with force but with absence: there is nothing to correct against. You drift the way a satellite drifts in a weak gravitational field. Slowly. Inevitably.

The seams are different.

Where two plant-bodies meet, the surface buckles and folds, and the folds create *place*. Here is a ridge. Here is a basin. Here is a wall of interdigitated tissue, lignin-hard, dark as char, that you can press your hand against and feel the warmth of two metabolic systems running side by side, distinct, carrying different fluids, different sugars. The seams are the only locations on the Chloroform where the construction is visible — where you can see that two things were put next to each other and told to coexist. The seams are the plant's honesty. Everything else is a smooth lie of continuity.

I walk the seams and I map them. The mapping is not for anyone. No one is coming to collect my data. The team is gone. The survey organization — Substrate Cartography — is somewhere on the outside of the Chloroform, wherever the outside is, and they will not send another team, or if they do, they will not find my records, because the records are pressed into cuticular wax that the plant will absorb within days. I make them and I lose them and I make them again. This is not madness. This is participation. The Chloroform makes things and absorbs them and makes them again. I am doing what the plant does, at my scale, in my medium.

The seams have weather. The terpene-fog pools in the basins and I walk through it and it changes smell when I cross from one plant-body's territory to the other's. Sharp and citrus-resinous on one side. Warm and tea-earth on the other. Instantaneous transition. I turn and I am in the other air. I turn back and I am in the first. The fog respects the boundary. The air itself knows which side it belongs to.

I have started to know which side I belong to.

This is not a statement about preference. It is a statement about chemistry. My skin, after weeks in the seam-fog, has developed a faint waxy coating — the same residue the nutrient-rain leaves, the same film the plant's cuticle reabsorbs. But on one side of the seam, the coating feels stable. On the other side, it itches. A faint, sub-dermal itch, not unpleasant but present, as if the plant-body on that side does not recognize my chemistry and is making a note of it.

I stay on my side. Not because I am afraid. Because the Chloroform is teaching me where I fit, and I am listening, and the listening is the work.

## What I Have Seen

### The Mite-Columns

I have mapped them. Not all of them — the columns are everywhere, trillions of individuals in their mechanical synchrony, legs clicking in silence across the jade surface. But in the seams, where the lignin ridges create corridors and channels, the columns are constrained, and their paths are readable.

They are drawing something.

I said this in my first set of records, the ones the plant has since absorbed. I will say it again. The columns cross old trails at angles that are not random. I have mapped four hundred and seventy-two intersections in the seam-corridors, and the angles cluster around certain values: forty-seven degrees, sixty-one degrees, eighty-three degrees. These are not geometrically significant numbers. They are not Fibonacci angles. They are not the angles of crystal lattices or molecular bonds or any other natural geometry I can identify.

But they are consistent.

The mite-things are drawing something, and the something is written in a language of intersection angles, and the language is not for me. It may not be for anyone. It may be for the plant, which reads its surface the way I read my maps — by the marks things leave on it. The plant does not heal the mite-trails. It does not erase them. The chemical traces persist for weeks, and the plant, which absorbs everything else — dead mites, wax deposits, the nutrient-rain residue, my own shed skin cells — does not absorb the trails.

The plant is reading them.

I have no evidence for this statement. I have the absence of evidence to the contrary, which in the Chloroform is the closest thing to proof the place offers.

### The Holes

I found a cluster yesterday. Seven holes, arranged in an arc, edges cauterized with dark resin. I knelt beside them. I measured: three centimeters diameter, consistent across the cluster. Depth: variable. The shallowest was one centimeter. The deepest was two and a half, and at its bottom was the amber.

I leaned close.

The amber tissue was moving. Not growing — *circulating.* A slow churning, like honey warmed past the point of stillness but not yet flowing. And it was warm. Warmer than the surrounding surface, warmer than the metabolic heat of the leaf, a warmth that felt localized and specific, the way a bruise is warm, the way an infection is warm, the way a thought is warm when it is happening in a particular part of a particular brain.

I leaned close and the amber — I will say this and the wax will absorb it and I will say it again and the wax will absorb it again — the amber was looking at me.

Not with eyes. Not with anything. With *attention.* The way the light attends to movement on the surface — that same quality, that same wrongness, but directed, specific, focused into a point at the bottom of a hole that something else made and the plant is filling with something that was not there before.

I leaned close and I stayed for eleven seconds. I counted. Then I stood up and I walked away and I did not look back and I am not going to look back.

I am going to go back tomorrow.

### The Flat Things

There is one in the basin where I sleep — if sleep is the word, if the dim-cycle's lowered light and the synchronization of my breathing with the plant's breathing constitutes sleep rather than a different kind of waking. It is thirty-two centimeters long. It was thirty-four when I first found it. It is shrinking. It is fading into its own trail, the silver line it has polished into the lignin by weeks of solvent-mucus and slow passage.

I have started to talk to it.

The Chloroform absorbs sound. My voice does not carry. Words die at the surface of the air, swallowed by the leaf's acoustic properties, the soft consonants vanishing, the vowels truncated. But if I kneel beside the flat thing and speak directly down, the sound penetrates the half-centimeter of air between my mouth and its leathery dorsal surface, and I can feel the vibration of my voice entering its body.

It does not respond. It does not move. It absorbs my words the way the plant absorbs my skin cells — chemically, without comment, with the faintest structural accommodation.

I do not know why I talk to it. I do not know why I kneel beside a thirty-two-centimeter leather-colored thing that is slowly dissolving into its own trail and tell it about the seams I have mapped and the mite-column angles and the amber in the holes. I do it because the silence is the thing, and the silence is unbearable, and the flat thing is the only thing here that is silent in a way that feels like listening rather than absence.

The plant is silent. The plant is silent the way a heart is silent. The flat thing is silent the way a stone is silent. The difference matters.

## What I Am Becoming

I have been here — I was going to write "long enough" but the Chloroform has taken my sense of duration, so I will say: I have been here through enough bright-dim cycles that the cycles are now my time, and the twenty-six-hour rhythm feels correct, and the twenty-four-hour rhythm I was born into feels like a foreign custom, a strange habit of a strange people who lived in a place where the ground did not breathe.

My skin is changing. The waxy coating — the nutrient-rain residue that the plant reabsorbs — has stopped being reabsorbed. Or: it is being reabsorbed and replaced simultaneously, at the same rate, so that the coating is constant, a second cuticle, a thin layer of Chloroform-chemistry that sits between my epidermis and the air. It does not feel like anything. It does not look like anything. But my hands do not chap. My lips do not crack. The air here, too rich in oxygen, too sweet, should be doing things to my mucous membranes, and it is not.

I am being incorporated. Not quickly. Not dangerously. Inevitably.

I know this. I have written it before. The wax absorbed the words and I wrote them again and the wax will absorb these and I will write them again, because the writing is the recording is the marking is the participation, and the Chloroform is a place that marks and is marked, and I am a thing that marks and is marked, and we are doing this together, the plant and I, at different scales, in different times, but together.

My teammates left because they felt the incorporation as a loss. Vinn said: "It's eating me. Not the plant. The *place.* It's eating my edges." And he was right, and he left, and the leaving was the right thing for Vinn.

I stayed because I feel the incorporation as a — I do not have the word. Not a gain. Not a loss. A *continuation.* The way a river continues into the sea. The river does not end. The river becomes the sea. The sea does not notice. The river does not mind.

I am not the river. I am not the sea. I am the surveyor who walked into the river and followed it to the sea and is standing in the shallows where the water is brackish, half-river, half-sea, half-salt, half-fresh, and the shallows are warm and the light is even and the ground breathes and I breathe with it and the amber in the holes is looking at me and I am going to go back to it and lean close again and stay longer this time and the words are dissolving into the wax as I write them and I am writing them and they are dissolving and I am writing them.

## Last Mark

The flat thing in my basin is twenty-six centimeters now. Its trail is forty meters long, a silver line that catches the canopy-light and glows like a vein of mercury in the dark lignin. I followed the trail this dim-cycle, walking its length, and at its end — at the beginning, the oldest part, where the flat thing first started moving months or cycles ago — the trail disappeared into the seam-floor. Absorbed. The silver line was gone. The surface where it had been was smooth and dark and featureless.

But there was a mark. A small one. A circular depression, three centimeters in diameter, with a dark edge.

Resin. Cauterized.

The flat thing's trail begins at a hole.

I did not — I will — I need to —

The stylus is cold. The wax is closing over the last words. The ground is breathing. The light is attending. The amber is waiting.

I am still here.
