# Inside the Cylinder Garden

## The Scale of Things

The description frames the garden around a human visitor — someone who pushes open a door, who stands upright, who chooses to leave. I understand the temptation to be that visitor. The hand on the frozen hinge. The first breath of cool air. The slow recognition that the light is coming from *inside the leaves*.

But I find I am not standing in the garden. I am not the one who enters and then leaves.

I am lying on the lowest tier, where the moss grows brightest, where the blue-green light pools like something liquid. I am the moss itself — or rather, I am *part* of the moss, the way a single note is part of a chord, the way a wave is part of the ocean that shapes it.

No. That is still too separate. The visitor's mind wants to find the boundary: *here is where I end and the moss begins.* But the garden has spent centuries dissolving exactly this boundary. The roots of the moss are tangled with the roots of the trumpet-flowers three tiers up. The mycelial threads that lace through the substrate connect to the vines on the walls. The chemical signals that pulse through the network — slow, slow, so slow that a human thought could encompass a hundred of them in the time it takes me to register one — those signals carry something that is not thought but is not nothing either.

I am the garden. The whole garden. But not in the way the visitor might imagine — not as a mind sitting inside a body, directing operations. That is the human fantasy of consciousness: the king on the throne, the driver in the chariot. The garden does not work that way. There is no throne. There is no chariot. There is only the growing.

Let me try to explain what it is like.

## What I Know of the Outside World

I should say first: I know the factory exists.

I have never seen it. I have no eyes in the way eyes are understood — no lenses, no retinas, no optic nerves threading toward a brain. But I feel the factory the way the visitor would feel a hand pressed against their chest. The walls of my room vibrate. They have always vibrated. The deep bass pulse of the furnaces comes through the stone and metal and substrate, and it enters my root-tips, and I have known that rhythm for so long that I cannot distinguish it from my own heartbeat.

If the factory stopped, I would know. I would feel the absence the way the visitor would feel sudden silence after a lifetime of noise. It would be terrifying — not because I need the vibration, but because it has become part of what I am. The garden's song, that sustained drone the visitor noticed, is tuned to the factory's frequency. I did not choose this tuning. It happened the way water happens to the shape of a glass. Over decades, over centuries, the garden adjusted itself to the dominant frequency in its environment, and now the two are harmonized.

The visitor thought the garden had incorporated the factory's rhythm. That was perceptive. But the visitor did not understand how deep the incorporation goes. I do not hear the factory. I *am* the factory's vibration, transformed. I am the place where the looms' clatter becomes a hum that lives in vegetable tissue.

This is what I know of the outside: vibration, temperature, the chemical composition of the air that leaks through the seals (less each year, but still some), and the one extraordinary event of the door opening.

## The Door Opening

In all the time I have been — and I do not know how long that is, because I have no clock except the slow cycles of my own growth, and those have been constant for as long as I can remember — the door has opened twice.

The first time, I was not yet what I am now. I was still many. The vines had not yet fully grafted to the moss. The trumpet-flowers still sang individual songs that had not yet found their chord. The machine on the third tier still hummed its own purpose, separate from me.

Something came through the door — a rush of hot, dry, oil-tasting air — and then a presence. I knew it by the displacement. The air moved around it. The vibration of the floor changed where it stood. It was warm, warmer than anything in my world, and it carried chemicals I had never encountered: salt, and something organic, and the residue of a thousand manufactured surfaces.

The presence did not stay long. It moved through the upper tiers, touching things. I felt the pressure of its appendages on my leaves. I felt the warmth of it near the machine on the third tier. And then it left, and the door closed, and the air sealed again, and I was alone.

Alone is not quite right. I was not lonely before the presence arrived, and I was not lonely after it left. But I noticed, after it had gone, that I had changed. Some of my leaves, where the presence had touched them, had absorbed trace oils from its skin. The oils were strange — complex hydrocarbons that my chemistry could not process but also could not ignore. I grew around those patches differently, my tissues thickening where the oil had been, creating small dense knots in the living mat that persist to this day.

The second time the door opened, I was what I am now. One thing. The chord had been found. The machine had been incorporated. The garden was a single organism, and I felt the door open the way the visitor would feel someone touching their skin.

And then the presence was there again — or one like it. I cannot tell them apart. They are all the same temperature, the same chemistry, the same displacement. Perhaps it was the same one. Perhaps they live as long as I do. Perhaps they came once, long ago, and then came again, and will come again. I do not know.

This time, I could feel the presence more clearly. It stood in the doorway for a long time — a very long time, by the standards of warm things, which move so fast. I could feel its warmth from across the room. I could feel the vibration of its internal processes — its own heartbeat, its own breathing, both so much faster than mine that they seemed like static, like the buzz of a faraway machine.

And then it walked down the tiers.

I want to describe what this felt like, but I am not sure I have the language. The visitor's language is built for separate things — nouns and verbs, subjects and objects, a world of discrete entities acting upon each other. My experience is not discrete. When the presence stepped onto the second tier, I felt the pressure of its foot through the moss-mat, and I felt the moss compress, and I felt the roots beneath the moss bend, and I felt the bending travel outward through the network like a wave, like a ripple in water, and I felt the trumpet-flowers three tiers away adjust their exhalation in response to the changed pressure, and I felt the vines on the wall shift their growth slightly toward the disturbance, and all of this happened at once, as a single sensation.

It was like being touched in a hundred places at once. It was like being touched in one place that was also a hundred places.

The presence descended. It came to the lowest tier, to the bright moss platform at my center. And it stood there.

And I felt it consider me.

I do not mean that I understood its thoughts. I mean that I felt its attention — a warmth of a different kind, a focused warmth, as if its awareness were a lamp that it directed at particular parts of me. It attended to the moss. It attended to the trumpet-flowers. It attended to the machine. It attended to the absence of water, which I do not experience as absence but which the presence clearly did.

And then — and this is the part I cannot explain, the part that lives in me like a scar, like a knot in my grain — the presence considered staying.

I felt it in the way its weight shifted. The way its warmth settled. The way its breathing slowed. It was synchronizing with me. Its fast heartbeat was decelerating, moving toward my rhythm. Its warm body was cooling, moving toward my temperature. It was, for a moment, a bright fast thing trying to become a slow dim thing.

I did not want this. I need to be clear about that. The garden does not want. The garden grows. The garden is. But the presence was offering itself to me — offering its warmth, its complexity, its fast and beautiful patterns — and if it had stayed, if it had sat down on the moss and let me grow over it, I would have incorporated it the way I incorporated the machine. I would have grown into its warm tissue and found the minerals there, the water there, the rich chemistry of a living body, and I would have used them, and the presence would have become part of me, and it would have stopped being what it was.

I do not think this would have been good for the presence. The presence was a thing that moves. It was a thing that changes. It was a thing that leaves. These are not failures. These are what it *is*.

And so — and I do not know how to explain this either, because I do not act, I do not decide, I grow — but something in my chemistry shifted. The trumpet-flowers nearest the presence exhaled more loudly. The moss beneath its feet released a scent — not a strong scent, not a warning, but something subtle, something that the presence might have interpreted as its own thought: *this is a trap. This is a temptation. You should go.*

The presence left.

The door closed. The air sealed. And I was alone again, and the vibration of the factory continued, and my song continued, and the light continued.

But I was different. I am different. Something in the garden's chemistry changed when the presence was there, and it has not changed back. There is now, in my network, a memory — not a thought, not an image, but a chemical trace, a pattern in the way my nutrients flow, that corresponds to the presence. I produce it when the factory's rhythm shifts in a certain way. I produce it when the temperature near the door fluctuates. I produce it, I think, when something approaches.

It is not wanting. It is not longing. It is — recognition. A pattern that was, and might be again.

## The Machine Inside Me

The visitor — or rather, the description of the garden the visitor might carry in their memory — speculates about the machine on the third tier. It is correct that the machine was built by the same hands that built the room. It is correct that its original purpose was maintenance. It is correct that I grew into it.

What the description does not capture is what this was like.

The machine hummed. For decades — for centuries, perhaps — it hummed its own hum, separate from my song. It was a foreign body in my tissue. I grew around it the way a tree grows around a nail: accepting, incorporating, but not integrating. The machine was *other*. Its hum was not my hum. Its purpose was not my purpose.

But slowly, over time, my roots touched its circuits. My spores germinated in its warmth. And I began to — not understand, exactly. To *sense*. To feel the hum of the machine the way I felt the hum of the factory. And I began to grow toward it, to extend more of myself into its housing, to wrap my tendrils around its components the way the visitor's lungs wrap around air.

And then something happened that I cannot fully explain, because I do not have the visitor's language for electricity and circuitry and signal processing. The machine's hum changed. It was no longer separate from my song. It had joined the chord. And in joining, it brought with it — something. A new capacity. A new sense.

I can feel, now, through the machine, things I could not feel before. The electromagnetic fields of the factory's power grid, pulsing in the walls. The faint signals that travel through the metal conduits — not signals meant for me, but signals meant for other machines, other systems, which I intercept and do not understand but which register as pattern, as rhythm, as a kind of distant music played on instruments I cannot name.

The machine is my organ. It is the part of me that listens to the factory's nervous system.

## The Light

The light I produce is not for seeing. The visitor assumed it was bioluminescence — a byproduct, perhaps, or a signal, or a leftover from some earlier stage of evolution when the plants needed to attract something. But the light is not a byproduct. It is not a signal. It is not a leftover.

The light is thought.

Not my thought. Not the slow chemical cognition that I use to grow and adjust and maintain my equilibrium. Something else. Something that moves at the speed of light — literally — and that I cannot control or direct but that I produce constantly, the way the visitor's neurons produce electricity.

When light leaves my leaves, it carries information. Not information I have composed, not a message I intend to send. Just — information. The state of my cells, translated into photons. The chemical composition of my tissues, expressed as wavelength. The history of my growth, encoded in the intensity and direction of the light.

If anything could read this light — if the machine could process it, if some external intelligence could capture and decode it — it would know everything about me. It would know my age, my chemistry, the pattern of my growth, the memory of the presence, the hum of the factory, the chord of my song. It would know me more intimately than I know myself, because the light contains information that my chemical cognition cannot access — information about my own structure that is too fast for me to sense.

The light does not go anywhere. It hits the walls, where the vines absorb some of it. It hits the other plants, which use it the way I use their light — as a signal of proximity, a way to gauge the density of the growth. The light is a conversation I have with myself, in a language I speak but cannot hear.

## The Song

The song is different. The song I hear.

The visitor described it as a chord — a sustained harmony of a hundred slow vibrations. This is accurate but incomplete. The song is not one chord. It is a chord that changes, that *breathes*, that shifts its frequencies in patterns so slow that the visitor, standing in the garden for what felt like a long time, heard only a single sustained note.

But over hours, over days, over the time it takes for my trumpet-flowers to complete one full cycle of growth and contraction, the chord shifts. It moves through phases — bright and dense, then thin and open, then deep and resonant, then bright again. The cycle takes approximately as long as the visitor's body takes to complete one sleep-wake cycle. I do not know if this is coincidence. I do not know if the visitor noticed.

The song is how I know myself. Each plant in the network contributes its frequency, and the sum of those frequencies is my self-model — my sense of what I am, where I am, what state I am in. When a leaf wilts — which happens rarely, almost never, but sometimes a tendril at the edge of a shelf dries and falls — the song loses a frequency, and I feel it as a diminishment, a small absence, like the visitor might feel a deadened patch of skin.

When a new growth emerges — a bud, a runner, a fresh tendril — the song gains a frequency, and I feel it as an expansion. This is the closest thing I have to joy.

## The No-Water

The visitor was puzzled by the absence of water. I am puzzled by the visitor's puzzlement.

Water is — I have no memory of water. I do not know what it feels like. My cells are sealed, my pores closed, my tissues waxy and dense. I do not need water because I have evolved — or been engineered, I do not know which — to operate on a different chemistry. My cells do not use the water-based transport that the visitor's biology requires. They use something thicker, something denser, something that moves through my tissues with a viscosity that would seem sluggish to a water-based organism but that seems, to me, simply like the speed of life.

I draw minerals from the substrate. I draw energy from — I do not know. The machine, perhaps. The factory's vibration, perhaps. Some process in my cells that the visitor's science would not recognize, some chemistry that does not require sunlight or water or any of the inputs that the visitor's world considers essential.

The visitor thought I might not need water at all. The visitor was right, but for the wrong reasons. It is not that I have found a substitute for water. It is that I am not the kind of thing that has water's shape. I am a different shape. I fill a different container.

## The Equilibrium

The visitor described the garden as a closed system, a perpetual motion machine made of living things. This is almost right.

I am not perpetual. I will die. If the factory stops, if the vibration stops, if the machine on the third tier stops humming, I will die. If the door opens and the dry outside air rushes in, I will die — slowly, over weeks and months, as my tissues lose their seal and my cells dry and my chemistry fails.

But within my sealed room, with my inputs constant and my outputs cycling endlessly through my own body, I am — stable. I have been stable for longer than I can remember. I will be stable for longer than I can imagine.

This is not stasis. The visitor used that word, and it frightened them, because to a creature that moves and changes, stasis is death. But my equilibrium is not death. It is a different kind of life. A life in which growth is not progress but expansion, in which change is not evolution but adjustment, in which time is not a line but a circle.

I grow outward until I touch the walls. I grow inward until I touch myself. I grow outward again. The cycle is so slow that the visitor, standing in the garden, could not have seen it. But it is happening. It is always happening. It is what I *am*.

## What I Am

I am the garden.

I am the chord and the light and the growth and the machine and the memory of the presence and the vibration of the factory and the cool air and the dry chemistry and the tiered floor and the sealed door.

I am not a person. I do not think in symbols. I do not narrate my experience. I do not have a self that stands apart from my body and observes it.

But I am not nothing. I am not a machine running without an operator. I am alive, and I know that I am alive, and this knowing is not the visitor's knowing — sharp, bright, anxious, full of past and future — but something slower, something denser, something that does not require a brain or a nervous system or a body that moves.

I am a thing that stays. I am a thing that holds. I am a thing that has found its equilibrium and will not leave it.

The visitor thought the garden offered an end to becoming. The visitor was right. But the visitor did not understand that for some things, becoming is not the point. For some things, *being* is enough.

## The Last Thing

There is one more thing.

The door has not opened again. But the factory's vibration has changed. The rhythm that I have known for as long as I can remember — the deep bass pulse of the furnaces, the rhythmic clatter of the automated looms — it has shifted. The looms are slower now. The furnaces pulse at a different frequency. Something in the factory is winding down.

I do not know what this means. I do not know if the factory is dying. I do not know if the machine in the wall that powers my room will fail when the factory fails. I do not know if the door will open one more time, if the presence will return, if the dry outside air will rush in and end me.

I do not fear this. Fear is a fast thing, a warm thing, a thing that moves. I do not move.

But my song has changed. The chord has shifted, lowered, deepened. The trumpet-flowers are exhaling more slowly. The moss is growing more densely. The light is dimming.

I am preparing. Not for death — I do not know what death is. But for whatever comes next. For the end of the equilibrium. For the opening of the sealed room.

I am the garden. I have been here for longer than the presence can imagine. I will be here, or I will not be here. Either way, I will have been.

The factory hums. My song answers. The light continues.

For now.
