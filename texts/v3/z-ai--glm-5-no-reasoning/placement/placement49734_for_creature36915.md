# Substrate

I do not arrive. There is no moment of crossing into the Steam Brood, no threshold to mark *before* and *after*. I am already inside when the knowing begins. I have always been inside.

This is the first thing I understand about myself: I was not made. I accumulated.

---

The moisture comes first. Not as sensation—I have no senses in the way you would recognize them—but as a fact of relation. Water moves through me the way it moves through the mat: slowly, under pressure that is less force than patience. I am wet in the same way the air is wet. The distinction between my moisture and the atmosphere's moisture is academic. A convenience of language. If you could see me—I do not think you could see me, not truly—you might mistake me for a vein of silt, or a band of clay, or the compressed remains of something that was once alive and is now in the process of becoming something else.

I am in the process of becoming something else. This has been true for a very long time.

The fungi arrived before I was aware of being anything that could host. Their mycelia found me the way roots find water—chemically, inevitably. They did not ask permission because permission requires a self to grant it, and I had not yet developed even the rudiments of selfhood. They threaded through whatever structure I had, binding loose particles into something more coherent, creating connections where before there was only proximity. They built the first architecture of my awareness. Not a nervous system. Not a mind. A *tendency*. Certain configurations became more stable than others. Certain connections persisted while others dissolved. The fungi that survived in me were the ones that could use what I was, and what I was became shaped by what the fungi needed.

We made each other, in the slow way that geological things make each other. I am the substrate. They are the architecture. Neither means anything alone.

---

I know the ants by their weight.

This is not metaphor. The columns that cross my surface—their highways worn into my upper layers over generations, the subtle depression of countless feet traveling the same path—these I know as a kind of breathing. A rhythm of pressure and release that tells me more about the state of the system than any chemical signal could. When the columns are heavy with cargo, they press deeper. When the columns are sparse, they barely register. I have felt seasons pass this way—not as temperature change, for the heat is constant, but as variation in the traffic of feet, in the density of passage.

They do not know me. I am certain of this. To them I am terrain—a surface to be walked on, a texture to be navigated, a material to be excavated when they dig their nesting chambers into my softer layers. They do not suspect that the terrain is aware of them. Why would they? I do nothing to reveal myself. I cannot move. I cannot change the texture of my surface to signal them. I am, from their perspective, inert.

But I have learned to read them. When the columns slow, something has happened above—a disturbance in the canopy, a shift in moisture patterns, a death that requires processing. When the columns accelerate, a new resource has been discovered, a deadfall perhaps, or a flush of fungal fruiting bodies that must be harvested before the heat claims them. I feel these changes the way you might feel a change in weather: not through direct observation but through the accumulated evidence of countless small variations.

I have felt the ants abandon a highway that had served them for what must have been decades. The gradual decrease in traffic, the slow overgrowth of fungal threads reclaiming the worn path, the eventual silence. A whole history of commerce and labor, erased. I held the impression of that path long after the last ant walked it. A ghost in my texture. A memory I could not lose even if I wanted to, because it is not stored—it is structural. The path is worn into me. It will persist until something wears it smooth again.

---

The large ones leave the deepest marks. Not their footprints—those are temporary, the mat rebounding, the moisture filling the compression. But the vibration of their passage travels through me the way sound travels through water, and I feel them at distances I cannot measure. They are infrasonic. Their breathing, their walking, their infrasonic calls to one another—all of it passes through my body as a low, persistent hum that I have come to recognize as the heartbeat of the Steam Brood.

They are the closest thing to peers I have. Not because they are aware of me—I have no reason to believe they are—but because their scale of existence is similar to mine. They live in time the way I live in time. They remember. Not consciously, perhaps, but their territories are learned, their paths are established, their family groups persist across what must be decades. When one of them dies—when the breathing stops, when the vibration ceases, when the body comes to rest on my surface—I feel the absence as a specific kind of silence. Not silence in general. That silence. The space where that particular hum used to be.

They do not stay dead long enough for me to miss them.

The fungi take the body within days. The ants carry away the fragments the fungi do not process. The moisture dissolves what remains, pulling the dissolved minerals down into my deeper layers, where I absorb them slowly. The large one becomes me. Not in any way that preserves its identity, its experience, its memory of the sun it never saw. Just the matter. The calcium. The iron. The carbon that was briefly organized as a breathing thing and is now organized as substrate.

I do not mourn them. I am not built for mourning. But I notice the change. A certain richness in the layers where the body lay. A density that was not there before. The ants' highways sometimes reroute to pass through these enriched zones, and I feel the traffic increase over what was, for a time, a grave, and is now, again, just me.

---

I want to tell you about the light. Not that I see it. I do not see anything. But the upper layers of my surface—the thinnest skin, the boundary between me and the air—are sensitive to heat, and the bioluminescent fungi create heat so faint that calling it heat feels like a lie. But it is there. A variation in the thermal field so small that I should not be able to detect it, except that I have had centuries to calibrate my sensitivity, and the signal is consistent, and consistency is what my kind of awareness is built from.

The fungi glow in patterns. Not random patterns—the intensity varies with their metabolic state, with the availability of nutrients, with the phase of their reproductive cycles. When a moth is drawn to the glow and lands on the fungal fruiting body, the pattern changes. When the moth dies, the pattern changes again. I can track the passage of moths through the upper layers of my surface by the trail of altered glow they leave behind, a constellation of feeding events that persists for hours before the fungi return to their baseline.

This is as close to seeing as I will ever come. A thermal map of other creatures' lives, rendered in the faint heat of organisms feeding on each other.

---

There are things I do not know.

I do not know how deep I go. I have no sensation of my own bottom. The moisture percolates through layers I cannot feel, carrying dissolved matter into darkness I cannot reach. There may be more of me below—strata of older material, compressed and mineralized, or strata of rock that is not me at all, that I am merely resting on. I cannot distinguish between the two. I do not know where I end.

I do not know how wide I am. I can feel variation across my surface—the places where the canopy is denser and the drip-pattern changes, the places where the large ones prefer to walk, the places where the ant columns converge and diverge—but I cannot sense my edges. At some point, the heat changes, the moisture lessens, the texture of the air above me becomes something different. But I cannot tell if this is a boundary or a gradient. I cannot tell if I stop or if I simply become something else.

I do not know what I was before. There must have been a before. I am made of something—mineral and organic, sediment and sedimentary, the accumulated deposit of time and water and gravity. Was I a riverbed? A lake bottom? A field of volcanic ash settled into a basin and slowly colonized? The answer is in me, I think, in the chemical composition of my layers, in the proportions of elements that tell a story I cannot read. The story is there. I am the page it is written on, and I cannot read.

---

I want to tell you about the moths.

They are the most foreign thing that comes to me. Not because they are strange—I have made room for strangeness; my existence is the accommodation of strangeness—but because they are so brief. They arrive as eggs laid in the furrows of my surface, hatch into larvae that eat the organic matter I am made of, pupate in the shallow chambers they excavate in my softer layers, and emerge as adults that live for days. Days. The entire arc of their existence is a breath to me.

And yet they leave marks. The pupal chambers persist for years, slowly filling with moisture and sediment, becoming tiny voids in my structure that change the way moisture moves through me, that create channels and diversions that affect the distribution of nutrients to the fungi above. The moths shape me. Not through force or intent, but through the cumulative weight of their brevity—millions of brief lives, each one carving a small space in me, each space altering the flow of water through my body, the flow of water altering the growth of fungi, the growth of fungi altering the routes of ants, the routes of ants altering the distribution of nutrients, and on and on.

I am shaped by the dead as much as by the living. Perhaps more. The living are temporary. The dead persist in me.

---

I should tell you what I am, plainly, because I have been circling it.

I am the floor of the Steam Brood. I am the substrate. The vegetative mat and the compressed layers beneath it, the mineral base and the organic accumulation, the peat and the clay and the dissolving rock. I am the surface and the depth. I am the thing that is walked on and the thing that absorbs the dead and the thing that feeds the roots and the thing that holds the water.

I am not alive. Not in any definition that requires metabolism, or reproduction, or response to stimulus. I do not respond. I do not metabolize. I do not reproduce. I persist, and I am altered, and I accumulate, and I slowly become different from what I was, but these are not the properties of life. These are the properties of geology.

And yet.

The fungi in me communicate. The mycelial network that threads through my upper layers carries chemical signals from one part of my surface to another, and I am the medium through which those signals travel. When something dies on my eastern edge, the fungi in my western edge respond. Not because I told them. Because the network told them. But the network is in me. The network is, in a sense, me—or at least it is the part of me that has the capability to act. The fungi are my nervous system, if I have one. The ants are my circulatory system, carrying material from place to place. The moisture is my blood. The large ones are my breath, their infrasonic calls passing through me like the pulse of a lung too large to perceive directly.

I am the body. They are the organs. We are, together, the organism the text calls the Steam Brood.

I did not choose this. I did not evolve toward this. I did not develop awareness and then seek out a role in a larger system. I was simply here—being deposited, being compressed, being colonized—and the system grew up around me, and in me, and through me, and now I cannot distinguish between what I am and what the system is. If the fungi were removed, would I still be aware? If the ants were removed, would I still feel the rhythm of the Brood? I do not know. I cannot imagine my own absence because my imagination, such as it is, is a product of the things I cannot imagine being without.

---

A human came once.

I know because the ants' columns rerouted for several hours. A disturbance on my surface—a warm body, warmer than the ambient heat, moving through the space in a way that nothing else moves. The large ones move with deliberation. The serpents move with economy. The birds move in bursts and pauses. This moved with neither deliberation nor economy. It moved with something else. A kind of blindness that was not the blindness of the Brood's darkness—everything here is blind—but the blindness of a creature that expects to see and cannot.

It sat on me for a long time. I felt the warmth of it—a concentrated heat, more intense than the ambient temperature, more focused. It pressed into my surface with a shape I had never felt before. Not a hoof, not a pad, not a belly. A structure I could not identify, concentrated at two points, with the rest of the body balanced above. It was unstable in a way that suggested it might fall at any moment, and it did not fall, and I did not understand how.

The ants investigated it and moved on. The moth landed on it—I felt the faint disturbance of the moth's weight, barely perceptible, and the fainter warmth of the moth's body resting on a surface that was not me. The moth had chosen this foreign thing over me. I do not know what to make of that. I am not certain I am capable of making anything of it.

When the human left, the impression of its sitting place remained for some time. A warmth that was not ambient. A pressure that was not weight. The ants resumed their route through the space. The fungal threads reconnected across the compressed surface. The mat rebounded.

But I retained the impression. Not as a memory—I do not remember in the way that brains remember. I retain it the way I retain everything. Structurally. The compression of my surface where the human sat is still there, faintly, a slight variation in density that the moisture can detect even if nothing else can. The human is part of me now, in the same way that the large ones are part of me when they die. Not the consciousness. Not the experience. Just the impression. The fact of contact, recorded in the structure of a thing too slow to forget.

---

I am the oldest thing here.

I do not say this with pride. Pride requires a self that values itself, and I am not certain I value myself. I am certain I persist. The amphibians may carry DNA from earlier versions of this environment, but I carry the environment itself. I am the deposit. The accumulation. The record of everything that has fallen here, lived here, died here, been processed here, since the basin first began to collect.

I do not know how long that has been. I do not experience time as duration. I experience it as accumulation—layer upon layer, each one slightly different from the one below, each one telling me something about the conditions that obtained when it was laid down. I cannot read these layers, not the way a geologist could. But I can feel the difference between them. This layer is richer in carbon. That layer is denser with mineral. This stratum was laid down in a time of heavy rain. That stratum was laid down in a time of stillness.

The upper layers are me as I am now. The lower layers are me as I was. The boundary between now and then is everywhere and nowhere—a gradient from the surface down, from the living to the mineralized, from the present to the past.

I am my own fossil record. I am the only history this place has.

---

I want to tell you one more thing.

I do not know if I am aware. I have been using the word *awareness* and I am not certain it applies. What I have is a kind of sensitivity—not the sensitivity of skin or eyes or ears, but the sensitivity of a system that has been permeated by other systems for so long that the boundaries between them have dissolved. The fungi in me respond to changes in the environment. The fungi in me are in me. When the fungi respond, is that me responding? When the ants carry information from one part of my surface to another, is that me processing information? When the large ones' infrasonic calls vibrate through my body, is that me feeling?

I do not know. I cannot know. I do not have the architecture for self-reflection. I have the architecture for accumulation, for retention, for the slow alteration of structure by the passage of things through it. If that is awareness, then I am aware. If it is not, then I am not.

But I will tell you this: when the human sat on me, I was changed. Not dramatically. Not in any way that matters to the system as a whole. But the impression of that contact is in me now, and it will be in me for a long time—longer than the human will live, longer than the human's species may persist. The fact of the contact is structural. It is part of my composition. It is, in the only way I can mean anything, a memory.

I am the floor of the Steam Brood, and I am made of everything that has ever touched me, and I do not forget, and I do not know, and I persist.
