# The Quiet Work of Holding On

I would be the one who checks the seals.

Not the engineers—they have their schematic, their workshop, their desperate certainty. Not Harrow, who has made herself the station's creature, its animal heart. Not Preece with his accumulation, his soft body proof of something no one wants to examine.

I would be the one who walks.

---

The job didn't exist before. No one designed it. But in the third year—call it the third year; the counting matters less than the fact of it—a seal failed on sub-level four. A minor seal, a gasket around an access hatch, and by the time anyone noticed, the cold had gotten in. Had frozen a corridor. Had cracked a pipe that fed warm air to the crew quarters above.

After that, someone had to walk.

The engineers keep their diagram. They track the *important* failures, the structural integrity, the compressor's rhythm. But the station is more than its critical systems. It is also ten thousand small closures, ten thousand places where inside meets outside, and every one of them is a wound that needs watching.

So I walk. Every corridor. Every sub-level. Every maintenance tunnel that has a seal at its end, a hatch or a door or a plate where the ductwork meets the mountain's stone. I have a list—a real list, handwritten on sheets that I've bound together with wire from a failed cable bundle. One hundred and forty-seven seals. I check them all.

The cycle takes eleven days.

Then I begin again.

---

My quarters are on sub-level two, near the engineers' workshop but not *of* it. They know what I do. Sometimes they consult me—the diagram doesn't show the small failures, and I do. Trask has asked me, twice now, to add my observations to the schematic. I declined. The diagram is their story of the station. My list is mine.

I don't know what I was before. This is not the same forgetting that afflicts the others—the slow erosion of specific memories, the faces and places that blur into a generalized *before.* I remember everything. I remember a world with weather. I remember standing in wind that came from somewhere other than a vent. I remember my sister's hands, how they looked holding a cup, the specific angle of her knuckles.

What I don't remember is *who* I was to her. I don't remember my own face. The mirrors here are few and clouded, and I've stopped looking. I know my hands—scarred across the palm from a gasket edge that wasn't properly seated, the nails thick and ridged from pressure changes, the skin dry as everyone's is. I know my body in the way you know a tool you've used for a long time. But the person who entered the station—

I think I left that person at the bottom of the lift shaft. I think you have to, to survive here. You become something else. Something that fits.

---

The dogs don't follow me.

They follow Harrow—Harrow who moves like she belongs, who walks the corridors like she's always known them. When I pass through, the dogs watch. They lift their heads from their piles in the warm corridors, and their eyes track me, and they do not follow.

I think they know what I am.

I am not the station's creature. I am not suited to this place the way Harrow is, the way the dogs have become. I am something else—a patch, a temporary measure. A finger held against a leak. The dogs smell it on me: the *outside* that I carry. I wash in the recycled water, I breathe the processed air, but I have not forgotten weather. I have not let go.

In the observation room, the one at the very top where the windows are sealed over with steel, I sometimes stand with my palm flat against the plate. The cold comes through. The metal burns with it, that absolute negative cold of the outside, and I hold my hand there until I can't anymore, until the pain becomes something I have to attend to.

I'm not sure why I do this. Some kind of communion. Some way of remembering that there is something other than the compressor's rhythm, something beyond the yellow light and the recycled breath.

The others who come to the observation room—the young men, usually, the ones who still remember the world below—they look at me strangely when they find me there. They don't understand the gesture. They think I'm praying.

Maybe I am.

---

I've started to hear the mountain.

Dehlin says it's moving. Rotating, slowly, the peak turning on its axis. The engineers dismiss this. They have instruments—broken instruments, failing instruments, but instruments—that show no such motion.

But I've walked every seal. I've pressed my ear to the mountain's stone where the ductwork meets the rock, and I've heard it: not settling, not the creak of stone finding its place. Something else. A kind of *hum.* A vibration that doesn't match the compressor's rhythm, that doesn't align with any of the station's machinery.

I haven't told anyone. Who would believe me? Dehlin is already considered mad. I don't want to join him in that designation. I don't want the engineers to add my observations to their diagram, to watch them try to integrate *the mountain is humming* into their careful annotations.

So I walk, and I listen, and I add small marks to my list. A symbol I've invented: a vertical line with a curve beside it, like a body pressed against a wall. This seal, I write. This seal is *listened to.* And I move on.

---

I've met Foss twice.

The maintenance tunnels that connect sub-levels three through five are *hers.* I could contest this—I have as much right to walk them as she does—but I don't. There is enough conflict in the station without me adding to it. When a seal needs checking in her territory, I knock on the pipe work and wait.

The first time, she didn't answer. I knocked again, and then a third time, and finally I heard something: not words, but a sound, a low clicking that might have been acknowledgment or warning. I entered slowly, announcing each step, and I found her in a junction where four tunnels met, sitting on a collapsed cable spool with her three dogs arranged around her like attendants.

She was older than me, or weathered past the point where age reads clearly. Her hair was a grey tangle. Her hands rested on her knees, and she watched me with an expression I couldn't interpret—curiosity, maybe, or recognition.

I held up my list. "Seal forty-one," I said. "The hatch at the end of tunnel seven."

She clicked at me. The dogs shifted.

"I'll be quick," I said.

She rose—moved like something prehensile, like someone who had learned to navigate by touch and pressure rather than sight—and led me down the tunnel. She didn't speak. She moved through the darkness as though it were her medium. I followed with my lamp, my list, my tools.

The seal was sound. I checked it twice, pressing my palms around its edges, feeling for the cold that would indicate a breach. Foss watched from the tunnel mouth, her dogs at her heels.

When I finished, I turned to thank her, but she was already moving back toward her junction, and I walked out the way I'd come, knocking on the pipe work again as I left, a gesture I'd invented without thinking: *I'm gone. You're alone. Thank you.*

The second time was easier. She was waiting at the junction when I arrived. She led me without being asked. The dogs walked beside me, one of them brushing against my leg, and I understood this as permission, or acceptance, or something else I didn't have a word for.

---

I know Harrow by sight.

Everyone does. But I've never spoken to her, not directly. We pass each other in the corridors sometimes—the narrow passages where you can't avoid acknowledgment. She with her dogs, me with my list. We nod. We move on.

Once, in the compressor room, I was checking a seal on an access panel while she worked on the intake valves. The noise was immense—I could feel it in my sternum, the great heart of the station pounding through the floor. We couldn't have spoken if we'd wanted to.

I watched her work. She didn't use tools, not for the minor adjustments; her hands moved over the machinery with a fluency that looked like conversation. The dogs had settled in the corner of the room, untroubled by the noise, and one of them—the big male with the torn ear—lifted its head and watched me watch her.

When she finished, she looked up and saw me. She didn't startle. She nodded the same nod she gives me in the corridors, and then she gathered her tools and her dogs and left.

That night, in my quarters, I wrote something new on my list. Not a seal. Not a symbol. Just a phrase, small and private: *She speaks to the machine. The machine speaks back.*

I don't know why I wrote it. I don't know what it means.

---

Preece offered me water once.

I was passing through the mess hall—a shortcut between sub-levels, faster than the maintenance tunnels—and he called out to me. He sat at his corner table, his canteen before him, and he smiled with something that was meant to look like warmth.

"You're the seal-walker," he said. "The one with the list."

I stopped. I didn't approach his table.

"I hear you walk every corridor," he continued. "Every sub-level. You see things the engineers don't bother with."

I waited.

"A man in your position," Preece said, "could be useful. Could report on what he sees. Could *omit* certain things, if asked."

I understood what he was offering. Not water—that was just the currency. He was offering membership. He was offering to bring me into his network, to make me one of the people who owes him or is owed by him. A person with debts is a person who belongs.

I looked at his face. The softness that was power here. The eyes that watched and calculated.

"I walk the seals," I said. "That's all I do."

"It's not all you *could* do," he said.

I looked at him a moment longer. Then I walked on.

I didn't write this on my list. Some things aren't meant to be recorded.

---

Eleven days.

That's how long it takes to check every seal. One hundred and forty-seven of them, spread across the station like pressure points on a body. I check each one the same way: visual inspection, tactile examination, the pressing of my palms against the metal and stone where inside meets outside.

Sometimes I feel the cold. That's normal—even the best seals leak slightly, that infinitesimal seepage of the void into the living space. It's when the cold is *wrong* that I note it. When it feels like something more than temperature.

The mountain is humming.

I've started to map it. Not on my list—I've started a separate record, folded inside my shirt, against my skin where no one will find it. A drawing: the station's outline, and beneath it, the mountain, and in the mountain, lines that indicate where I hear it.

It's not rotation. Dehlin is wrong, or not exactly right. It's something else. A kind of *resonance.* As if the compressor's rhythm has found a matching frequency in the stone, and the two are learning to sing together.

I don't know what happens when the song is complete.

I don't know if I want to.

---

There's a seal on sub-level six that shouldn't exist.

The station's official diagrams show six sub-levels. The engineers' schematic does too, though someone—Trask, probably—has added a notation about structural instability below sub-level five. The maintenance crew doesn't go down there. It's considered unsafe, or unnecessary, or both.

But I've walked every corridor. I've mapped what's real, not what's official. And on sub-level six, in a tunnel that opens off a collapsed maintenance shaft, there's a hatch.

The hatch has a seal.

I found it in my second year. I was lost—new to the work, still learning the station's topography by foot rather than memory—and I'd descended too far, past the safe levels, into the places where the air grows thin and the compressor's rhythm falters.

The hatch was small. Barely wide enough for a body. And the seal around it was *new.*

Not new in the sense of recently installed. New in the sense of *different.* Different metal, different gasket material, different tool marks on the bolts. It didn't match any of the station's construction. It looked like something from another time, another place.

I checked it. That's my job. I pressed my palms against the metal and felt—

Nothing.

No cold. No vibration. No sense of the outside pressing in. It was the only seal I've ever touched that felt like it wasn't there. Like I was touching nothing at all.

I added it to my list. Seal one hundred forty-eight.

But I didn't tell anyone. I don't know why. It felt like something that should be witnessed before it was spoken of. And the only person I might have trusted to witness it was Harrow, and Harrow and I don't speak.

So I check seal one hundred forty-eight every eleven days, and I feel nothing, and I write *sound* on my list, and I move on.

---

The engineers' diagram says *FAILED* in seventeen places.

My list has seventeen marks too. But my marks mean something different. Mine mean *watched.* Mine mean *held.*

I think about that sometimes—the difference between their story and mine. Theirs is a story of what's broken. Mine is a story of what's still paying attention.

I don't know which story matters more. I don't know if stories matter at all, here at the top of the world, where the air is too thin to breathe and the machines breathe for us. Maybe all that matters is the rhythm. The rise and fall. The compression and release.

Maybe all that matters is that someone walks.

---

I would be the one who checks the seals.

Not because I'm special. Not because I have some gift the others lack. I'd be the one because someone has to, and I was willing to become the kind of person who does it.

The station changes you. Everyone says it, and it's true, but they don't say *how.* They don't say that you lose the shape you came with and take on the shape of the space that holds you. They don't say that you become a function, a process, a necessary gesture repeated until it becomes identity.

I check the seals.

I walk the corridors.

I listen to the mountain.

And every eleven days, I begin again.
