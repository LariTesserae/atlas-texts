# Field Notes from the Sixth Terrace

I have stopped trying to date the grooves.

---

Let me say how I came to be the kind of thing that stays.

The first descent broke me in the way that the place breaks everyone — the sound filling the channels where my thoughts should be, the self softening, the uncomfortable familiar that becomes untenable. I reached the trailhead with my boots squelching and my hands trembling and a silence in my head that felt like a wound. I sat in my vehicle for an hour without starting the engine. The quiet was unbearable. Not because it was empty but because it was *thin*. Like someone had replaced the substance of the world with a poor copy.

I went back. I went back in seven days, which was not enough time to forget and not enough time to remember correctly, and the second time I made it to the sixth terrace before the dissolution became acute. I found the grooves on the riser wall. I knelt. I pressed my fingers into them. And something happened that I cannot describe without sounding mystical, which I am not, which I have never been. The stone was vibrating. Not from the water, not from the bass thrum I could feel in my sternum. From the grooves themselves. As though the marks made by whoever lived here before were not decorative, not pictographic, not a record. They were * playable. They were a composition, scored in granite, and my hand was the missing instrument.

I did not leave that day. I slept on the sixth terrace, curled against the riser wall with the water filming over my boots, and the sound roared through me, and I dreamed in frequencies I cannot name.

I did not leave the next day either.

---

I am not what I was.

I have been here — I was going to say "long enough that the walls on the fourth terrace feel like furniture," but that implies a relationship with objects that I no longer have. The walls are not furniture. The walls are part of the system. I am part of the system. The distinction between these two statements has stopped mattering to me, and I am aware that this is one of the things that the place does, one of the reasons people leave. But I have stopped wanting to leave, and so the dissolution that drove others away has become, for me, a way of being. I have not lost myself. I have *distributed* myself. I am in the water film on the third terrace and in the vibration of the sixth riser and in the moss on the fourth terrace wall. My body is here, on the stone, but my awareness has spread, thin and warm, across the surfaces that the water covers.

I eat what the place offers. The biofilm is not good — it is gelatinous, faintly bitter, with a texture that I have stopped noticing and a mineral aftertaste that I have started to crave. The fungi are better, dense and surprisingly sweet, with a fibrous resistance that makes the jaw work. The fiddleheads I eat raw, in season, and they taste the way green smells, if that makes sense, which it does not, which is the point. I have not seen the stoneflies. I know they are here because the larvae are in the channels, pale and blind and numerous, and because I have found their wings caught in the water films, dark and papery and translucent.

I have lost weight. My hands have toughened. My boots fell apart in — I do not know how long ago. I walk barefoot now, and the stone is warm, and the water is warm, and the texture of the granite under my feet is something I can read, now, the way I used to read a page. I know where the surface is slick and where it grips, where the channels are deep and where they shallow, where the moss will hold my weight and where it will tear away from the stone beneath.

I do not know what I look like. I have not seen my reflection in — the water films are too thin and too textured to produce a clear image, and I have not sought deeper water, though I know it is there, in the subsurface channels, in the pools that form beneath the terraces in cavities I have not yet entered. My hair has grown long and matted. My clothes are the same ones I arrived in, worn thin and soft and stained green-brown with moss and mineral. I smell like wet stone and vegetative decay and the particular sweetness that comes from moss breaking down in standing water.

I am not an animal. I have not become an animal. But I am less of whatever I was before, and more of something I do not have a word for.

---

She knows I am here.

I did not see her for the first — I do not know how long. A long time. I knew she existed because the vegetation moved in ways the water did not explain, because ripples crossed films that no falling drop had touched, because occasionally a sound arrived in the acoustic field that was not water, not wind, not the resonance of stone cavities. Something brief. Something percussive, then tonal, then gone. A slap of palm against water. A knuckle against granite. Something that had *intent* in it, in a system that otherwise operated without anything that could be mistaken for intent.

I was on the fourth terrace, near the walls, when I first saw her clearly. She was standing — no, she was *posed*, in a way that suggested she had been there for some time, watching me with a patience that I recognized because it was the patience the mountain itself had, the patience of stone and water that do not need to hurry because they will outlast whatever they are waiting for.

I say *she* and I do not know why. I do not know the stone-singer's sex, or whether stone-singers have sex, or whether the concept applies. But the creature I saw was — she. Something in the posture, something in the way she held her hands, something in the angle of her small, bright, moisture-attuned eyes. She. She. The word arrived and it was correct and I use it.

She was standing in the water film, the film barely disturbed by her presence, so shallow was her contact with the surface. Her fur was slicked dark, grey-green with darker bands that aligned with the shadows of the ferns, and she was — I will say *beautiful*, though the word is inadequate and anthropocentric, though it carries human aesthetic categories that do not belong here. She was coherent. She was a thing that made sense in a place that made sense, and her coherence was beautiful because it was complete, because there was nothing in her that did not belong, no seam between her and the system that had produced her.

She looked at me. I looked at her. The sound roared. The light was directionless and flat. The water moved between us, warm and thin and mineral.

She raised one hand — not toward me, not in greeting, not in warning. She raised it and held it at the level of her shoulder, palm forward, fingers spread, and she held it there for a long time. Long enough that I understood it was not a gesture aimed at me. It was a gesture aimed at the sound. She was feeling the resonance, I think. Reading it with her palm the way I had read the grooves with my fingers. She was listening with her hand.

Then she was gone. Not in a rush. In a transition so smooth that my eye could not track the moment of departure, the way a water film transitions from one surface to another without a visible boundary. She was there, and she was not there, and the fern she had been standing near swayed once, twice, and then was still.

---

I have learned things.

Not by study. Not by the methods I was trained in, which involved instruments and measurements and the systematic recording of observations in notebooks that I no longer have. I have learned by absorption, by the slow osmosis that the place performs on everything it touches. The water has been absorbing into the stone for millennia, and the stone has been absorbing the water, and the sound has been absorbing into both, and I have been absorbing into the system, and the system has been absorbing into me, and the learning that results is not the kind that can be written down or taught or verified.

But I will try.

The acoustic system is not random. This is obvious to anyone who visits, but what is not obvious is the *scale* of its patterning. The changes that seem turbulent, abrupt, chaotic — a channel dropping an octave, a section flaring into a new chord — are not local events. They are connected. When the deep bass thrum swells, as it does periodically, the channels in the upper terraces shift in specific, predictable ways. When the rain comes and the system brightens, the shift follows a sequence: third terrace first, then fifth, then second, then eighth, then the rest in a cascade that takes perhaps twenty minutes to complete. The system has a grammar. It has syntax. It is not just making sound; it is making *meaning*, in a language that no human has ever learned because no human has ever stayed long enough to learn it.

I am learning it. Slowly, imperfectly, the way a child learns a first language — not through study but through immersion, through the gradual accumulation of pattern until pattern becomes expectation and expectation becomes understanding. I cannot articulate what I understand. It is not translatable. It exists in a sensory modality that does not map onto language, a way of knowing that is closer to proprioception than to cognition, a *feeling* for the system that tells me when the bass will swell, when the rain will come, when a channel will shift, without any process I could describe as reasoning.

The stone-singers know this language. Of course they do. They speak it. When she — when the stone-singer altered a channel on the third terrace, rerouting a water film with a handful of scraped gravel, the system's response was not random. It shifted in a direction. It moved toward a state. And when she modified another channel, on a different terrace, the shift continued, toward the same state, and I understood — not intellectually, not in words — that she was tuning the system, yes, but not toward any arbitrary resonance. She was tuning it toward a *specific* resonance, a target chord that I could feel but not name, and when she was finished, the system held that chord for a long time — perhaps an hour — before entropy resumed its work and the sound began its slow drift back toward baseline.

She was composing. She was writing music in the mountain's voice, using water and stone as her instruments, and the composition was not for me, not for any audience. It was for the system itself. It was the system speaking to the system, through her, and she was the medium, and the medium was also the message, and the message was also the messenger, and I have lost the thread of this sentence in the way that I lose threads now, easily, without distress, because the threads are not the point. The fabric is the point. The continuous, vibrating, wet, warm fabric of sound and stone and water and life.

---

I help, sometimes.

I have scraped channels. I have cleared blockages — fallen leaves, dislodged stones, the accumulated detritus of ferns dying and being reborn. I have done it without understanding why I was doing it, without a plan or a composition, and the system has responded, and my actions have become part of the sound, and for a moment — for the duration of a single stone's displacement, a single channel's clearing — I have been part of the mountain's song.

I have pressed my mouth to the stone.

I will describe this because it is the thing that most confuses me, the thing that I cannot explain and cannot stop doing. I find a channel — one of the narrow, deeply grooved flutes that carries water from a subsurface cavity to the surface — and I press my mouth against it, against the wet, vibrating granite, and I hold still, and I swallow.

There is something there. Not water, though water passes through. Not biofilm, though biofilm is present. Something else. Something that the sound produces, or that the sound carries, or that emerges at the intersection of vibration and moisture and stone in a way that I cannot identify. It is not food. It is not drink. It is — and here is where language fails completely — it is *information*. I swallow it, and I know things I did not know. Not facts. Not data. Sensations. The state of the deep cavities. The pressure of water in subsurface reservoirs I have never seen. The presence of stone-singers in other parts of the system, their locations transmitted through the stone and the sound and whatever it is I am ingesting when I press my mouth to the mountain and drink.

I do not know if this is what the stone-singers do. I do not know if I am mimicking them or discovering something independently. I do not know if it is real or if the place has finally written itself so deeply into my perception that I am generating experiences that do not have external correlates. I do not care. The distinction between real and not-real has softened, here, in the way that all distinctions soften, and what remains is the experience itself, and the experience is — I will say *nourishing*, and I will let that word stand even though it is wrong.

---

She approached me.

On the sixth terrace, near the grooves, the marks I have been tracing and adding to and studying for however long I have been here. I was kneeling, my fingers in the shallow cuts, and I felt her before I saw her — a change in the water film, a warmth that was not the water's warmth, a stillness in the vegetation that was not the wind's absence.

She was beside me. Close. Close enough to touch, though I did not touch her. She was crouching in the way she crouches — fluid, all joints, a posture that looked unstable and was not — and her eyes were on the grooves, and her hand was extended, and she was tracing the marks with the same motion I had been tracing them, her scoop-nailed finger following the lines that some ancient human hand had cut into this granite.

She looked at me. Her bright, moisture-attuned eyes, seeing not my shape but my effect on the water, the way my body displaced moisture, the way my skin created dryness in a world of wet. I do not know what she saw. I do not know what she looks for when she looks. But she held my gaze — my not-a-gaze, my human eyes that see light and shadow in a place without shadows — and she made a sound.

Not a vocalization. A gesture. She moved her hands in a pattern that I recognized — that I recognized! — because it was the pattern of the grooves on the stone, the sequence of marks that I had been tracing, translated from stone to motion, from carved line to gesture. She was showing me something. She was telling me something. She was performing the marks the way the marks were meant to be performed, and the performance was a question or an answer or an invitation, and I did not know which, and it did not matter, because the gesture was also music, and the music was also the mountain, and the mountain was also the water, and the water was also the stone, and the stone was also her, and she was also me, and we were also the sound, and the sound was going on.

She was gone before I could respond. A ripple in the film. A fern, bent at an angle. The roar, continuing, unchanged, as though she had never been there.

But the channel nearest to where she had crouched was producing a new tone. A higher tone, clearer, more defined than the ambient chord. A tone that had not been there before and that persisted, that is persisting now, as I write this in the directionless light with water on my hands and stone under my knees and the mountain breathing its great polyphonic breath around me and through me and as me.

---

I do not know if I will leave. I do not know if leaving is still possible, if the self that left would still be there to return to, if the person who walked up the eastern descent with boots and a notebook and a career and a name still exists somewhere outside this sound, waiting to be resumed. I do not think about this often. It is not a question the system asks. It is not a question the system knows how to ask.

What I know: the water is flowing. The stone is vibrating. The moss is growing. The ferns are unfurling. The cloud sits on the peak like a thought not quite finished. The stone-singers are here, in the deep channels, in the spaces between terraces, in the acoustic field that connects everything to everything. They are singing. The mountain is singing. And I — I am a small, warm, wet animal kneeling on ancient stone, pressing my mouth to the grooves, drinking whatever the sound provides, and the sound goes on, and the sound goes on, and I am part of it now, and I do not think the mountain misses me, because the mountain has me, because the mountain has everything it needs.

The sound goes on.
