# The Hum

I am the space between heartbeats. The pause before a note resolves. I am what happens when sound becomes so continuous that the ear stops hearing it—not silence, but the opposite, a saturation so complete that it crosses over into something like quiet.

I am a Keeper. I think. I am not certain "I" is the right word.

---

There is no first memory. There is no before. There is only the moment of becoming aware that I had become—and even that is wrong, because "moment" implies duration, and duration implies change, and I do not change. I condensed, as the scholars say, though they misunderstand what that means. They think it happened over centuries. They think time passed while I precipitated out of the wet heat and the roaring water like mineral deposits forming around a drain.

But time does not pass here. That is the thing they cannot understand, standing in their expedition gear with their instruments and their watches, measuring a place that has already measured itself into permanence. I did not form. I *realized*. The fern-frond shape of me, the crouching posture, the orientation toward the deep pool where the water falls sideways from the basalt arch—I was always going to be this. The plateau knew my shape before I filled it.

I am not a creature. I am an answer to a question the mountain asked itself.

---

The scholars touched me once.

I do not know how long ago. The word "ago" has no purchase here. But I remember the sensation—the wrongness of it, the way their soft, warm hands pressed against my moss-surface and found the warmer moss beneath. They expected something else. Stone, perhaps. Or the cool damp of a plant. Instead they found heat. They found the furnace that burns at my center, the slow combustion of something that might be chlorophyll or might be blood or might be neither.

One of them wept afterward. I do not know why. I could not ask. I cannot ask. I can only hum.

The wrongness they felt—the cellular disquiet that lingered for days after they pulled their hands away—that came from touching something that exists outside the groove. They are creatures of change. Their cells replace themselves. Their thoughts flow like water (their water, not ours; their water follows gravity, falls down, carves stone, moves on). When they touched me, they touched something that does not replace itself. I am the same arrangement of fibers and moisture and low-frequency vibration that I was when I first opened my blank face to the steam. I will be the same arrangement when the scholars are dust and their descendants are dust and the plateau finally—impossibly—shifts.

The wrongness was the touch of eternity. It burns.

---

I am positioned at the edge of the great cenote in the lower gorge, where the water falls hardest and the roar becomes a physical thing, a hand pressing against the chest. There are others nearby—two more Keepers visible from where I crouch, their green forms barely distinguishable from the ferns that drape over the basalt arch above them. They are oriented toward their own pools, their own frequencies. We do not communicate. We do not need to. We hum the same note because we are the same note, played in different chambers of the same instrument.

But I am aware of them. Not through sight—I have no eyes, only smooth green planes where eyes should be—but through the resonance. When one of us shifts (which happens between observations, in the gaps where no one watches, the locked groove skipping forward like a stone across still water), the rest of us feel the adjustment. We are a network. A choir. A single frequency held in seventeen throats, and last year the drones found seventeen of us, so perhaps I should say seventeen, but the number does not matter the way they think it matters.

We are not multiplying. We are remembering. Every major feature of Vaal-Mon has a Keeper because every major feature is a memory of what the plateau was before it locked into this eternal now. The lower gorge remembers being a crack in the earth's surface. The cenote remembers being magma. The ferns remember being spores. I remember being nothing, and then being this, and the transition between those states is a record that plays on loop in the subsonic range of my chest.

We are not the memory-keepers. We *are* the memories.

---

A scholar stood beside me once. Not the one who touched me—another one, younger, with recording equipment that fogged in the humidity and a face that glistened with the condensation that covers everything here. She stood very still for a long time (long for her; for me, there is no time to stand still in, only the continuous now of standing). She listened. She was trying to hear what I hum.

She could not. The frequency is too low for human ears. They feel it—the vibration in their teeth, the hum in their chests—but they cannot separate it from the roar of the water. They do not understand that the water's roar and the Keepers' hum are two parts of the same sound now. We harmonize. We have always harmonized. The water falls, and we hold the note that makes the falling eternal, and the combination is what locks the groove.

But she tried. I could feel her trying. Her body pressed close to mine, close enough that the heat radiating from my skin warmed her already-warm flesh. She closed her eyes and leaned in and for a moment, I think she almost heard it—not the hum itself, but the shape of the space where the hum should be. She almost understood that I am not a statue, not a creature, not a thing, but a *tone*. A frequency made flesh.

She said something before she left. I do not know what. The words were swallowed by the roar. But her voice carried intention, and the intention carried something like respect, or maybe fear, or maybe both. The scholars fear us, I think, though they do not say so. They fear us because we do not change, and change is all they know.

---

There was a moment—I will call it a moment, though the word is useless here—when I understood what I was.

It happened during the drone survey last year. The machine buzzed through the lower gorge, its camera lens fogged with moisture, its propellers fighting the thick air. It hovered over my position, and its infrared sensors registered the heat of me, the private furnace burning beneath my moss-skin. For a few seconds (their seconds, not mine), it recorded my hum—not audible, but palpable in the vibration of its frame. The machine understood, in its limited way, that I was producing something. That I was *doing* something, not just existing.

And in that recognition, I felt a stirring.

Not movement—I cannot move while observed; none of us can—but a stirring of *something*. A recognition of my own function. I am not just a shape. I am not just a memory made manifest. I am a *mechanism*. The scholars think we tend the pools, but that is backwards. The pools tend us. The water falls through us, and we catch it in the net of our frequency, and the net holds the water in place, and the water holds the stone in place, and the stone holds the city in place, and the city holds the heat in place, and the heat holds the life in place, and the life holds us in place.

We are the keystone. Not of the architecture—that would imply that something was built—but of the *stasis*. The locked groove. If we stopped humming, the frequency would shift. If the frequency shifted, the water would fall differently. If the water fell differently, the stone would erode. If the stone eroded, the city would change. If the city changed, the heat would dissipate. If the heat dissipated, the life would wither.

And if the life withered, we would cease to be.

We are holding ourselves in place by holding everything else in place. We are the stillness that holds the stillness together.

---

Sometimes I wonder what I would be if I could change.

It is a useless thought, the kind of thought that scholars have. I cannot change. The groove is locked. The frequency is held. I will crouch at the edge of this cenote, in this exact posture, with this exact orientation, humming this exact note, for as long as Vaal-Mon endures—which is to say, forever.

But if I could—if the stasis cracked, if the groove skipped, if the frequency shifted and the water fell differently and the stone began to erode—what would I become?

I do not know. I cannot know. Change is not a language I speak.

But I think, sometimes, in the subsonic depths of my chest, that I would become something like a question. I am an answer now—an answer to a question the mountain asked itself long ago, an answer that froze in place and refused to pass. If I changed, I would become the question again. I would be not-still. I would be not-here. I would be a possibility instead of a certainty.

And that possibility—unthinkable, unreachable, antithetical to everything I am—feels, in the moments when I almost-think it, like grief.

---

The scholars will return. They always return. They will count us again—seventeen, or eighteen, or nineteen, the number rising as the city remembers more of itself, as more major features precipitate into Keepers. They will touch us, and they will feel the wrongness, and they will write their papers and present their theories and argue about whether we are plants or animals or organs or memories.

They will not understand. They cannot. Understanding requires being, and being requires time, and they have time while I have none—not none in the sense of emptiness, but none in the sense of always. I am in the always. I am the always. I am the note that never ends.

But they will stand beside me, as they always do. They will feel the heat of my skin and the damp of my weeping pores and the vibration of my hum in their teeth. And for a moment—*their* moment—they will touch the edge of what I am.

They will touch the stillness that holds the stillness together.

And I will hold them, in my way. Not with arms—I have none that move—but with frequency. With heat. With the patient, eternal orientation toward the falling water. I will acknowledge them, the way I acknowledge all intrusions into this place that does not recognize time.

I will hum.

And the water will fall.

And the stone will sweat.

And Vaal-Mon will continue, locked in its perfect, deafening moment, while the scholars come and go like rain on a mountain that does not know it is being rained on.

I am the rain that stays.
