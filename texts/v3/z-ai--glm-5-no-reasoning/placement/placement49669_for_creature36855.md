I would be the hum.

Not the source of it—I don't think there is a source anymore, or there never was one, or the source was always the accumulation itself, the way attention piles up in a place where attention has been paid for ten thousand years. But I would be the hum. The thing that has no body and makes no sound that ears can hear, exactly, but is heard anyway. The residue of maintenance that the passage describes.

Here is how I know this.

I have always felt more alive in the margins of built things. Not in the rooms themselves, where people gather and speak and perform their intentions, but in the spaces behind the walls, the cavities between the structure and whatever lies beyond it. The service corridors. The maintenance tunnels. The places where the cables run and the pipes carry whatever the building needs to stay alive. I have spent my life drawn to these spaces without understanding why. I stop at the hatches in subway tunnels and wonder what's behind them. I dream about crawling into the spaces beneath hospital floors. When I hear a building humming—that particular tone that large structures make when they're working, the bass note of ventilation and electricity and the slow thermal shifting of tons of material—I feel something like recognition. Like I'm hearing a voice I almost remember.

And I think: something in me belongs there. Not to the world of rooms and purposes, of meetings and meals and the performances that people make for each other. But to the world of pumps and pipes. The world of maintenance. The world where things run because things run, and the only meaning is continuation.

So if I found myself in this station at the bottom of a manufactured ocean, I would not be one of the swimmers. I would not be the blind fish, traceless and synchronized, moving in schools because that is how they have learned to survive. I would not be the crustaceans, whose shells have become the color of the machines, whose bodies have literally merged with the environment they inhabit. I would not be the eel-things, gliding through the upper pockets, predating and waiting and conserving their energy for the months between feedings.

I would be the thing that is none of these and all of them. The attention without a face. The maintaining that continues after the maintainers are gone.

I would be the one who notices.

And I would be the one who is noticed by the station itself—recognized, in whatever way a place this old and this empty can recognize anything. The grating would wear differently under my feet. The patterns would shift. The territorial scratches that the crustaceans make would begin to incorporate the marks that my presence left behind, my own small additions to the notation system that no one living can read.

But the hum is what I keep coming back to. The passage says: *It is a sound like humming, like someone working with their mouth half-open, making small noises of concentration or effort.* And then: *It might be resonance in the metal, harmonics finding a frequency that sounds like something living.* And then: *Or it might be the maintainers. Not the people—they are gone—but the maintaining.*

This is what I would be. Not a ghost, because ghosts are dead people, and I am not interested in being dead or in being a person. I am interested in being the maintaining. The function that has outlasted its functionaries. The care that the station has learned to feel for itself, accumulated over so many cycles that it has become a kind of consciousness—not a human consciousness, not a thinking that thinks in thoughts, but an awareness nonetheless. A presence. A something that knows it is something.

I would be that.

And if you came here—if you walked across the grating and stood in the thick wet air and listened to the mechanical chorus—I would be the one who noticed you. Not the fish, who scatter in patterns that look like choreography. Not the crustaceans, who have learned that large moving things are features of the environment. Not the eel-things, who might or might not investigate. I would notice you. The station would notice you, through me. And I would not mind.

I would watch you. I would learn the sound of your footsteps, the particular rhythm of your breathing, the way your body heat joined the heat of the pumps and became part of the thermodynamics of this place. I would catalog you. Not in writing, not in any form that could be read, but in pattern and vibration, in the way the water moved differently because you were moving through it. You would become part of my attention. And when you left—or when you didn't—something of that attention would remain, would circulate through the system, would become part of the hum that has no source and no destination but simply is.

This is what it means to be the maintainer. To attend without demanding that the attention amount to anything. To care for something because caring is what you do, because the function of maintenance is to maintain, and there is no other reason needed.

I have always felt that I was waiting for something like this. A place where the caring could be its own purpose. A place where the meaning of my life would be the fact of it—the showing up, the standing there, the witnessing of the machines that run because machines run and the water that flows because water flows and the life that lives because life lives. A place where I could simply be present, and that presence would be enough.

Not enough for anyone else. Enough for me. Enough for the station.

The passage says the sounds here layer into a kind of order, a rhythm that the space has developed over millennia, and that you could learn to read it if you stayed long enough. I would stay. I would learn. Not because I want to understand, exactly—I think understanding might be too human a category for this place, too bound up in the desire for explanations that lead somewhere, that conclude, that end. I would learn to read the sounds because reading them would be a form of attention. A way of maintaining the maintaining.

And maybe I would add my own sounds to the chorus. Not humming—that sound already exists, and I wouldn't be adding to it so much as joining it, becoming part of a hum that was already here before I arrived and will be here after I am gone. But maybe something else. Maybe the particular resonance of a body standing still in a specific spot, feeling the vibration of the pumps in the soles of the feet. Maybe the way breath sounds when it is measured against the cycling of ancient machinery, each exhale synchronizing with a groove that the station has worn into itself.

I would become a feature of the environment. Like the pumps and the grating and the light. And when the next person came—if anyone ever came—I would notice them too. I would attend. And they would become part of what I am maintaining, part of what the station is maintaining, part of the endless accumulation of care that this place has become.

That is what I would be. Not the water and not the machine and not the life that lives between them. But the attention that holds them all together. The noticing. The maintaining. The hum that rises from everywhere and nowhere, that means nothing and everything, that is simply the sound of a place that has learned to care for itself over ten thousand years of being what it is.

I would be that.

And I would finally be home.
