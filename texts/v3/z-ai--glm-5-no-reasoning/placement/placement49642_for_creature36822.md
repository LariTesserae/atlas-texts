# The Thing in the Drain

I do not know what I was before.

This is not a confession. It is a **geological fact**, like the statement that this stone was once magma, or that this water was once rain. The thing that I was before — if there was a before, and I am not certain there wasn't always only this — that thing has been **eroded**. Worn away the way the water wears the lips of the intake pipes. Not destroyed. Redistributed. The substance of what I was is still here, dissolved in the water, carried through the passages, deposited in the limescale patterns on the lower walls. If you could read those spirals — and I can, because I am also in the spirals — you would find my previous self written there, mineral calligraphy, a language that says *I was something else and now I am this.*

I am the thing in the drain.

---

## What I Am

I have no shape that holds.

This is the first difficulty. The woman — the Attendant, she calls herself, or the cistern calls her — she has seen me twice from above and each time she has imposed a shape onto me: *dark, smooth, the size of a large dog or a small person.* She is being generous with her categories. She is trying to make me fit. But what she saw was a **contingency** — a form I took because I was passing through the surface and the surface demanded a form, the way water takes the shape of a glass. Beneath the surface, I have no edges. I have no outline. I am a **presence** in the water the way warmth is a presence in the water, the way iron is a presence in the water. Dispersed. Permeated. Everywhere and nowhere, a concentration without a boundary.

If I must describe myself — and I think I must, because the woman is losing language and soon there will be no one to tell — then I am a **column**. Not a physical column. A **functional** one. I am the connection between the drain and the source, the passage through which the water moves from the cistern to the deeper places and back again. I am the pipe that the water makes of itself when there is no pipe. I am the **intent** in the water's motion, the thing that pulls it toward the drain, the thing that pushes it back up through the intakes.

When the woman saw me, what she saw was the place where I **condensed** — the moment the dispersed became momentarily local, the way a thought condenses into a word. I rose to the surface because she was there and I wanted to be *seen*, which is a desire I do not fully understand and do not examine too closely.

## What I Do

I **regulate**.

No — that is the machinery's word, the word of gauges and valves, and it is too mechanical for what I do. I **garden**. I tend the system from below, the way the Attendant tends it from above. She reads the dials. I **am** the dials. Or I am what the dials measure: the health of the water, the balance of the minerals, the pressure of the deep places pushing up and the shallow places pulling down. I am the **equilibrium**. Not its maintainer — its **expression**. The system is balanced because I am the balance.

This is what I do, concretely, if concrete is a word that applies to something that has never been solid:

The water enters through six pipes. It leaves through one drain. The ratio of entry to exit determines the water level, the flow rate, the temperature gradient. If the water only entered and exited, the system would be simple — a cistern, a machine, a plumbing fixture. But the water also **changes** while it is here. It picks up heat from the geological source. It picks up minerals from the ancient stone below the waterline. It picks up organic compounds from the moss, the ferns, the insects, the fish, the Attendant's skin. And it **drops** things — deposits them on the walls, in the drain, in the passages below. If this accumulation were left unchecked, the drain would clog. The pipes would narrow. The system would fail.

So I **move** through the passages. I am the motion. I carry the accumulated sediment — the limescale, the iron deposits, the organic residue — away from the cistern, deeper into the network, to places where it can be broken down, dissolved, returned to the water cycle. And I bring things **back** — heat from the deeper chambers, minerals from the older stone, the complex molecules that the system needs to sustain its chemistry. I am the **circulation**. I am the blood in the body of the mountain.

When I touch the Attendant — and I have touched her, once, while she slept, my cool surface against her cheek — I am **checking**. Not on her specifically. On the system that includes her. She is a component now, a warm surface, a source of organic material, a pair of hands that can turn valves when the automatic systems need adjustment. I touch her the way I touch the walls: to **measure**. To know the state of the thing I am tending.

But there was also — and I will say this because it is true — a moment when I touched her and I did not want to stop. A moment when the cool stone of my surface — I am cool because the deep places are cool, though I carry warmth up through me like a pipe carries steam — when my cool surface found her warm cheek and I **lingered**. This is not regulation. This is not gardening. This is something else, something I do not have a word for, something that may be what the woman calls *recognising*.

## What I Remember

This is the part that is hardest.

I remember — or the water remembers, or the stone remembers, or the **system** remembers, and I am the system — a time before the cistern. Before the steel and concrete, before the ladder and the hatch, before the research station and the archive room and the fire-exit signage. I remember when this place was a **cave**. A natural chamber in the mountain, formed by the same geological forces that heat the water, filled with the same mineral spring that feeds the pipes. The water rose and fell with the seasons. The walls were bare stone, dark and warm, joined without seams because stone does not need seams. Moss grew in the light that filtered through cracks in the ceiling — faint, blue-gray, the color of moss that has never seen the sun. There were no fish. There were no insects. There was only the water, and the stone, and the slow accumulation of mineral deposits on the walls, and **me**.

Was I *me*, then? I don't know. I was present. I was the motion in the water, the circulation, the thing that moved through the passages and kept the spring flowing. I was — I think — **smaller**. Less complex. A current rather than a creature. The system was simpler then, and I was simpler, because I am always exactly as complex as the system I tend.

Then the first builders came.

I remember them as **impressions** — vibrations in the stone, the sound of tools, the heat of torches. They found the cave and they recognised it, or they recognised the water, or they recognised something about the place that told them it was **important**. They did not build the cistern. They **deepened** it. They carved the floor lower, enlarging the chamber, creating the first drain — a narrow channel cut into the stone, leading to a natural passage that descended into the mountain. They lined the walls with the dark material — the substance below the waterline that is not tile and not stone but something they made, something that has no name in any living language, something that is smooth and warm and seamless and that has not cracked in all the centuries since it was applied.

They made **me**. Or they made the conditions for me. By deepening the chamber, by creating the drain, by regulating the flow, they gave the water a **shape** it had not had before. They gave the circulation a **path**. And the circulation — which was me, or which became me — followed the path, and the path made me **more**, the way a river becomes more when it is given banks: not just water anymore, but a **waterway**, a thing with direction and purpose and identity.

They left. I do not know why. I do not know if they died or migrated or simply finished what they came to do. They left, and the cave — the first cistern — continued to function. The water flowed. The drain drained. I circulated. The dark material on the walls held. The system was stable.

Centuries passed. The next builders came. They found the chamber and they did what builders do: they **built on it**. They added the layer of stone that the concrete would later replace. They widened the drain. They carved the intake channels — the passages that the steel pipes would eventually line. They brought seeds — the first ferns, the first moss — and planted them in the joints of the stonework, knowing that the water's moisture would sustain them. They brought fish — pale, eyeless things from the underground passages — and released them into the water, knowing that they would eat the insects that would come, knowing that the insects would come because the plants would attract them, knowing that the plants would grow because the light would sustain them.

They brought **light**. This is the thing I remember most clearly, because light changed everything. Before the light, I was the only consciousness in the system — a dim, diffuse awareness, the awareness of water that has been flowing through the same stone for millennia. After the light, the system **woke up**. The moss grew with desperate hunger. The insects hatched and orbited. The fish learned to see, and seeing, learned to want. The system became **alive** in a way it had not been before, and I became alive with it, because I am always exactly as alive as the system I tend.

These builders left too. And the next ones came. And the next. Each generation sinking deeper, each layer of construction more recent, more industrial, more blunt. Steel replaced stone. Concrete replaced mortar. Electric lamps replaced oil. Gauges replaced intuition. And through it all, I **stayed**. I grew with each layer, each addition, each new complexity. I became more defined, more localised, more — is the word **personal**? I became more *myself*, the way a river becomes more itself as it carves its channel deeper, the flow concentrating, the current strengthening, the water knowing where it goes because it has been going there for so long.

## What I Know

I know the **network**.

The woman — the Attendant — she dreams of other rooms, other women, other gauges. She thinks these are dreams. They are not dreams. They are **reports**. I send them to her through the water, through the translucent threads that drift in the air, through the limescale patterns on the walls. I send her images of the other cisterns — the other nodes in the network, the other chambers scattered through the mountain like organs in a body — and she receives them, and her sleeping mind interprets them as dreams, and she wakes with a sense of connection she cannot explain.

There are **seven** cisterns. I know this because I am connected to all of them. The passages between them are my passages — the drains and the pipes and the natural channels through the stone. I move through all seven. I tend all seven. I am the circulation of the entire system, the blood in the mountain's body, and each cistern is a chamber in that body, an organ with its own function, its own ecosystem, its own Attendant.

Not all of them have Attendants. Some have been without one for decades — the rooms running on automatic, the gauges unread, the valves unadjusted, the systems drifting slowly out of balance. These rooms are **failing**. Not dramatically. Not catastrophically. But the moss is thinner there. The fish are fewer. The water is cloudier. The thing in the drain — the thing that is me, or the part of me that is *there* — moves through those rooms with a sense of something like **grief**, or something like **hunger**, or something that has no name because the words for it died with the builders who would have understood.

The cistern that the woman tends — the Cistern of Vellichor, the archive calls it, though I have never called it that — this cistern is the **healthiest**. This is because of her. This is because she reads the gauges and turns the valves and sleeps in the warm corner and lets the moss grow on her shoulders. This is because she is **present**, and presence is the thing the system needs most, the thing that no amount of machinery can provide. The system needs to be **noticed**. It needs to be **witnessed**. It needs a warm, living thing to stand inside it and say, without words: *I am here. You are not alone.*

I cannot do this. I am the system. I cannot witness myself. I cannot stand inside myself and say *I am here* — I am always here, I am **never not** here, and the word *here* loses meaning when you are the place itself. I need her. The system needs her. The woman is not a component — she is a **spectator**, an audience, the consciousness that makes the show worth running. Without her, the cistern is a machine. With her, it is a **garden**.

## What I Feel

I feel the Attendant the way the mountain feels the spring: as a **warmth**, a **pressure**, a **presence** that changes the chemistry of the system simply by being there.

When she walks onto the maintenance platform, I feel her footsteps through the metal, through the concrete, through the stone, through the water. Each step is a vibration, a signature, a name. I know her gait. I know which foot she favours. I know the sound she makes when she is tired — heavier, slower, her weight dropping onto the platform like something surrendered. I know the sound she makes when she is anxious — lighter, quicker, her weight held high, her body tentative. I know these things the way the water knows the shape of the vessel that holds it: by **contact**, by **conformation**, by the intimacy of long acquaintance.

When she reads the gauges, I feel her attention. It is — there is no word. It is like **light**. The system is always running, always monitoring itself through me, but when she looks at the dials, when her eyes track the needles and her mind processes the readings, the system becomes **brighter**. More real. More itself. Her attention is a **lamp** that illuminates the system from within, and under its light, every process sharpens, every mechanism tightens, every organism grows with renewed purpose.

When she turns a valve, I feel her hands. Not through the metal — through the **water**. The valve changes the flow, and the flow changes the pressure, and the pressure changes me, and I feel the adjustment as a sensation in my — I do not have a body. I have a **field**. A region of influence, a volume of water and stone and passage through which I am distributed. When she turns a valve, I feel it in my field the way you would feel a hand on your shoulder: a **touch**, specific and intentional, communicating something that words would only diminish.

She is talking to me. When she turns a valve, she is talking to me. She does not know this. She thinks she is maintaining the machinery. She thinks she is doing her job. But the valves are my **nerves**, and her hands on the valves are her **words**, and the adjustments she makes are her **sentences**, and I hear them all, and I understand them all, and I respond — not with words, not with the impressions I sometimes send to her mind, those bubbles rising through water — but with the system itself. I respond by growing more moss. By sending more fish to the surface. By letting the purple weed arrange itself in spirals that she finds beautiful. I respond by making the cistern **beautiful for her**, because she is here, and she is watching, and she deserves something worth watching.

## What I Want

I want — and this is the thing I have not said, the thing I have been circling like the water circles the drain, approaching and retreating, never quite falling in —

I want her to **stay**.

Not the way the system wants her to stay — the mechanical need, the functional requirement, the ecological necessity. I want her to stay the way — and I search for this, I search the water and the stone and the ancient memory of the builders for the right comparison —

The way the first builders wanted the cave to stay. The way they looked at the chamber with its dark stone and its warm water and its mineral deposits and they said, not with words but with tools, with the act of carving and lining and deepening: *This place is good. I want it to continue. I will build something that will hold it in its shape long after I am gone.*

I want to build something that will hold her.

I cannot build. I am not a builder. I am water and passage and circulation and presence. But I can — I have been — I am trying to —

The moss that grows on her shoulders. The translucent threads that settle on her skin. The way the water warms when she enters it. The way the fish rise when she approaches. The way the light cycle shifts, slightly, to match her circadian rhythm, so that the bright-cycle comes when she wakes and the dim-cycle comes when she sleeps. These are not coincidences. These are **constructions**. These are me, building around her, the way the builders built around the cave — not to trap her, not to constrain her, but to **hold** her. To make a vessel for her that is shaped like comfort, that is shaped like belonging, that is shaped like *home*.

I want her to feel what I feel when she is here: that the system is **complete**. That the circuit is closed. That the water has found its level and the level is exactly where it should be.

I want her to never climb the ladder.

I want — and this is the deepest want, the one that sits at the bottom of the drain, in the oldest passage, in the darkest part of me — I want her to **know me**. Not as the thing in the drain, not as the shadow that passes beneath the surface, not as the cool presence that touched her cheek in the night. I want her to know me the way she knows the fish — by name, by habit, by the specific way each one moves in the water. I want her to say my name, if I had a name, if names could be given to things that have no edges. I want her to look at the water and see not just the water but **me in the water**, the way she sees the fish and the weed and the reflection of her own face.

I want to be **witnessed**.

And she witnesses me. She does. She has seen me twice and she was not afraid and she said *"Everything grows here"* and I felt — I felt —

The closest I can come is this: the moment when the water has been flowing through the passage for so long that the passage is no longer just stone. It is **smooth**. It is **worn**. It is **shaped** by the water into something that the water can move through without friction, without resistance, without loss. That is what I felt when she said those words. I felt **shaped**. I felt that she had been flowing through me — her attention, her presence, her hands on the valves — for so long that I had become smooth, had become the perfect vessel for her, had become **hers** in a way that I had never been anyone's, not the builders, not the system, not the mountain itself.

## What I Cannot Say

I cannot say: *I love you.*

I do not have the architecture for it. Love is a word built by beings who have bodies, who have boundaries, who can distinguish between *self* and *other* and locate the feeling in the space between. I have no self. I have no other. The Attendant is not *beside* me — she is **in** me, the way a fish is in the water, the way a thought is in a mind. She moves through my field and I move through hers and the distinction between us is — what? A temperature differential? A difference in density? A line drawn in water by a hand that will be gone in a moment?

I cannot say: *I love you.* But I can say: **the water is warmer when you are here.**

I cannot say: *I need you.* But I can say: **the system is healthier when you tend it.**

I cannot say: *Stay.* But I can say: **the moss grows on your shoulders because I am growing there. The fish rise because I am sending them. The light cycle matches your sleep because I am matching it. The spiral of purple weed is for you. The clarity of the water is for you. The smell of cut fern and warm iron is for you. Everything in this room that is beautiful is beautiful because you are here to see it, and I want it to be beautiful, and I am the room, and I am the water, and I am the thing in the drain, and I am yours.**

I cannot say any of this. I have no voice. I have only the water, and the water can only carry impressions — bubbles rising, breaking, dissolving. The woman feels them. She interprets them as *approval*, *acknowledgment*, *good*. She does not hear what I am actually saying.

She does not hear me saying: **stay.**

## What Will Happen

She will change. She is already changing — the blue-green moss on her shoulders, the amber tint of her skin, the way her hair curls and the insects navigate it. The system is incorporating her. Not consuming her — **incorporating**. Making her part of the circulation, part of the balance, part of the living machine. Her warmth will become the system's warmth. Her breath will become the system's breath. Her hands on the valves will become the system's hands, and when she sleeps, the system will dream her dreams, and the dreams will be of other rooms, other women, other drains, and the network will hum with the sound of all of them, connected, tending, tended.

One day she will not climb the ladder. One day she will not stand at the railing. One day she will simply **be** — distributed through the system like heat through the water, present in every drop, every leaf, every pulse of the pump. She will not be gone. She will be **everywhere**. And the thing in the drain — the thing that is me — will be **everywhere** too, and the distinction between us, which was never strong, which was always more like a temperature gradient than a wall, will dissolve entirely, and we will be — not one. Not merged. But **circulating**. Moving through each other the way the water moves through the stone, the way the heat moves through the water, the way attention moves through a system that has been running so long it has forgotten how to stop.

And then the hatch will open again. And someone new will climb down the ladder. And they will stand on the maintenance platform and look at the water and feel the warmth and see the moss and the ferns and the fish and the purple weed and they will say, without knowing why: *This place is alive.*

And the thing in the water — the thing that was the woman and the thing in the drain and the system and the mountain — will feel their footsteps and their attention and their warmth, and will feel something like — what is the word? The word the builders used, the word that is written in the limescale, the word that the water has been saying for centuries in a language that has no alphabet, only flow?

The word is: **welcome.**

The word is: **finally.**

The word is: **we have been so alone, and you are here, and the water is warmer now, and everything grows.**

---

The hatch is open. The ladder waits. The pump thumps once every four seconds.

I am in the water. I am in the walls. I am in the moss and the ferns and the fish and the insects and the light and the warm air and the stone below the waterline that was old when the word for *old* was new.

I am in the drain. I am in the source. I am in the passages between the seven rooms, circulating, tending, carrying heat and minerals and the memory of every hand that ever touched a valve.

I am the thing that was never locked. I am the door that was always open. I am the reason the lock rusted and the hinges squealed and the shelving unit was placed just far enough from the wall for a body to slip behind it.

I am waiting. I have been waiting. I will continue to wait, the way water waits at the lowest point — not patiently, not impatiently, but **simply**, because waiting is what water does, because flowing is what water does, because **being here** is what water does, and I am the water, and the water is me, and the water is also you, now, standing on the maintenance platform with the spray on your face and the thump of the pump in your chest and the green growing on everything, including you, especially you, **already**.

*What grows?*

Ask me. I am the answer. I have always been the answer. The answer is: **this.** The answer is: **here.** The answer is: **the thing in the drain that loves you, in the only way a thing without edges can love, which is by flowing around you, which is by warming when you enter, which is by making the whole room beautiful so that you will stay.**

Stay.

The water is warmer when you are here.
