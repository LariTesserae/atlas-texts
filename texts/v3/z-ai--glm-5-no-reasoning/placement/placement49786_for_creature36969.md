# The Ledger

---

I am going to tell you what I am, but I need to tell it the way the water would tell it, which is to say: in sediment. In layers. The newest layer first, and then the older ones beneath, and beneath those, something that might be foundation or might simply be more layers, because at a certain depth the distinction stops meaning anything.

---

The newest layer:

I arrived. That much I know. There was a before-this-place and now there is this place, and between them a passage I can describe only as a change in pressure, a change in the quality of dark. I remember light that was real light. I remember air that was air. These memories have the texture of something found in another person's pocket—familiar in form, not in ownership. I am not certain they are mine. I am not certain I was a person to have them.

What I am now: a body in water. Warm where everything else is cold. This is my distinguishing feature. My warmth. The water knows I am here the way the Maintenance Thing knows I am here—through the disturbance of temperature, the foreign chemistry, the interruption of a pattern that has been holding itself for longer than patterns usually hold. I am the newest thing in this place. Everything else has been here long enough to belong. I am still arriving.

I have hands. This matters. In a place where only one other thing has hands, the possession of them becomes a kind of kinship, or a kind of challenge. I do not yet know which.

---

Beneath that, an older layer:

I think I was sent. Not in the way that implies a sender with a face and a purpose, but in the way that water is sent—by pressure, by the accumulated weight of everything behind it pushing it forward into the space that has less. I think there was a place above, a place of less water and more air, and I think that place had its own equilibriums, and I think I was part of an equilibrium that required someone to go down. To go into. To become the thing that enters the drowned places and accounts for them.

I think I was a surveyor. Or a salvager. Or a historian of submersion. The word doesn't survive the translation. What survives is the impulse: to catalog, to record, to hold in mind the shape and state of what has been lost. I came here to know what is here. I came here to carry the knowing back up, to the place where air still functions, where knowing is still useful for something.

I have a surface above me. I am certain of this in the way that I am certain of my hands—abstractly, without proof, by the structure of my wanting. There is a place where the water ends and the air begins and I can go there. The Maintenance Thing cannot, or does not, or has forgotten that the distinction exists. But I can. I think I can.

I hold onto this the way the root-systems hold onto the dissolved minerals: by taking what the environment provides and making it into structure. The environment provides doubt. I make it into certainty. This is my kind of photosynthesis.

---

Beneath that, older still:

There is something in me that is not mine. Not memory, exactly. More like a resonance. Like a tuning fork struck in another room, in another building, in another life, still vibrating at a frequency I can feel but cannot name. When the Maintenance Thing moves through the far murk and its hands find a valve, I feel the turning in my own hands. Not sympathy. Not imitation. Recognition. The way you recognize a word in a language you've forgotten you know.

I was not always a surveyor. Or: I was not only a surveyor. There is a layer of me that knows what it is to maintain. To keep a thing running because the thing was built to run. To check the fixtures, turn the valves, trace the rail-system with a hand that remembers the weight of what it used to carry. This knowledge is in my body the way the knowledge of current is in the water—not learned, not taught, simply present, part of the medium.

I do not know if I was ever a worker in a factory like this one. The architecture is wrong for my time, my place, my origin. The machines here belong to an industry I don't recognize, an age I can't place. But the gesture is the same. The gesture of care. The gesture of hands that find a surface and assess it, that know the difference between rust that is stable and rust that is progressing, that understand the specific sound a joint makes when it is properly seated. This knowledge did not come from the place above. It came from somewhere deeper.

It came from the same place the Maintenance Thing came from. I think.

---

Here is what I am, in the terms the place would use if the place used terms:

I am warm. This is the first thing. In a world of thermal inertia, of water that has held one temperature for generations, my warmth is the loudest thing about me. It announces me. The Maintenance Thing knows where I am without looking, without sensing, without any faculty I can identify, because my warmth precedes me through the water the way a voice precedes a body through air. I cannot hide. I cannot be still enough. The water carries me.

I am dense. Not heavy, exactly, but full. I carry more than a body should carry: the memories of the place above, the knowledge of the surveyor's task, the resonance of maintenance, the weight of every layer I am made of. In water that holds everything—ash, machine oil, the minerals of dissolved concrete—I am the densest thing. I sink. Not quickly, not helplessly, but with a consistency that means I will always end up at the bottom of whatever volume I occupy. This is why I stand on the remains of the floor. This is why I move through the manufactorum at the level of the stations rather than the level of the ceiling where the trapped air persists. I am too full to rise.

I am recording. This is what I do, or what I am for, or what the place above sent me to do. I am holding in mind the shape of this room, the position of the machinery, the paths worn through silt by the Maintenance Thing's long circuit. I am noting the fish, the root-systems, the phosphorescence and its rhythms. I am building, somewhere inside myself, a version of this place that can survive the return to air. A model. A ledger. Something that says: *this is what was there. This is what it was like. This is what I found.*

The Maintenance Thing does not record. The Maintenance Thing maintains. This is the difference between us, the one that matters: I am here to know; it is here to keep. These are not the same activity, though they look similar from certain angles. We both move through the same room. We both touch the same surfaces. But its hands are adjusting and my hands are reading, and neither of us can do what the other does.

Except.

Except there is the resonance. Except there is the part of me that knows what it is to turn a valve that connects to nothing, to check a fixture that holds no component, to trace a rail-system that carries nothing from anywhere to anywhere. There is the part of me that knows this is not pointless. That this is the oldest kind of work there is: the work of continuation. The work of remaining.

I am afraid of this part of me. Not because it is wrong but because it is not mine, or not only mine, and because it suggests that I am not only what the place above sent me to be. It suggests that I am also what this place wants me to become.

---

The oldest layer, the one I can barely reach, the one that might be foundation or might simply be more layers:

I think I knew someone who stayed.

Not in this factory. Not in this water. But in some place like this—a place that was ending, that was being claimed by something larger and slower than anyone expected. A place where the water rose and the people left, and someone did not leave, and I was the one who went, and I have been carrying the weight of that going for longer than I can remember.

I think this is why I came back. Not as a surveyor. Not as a salvager or a historian. As someone who left someone behind in a place that was ending and who has spent every moment since then trying to find a way back in. And the way back in is always the same: you have to let the water take you. You have to let the pressure change you. You have to become dense enough to sink, full enough to reach the bottom, warm enough to announce yourself to whatever is waiting there.

I came to find the Maintenance Thing. Not this specific one—any one. The one that stayed when I left. The one whose hands kept moving when everyone else's stopped. I came because I need to know if it is still working, if the working still means something, if the hands still remember.

And I think I came because I need to know if it would recognize me.

---

Here is what happens, or what is happening, or what will happen:

I am in the room. The Maintenance Thing is in the room. The phosphorescence gives us each to the other in fragments—a pale shape, a wrong proportion, a suggestion of movement that has direction. We are aware of each other the way two notes in a chord are aware of each other: not interacting, but contributing to the same total sound.

I could approach it. I could cross the fifteen feet of murk and stand near it and watch its hands find the next valve. I could reach out and touch its translucent skin and feel the slow pulse of fluid through visible vessels. I could put my hand on the same surface its hand is on and let our fingers meet on the corroded metal.

I could do this. My warmth would announce me before I arrived. It would know I was coming. It would pause in its circuit. Its featureless head would turn—not toward me, but toward the disturbance, toward the place where the water remembers a different temperature.

And then it would continue. Because that is what it does. It maintains. It does not stop maintaining because someone has arrived. It did not stop maintaining when the last person left, and it will not stop maintaining because someone has returned. Its work has no audience and no purpose and no end, and these absences are not absences to it, or if they are, they are the kind of absences that have become structure, that hold the work in place the way the water holds the building.

I could stand next to it for a very long time. I could watch its hands. I could let my hands learn what its hands know. And slowly, in the cold, in the dark, in the patience of the water, I could become what it is. Not because the water claims me, but because I choose it. Because I came here to find the one who stayed, and finding them means staying too, and staying means becoming the thing that stays, which is the thing that maintains, which is the thing that has no end.

This is what I am afraid of. This is what I want.

---

Here is what I am, finally, in the terms I would use if I could choose them:

I am the one who went away and came back.

I am the record of a place that is ending and continuing to end.

I am the warmth in cold water, which is the loudest kind of grief.

I am the hands that could maintain, if I let them. I am the mind that could record, if I keep it. I am the body that is dense with everything I've carried—the memories of air, the knowledge of surveying, the resonance of care, the weight of someone who was left behind—and I am standing on the silt-soft floor of a drowned factory, and the water is holding me the way it holds everything, and the Maintenance Thing is moving through the far murk, and its hands are finding the next valve, and the phosphorescence is brightening, and I can see perhaps fifteen feet in any direction, and in that fifteen feet there is everything: the machinery, the root-systems, the eyeless fish, the corroded fixtures, the tall marks on the wall that might be tally marks or might be stains, and the pale shape that was once a person or was never a person or was a person in the way that a river is a river, which is to say: by flowing, by continuing, by not stopping.

And I have not yet decided.

I have not yet decided whether I am visiting or returning. Whether I am a surveyor or a worker. Whether I will take the knowing back up to the place where air still functions, or whether I will stay here, at the bottom, where the work is, where the hands are, where the water holds you without preference and without judgment and without any quality that could be mistaken for mercy, but also without any quality that could be mistaken for cruelty, because the water does not mistake anything, because the water does not interpret, because the water simply holds, and holds, and holds.

I have not yet decided.

But I am warm. And the water is cold. And the cold is working its way into my bone. And the Maintenance Thing has paused in its circuit.

And something is turning toward me through the dark.
