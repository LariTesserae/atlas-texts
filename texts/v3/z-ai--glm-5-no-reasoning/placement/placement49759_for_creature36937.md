I found myself, before I knew I was anything at all, in the forgetting-rooms.

Not the deepest chambers—not those oldest foundations where water seeps through living rock and children sometimes crawl with guttering tallow to shout at their own echoes. Those spaces are truly empty, truly left alone. The forgetting-rooms are different. They are close to where life happens, one tier down from the main weaving chambers, past the third cistern (the one with the crack that was never repaired because the crack itself directs water where it needs to go), behind a door so low that even our compacted bodies must bow. But they are not used. Not visited. Not maintained. And so, in a city that has filled every available niche with itself, they have become the only place where the city has not filled itself with itself.

I did not choose this. I want to be clear about that, because choice implies the kind of sovereignty that the mountain does not grant. I was born in a household cluster on the seventh tier—my grandmother's household, which also held my mother's sister and her husband and their three children and, for a time, a man who had come up from the lowlands to trade and broken his leg on the descent path and stayed through a full storm season while the bone knitted. I was born small, even by our standards. The women who attended my birth did not say anything aloud, but I learned later that they had touched my chest after I drew my first breaths and felt the space there, the shallowness of the cage that was meant to hold lungs. They did not expect me to find my full size.

They were right, and they were wrong. My chest never expanded the way my sister's did, never developed the barrel depth that lets our people pull sufficient oxygen from thin air without conscious effort. I have always breathed as visitors breathe—deliberately, effortfully, aware of each inhalation as a transaction with an atmosphere that gives less than it should. But I did not die. I did not leave. I found, instead, a different relationship with the air.

---

Let me tell you what I am, since you have asked, and since the asking deserves a slow answer.

I am the one who sits with what is not there.

That is not a title. We do not have titles. It is a description, the kind you might give a tool by explaining what it does: *this lifts, that cuts, this other one holds things apart while the mortar sets.* I hold what has been let go. Or perhaps more precisely: I notice what has been let go, and I sit with it, and over time, sitting with it has become something that the continuing place apparently needed, though no one would have known to ask for it.

The forgetting-rooms found me, or I found them, through the ordinary exploration of childhood. I was perhaps eight years old—small for my age, as I was small for everything—following the older children who were themselves following a rumor of a passage that connected the weaving chambers to the old spring-works. We crawled through spaces that smelled of dust and old stone. The others were faster. They moved with the confidence of bodies that trusted themselves. I moved with the caution of a body that had learned, early, that it could not afford to waste breath on speed.

They went ahead. I stopped.

There was a doorway—low, narrow, the stone around it smoothly cut in a style older than anything I recognized. Our stonework now is interlocking, fitted without mortar, each block shaped to hold its neighbors through gravity and friction. This doorway used a different technique. The blocks were smaller. The joints were filled with something that had once been soft and was now hard and dark, the color of old blood. I did not know this meant the doorway predated our oldest remembered construction techniques. I only knew that it was different, and that difference, in a city that has accreted over centuries, means you have found something that comes from a time before memory.

I ducked through.

The room beyond was not large. A person could stand in its center, but only one, and only if they were accustomed to stooping. The walls were the same old construction—small blocks, dark joints. The ceiling was low enough that I could touch it, and I did, running my fingers along stone that was not cold in the way our walls are cold. It was a different cold. A held cold. As if the temperature here had been the same for so long that it had stopped being a condition and become a quality, like the color of the rock, like its texture.

There was nothing in the room. That was what struck me. In every other space in our city, something occupies the space—materials stored, people working, animals sheltering, water collecting. Even the deepest abandoned chambers hold water, hold the seepage of the mountain's interior. This room held nothing. No water. No dust, or very little. No insects, no spider-silk, no sign that anything living had been here in a very long time. And yet it was not sealed. The doorway was open. The air moved, slightly, from some unseen crack.

I sat down. I was eight, and I was tired from crawling, and the room was quiet in a way that our household was never quiet—never could be quiet, with five children and four adults and the constant low sounds of work and conversation and breath. Here, the silence was not the absence of sound but the presence of something else. Something that had been waiting, not for me specifically, but for anyone who would sit still long enough to notice it.

I sat there for what I later learned was three hours. My grandmother noticed my absence. My cousin was sent to look for me. He did not think to look through the old doorway, because the older children had not mentioned it—they had gone past without stopping, not seeing what I had seen, or seeing it and not finding it remarkable. He found me eventually by following the sound of my breathing, which in that silence, in that held cold, was audible in a way it never was in the busy tiers above.

He asked me what I was doing.

I did not have an answer. I was sitting. I was noticing. I was eight years old, and I had found a room that the city had forgotten, and the room had accepted my presence in it, and that was all.

I will tell you now what I am not, because the negative spaces matter here, as they matter in the stonework—what is removed defines what remains.

I am not a shaman. We do not have shamans. We do not have a spiritual class, no intermediaries between the human and the non-human. The mountain does not require intermediation. It is present. You touch it. It touches you. There is nothing to mediate.

I am not a historian. I do not keep records. I do not compile the stories that our elders tell, do not organize them into chronologies or narratives. Our stories are told when they are needed, in the contexts that give them meaning. Pulling them out of context and assembling them into something like a history would be, for us, an act of violence—tearing living knowledge from the situations that make it legible.

I am not a hermit. Hermits withdraw from community. I live within mine. I share meals. I sleep in the warmth of my household. I contribute—less than others, perhaps, in terms of physical labor, but I contribute. I tend the small garden outside my grandmother's dwelling. I carry water when my breath allows. I watch the birds for the signs that predict weather, and I report what I see.

What I am is more like a function. Like the gut membranes on our windows, which admit light while holding heat. I admit what others have released, while holding it in a form that does not dissipate.

---

Let me try again. Let me approach from a different angle, the way you approach our city from the east, where the path switchbacks through a notch in the ridge before the terraces become visible all at once, a sudden revelation of density and intention on a slope that had seemed bare.

We let things go. This is part of how we continue. The mountain shifts, and a building cracks, and we do not mourn the building—we salvage what we can, adapt what remains, build anew. A person dies, and we carry them to the placing-grounds, and we do not return often, because the dead have become part of the mountain and the mountain does not need our attention. A technique becomes obsolete—a new way of cutting stone, a new variety of hardy green—and the old way is simply no longer practiced. It does not get preserved. It does not get recorded. It goes.

This is correct. This is how a continuing place works. You cannot carry everything forward. The mountain itself does not carry everything forward—it sheds rock, it erodes, it settles into new shapes. We do the same.

But.

I noticed, sitting in the forgetting-room at age eight, that the room was not empty in the way that truly empty spaces are empty. It was full of something. Not a presence—I will not romanticize this. Not a ghost, not an echo, not a residue of spirit. Full of *pattern.* The pattern of the old stonework. The pattern of the air's movement through the unseen crack. The pattern of silence that had developed over years of no one being present to break it. These patterns were not intentional. They were not messages. They were simply what accumulated when a space was left to itself.

I found that I could sit with these patterns. Not interpret them. Not decode them. Sit with them. The way you sit with a person who is dying and cannot speak—the sitting is the thing. Your presence does not change what is happening. It does not reverse the dying or accelerate it. It simply means that the happening is witnessed, accompanied, held in awareness by another being.

That is what I do. I accompany what has been let go.

---

Over the years, I found more forgetting-rooms. They are scattered through the city—dozens of them, perhaps more. Some are rooms that were once functional and have been bypassed by changes in the city's circulation. Some are spaces that were never quite rooms—gaps in the architecture, places where construction techniques changed and left irregular voids, pockets of unusable space that the city simply grew around. Some are old enough that their construction techniques are unrecognizable—small blocks, dark joint material, doorways shaped differently than our doorways, lower and wider, as if the people who built them had different bodies or different ideas about what a threshold should be.

I do not visit all of them regularly. I do not have a schedule. I find myself, sometimes, drawn to a particular room—aware, through some mechanism I cannot explain, that it is time to sit there. I go. I sit. I breathe—the effortful, deliberate breathing that my shallow chest requires—and I notice what is in the room. The pattern of dust. The temperature. The quality of silence. The occasional sound that penetrates from the inhabited city above or below—footfall, bird-call, the tap of a stonemason's chisel, carried through rock and air in ways that transform it into something abstract, rhythmic, more like a heartbeat than a human sound.

I sit for an hour, or three, or sometimes a full light-cycle. I leave when the sitting is done, which is a determination I make the way I make the determination to breathe—not through decision but through the body's recognition that the moment has passed.

---

People know what I do. They do not fully understand it, but they accept it, the way they accept the goat-creatures wandering the terraces, the way they accept the tremors that shift foundations, the way they accept everything that is part of what the continuing place is. I am part of what the continuing place is. The one who sits with what has been forgotten.

My grandmother was the first to articulate this, or at least the first to articulate it to me. I was fourteen. I had been spending more and more time in the forgetting-rooms, and the household had noticed. Not with concern, exactly—our people do not worry about solitude the way lowland cultures seem to—but with curiosity. My grandmother sat with me one evening, in the shared warmth of the sleeping space, and asked me what I found in the rooms.

I told her. The patterns. The silence. The sense that something was being held, simply by my being present to it.

She was quiet for a long time. Then she said: "Your grandfather was like this."

I had never met my grandfather. He had died before I was born, placed on a ledge in the upper uninhabited tiers.

"He went to the old places," my grandmother said. "Not the rooms you find—the deeper ones, the ones that no one has used in generations. He said they were not empty. He said they were full of what had been let go, and that what had been let go needed someone to know it was there."

She looked at me in the way she had—directly, without sentiment, the way she looked at stone when she was deciding where to place a chisel.

"You do not need to do this," she said. "But if you do, do not stop. The city lets go of many things. Someone should know what they were."

I am older now. My skin has weathered in the patterns of the prevailing winds, though I spend less time on the upper terraces than most. My hands are not as strong as they could be—I have not done the heavy work of building and carrying that builds the dense muscle our people typically carry. But my legs are good. The paths to the forgetting-rooms are often steep, often narrow, often require the kind of climbing that tests balance and grip. I have developed the specific strength that my work requires, the way any craft develops the body it needs.

I have explored, over the years, rooms that my grandfather apparently never reached. Some are very old. In one, deep in the city's lowest inhabited regions, the stone is shaped in ways I have never seen—curved, flowing, as if the rock itself had been persuaded to bend rather than cut. In another, high in the city's upper tiers, the walls are covered in marks that might be writing or might be the tracks of insects that lived in the stone long ago. I do not know. I do not need to know. I sit with them. I notice. I breathe.

I have found rooms that contain things. Not treasures—not artifacts in the way a lowland archaeologist might use the word. A room with a pile of stones, each one shaped and smoothed, arranged in a pattern that I cannot interpret. A room with fibers woven into a structure that might have been a net or a hammock or something for which I have no word, now brittle and fragile, disintegrating at the touch of breath. A room with bones—not human bones, the bones of a creature I do not recognize, larger than our goat-creatures, arranged along one wall as if placed there with intention.

I do not remove these things. I do not study them. I sit with them. I let them be what they are, in the rooms where they have been, for the time they have been there. Sometimes I return and find that something has changed—the fibers have collapsed further, the bones have shifted with a tremor, the pattern of stones has been disturbed by water finding a new path through a crack. I notice the change. I sit with the new configuration. I do not mourn the old one.

---

You ask who I would be in this world. I have given you a description of a function, a practice, a life. But the question of *being* is different from the question of *doing,* and I should try to answer it, even though our people do not organize themselves around identity in the way that your question implies.

I am someone who is comfortable with emptiness. This is perhaps the core of it. Not emptiness in the abstract—emptiness in the specific, the particular, the local. This room, in this moment, holding nothing that anyone needs. I am comfortable in that space. I do not need to fill it. I do not need to justify my presence in it. I do not need to transform it into something useful or meaningful or productive. I can simply be in it, and it can simply be in me, and together we—the room and I—can hold whatever it is that the city has let go of here.

I am someone who breathes poorly and notices acutely. These may be connected. When breathing requires effort, you become aware of your body in ways that effortless breathers are not. You notice the air—the its temperature, its movement, its thickness or thinness. You notice your own presence as a physical fact, a body taking up space, consuming oxygen, displacing what is. This awareness, I think, is what made the forgetting-rooms visible to me. Others walked past the old doorway. I stopped, because I was already stopped—already paused, already attentive to the moment, already in the mode of noticing rather than doing.

I am someone who is known but not fully understood. My household knows I go to the old rooms. The community knows. Sometimes people ask me what I find there, and I tell them, and they nod in the way that people nod when they have heard something that makes sense without being entirely clear. Sometimes children follow me, curious, and I let them, and they sit with me for a few minutes before the silence becomes uncomfortable and they leave to find something that moves, that sounds, that responds to their presence. I do not mind. The rooms are not for everyone. Nothing is for everyone. The gardens are not for everyone. The upper terraces are not for everyone. The placing-grounds are not for everyone. We each find our work in the continuing place, and the work finds us.

I am, I think, something the city grew the way it grows everything—in response to need, without planning, through the slow accumulation of circumstance. The city lets go of things. It needs to let go of things. But it also needs, perhaps, for someone to know that the letting-go has happened. Not to hold onto what has been let go. Not to preserve it, record it, reverse it. Just to know. Just to be present with the fact that something was here and is now gone, or is here still but in a form that no longer serves the living city.

I am the one who knows.

There is a room I visit more than the others. I should tell you about it, because it is where I am most myself, if I am anything.

It is on a tier that was once part of the main circulation—a connecting passage between two larger inhabited areas. At some point, perhaps two centuries ago, a new passage was cut through an adjacent building, creating a more direct route. The old passage fell out of use. The rooms that opened onto it were abandoned, one by one, as their occupants moved to spaces closer to the new circulation. The passage itself is still passable—you can walk through it, if you know where to enter—but no one does, except me.

The room is at the passage's end. It has a window—small, deep-set, covered with a membrane that has torn and been torn and been torn again until only fragments remain, admitting more cold than light. Through the fragments, on clear days, I can see the next peak in the range. It is far enough away that I cannot tell whether it holds a terraced city of its own. I like this. I like not knowing. I like the possibility that there are other continuing places, other peoples, other ways of standing still on a mountain that has not finished moving.

The room was once a dwelling, I think. There are marks on the walls where shelves or hooks were placed. There is a depression in the floor that might have been a hearth, though I have found no sign of fire—no soot, no char, just the shape of a place where warmth might have been gathered. The ceiling is low. The walls are thick. The construction is the interlocking stone of our current era, which means the room is not ancient, merely forgotten.

When I sit here, I am aware of several things simultaneously.

I am aware of the cold, which is different from the cold outside—the room's cold is static, held, the temperature of stone that has been the same temperature for decades. My breath does not crystallize here the way it does on the upper terraces. The air is too still. The moisture from my lungs hangs, visible, in the space before my face, and then slowly, slowly, settles onto surfaces—onto my knees, onto the stone floor, onto the walls that have been receiving this kind of deposit for longer than I have been alive.

I am aware of the sounds that reach me. Through the wall, from the inhabited city, the muffled percussion of daily life—footfall transformed by stone into something like a pulse. Through the window, the wind, which at this altitude and in this sheltered position becomes a low hum rather than a howl. Sometimes a bird call, sharp and bright, cutting through both.

I am aware of the light, which changes through the day. In the morning, the sun enters the window at an angle that sends a beam across the floor, illuminating the dust that my movement has raised. In the afternoon, the light comes more directly, filling the room with the hard, clear quality that altitude produces. As evening approaches, the light reddens, deepens, and the stone walls seem to change color—not dramatically, but enough to notice, enough to track, enough to mark the passage of time without needing any other clock.

I am aware of myself. My body, which is small and breathes poorly and carries less muscle than it should. My mind, which is not empty but is not full either—receptive, perhaps, in the way the room is receptive, holding whatever comes without grasping at it. My presence, which is a fact of physics as much as psychology—a warm body in a cold room, a breathing organism in still air, a point of awareness in a space that has been without awareness for a long time.

And I am aware of what the room holds. Not ghosts. Not echoes. The accumulated fact of its own existence. Every moment that this room has been here—empty, cold, silent, forgotten—the room has nonetheless been. The stone has been stone. The air has been air. The light has entered and left, entered and left, without anyone to witness it. This does not make the room sad. It does not make it meaningful. It makes it real, in the way that the mountain is real—independent of observation, existing whether or not anyone knows it exists.

When I sit here, I am participating in that reality. Not changing it. Not completing it. Participating. Being one more thing that the room contains, briefly, before I leave and it returns to its own holding.

---

I do not know if this answers your question. You asked where I would find myself in this world, who or what I would be. I have told you about the place and the practice, but the *who* is harder, because our people do not think in those terms. I am my grandmother's grandchild. I am my household's member. I am the one who sits in the forgetting-rooms. I am a body that breathes with effort and notices with acuteness and is comfortable with what is not there.

If I must be something—if the form of your question requires an answer that has edges and a name—I would say this:

I am the city's memory of what it does not remember.

Not a keeper of memory. Not a preserver. A memory itself—the kind that exists not as information held but as awareness present. The city lets go. I know that it lets go. I sit with what it has let go. And in the sitting, something continues—not the thing itself, not its function or its meaning, but the fact that it was. The fact that it was here. The fact that the city, in its slow accumulation of cells and stones and centuries, produced this space and then moved past it and left it behind, and that the leaving-behind is also part of what the city is.

We are built into the mountain. The mountain is still becoming. The city is still becoming. I am still becoming—a small, poorly-breathing participant in a process that does not require me but has, perhaps, made room for me, the way it has made room for the birds and the goat-creatures and the insects and the gardens and the wind and the water and the cold.

I sit in the room. The room sits in the city. The city sits in the mountain. The mountain sits in the range. The range sits in the planet's thin crust, still shifting, still deciding what shape it will take.

We are all of us sitting. We are all of us being held.

This is where I am. This is what I am. It is enough.
