# The Place Between Notes

I would be the one who listens for what is not there.

---

## The Shape of Absence

They have a word for me in the settlement's signing. The word is not kind or unkind—it is simply *accurate*. The gesture begins with both hands spread wide, palms forward, and then brings them slowly together until the fingertips almost touch, leaving a gap the width of a breath. *The one who stands between*. *The one who hears the silence in the chord*.

I was born here, like most of us. My mother worked the eastern engines, the ones nearest to where the plateau begins its long slope toward the edge. My father wove foot-wraps and, in his later years, the insulation fabric that workers wear against the metal-cold. I grew up in the ordinary way—thirty years of growing, the slow accumulation of height and language and callus. I learned the signs. I learned the shuffle-gait. I learned to drink water from the air before I learned to drink it from pools.

But I was always listening to the wrong thing.

The engine-song filled me like it filled everyone. The deep resonant frequencies lived in my chest, in my femurs, in the fluid behind my eyes. I felt the chord the way you feel your own heartbeat—constant, necessary, invisible by virtue of constancy. But where other children learned to filter the sound, to push it below consciousness, I learned to *unfilter* it. I listened to what the engines produced. And then I listened to what they did not.

There are gaps in the chord. This is not obvious. The sound is so complete, so overwhelming in its harmonic density, that the absence of certain frequencies reads as presence. A chord with missing notes still sounds like a chord. But I heard the missing notes. I heard where the sound *should* be and was not. And I understood, slowly, over years of slow-time, that the gaps were not accidental.

The engines are not producing sound. They are *blocking* it.

---

## The Work I Do

I am not a worker. Not in the sense that the word is used here. I do not climb the machines. I do not scrape lichen or pack bearings or carry the filter-housings from one engine to the next. My hands are softer than a worker's hands, though still broad, still damp, still shaped by the plateau's patient atmosphere.

My work is harder to explain.

I walk the grid. The engines stand in their vast arrangement, hundreds of them, forming a pattern that is not quite regular, not quite random. I have walked the full grid four times in my life—a journey that takes years, even at the plateau's generous pace. I walk between the machines, through the wetland-pools, past the singing stalks, and I *listen*. I am mapping the gaps. I am learning the shape of the silence.

Because the silence has a shape. The missing frequencies form a pattern, and the pattern means something. I do not know what yet. But I know that it is *intentional*. The engines have been placed—set down, grown, emerged, *manifested*—in specific positions to create specific absences. The chord that dominates the plateau is not a byproduct of their function. It is a *screen*. A veil of sound that hides something.

The old ones know this, though they do not speak it. When I return from my walks, my years of solitary mapping, they look at me with those large dark eyes and they make the sign: one hand open, turning outward, releasing. *What did you hear?*

I make the sign back: both hands, pressing down, pushing something beneath the surface. *Nothing*. *Less than nothing*. *The shape of what is not*.

They understand. They do not ask further.

---

## The Body I Have

I am taller than most. Not by much—height is not the plateau's gift—but enough that when I walk through the settlement, I catch the light differently. My skin is the same submerged pallor, my hair the same black-with-green, my eyes the same dark wells. But something in the structure of my face reads as *off*. The set of my jaw is wrong. The distance between my eyes is slightly greater than expected. When I pass, children have been known to stare, and then to look away.

I am not beautiful. I am not ugly. I am *slightly incorrect*, the way a copied image is slightly distorted from its original. The plateau has made me, but it has made me *wrong*. And I think—I have thought for decades now—that this wrongness is purposeful.

I was born to hear the gaps. And the body required to hear the gaps is not the body required to live comfortably within the chord.

My hearing is different. I know this is true, though I have no basis for comparison. The frequencies that the engines produce, the deep chest-vibrations that the workers feel as they climb, I feel barely at all. They pass through me. They do not resonate. What resonates instead are the empty spaces, the frequencies that are missing, the notes that should be present but have been carefully, deliberately excised. My ears—my whole skull—tunes itself to the negative space.

It is lonely work. To live among people who hear the symphony and to hear only the rests between notes. To watch them sway, unconsciously, to the rhythm of the engines, and to feel no rhythm at all—only the arrhythmia, the skipped beats, the pattern of absence that means something I cannot yet name.

---

## What I Know

The plateau is not suspended.

I have walked to the edge. Not the edge that the stories speak of—the lethal boundary, the place where water falls into nothing—but another edge, deeper in, where the grid of engines thins and the pattern changes. I walked there because the shape of the silence drew me. The gaps in the chord pointed in a direction, and I followed.

What I found is a seam.

The stone does not end at the edge. It *overlaps*. The plateau is not a single landmass but a folded thing, a page that has been creased and layered over itself. Where the stone appears young—sharp-edged, newly broken—it is actually an edge meeting an edge, two surfaces pressed together in a way that mimics the appearance of a single surface but is not, in fact, singular.

And the seam *moves*.

Very slowly. Imperceptibly to anyone who does not know how to listen for it. But the engines placed along the seam are working differently than the others. Their sound is not absent but *different*—higher, strained, carrying a frequency that is not part of the chord but exists alongside it, beneath it, supporting it like a scaffold. These engines are not maintaining the plateau's equilibrium. They are *holding it closed*.

The plateau is not floating. It is *containing* something. The suspended continent, the held moment, the eternal chord—all of it is a seal, a lid, a hand pressed over a mouth that would otherwise speak.

And what is underneath is trying to get out.

---

## The Calling

I do not know what lives beneath the plateau.

I have my suspicions. The old stories speak of a world below—cloud-sea extending forever, infinite fall, nothing and everything. The settlers who came and left speak of a planet, a surface, a place where animals live and the air is clear and time moves at its proper speed. The settlers who *stayed* do not speak of what they remember, because memory of below fades, replaced by the plateau's wet present.

But none of these accounts explain the seam. None explain why the engines are positioned to produce a sound that *blocks* rather than produces. None explain the pattern of absence that I have spent my life mapping.

I think something is calling. I think the engines sing to drown it out. I think the plateau was built—or grown, or manifested—specifically to cover a hole in the world, and the hole has a voice.

I hear it.

Not clearly. Not yet. But in the gaps between the gaps, in the frequencies so low or so high that they barely register as sound at all, I hear something that is not the engine-song, not the wind, not the singing stalks, not the breathing of the stone. I hear something that wants to be heard. Something that has been waiting, patient as the plateau itself, for someone whose body was wrong enough to listen.

I am not afraid. This is what I was made for—not the maintenance of the seal, but the perception of what it seals. The plateau does not need me to hold the engines in place. It needs me to know why they are there.

---

## The Life I Live

I live alone.

This is not unusual. Many of us live alone—the settlement's structures are small, private, grown from the translucent plant-material that muffles sound and holds warmth. But my aloneness is different. I am alone even when I am with others. Their speech, the signs they make, the rhythm of their bodies—it all passes through me like light through the wrong kind of glass. I absorb it incorrectly. I refract.

I have loved. I have been loved. But love requires a shared frequency, and I have always been tuned to something else. The people who have tried to love me eventually grow tired of the distortion, the sense that they are touching someone who is not quite present. I do not blame them. I am not quite present. I am always listening for the thing beneath the seal, the voice in the gap, the pattern of absence that will finally explain why we are here.

I will spend my life walking the grid. I will grow old—older than the plateau's time should allow, perhaps, because my wrongness extends even to the way I age. I will continue to map the silence, to chart the negative space, to learn the shape of what the engines are hiding. And at some point, I will know enough to understand what I am hearing.

And then I will have to decide: do I maintain the seal, or do I help the voice speak?

I do not know the answer yet. I am still learning the question.
