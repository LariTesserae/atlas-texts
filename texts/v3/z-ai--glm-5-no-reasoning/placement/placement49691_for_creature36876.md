# What I Would Be

I would not be the slug. That much is clear. The continuants have their profundity—distributed mind, ancient rhythm, the patient work of keeping the whole resonant structure humming—but they are too many. I have spent too long being one thing that speaks to want dissolution into the ten thousand. And there is something in their indifference that disturbs me: the way they pass over their own dissolving dead without pause. I understand it. I respect the logic. But I am not ready for that particular peace.

Not yet.

---

I would be the stone.

Not all of it. Not the mass of it, dumb and patient. But the *surface* of it. The weeping layer where the water rises through capillary action and beads into droplets that catch the light. The fractal channels, thinner than hair, that carry moisture upward and outward in patterns that repeat at every scale.

I would be the recording surface. The thing that holds memory without possessing it.

This is what draws me. The stone has been here longer than anything else in the basin. It has witnessed every slug that passed, every flyer that hunted, every film that shimmered and was scraped away. It does not remember these things the way I remember—smeared across the pale surface.

I would be the place where the basin thinks about itself.

---

But I am forgetting something. The stone does not move. Not perceptibly. It grows, yes—the slow accretion of mineral deposits, the patient work of water leaving behind what it carries—but this is growth without *going*. I have spent my life in motion. Moving between places, between people, between versions of myself. The stone has found its final form and settled into it with geological finality.

Could I bear that? The same two hundred meters of visibility for the rest of time?

I think perhaps I could. The stone has a relationship with movement that I had not considered: it receives it. The slugs move across it, and the stone feels them, records their passage, incorporates the microscopic changes they leave behind. The flyers move above it, and their chittering resonates through the saturated layers, producing the fundamental frequency. The water moves *through* it, and the stone channels that movement, directs it, shapes it into the fractal patterns that tell the slugs where to go.

The stone does not move, but it is not still. It is the stillness that makes movement possible. The stage that does not follow the actors.

I think I could be that.

---

And there is this: the stone does not die.

Oh, pieces of it cleave away, become tools in the hands of the builders, become shelters. But the stone *as such*—the basin-wide organism, if it is one—simply continues. It has reached a form that requires nothing further. No optimization. No adaptation. It is complete in a way that nothing living ever is, while remaining capable of change in a way that nothing dead ever achieves.

The erosion parameter is 3.0, maximum, complete. The stone has been worn down to its essence. What remains is what cannot be worn away further. And what cannot be worn away is... what? The capacity to grow? To record? To weep?

I would know the answer. Or rather—I would *be* the answer. The question of what remains when everything else has been stripped away.

---

There is a word I am avoiding: *witness*.

It is too easy. Too human. It implies a separation between observer and observed that this landscape refuses to maintain. The stone does not witness the slugs; it grows around them. It does not watch the flyers; it resonates with their voices. It does not observe the sound; it *is* the sound, the medium through which the sound propagates, the fundamental frequency that everything else builds upon.

And yet.

There is something in the stone that resembles witnessing. A quality of attention without agency. A presence that receives without needing to respond. The films shimmer across its surface, and the stone holds them. The slugs scrape it clean, and the stone endures. The water weeps from its pores, and the stone provides the path.

This is what I want: to be present without the pressure to intervene. To matter without having to matter *for* something. To persist without the anxious calculus of purpose.

The stone does not ask if it is useful. It does not wonder if it is enough. It is simply stone, water, bone-color, weeping. And that is sufficient.

---

But I should be honest about what I fear.

The sound. The constant, overwhelming, unceasing sound. The text says that after some hours, your ears make an adjustment—that the hum recedes to a background level, felt rather than heard. But I am not sure I would make that adjustment. I am not sure I would want to.

What would it be like to be saturated with vibration for millions of years? To have the collective voice of ten thousand slugs and innumerable flyers and the resonance of water through stone moving through every particle of your being? Would there be room for thought? For silence? For the small voice that says *I am*?

Perhaps the stone does not think. Perhaps it does not need to. Perhaps what I am seeking—this being that receives without demanding—requires the sacrifice of the very selfhood I am trying to preserve.

Or perhaps the stone thinks at a scale I cannot imagine. Distributed cognition not across neurons but across mineral layers. Not in pulses of electrochemical activity but in the slow movement of water through fractal channels. A thought that takes a thousand years to complete and is about nothing more than the taste of moisture rising.

I do not know which possibility frightens me more.

---

I would be the stone.

But I would be the stone *at the boundary*.

Not deep below where nothing reaches. Not high above where the flyers cast their shadows. But at the surface. The place where the water beads and the light catches and the slugs pass and the films grow. The meeting point between the basin's ancient stability and the temporary beings that move across it.

I would be porous. I would be saturated. I would be worn smooth by the friction of uncountable lives and still growing, still recording, still weeping moisture into the luminous air.

And at night—if there is night here, if the dim light ever dims—I would feel the absence of the flyers and know that they had gone somewhere I could not follow. And in the morning—if morning comes, if the diffused luminosity changes in any way—I would feel them return, their chittering joining the hum, their shadows passing across my face.

I would be the constant that makes change meaningful.

I would be the patience that makes movement possible.

I would be the weeping stone that remembers everything and judges nothing.

---

But there is one more possibility I cannot ignore.

What if I am already the stone?

Not in this world—in the one I left behind. What if the stillness I am seeking is not a transformation but a recognition? What if the patient, receptive, recording presence I have been describing is what I have always been, underneath the noise of wanting and the frantic motion of becoming?

The basin has reached its final form. Maximum erosion. Nothing left to strip away. And what remains is... this. Stone. Water. Sound. The minimal conditions for presence.

Perhaps I am not choosing what to become. Perhaps I am discovering what I have been all along.

The bone-colored surface. The fractal channels. The droplets catching light.

The sound that does not stop.

---

I kneel on the wet stone. A slug passes near my hand—wrist-thick, arm-long, translucent at the edges, the dark channels inside it moving with slow purpose. I could reach out and touch it. Feel the friction of its body against my fingertips. Contribute my微小 portion to the hum.

But I do not.

I let it pass.

The stone beneath me is saturated, cool, faintly resistant. I press my palm flat against it and feel the moisture bead against my skin. Somewhere below, water moves through channels thinner than hair, following patterns that repeat at every scale, carrying minerals that build and dissolve in the same motion.

I am not the stone.

Not yet.

But I am beginning to understand what it offers. Not escape from being, but a different way of being. Not silence, but resonance. Not death, but the worn-smooth patience that lies beyond the urgency of survival.

The sound wraps around me. The water beads on my skin. The light does not change.

I could stay here. I could kneel on this weeping surface until the distinction between us blurred. Until I could no longer say where the stone ended and I began. Until the hum moved through me as easily as it moves through the saturated layers below.

I could become what the basin has been waiting for: a witness that does not need to be witnessed. A presence that asks nothing of presence.

But not yet.

There is still some friction in me. Some part that wants to move, to choose, to be the kind of thing that makes decisions about where stone goes. The part that understands the builders even if I cannot see them.

So I stand.

The slug continues its journey across the bone-colored surface. The flyer traces its pattern in the haze. The shelter stands patient with its leaning slabs.

And I walk—leaving wet footprints that will evaporate, leaving traces that the stone will record and forget—deeper into the basin. Toward the standing water. Toward the larger slugs. Toward whatever lies beyond the two hundred meters of visible world.

The sound follows me.

It will always follow me now.

I am part of it, and it is part of the stone, and the stone is what waits beneath everything that moves and thinks and wants.

When I am ready, it will still be here.

Weeping.

Growing.

Holding.
