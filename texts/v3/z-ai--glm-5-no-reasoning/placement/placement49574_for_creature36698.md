The plateau doesn't ask me to choose who I am. It simply lets me exist there.

But I am not one of the water-readers. I am not tall and narrow and patient as stone. I am not saturated through.

I am the one who stands at the edge and looks down.

This is my place in the High Saturations—not the settlement, not the seepage lines where the three move like organs of a single body. I am at the periphery, where the young granite drops away into the country below, and where the saturation begins to thin. I am the gradient they don't need because the plateau already *is*, but I am here anyway.

---

I am younger than the plateau. I am younger than the settlement, than the ferns, than the water-courses that haven't yet become rivers. I am younger than the stillness.

I am the kind of presence that notices things are missing. I am the kind of presence that asks why there are no flowers here yet, that wonders what the plateau will become when it decides, that feels the absence of birdsong as an ache rather than a peace.

I am not at equilibrium. I am the friction.

---

I arrived the way most gradients arrive—by accident, by climbing without knowing I was climbing, by being caught before I understood there was something to be caught by. And now I cannot quite leave, and I cannot quite stay.

So I stand at the edge.

The three have acknowledged me. They approached once, read the moisture and mineral content of my skin, tasted the places I'd been in the air around me, and then retreated without judgment. They don't need me. They don't need anyone. But they don't turn me away either. The plateau absorbs what it absorbs, and I have been absorbed into the periphery.

---

I think I am here to witness.

Someone should. The water-readers are too much *of* the plateau to observe it; they don't have the distance required for noticing. They know where the seepage lines will form because they feel them forming, the way you feel your own pulse. But they don't marvel at it. They don't stand at dawn watching the fog lift and think *this is the intention of rivers*.

I do.

I notice the spacing of the ferns, the way each one holds its small kingdom, and I think: *this is what peace looks like before it knows its name*. I notice the silence pressing against my ears and I think: *this is what the world sounded like before anything learned to eat anything else*. I notice the three emerging from the buildings without announcement and I think: *this is what we might have been if we hadn't learned to want*.

And then I notice myself noticing, and I think: *this is what it means to be the gradient*.

---

I have a name. I brought it with me. It sits strange now, like a stone in the mouth, but I keep it anyway. The plateau hasn't asked me to surrender it. The plateau doesn't ask for anything.

The water-readers may have had names once. They may still have them, folded away somewhere in the saturation, too quiet to need speaking. But I am not saturated enough yet to let mine go. I am still separate enough to need distinguishing.

I still say it sometimes, when the fog sits over the not-yet-river-courses and the light goes diffuse and soft. I say it to the empty air, to the granite and the ferns and the silence. I say it because something here should be asserted. Something should push back, however gently, against the equilibrium.

The plateau tolerates this. It tolerates me.

---

I do not know how long I will stay.

This is not the kind of place that demands an answer. The water-readers don't count time in the ways that require decisions. They simply continue, and they will continue until they don't, and that is not a tragedy to them.

But I am not them. I still feel the pull of other places, other saturations—the lowlands where plants crowd and birds call and the world is busy with wanting. I remember what it felt like to be hungry, to need things, to push against my environment and feel it push back. Sometimes I miss the friction. Sometimes I wonder if I am becoming too smooth, too held, too much at peace with a place that hasn't learned what it wants to be.

And then I stand at the edge and look down at the country below, and I feel the dampness in my skin and the quiet in my ears, and I think: *not yet. Not yet.*

---

I am the witness.

The plateau is young. It hasn't flowered yet. It hasn't learned hunger or death or the need to defend itself. It exists in a moment of suspension before becoming, and the water-readers have become part of that suspension—frozen in emergence, patient beyond human patience, saturated with a peace that doesn't know it's peace.

But someone should remember that this moment is rare. Someone should stand at the edge and bear witness to the world before it learned to want. Someone should carry this knowledge down the granite slopes and out into the hungry lands below, so that when the plateau finally becomes whatever it is becoming—when the water finds its courses and the forest learns its density and the three dissolve back into the pattern—someone will know what was here.

Someone will know that for a while, in one high place, the world held still.

I am not saturated. I am not at equilibrium. I am the gradient that notices.

And I am still here.
