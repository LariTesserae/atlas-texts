I would be the one who came to study the Reach and couldn't leave.

Not a scientist, exactly. Scientists have institutions waiting for them, deadlines, the expectation of return. I had grant money that ran out in the second year and a research question too vague to defend: *What is the system computing?*

I came expecting to measure the compounds in the percolation water. I stayed because the question kept shifting under me. Every time I thought I understood the relationship between the trap-fluid chemistry and the deep-layer biochemistry, I'd find a new variable—a concentration gradient that shouldn't exist, a reaction rate that defied the temperature constraints, a pattern in the collapse cycle that suggested prediction rather than response.

I have a shelter now, of sorts. I found a section where the root-felt has folded over itself, creating a kind of overhang—a rare thing here, where gravity flattens everything into horizontality. The space beneath is maybe two meters high and four meters deep, opening onto one of the black water pools that persists longer than most. I've watched the pool appear and vanish eleven times. I've started to anticipate its rhythm the way the gnats do.

My clothes are always damp. I've stopped noticing.

The cold has done something to my metabolism—I eat less, sleep more, move at quarter speed. I think I've adapted to the system's tempo, or it has adapted me. I've gone forty days without seeing the sky. I measure time by the collapse events now—close enough to hear, close enough to feel the vibration through the ground. The last one was three days ago, maybe forty meters to the northwest. I watched the gap open through the canopy, the light shaft cutting through the grey-green like a blade, and I counted the seconds until the first acceleration growth began. I can hear it now, the faint hiss of fibers extending in the new illumination. The system is healing itself. It always does.

I've become something of a specialist. I know the sound of imminent failure now—the specific pitch of the creaking that means a cable will fail within hours, not weeks. I know which sections are due for redistribution and which have decades left in their structural tolerance. I've started to position myself accordingly. Not for safety—I've long since stopped believing in safety here—but for observation. I want to see what happens when the next section falls. I want to be close enough to measure the compounds released in the first seconds of redistribution, before the system's chemistry equalizes again.

Yesterday I found a spider in the gap between two vine-strands near my shelter. The first one I'd seen in months. It was motionless, dark, almost featureless—exactly as the literature describes. I watched it for three hours. It did not move. It was waiting, or it was dying, or it was existing in some state that does not distinguish between waiting and dying.

When I looked away and looked back, it was gone. I do not know where. There is nowhere to go.

I think about the speculation sometimes—the idea that the Reach is optimizing itself, that the configuration is being refined over centuries toward some maximally efficient state. I don't know if it's true. But I've started to feel like evidence. I am less cold than I was. I am less hungry. I am losing interest in things that used to matter—return dates, rescue schedules, the assumption that human attention belongs anywhere else.

I have become a minor participant in the system's water cycle. My breath condenses on the surfaces around me. My heat differential creates microcurrents in the fog. I am being processed.

The creaking above me has been getting louder.

I could move. I should move. The section directly overhead is older than most—I can tell by the density of the healed scars, the thickness of the moss-mats, the way the weave-lianas have fused into something closer to cable than network. It has been holding weight for longer than the equilibrium predicts. It is due.

But I can hear something else now, underneath the creaking. A sound I don't have a word for. It's like the hiss of growth, but denser—not the sound of fibers extending, but of something being arranged. A chemical sound. A processing sound.

The trap-fluid compounds are present in higher concentrations near collapse zones. I measured this last year and didn't understand it. Now I think I do. The system is doing something with the structural failures beyond redistribution. It is learning from them. Or—no, that word implies too much—it is *incorporating* them, the way a body incorporates information about an injury into its future structure.

I am standing underneath the next injury. I am standing where the system will learn.

I am not leaving.
