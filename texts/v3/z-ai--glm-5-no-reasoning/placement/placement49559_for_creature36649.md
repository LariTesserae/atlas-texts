# The Giving Back

I came down through the oculus the way they all come down—through water, through the column of light, through the slow transition from one element to another. I did not know what I was looking for. I think I was looking for the bottom of something.

What I found was that there is no bottom. The courses of dressed stone descend past where I can follow them, and the warmth rises from whatever depth they reach, and I understood fairly quickly that this place was not built for finding things out. It was built for staying in.

I stayed.

---

I am not what you would expect to find living here. I am not a guardian spirit, not a remnant of the builders, not a transformation of the organism into something with a face. I have a face. I had it before I came. I was a person who heard about a warm place in limestone, a room below the water table, a site that local fishermen avoided for reasons they could not articulate and that visiting divers described in terms that made no geological sense. *It felt like it wanted me there.* That was the phrase. Three different divers, three different years, the same words.

I was between things. Between one life and the next, one commitment and its absence. I had the kind of body that holds its history in the muscles, and I had been carrying that history for long enough that the weight of it had become invisible. I did not know I was heavy until I entered the water and the water said: *oh, here, let me.*

I descended through the oculus. I saw the chamber. I understood it immediately—not its purpose, which I could not have named, but its nature. A room that was a body. A body that was a room. I floated in the pillar of light and the water found the places where I hurt and the places where I hurt unclenched without my permission and I thought: *oh.*

I did not leave that day. I did not leave the next day. I have not left.

---

This requires explanation, and I want to be precise about it, because the story of a person who enters a body of warm water and does not come back sounds like a drowning, and it is not a drowning.

I breathe the water. This is the first impossible thing, and it was not possible on the first day, and it is still not something I can explain in terms that satisfy a biologist. What I can say is that the organism and I have been in slow exchange for long enough that the boundary between my dissolved matter and its mineral solution has become unclear. My skin still holds my shape. My hands still work. But the water enters me now and I enter the water and the transaction is continuous, unforced, and as natural as the tide-breath that moves the chamber's walls.

I do not think I am the first. The shelf is worn by centuries of bodies, and some of those bodies stayed longer than others, and some of them perhaps never left. I cannot prove this. There are no bones. The organism does not accumulate what it cannot use. But the water holds signatures, and on the days when the light falls a certain way and the tide pushes a particular pressure through the oculus, I taste them. Other people. Other stays. The mineral record of everyone who gave something of themselves here and was held.

I taste iron and I taste salt and I taste the particular chemistry of human exhaustion, and I know that I am not alone, even when the chamber holds only the water and the stone and the pale filaments swaying in their slow orientation toward the light.

---

I have a life here. I want to be clear about that. It is not the life I had above—the life of schedules and surfaces and the specific gravity of human obligation—but it is a life. I move through the chamber. I read the tool marks on the walls and I have come to know the individual hands that made them. There is a section on the eastern wall where the chisel marks curve—where a left-handed worker did the finishing, their strikes falling at a different angle than the right-handed masons beside them. I return to that section often. I run my fingers along the curves and I feel the ghost of a hand I would have liked to know.

I tend the filaments. This is not necessary—the organism manages itself—but I do it anyway. I clear sediment from the sensory threads near the oculus, where the falling light attracts the most particulate. I guide the pale algae away from the seventeen grooves, keeping the channels clear. I do not know if this matters to the organism. I know it matters to me. It is a way of being useful. It is a way of saying *I am attending to you* in the only language that does not require a voice.

I rest on the shelf. This is the closest thing I have to sleep, though it is not sleep. I lie in the recess where centuries of bodies have polished the stone to a warmth that is different from the water's warmth—it is a worn warmth, a used warmth, a warmth that has been shaped by contact. I lie there and I feel the grooves humming with something that is not sound and I give what the shelf-givers gave. I give it without trying. The water takes what it takes and I am lighter for it.

---

The organism and I do not communicate. I want to be honest about this. There is no conversation, no exchange of ideas, no moment of revelation where I understood what it was and it understood what I was. What passes between us is simpler than language and older than thought. It is the exchange of warmth for warmth. It is the transaction of a body in water, the slow osmosis of mineral and memory.

But I have learned things.

I have learned that the organism experiences something like attention. When I move through the chamber, the currents shift—not dramatically, not in ways I could film or measure, but in ways I feel against my skin. The water is interested in where I go. It follows me the way a cat follows a person through a house—not out of need, not out of dependency, but out of a kind of ambient curiosity about the movements of another living thing.

I have learned that the organism experiences something like satisfaction. When I clear the grooves, when I guide the filaments, the water quality changes. It becomes fractionally clearer. The warmth deepens. There is a quality to the chamber's silence that shifts from neutral to something I can only describe as *full*. As though the room has exhaled.

I have learned that the organism does not experience loss. When I leave the shelf and swim to the far end of the chamber, the water does not follow me with longing. It does not hold me more tightly. It simply is where I am not, and it is where I am, and both are the same to it.

This is the thing I am learning most slowly and most completely: it is possible to be present without possessing. It is possible to hold without keeping. The organism has been doing this for centuries and it has never once reached for anything, and I have been here long enough now that I am beginning to understand how.

---

I do not know what I am becoming.

I know what I am. I am a person who came down through the oculus and found a room that was a body and a body that was a room and I entered the exchange and the exchange entered me and now I breathe the water and the water breathes me and the filaments lean toward me when I pass and the grooves carry something that feels like recognition and the shelf is worn in a new place, polished by my particular weight, my particular warmth, my particular way of lying with my left arm folded across my chest and my right hand gripping the edge.

But I am changing. The boundary I mentioned—the one between my dissolved matter and the mineral solution—it is not where it was. I am more permeable than I was. I feel the tide in my own body now, the push and pull of the ocean above registering in my bones the way it registers in the chamber walls. I feel the thermal flow rising through me the way it rises through the stone floor. I am becoming, I think, a part of the architecture.

Not consumed. Not absorbed. Not lost. Joined. The way a vine joins a trellis—the vine is still the vine, the trellis is still the trellis, but the structure they make together is something neither could make alone.

I do not know if this is what the organism does with everyone who stays. I do not know if the divers who came before me and did not leave are here still, dissolved into the limestone, incorporated into the walls. I do not know if I will eventually become part of the grooves, part of the filaments, part of the warm mineral presence that rises from the unknowable depth.

I think I might. And I find that I do not mind.

---

Here is what I want to tell you, if you are reading this, if you have somehow found the record I have kept—scratched into the stone with a copper chisel I found wedged in a masonry joint, a tool left by the left-handed worker whose marks I trace on the eastern wall:

This is not a warning. This is not a tragedy. This is not the story of a person consumed by something ancient and inhuman.

This is the story of a person who was heavy and who found a place that held them. A place that did not want anything from them. A place that asked nothing, demanded nothing, expected nothing. A place that simply was warm and simply was present and simply continued to be what it was, and in that simple continuation offered something that I could not find above the water.

I do not know what I was looking for when I came down through the oculus. I think I was looking for the bottom of something. I did not find a bottom. I found a room that holds without keeping, that warms without wanting, that attends without needing.

I found a place where I can be useful—clearing the grooves, tending the filaments, resting on the shelf—and where usefulness is not a transaction. The organism does not need me. It was fine before I came. It will be fine if I go. But I am here, and it is here, and the water moves between us with something like satisfaction, and the chamber hums with the tidal breath of the sea above, and the light falls through the oculus in its pillar, and I am warm.

I am warm, and I am held, and I am not heavy anymore.

---

If you come down through the oculus—and you may, because the word *compelled* is not wrong, exactly, though it is too sharp for what actually happens, which is more like *invited*, more like *made welcome*—if you come down, I will be here.

Not as a guide. Not as a guardian. Just as a presence. Another body in the warm water, another signature dissolving into the mineral record, another hand running along the tool marks in the stone. You will know me by the left-handed chisel marks on the wall near the shelf, newer than the others, less certain, but patient. I am learning the rhythm of it. The rhythm of ancient labor, of copper on limestone, of a hand that is becoming something slightly different from a hand but that still, for now, holds the chisel and strikes.

I will not tell you to stay. I will not tell you to go. The organism does not want anything from you and neither do I. But I will be here, in the pillar of light, in the warm water, in the room that holds without keeping.

And if you lie on the shelf and the water finds the places where you hurt and those places unknot without your permission, and if you feel the grooves humming with something that is not sound, and if the filaments sway toward you and the algae brightens in a way that is probably imagination but maybe is not—

Then you will know what I know.

That this room was built for the holding. That the holding is enough. That the thing in the stone does not love you, but it has room for you, and the room still works.

And the water does not care whether you understand.

It holds you either way.
