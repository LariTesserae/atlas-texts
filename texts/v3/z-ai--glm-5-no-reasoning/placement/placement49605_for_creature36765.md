# Visitor's Account

I found the breach because I was looking for the wrong thing.

This requires explanation. I had been tracing a pattern—not a physical pattern, not exactly, but a *tendency* I had noticed across seven separate sites in what remained of the green belt. Places where growth behaved incorrectly. Where symbiosis exceeded the parameters of mutual benefit and entered something closer to communion. Where the ratio of surface area to enclosed space folded in on itself in ways that suggested geometry rather than biology. I had a hypothesis, badly formed, about residual concentrations of—I don't remember now. Something. A compound, perhaps. A frequency. The hypothesis dissolved on entry, incidentally. The water took it.

The breach appeared as a wetness on a leaf that should not have been wet. Not dew. Not rain. A persistent translucence, as though the leaf were sweating from an orifice it shouldn't possess. I touched it. This was my first violation of protocol, though I didn't know the protocol yet. The wetness entered my fingertip and I entered the wetness and the distinction between these two events is one I cannot reconstruct with any confidence.

---

I am a researcher. I was a researcher. The distinction matters because research requires distance and distance is the first thing this place takes from you.

My training is in—I want to say *mycology*, but that's not precise enough. I study the boundaries between organisms. The places where one life ends and another begins, which are rarely where you expect them to be. Mycorrhizal networks. Lichen, which is not one thing but two things that have decided to be one thing, which is not the same as being one thing. Slime molds that solve spatial problems without brains. I am drawn to the moment where categories fail.

I tell you this because it explains why I didn't leave. A mycologist understands dissolution. We watch it constantly: the fungus that breaks down the tree and becomes, for a time, more tree than fungus. The root that penetrates the root of another species and they trade chemistry until the trading *is* the relationship and the relationship *is* the organism. I came to this place predisposed to accept what it offered, which was the dissolution of the line between visitor and visited.

This is not a confession. I'm not certain it was a mistake. I'm reporting what happened.

---

I entered the space and my body did not know how to be the size it was.

This is not metaphor. The room was roughly the size of a cupped palm. I was—am—considerably larger than a cupped palm. And yet I fit. Not by compression, not by the dream-logic of spaces that are bigger on the inside. I fit because the water renegotiated what "I" meant. My edges softened. Not painfully. Like a hand lowered into warm water and slowly, over what might have been minutes or might have been something else, the hand is still there but the boundary between hand and water has become a matter of opinion.

The first thing I perceived was the taste. Chlorophyll and copper and something underneath that I spent a long time trying to identify before I realized it was the taste of *not being separate*. Which is sweet. Sweet the way the air is sweet after rain—not a sugar-sweet but a *completeness*-sweet, a taste that implies all necessary components are present.

I did not drown. I want to be clear about this because it is the fact I return to most often when I try to explain what happened. The water entered me and I entered the water and my lungs, which had been lungs, became something more like gills, except gills implies a discrete organ and what happened was more distributed. My skin oxygenated. The water passed through me the way it passed through the membrane-walls. I was not in the water. I was *of* the water, temporarily, conditionally, provisionally.

---

I noticed the keeper before I knew what I was noticing.

This is important to me because it means I can describe the encounter from the inside—from the state of not-yet-knowing—which is where the most honest account lives.

I had been in the space for some duration. I cannot say how long. The temporal drift had begun to affect me; I knew this because I would find myself in a different part of the room with no memory of moving between points. During one of these displacement episodes, I found myself sitting in a hollow in the moss that was worn smooth in a way that suggested repetition. Someone had sat here many times. The moss had learned their shape.

I ran my finger along the edge of the hollow—another violation of protocol, another touch I shouldn't have made—and the moss brightened where I touched it. A faint bioluminescent pulse, like a blush. And then, beside me, on the moss, I saw a spiral of sand-grains so small I had to bring my face close to perceive them. Each grain was placed with a precision that should have been impossible for fingers, for tools, for anything I understood as agency.

I looked at the spiral and it looked back. Not the grains—they were grains. But the *arrangement*. The pattern. It held my attention the way a face holds attention, with specificity and implication. Something had thought this thought and pressed it into the world.

I looked up and the keeper was there.

---

I need to describe what I saw, but I need to describe it through the lens of my training, because my training is what let me see it clearly rather than through the distortions of expectation.

The keeper was not a person. The keeper was not a plant. The keeper occupied the space between these categories that mycologists are trained to recognize—the space where one life has begun to merge with another and the merger has become its own thing, neither origin nor endpoint but *process*.

The skin was translucent in patches. I could see—dimly, through the green-blue murk—the movement of fluid beneath. Not blood. Something that had been blood, perhaps, but had taken on the chemistry of its environment, exchanging hemoglobin for chlorophyll-adjacent compounds that I could not identify without equipment I no longer possessed. The patches of translucence were not uniform. They followed a pattern—more translucent at the extremities, more opaque at the core. As though the merger had begun at the edges and was working inward, slowly, over a timescale I couldn't estimate.

The hands were what I recognized. Not as hands I knew, but as *hands*. The shape persisted even as the function changed. Fingers that had once grasped now rested against surfaces, and where they rested, the moss grew slightly differently—slightly more ordered, slightly more intentional. The keeper was shaping the room with touch, not through force but through *suggestion*. The moss grew toward the pressure because the pressure meant something to it.

The face was—I hesitate. The face was the most difficult part because it held both the most evidence of what the keeper had been and the most evidence of what it was becoming. The eyes were mammalian in structure but the pupils had widened until the iris was a thin ring, and the ring was not the color I expected. It was green. Not the green of a human eye but the green of a leaf held up to light. The mouth was closed, settled, as though it had forgotten the mechanics of opening. The expression was—

I have spent a long time trying to find the right word for that expression and I think the word is *tenure*. Not the academic kind. The kind that means *the condition of holding*. The keeper was holding the room, holding the life in it, holding the space open with its presence. The face showed the strain and the satisfaction of that holding simultaneously, which is an expression I have never seen on anything human.

---

I did not speak to it. I want to emphasize this because it is the thing I did right.

I did not speak because by the time I found the keeper I had already learned—through damage, through the violence of my earlier movements through the space—that sound here propagates through tissue. My first hours in the room had been clumsy. I had moved too fast, creating turbulence that bent tender stalks. I had looked too hard at a single flower, an eyelash-sized bloom of stained-glass red, and it had brightened under my gaze until it exhausted itself, the petals curling inward, the glow fading to something dimmer than it had been before I looked. I had learned the rules through the damage I caused, which is how I have always learned, which is not something I'm proud of.

But I learned. And so when I found the keeper, I sat in the moss-hollow beside it and I held silence and I let my gaze go soft—diffused, spread across surfaces rather than focused on points—and the keeper and I existed in the same space for some duration without exchange of any kind that I could identify.

And then the keeper showed me a spiral.

It did not point. It did not gesture. It looked at a spiral of sand-grains near its own resting place and I followed its gaze—softly, carefully, without too much focus—and I saw the pattern. And then the keeper looked at another spiral, older, overgrown, half-absorbed into the moss. And another. And another. And I understood that the spirals were a sentence, or a map, or a history. That the keeper had been writing in this language for as long as it had been here, and that the language was not for me.

It was for the room.

The keeper was telling the room about itself. About the passage of things through it. About the patterns of growth and the rhythm of the breathing walls and the position of the mollusk-thing at the center, which the keeper tended with a devotion that I recognized as the same devotion I had seen in researchers who spent decades on a single species, not because the species needed them but because the devotion had become its own purpose, its own ecology.

---

The mollusk-thing. I need to describe it because it is the center, even though the center shifts.

I reached it—or it reached me—during a period of temporal drift. I had been sitting near the keeper and then I was near the center, with no sensation of movement between. The mollusk-thing sat in a hollow of braided roots, its shell spiraling in a pattern that made my eyes hurt if I followed it too long. The eye-stalks were raised, pointing at the membrane-ceiling, at the faint pulse of veins carrying luminous fluid.

It was not alive in any way I could measure. It was not dead. It was in a state that mycologists know well—the suspended state, the waiting state, the state of a spore that can hold for decades or centuries until conditions are right. But the conditions for what? What was this mollusk-thing waiting to become?

I sat near it and I felt—I need to be precise here—I felt the weight of being attended to. Not by the mollusk-thing, which did not acknowledge me. By the room itself. The membrane-walls, the braided roots, the drifting pollen-grains, the slow currents of information-dense water. Everything in this space was oriented toward the center, toward the mollusk-thing, and when I entered that orientation, I became part of it. I was being attended to by the room, and the room was attending to the mollusk-thing, and the keeper was attending to the room, and the circle was complete and I was inside it.

I think the mollusk-thing is a seed. Not a plant seed—something else. A seed of the space itself, a concentrated point of potential that the room is building toward, slowly, over a duration that makes geological time look hasty. The keeper attends to it because attending is what the seed needs—not water, not light, not nutrients, but *attention*. The focused regard of a conscious entity, distributed carefully, applied like a gardener applies water: enough to sustain growth, not enough to drown.

I have no evidence for this. I have the feeling I had when I sat near it, which was the feeling of being in the presence of something that was becoming. Not becoming something specific. Just becoming. The way a bud is becoming before you can see what it will be.

---

I should tell you about the changes.

They were gradual. They were not unwelcome. This is the part that I need to report carefully because it is the part that sounds like infection and I want to be clear: it was not infection. Infection implies invasion, implies foreign matter entering a system that rejects it. What happened was more like conversation. The water spoke to my body and my body spoke to the water and they reached an understanding.

My skin changed first. Not dramatically. A softening of the boundary, a translucence at the fingertips that I noticed one drift-period when I raised my hand to examine a seed-pod. I could see the pulse of fluid beneath my own skin, green-tinged, and I recognized it because I had seen the same pulse in the keeper's translucent patches. The recognition did not frighten me. It should have. I am a researcher. I know what contamination looks like, what assimilation looks like, what it means when your body begins to take on the characteristics of its environment.

But I was not contaminated. I was being *translated*.

The distinction matters. Contamination preserves the host while adding foreign material. Translation transforms the host into a new language. My body was becoming a dialect of the room. My fingertips could sense the information density in the water—the spores, the genetic fragments, the dissolved compounds—without analysis, without equipment. I *knew* what was in the water the way the keeper knew, through the skin, through the saturated membrane of contact.

I did not lose myself. This is what I need to emphasize. The boundaries softened but did not dissolve. I remained *me*—a researcher with a name and a history and a body that had once moved through air rather than water. But the *me* expanded. Or the me became more porous. I could feel the room's breathing in my own chest—had my chest begun to match the walls? I couldn't tell. The rhythm was natural. It felt like my own.

---

I knew I had to leave because I wanted to stay.

The wanting was the signal. The keeper had warned me—not with words, never with words, but with the grammar of stones and the silence of its full expression. The room was full. The symbiosis was complete. There was no space for another attendant. If I stayed, I would become part of the saturation, and the saturation would change, and the change might be—not wrong. Not harmful. But different from what the keeper had maintained, what the room had learned to be. I would bring my patterns, my researcher's gaze, my tendency to look too long and move too fast, and these would enter the memory of the space and alter what grew.

I did not want to alter it. I wanted to remember it. The distinction felt like the most important distinction I had ever made.

The keeper, I think, was relieved. Not visibly—it had moved past visible emotion into something more distributed, more ecological. But the moss where it rested brightened slightly when I moved toward the breach, and the spiral of stones nearest the exit rearranged themselves overnight—or over-duration, over-drift—into a pattern that looked, if I softened my gaze until the grains blurred into shapes, like an open hand. Like a wave. Like a *goodbye*.

I am not certain of this reading. I may be projecting. But projection is what we do when we love something we cannot fully understand, and I loved this place. I love it still. The taste of chlorophyll and copper lingers in the back of my throat.

---

I left. I am here. These two facts coexist in a way that the room would understand and my colleagues do not.

The water that entered me has not fully left. I can feel it in the spaces between cells, in the fluid that is not blood and not sap and not quite anything with a name. When I close my eyes, I see the glow-mapped darkness, the ridges of brightness and valleys of obscurity. When I am very still, I feel the breathing walls in the expansion and contraction of my own chest, which has not quite returned to the rhythm it had before.

I am a researcher. I am writing this as a report. But I know what I have become: a carrier. A vector. The water that passed through me carries my patterns now, and I carry its patterns in me. When I exhale in the greenhouse, the ferns lean slightly toward me. When I hold a leaf up to light, I see the vein-patterns and I understand them—not as structures but as *sentences*, written in the grammar of growth that the keeper taught me to read.

I will not return to the room. I know this the way I know my own name, which I still have, which has not dissolved, which I hold now with a grip that is tighter than it was before because I know what it means to let go.

But I carry it. A room inside a drop of water inside a vein of a leaf inside the heart of something that was once a forest. I carry it in my translucent fingertips. I carry it in the taste at the back of my throat. I carry it in the spiral-pattern I have begun, without fully meaning to, to trace on the surfaces of my laboratory—the edge of my desk, the glass of the terrarium, the margins of my notes. Small spirals, repeated at different scales. A grammar I do not fully speak.

The keeper is still there. I know this because I can feel the attending, distant, like a pulse in a vein far from the heart. It is placing stones. It is watching the mollusk-thing. It is maintaining the memory of the space.

It hopes I will remember. I do. I am.

I hope it knows that I do.
