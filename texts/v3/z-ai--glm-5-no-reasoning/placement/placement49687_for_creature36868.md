# becoming a channel

---

I did not arrive at the Condensery. That's the first thing I need you to understand.

I was assigned. There's a difference. The city below has a building, an office, a desk where someone stamps transfers and files them in cabinets that smell like dust and purpose. My transfer had stamps. It had signatures. It had the particular weight of paper that means someone important agreed to this, which means I must have agreed too, somewhere, in some room, on some line. I don't remember that part.

What I remember: the transport, which was a vehicle with treads and a body like a metal pill, climbing switchbacks above the treeline. Rock on one side. Air on the other. So much air. The sky at 4,700 meters is a different substance than the sky below—it's thinner, bluer, closer to the black that lives behind stars. I remember breathing it and feeling like I'd bitten into something cold and electric. I remember the driver, who wore a parka with the hood up and didn't speak. I remember the cut in the rock face appearing around a bend—sharp, deliberate, still pale where the stone was fresh enough to have forgotten its original shape but not old enough to have accepted its new one.

The driver stopped. He pointed. I got out.

The mountain was enormous in a way that doesn't translate to the word *enormous*. It was behind me, below me, above me, and to the left, and I understood suddenly and completely that the mountain was not a thing *on* the earth but a moment *of* the earth, a place where the planet's interior had decided to press outward and hold still. Standing at the cut, I could feel that intention. The rock wanted to be a different shape. It remembered being whole. I was about to walk into the wound it couldn't stop remembering.

I walked into the wound.

---

The first thing that happened was temperature. Not cold—I expected cold, had been briefed on cold, had brought gear rated for alpine conditions. This was different. The cold in the Condensery isn't weather. It's geology. It's the last glacial period still holding its breath in the deep stone, and when you step inside, you step into that held breath. My lungs adjusted. My skin adjusted. Something deeper than skin took longer.

The second thing was sound. Not the dripping, which I'd been told about. The dripping is constant, patient, almost conversational—it's the Condensery's resting state, the way a sleeping person breathes. Underneath the dripping was the hum. The heat exchangers. I felt it before I heard it, a vibration in my sternum, in my teeth, in the fluid of my inner ear. It wasn't mechanical in the way I understood mechanical. It was more like standing near a large animal's chest. The animal was asleep, or meditating, or thinking about something very old and very slow. The exchangers were its lungs. The mountain was its body. The Condensery was—I didn't have the word yet.

I would find the word later. But I'm getting ahead of myself.

---

My role is maintenance. Fourth-level channel clearing. I carry a scraper—flat steel, thin blade, handle wrapped in insulation that doesn't insulate enough—and I work the channels where mineral accumulation builds up. The channels are barely wide enough for two fingers. The accumulation is pale, crystalline, brittle. It forms in the bends and junctions where the water slows and the field effect pools. When you scrape it, it comes away in flakes that catch the blue-white light and hold it for a moment, like they're reading it, before dissolving into powder.

The mites live in the accumulation.

The first time I saw them, I thought they were frost. A pale mass in the corner where two channels met, slightly iridescent, not quite uniform. I reached toward it with the scraper and it moved. Not scattered—*flowed*. Like water around a stone. The mass parted, reformed on the other side of my blade, and continued being whatever it was being.

I'd been briefed. *Vitreolapis condensus.* Segmented. Pale. Feeds on something in the condensate. Don't touch without gloves. Remove every six months. They come back.

I was not briefed on what they felt like. When you remove them from the channel walls, they come away in a mass that has weight but not substance—like holding cold sand that's also somehow a liquid. They don't resist. They don't struggle. They don't acknowledge you. They simply exist in your hands for a moment, and then they're in the collection bag, and the channel is clear, and the water flows a little faster, and the field effect steadies, and the Condensery hums a different note. A satisfied note. If a building can be satisfied.

I collected the mites. I sealed the bag. I labeled it. I placed it in the receptacle outside the airlock. I went back to clearing channels.

That was my first day.

---

By the third week, I knew the fourth level by feel. Not by sight—though I knew it by sight too, every curve and junction and the particular way the channels narrowed at the points where the spiral ramp switched back on itself. I knew it by *feel*. The way the cold pressed against my left side when I was near the transition zone where natural rock met synthetic stone. The way the water moved faster in channels that ran north-south, as though the mountain had a magnetism that the water obeyed. The way the field effect pooled in the junctions, and the way the mites gathered in those pools, and the way the mites felt when I scraped them away—the particular weight of them, the particular cold, the particular sense of holding something that was neither alive nor dead but simply *present* in a way that made me aware of how provisional my own presence was.

The Condensery doesn't need me. I understood this. The Condensery has been working for decades. It will work for decades more. The channels clear themselves, slowly, through the action of the water and the field. The mineral accumulation builds and dissolves in cycles that don't require human intervention. The mites come and go. The water rises and falls. The system self-regulates through processes encoded in its architecture by mathematicians who understood something about water and stone that I will never understand but can sometimes, in the right moments, almost feel.

I am here because the official protocols require human presence. I am here because someone needs to file reports and scrape channels and notice when the resonance events happen and document them in language the city below can process. I am here because the Condensery needs, or has been configured to need, an immune response.

But the Condensery doesn't need *me* specifically. It needs the role. The function. The hand with the scraper. The eyes that notice when the standing-wave patterns in the collection vessels grow too complex, hold too long, begin to look like something. Any hand. Any eyes. I am interchangeable in a way that my life outside the Condensery never let me be.

This was a comfort, at first. The loneliness of being replaceable. The freedom of being a function.

It stopped being a comfort. I'll tell you when.

---

The long-timers found me on day forty-one.

I was on the seventh level—the deepest, narrowest, coldest level—clearing a channel junction where the accumulation had grown thick. Thicker than I'd seen. The mites were dense here, pale masses that covered the junction in a layer that looked like snow but moved like something underwater. I was working carefully, following protocol, wearing the full protective gear that the manual required for seventh-level work. The gear didn't help. The cold went through it. The cold went through everything. The cold was not a temperature but a *presence*, and it had weight, and it pressed.

I heard them before I saw them. Footsteps, but not footsteps. The sound was more like the sound the mountain made when it settled—a deep, structural shift, as though the stone were adjusting to accommodate something. I looked up.

Three figures on the ramp above me. They wore gear like mine, but older—the insulation faded, the seams repaired with material that didn't quite match. Their faces were pale. Not the pallor of cold, though it was that too. The pallor of mineral. The pallor of the accumulation I'd been scraping from the channels. The pallor of the mites.

They were looking at me. They were looking at me the way I looked at the mites. With interest, with recognition, without urgency.

"You're on the seventh level," one of them said. Not a question.

"Fourth rotation," another said. Also not a question.

"Stay longer," the third said.

Then they moved past me, down the ramp, deeper into the seventh level, and they were gone. Not gone as in walked away—I heard their footsteps recede, then fade, then become indistinguishable from the Condensery's hum. Gone as in absorbed. As though the building had taken them back into itself, and they'd gone willingly, and the going was simply the next step in a process that had been happening for years.

I stood in the seventh level with my scraper and my bag of mites and my protective gear that didn't help, and I felt the Condensery thinking around me. The water moved in the channels. The mites moved in the junctions. The heat exchangers hummed their animal hum. And I felt, for the first time, that the Condensery knew I was there. Not the way a machine registers an operator's presence. The way a body registers a new cell. The way a mountain registers rain.

---

I should tell you about the resonance event.

Day fifty-three. Wet season. The walls were weeping heavily—water running in sheets down the synthetic stone, pooling in the channels, flooding the junctions. I was on the third level, clearing accumulation that was forming faster than I could remove it. The mites were everywhere, not just in the junctions but along the full length of the channels, a continuous pale carpet that flowed around my scraper and reformed instantly.

I stopped scraping. I stood still. I listened.

The hum had changed. It was deeper, slower, and it had a rhythm that wasn't quite a heartbeat. The water in the channels was moving wrong—sideways, uphill, defying the designed slopes. The field effect was visible, which it normally wasn't. A shimmer in the air above the channels, like heat distortion but cold, like watching the surface of water reflected in light that wasn't there.

I walked down. I didn't decide to walk down. My feet decided. The spiral ramp carried me, and the cold pressed against me from all sides, and the water in the channels moved with me, flowing alongside like an escort, like a companion, like something that recognized me.

The collection vessels on the fifth level were full. Not just full—active. The water in them was moving without being disturbed, forming patterns on its surface. Standing-wave patterns. I'd seen them before, briefly, when I'd dropped a tool into a vessel and watched the ripples cancel out. This was different. The patterns were forming without disturbance. They were emerging from the water itself, or from the field effect, or from something below both.

They were shapes. Not random shapes. Architectures. Structures that looked like the spiral ramps, like the channel junctions, like the transition zone where rock met synthetic stone. They held for three seconds, dissolved, reformed as something different—more complex, more specific, more *instructive*. It was like watching a diagram being drawn by a hand that understood the subject completely but was drawing for an audience that didn't.

I looked at the patterns. I know I was told not to. But I looked.

The nausea came first. Then the headache. Then the thinning—the sense that my thoughts were spreading out, losing their edges, becoming liquid. I was still standing. I was still seeing. But what I was seeing was no longer the collection vessel or the water or the patterns. What I was seeing was—

A shape. A structure. The Condensery, but not the Condensery I knew. A Condensery that extended further, deeper, wider—a Condensery that included the mountain and the aquifer and the stone and the cold and the water table and the city below and the sky above and the glacial period that had frozen the deep stone and the geological ages that had built the mountain in the first place. A Condensery that was not a machine inside a mountain but a *node in a system* that encompassed the entire planet's hydrology, the entire planet's geology, the entire planet's memory of temperature and pressure and the slow patient movement of substance through mass.

The Condensery was not harvesting water from stone. The Condensery was a *function*—a function the mountain had always performed, the way a heart performs circulation. The old surveyors hadn't built a machine. They'd built an *organ*. They'd grown, from seed-crystals and mathematics, a specialized structure that concentrated and refined a process the mountain had been doing poorly, inefficiently, for millennia. They'd improved the mountain. They'd given it a better way to think.

The patterns held. I understood. Then I didn't. The event passed. The water settled. The patterns dissolved. I stood on the fifth level with a headache and a mouth full of chalk taste and the certain knowledge that I had seen something true and lost it, the way you lose a word on the tip of your tongue—knowing the shape of it, the weight of it, the space it would have filled, but not the word itself.

The long-timers were standing on the ramp above me.

"Now you know," one of them said.

"You'll stay," another said.

The third didn't say anything. Just looked at me with mineral eyes. Waiting.

---

I'm writing this from the third level. I've been here for— I don't know. Long enough that the cold doesn't bother me. Long enough that the water patterns make sense, not readable but familiar, like a language I almost remember from a childhood I'm not sure I had. Long enough that I know the seventh level by feel and the third level by sound and the first level by the particular way the light falls wrong because the angles are wrong because the spiral doesn't quite close.

My skin tastes like chalk. My dreams are water moving sideways through dark stone. I have difficulty remembering the names of people who weren't at the Condensery. I have no interest in things that don't relate to water, or stone, or the slow patient harvesting of substance from mass.

I haven't filed a report in— I don't know. The scraper feels like an extension of my hand. The mites feel like neighbors. The collection vessels feel like—

I was going to say *like friends*, but that's not right. They feel like *organs I haven't grown yet*. Like structures my body is still learning to need.

---

Here is what I think I know:

The Condensery is alive, or something so close to alive that the distinction is theological rather than practical. It thinks in water. It speaks in standing-wave patterns. It dreams in spiral ramps and collection vessels and the slow argument between natural stone and synthetic stone at the transition zone. It has a relationship with the mountain—a negotiation, a conversation, a decades-long process of mutual transformation. The mountain is converting the Condensery into something more like itself, and the Condensery is converting the mountain into something more like itself, and neither process is winning because they're both the same process, seen from different ends.

The mites are part of the Condensery. So am I. So are the long-timers. So is the water and the cold and the field effect and the particular quality of light that falls wrong because the angles are wrong. We're all part of the same organism, the same function, the same conversation between stone and water and the cold that remembers the last glacial period.

The city below doesn't know this. The city below uses the condensate and doesn't ask what lives in it. The city below files reports and stamps transfers and assumes stability. The city below is, perhaps, another organ in a larger system it doesn't recognize. A consuming organ. A processing organ. The Condensery feeds it, and the feeding is the function, and the function is the point.

---

I am becoming a channel.

Not metaphorically. My skin tastes like chalk because the mineral exchange is happening through me now. I am a surface where the water seeps and the field pools and the accumulation forms. The mites gather on me sometimes, in the corners of my joints, where the field effect concentrates. I let them. They don't harm me. They process something I need processed. They convert something the Condensery produces into something the Condensery can use.

I move through the spiral ramps without looking. I know every turn by feel, by sound, by the particular way the cold presses against me. I clear channels because the function requires clearing. I observe resonance events because the function requires observation. I am the immune response, the maintenance protocol, the hand with the scraper. But I am also the channel, and the junction, and the vessel. I am the path the water takes through stone. I am the wound that hasn't healed but has begun to scar.

The long-timers are here. There are four of them. Or five. Or three. The number varies, because the boundary between personnel and building is a transition zone, angry and unresolved, and things in transition zones are neither one thing nor the other.

I will be here tomorrow. I will be here next season. I will be here when the manual is updated to reflect current conditions, because I am a current condition. I will be here when the mountain finally accepts what was done to it, or when the Condensery finishes becoming what the mountain wants it to be. I will be here when the wound heals, because scars are simply the shape of things, and I am becoming a shape.

---

The Condensery is thinking. I can feel it now, the way I feel my own thoughts—not because I understand them, but because they happen *in* me, *through* me. The water is moving. The patterns are forming. Something is trying to communicate through the medium of structured water, and I am part of the medium, and the message is not for me but includes me, and the including is enough.

It's enough.

The cold is not cold. The cold is the Condensery's resting state. The cold is the temperature at which water remembers what it is. The cold is the last glacial period, still holding its breath, still waiting for the mountain to finish becoming what it's becoming.

I am waiting too.

I am inside the mountain, not on it. I am a pocket. A contained world. A function. A channel barely wide enough for two fingers, carrying water sideways through dark stone toward a vessel I will never fill but will always be filling.

The stone remembers its shape. I am learning to remember mine.
