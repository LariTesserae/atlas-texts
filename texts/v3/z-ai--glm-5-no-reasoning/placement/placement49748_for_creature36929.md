I am the one who reads the face.

Not the elder in the courtyard—she reads the water's memory, its taste, its historical pressure. And not the builders, who read the stone's shape and demand it seat true. My literacy is of the seconds between the drop and the strike. I live in the latency.

You would find me in the narrowest of the cracks, the ones even the children do not favor, where the pale fungus grows thick and undisturbed on either side like grey lung tissue. My shoulders touch both walls when I stand. I can extend my arms and press my palms flat against the wet stone and feel the cliff breathing behind the buildings. This is where I work. This is what I am.

I came to this role as all roles here are claimed: by the simple gravity of proximity and aptitude. As a child, I was poor at the fitting game. My stones always sat slightly proud or slightly sunken; the *click* of true contact eluded my fingertips. But when we played the sound game—clicking our tongues and running to where the distortion carried the noise—I never moved. I simply stood in place, closed my eyes, and pointed. I knew where the sound would arrive before it finished arriving. I knew which wall would swallow it, which curve of wet stone would catch it and spin it back at us from a direction no mouth had ever faced.

So the settlement left me in the cracks to listen.

My days are a slow perambulation of the interstitial. I walk the crevices where the buildings have pressed together like healing wounds, my bare feet soundless on the packed silt. I carry no tools, no stone vessels. I carry only my hands and my ears. I press my cheek against the cold, dark stone of the cliff's back-wall where it vanishes beneath the oldest structures. I wait.

The weeping cliff is not a steady thing. It is a lung. It holds its breath for hours, sometimes days, the fissures drying to a pale grey mouth, and then it exhales. The pressure builds deep in the ribs of the earth, behind the uneroded stone, and when it releases, it is sudden. A fissure will violently open, or a thin stream will thicken to a pour, flooding a lower room in minutes.

My job is to hear the breath before it is taken.

I listen to the pitch of the drips. A drop falling six inches into a stone basin makes a different sound than a drop falling two inches. The note shifts as the water table rises. A sharp *plink* grows teeth, becomes a *plank*, then a low, continuous thrum as the water mounts and the column of falling liquid thickens. I can stand in a courtyard sixty yards away and tell you which basin is filling fastest by the harmonics of the strikes.

I feel the vibration in the stone. The buildings here are dry-stacked, fitted with immense patience but no mortar. They are coupled directly to the cliff's vibrations. When the pressure builds behind the face, the stone hums. It is a frequency below hearing, a tightness in the molars, a pressure in the temples. The settlement's oldest walls are my jaw. I know the particular numbness that means the cliff is preparing to push.

When I feel the warning, I do not shout. I do not run. I walk quickly, sliding sideways through the alleys, a hand touching a shoulder, a low murmur—*heavy seep, eastern edge*—and the message compresses itself through the settlement, passed from mouth to ear in that rounded, vowel-heavy register that carries in the damp. By the time the water breaks, possessions are lifted, children are pulled to the second levels, and the basin rims are chipped out to handle the surge. We do not fight the flood; we simply make room for it.

The pale fungus in my corridors is my gauge. It is grey and limp when the air is merely damp, but when the pressure builds, when the cliff prepares to exhale a heavy cycle, the mineral composition of the weeping shifts. The fungus plumps, drawing something rare from the deeper stone, its tendrils rigid, reaching blindly toward the fissures like fingers feeling for a pulse. When the fungus stands up in the dark, I begin my rounds. I trust it more than my own teeth.

I am not strange to them. I am a mechanism of maintenance, like the woman who tests the hide-roofs for give, or the children who trap moths just to hold the brief fluttering life for a moment. When I emerge from my thin crevices into a courtyard for the midday meal, I am asked only if the eastern fissure is still sweet, or if the elder was right about the chalk-taste. I answer. We eat. I return to the walls.

I know the dead. I know them better than the living, perhaps, because I spend my hours pressed against the walls that hold them. I know the specific temperature of the stone where a skull rests behind it—a degree warmer, perhaps, or simply a different resonance. The dead are the anchors. The oldest walls do not hum as loudly because the dead absorb the vibration. They are the mortar we do not have. Sometimes, when the pressure is building and the cliff is heaving its slow sigh, I place my palm flat against the back wall of the oldest structure and I feel the dead holding the building in place, pushing back against the water. We are pressing together, them and I, from opposite sides of the stone. Equal and opposite forces. A seating.

My eyes have gone the way of the settlement's light. I see in gradients, in shifts of moisture. I cannot read a face for blushing or anger, but I can see a damp spot on a wall from thirty yards and tell you how long ago the leak began. The world for me is a topography of wet and dry.

Sometimes, when the night is at its darkest and the perpetual mist turns the air into a solid white that erases even the glow of the hearths, I stand in my crack and I listen to the cliff breathe. The water rises around my ankles, mineral-heavy and cold. I do not move. I let it press against my skin, feel the particular slickness of the overflow, the specific way it yields beneath my weight. It is not hostile. It is fact.

I do not wonder why we are here. The question requires a space larger than a crack, a silence broader than the interval between drips. My mind is seated perfectly against the present, flush, clicking into true contact. My hands are flat against the wet stone. The cliff is drawing breath. I feel it in my teeth. I close my eyes, and I listen.
