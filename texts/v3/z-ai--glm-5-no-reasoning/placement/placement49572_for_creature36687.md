I find myself in the Drainage Under-level, where the pumps fight their endless war. This is not by choice—not originally. I came down through an access shaft thirty-seven days ago, following a maintenance route that I hoped would connect to a surface exit three kilometers north. The map was wrong. The route flooded sometime in the past decade, and by the time I realized my mistake, the water had risen enough to make the ladder rungs too slick for climbing back.

So I stayed.

---

**What I Am Now**

I am a surveyor by trade, or I was. The designation feels less meaningful here. I still carry the instruments—chloride meter, vibration counter, the handheld that was supposed to map my progress through the Works and now serves mostly as a light source during the dark cycles. But a surveyor implies someone who leaves, who returns to report. I have become something else: a resident, though I don't use that word.

The cold has changed me. My hands are always stiff now, and I've lost feeling in the tips of two fingers on my left hand—frostbite damage from a night spent too close to a drainage channel when the temperature dropped faster than I expected. I've learned to compensate, to move more carefully, to test surfaces before committing weight.

My sense of hearing has sharpened in ways that would be useful on the surface. I can identify specific machines by their acoustic signatures now: the deep-throated pump in Sector 7 that shudders on its downstroke, the conveyor in Gallery 3 whose bearing needs grease so badly I wince each time it cycles past my position. I've started giving them names, which is probably a bad sign.

---

**My Territory**

I've established myself in a maintenance alcove between two pump housings. The location is defensible—only one approach, visible from my sleeping position—and warm enough that the water in my collection vessels doesn't freeze overnight. The pumps here run hotter than the Works average, which I attribute to the growth thickening inside their intake valves. They're fighting harder to move the same volume of water, and the excess heat radiates into the surrounding air.

I've mapped approximately two hundred meters in each direction from my position. The Under-level has a different character than the upper Works—more vertical space, more water, more darkness. The lighting here failed long ago, and I navigate by the handheld's glow, by touch, and by the dim bioluminescence that clusters near the oldest drains.

I know where the water pools during flood cycles and which grating to lift to reach the channels that stay accessible. I know which pipes vibrate with pressure and which have gone silent, clogged by the growth. I know that if I follow the drainage channel north-by-northeast for approximately forty minutes, I will reach a reservoir where the splashing sounds originate, though I have not approached close enough to see what makes them.

---

**The Encounter**

I saw one of the swimmers on day twenty-three.

I had ventured toward the reservoir during a quiet cycle, drawn by curiosity that outweighed caution. The machines had synchronized—a rare event, perhaps once per week—and the stillness felt like an invitation. I stood at the water's edge, handheld casting its yellow glow across the surface, and waited.

It rose slowly. A pale mass, darker than the water it displaced, trailing tendrils that gripped nothing because there was nothing to grip in the open reservoir. It didn't break the surface—just hovered below, rotating slightly, testing the boundary between its world and mine.

I couldn't see eyes. I couldn't see anything that would register my presence. But I felt observed. Not in the paranoid way of feeling watched in the dark—this was more specific, more localized. The mass drifted closer, stopped, drifted closer again. Testing. Learning.

Then the machines resumed their rhythm, and it sank back into the depths without hurry or alarm. The splash when it submerged was barely audible over the returning noise.

I have not approached the reservoir since. Not from fear—the encounter didn't feel dangerous—but from a sense that I had trespassed far enough. What lives in the water has its territory. I have mine.

---

**The Growth I Carry**

On day thirty-one, I cut my palm on a jagged pipe fitting. The wound wasn't deep, but it bled freely, and I dressed it with cloth torn from my undershirt. Standard procedure, the kind of basic first aid that any surveyor learns.

Three days later, I noticed the edges of the wound had changed. Not infected—there was no inflammation, no heat, no pain. But the skin around the cut had developed a faint translucence, a quality I recognized from the interior of the pipes. The growth, or something like it, had colonized the injury site.

I should have been alarmed. I should have sought higher ground, more light, some way to sterilize the wound. Instead, I watched. Over the following days, the translucent patch spread slowly across my palm, thickening into a smooth, cool layer that no longer registered temperature or texture. The wound beneath healed completely, sealed beneath the new growth.

I can still flex my hand. The growth hasn't affected my range of motion, though the sensation is strange—my palm now feels the world through a filter, dampened and distant. When I press my hand against the pipes, against the wet ceramic, against the metal grating, I feel pressure but not texture.

I think the growth recognizes me. Not in any cognitive sense, but in the chemical sense of an organism that has incorporated foreign material and found it compatible. I am becoming, in my small way, part of the Works' internal ecosystem.

---

**The Silent Zones**

I have not entered the Silent Zones. I have approached their edges twice, and both times I turned back before crossing the boundary.

The transition is subtle but unmistakable. The machine noise doesn't fade—it stops. Abruptly, like a door closing. And on the other side of that threshold, the darkness gathers differently. It doesn't block the light exactly; it simply makes the light feel irrelevant, as if seeing would not change what was there.

I stood at the edge for forty seconds on my first approach. During that time, I felt the weight I've come to associate with being watched—but heavier here, denser, as if the attention had accumulated over decades of silence. I felt no hostility. I felt no welcome. I felt only a vast, patient registering of my presence, a witnessing that required nothing from me and offered nothing in return.

I turned back because there was nowhere to go. The Silent Zones have no machine-rhythm to navigate by, no water channels to follow, no infrastructure to orient within. They are simply present, and to enter them fully would be to become present too—to be added to whatever the Watcher is, without distinction or remainder.

I am not ready for that. I may never be.

---

**What I Have Become**

I am not the person who descended thirty-seven days ago. That person expected to leave. That person carried emergency rations for seven days and planned to reach the surface exit by day two.

I have stopped rationing. The Works provides—in its strange way, it provides. The water is potable if I let the mineral content settle. The fungi are edible, barely, though they taste of metal and something else I don't have a word for. My body has adjusted to the caloric deficit, the cold, the darkness. My eyes see differently now, picking out shapes in the dim light that would have been invisible to my surface-trained vision.

I think about the upper world sometimes. I remember its noise and its urgency, its categories of success and failure. They seem very far away now, pressed thin by the weight of stone above.

Here, there is only continuation. The machines run. The water rises. The swimmers navigate the reservoirs. The growth spreads through the pipes. And I navigate my small territory, tending my pump-housing shelter, watching the translucent patch spread slowly up my palm, becoming part of what the Works is becoming.

I don't know if I will ever leave. I don't know if leaving is still possible, or if it ever was. The Weeping Works was built to process something that no longer comes, and perhaps I was always going to be processed too—broken down into components, separated from what I thought I was, reconstituted as something that can live in the dark.

---

**The Rhythm**

The machines are cycling into synchrony now. I can feel it in the floor, in the air pressure, in the way the sound layers are thinning toward silence. In a few minutes, there will be a moment of near-stillness. The pumps will pause. The conveyors will coast. The Works will hold its breath.

I will stand at the edge of my territory, at the threshold where the functional section meets the Under-level reservoirs. I will listen for the splash. And if I hear it—if something moves in the dark water, testing the boundary between its world and mine—I will not approach, and it will not approach, and we will continue in our separate ways until the machines resume their rhythm and the Works exhales.

This is my life now. This is what I have become.

A witness to the endless process of processing nothing. A resident of the infrastructure. A small, strange thing in a place built for something else, learning to live in the gap between purpose and persistence.

The Works does not need me. But it has me now, and I am becoming the kind of thing that can be kept.
