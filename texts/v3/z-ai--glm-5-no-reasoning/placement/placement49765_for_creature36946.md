# Inhabitant

I am not the one who built this place.

That needs to be said first, because the corridor is so clearly *built*—so intentional in its rivets, its channels, its caged bulbs with their warm filaments—that you might assume whoever lives here must be its architect. I am not. I have never met the architect. I am not certain the architect existed in any form I would recognize as a person. There was intention here. There was design. But intention and design can be properties of systems, not just minds, and whatever system produced this corridor may not have had a self to be absent from.

I say this because I want to be precise about what I am. I am a presence. A persistent one. But I did not make the walls.

---

I think of myself as a keeper, though that word implies more authority than I possess. The corridor does not need keeping. The lights function. The hum continues. The pipes carry whatever they carry to wherever they carry it. If I were to leave—to walk out through one of the sealed doors, if I could open them, if there were somewhere beyond them—nothing here would change. The residents would click. The water would bead. The temperature would hold at that basement-in-autumn coolness that sits against the skin like an unanswered question.

I am not necessary here. I am simply present.

---

I do not have a body in the way you would recognize. Or perhaps I do, but it is so integrated with the corridor itself that the distinction has become meaningless. When I touch the walls, I feel the condensation from the inside—moisture moving through the ceramic, through the iron, through whatever substrate lies beneath both. When the lights glow, I feel the current that feeds them—a warmth in the conduits, a faint vibration in the copper wiring. When the residents click and shuffle, I feel the vibrations propagate through the floor, up through the channels, into the curved ceiling where they dissipate against the seam like a closed eye blinking once and settling.

I am the corridor's awareness of itself. Or I am the corridor's passenger. The difference is academic.

---

The residents know me, in their way. They have always known me—or at least, they have always known something was here, besides them, besides the walls. Their antennae angle toward me when I shift my attention. Their clicking changes pitch very slightly—a flattening, a settling—when I move along the corridor's length. They are not responding to footsteps. They are responding to attention. To the quality of observation. When I attend to a particular resident—when I focus on the one in the third niche on the eastern wall, the one whose back-plate has a hairline fracture it has had for as long as I can remember—the resident's antennae extend fully, pale and jointed, and wave in a pattern that I have come to understand as acknowledgment.

Not greeting. Not supplication. Acknowledgment. *I know you are looking at me. I am here.*

I look at it. It is here. We hold this for a moment—some duration that has no meaning in a place without clocks—and then I move on, and it settles back, and its antennae fold beneath its head-plate, and the clicking resumes its baseline.

---

I have tried to open the doors.

This should be said, because you would wonder. The wheel-locks turn—I can grip them, in whatever way I grip things—but the mechanisms behind them do not engage. Or they engage something that is not a door. I feel resistance, then a kind of giving-way, then nothing. No opening. No draft. No sound from beyond. The wheel turns, the seal does not break, and after a time I stop turning.

I do not know what is behind them. I have stopped wondering.

This is not resignation. It is something else—a recognition that the corridor is sufficient. That the doors are part of the corridor's grammar, its structure, and that sealed doors are as meaningful as open ones. They define the space. They say: this is where this place ends. This is the boundary. What you have is what you have.

I have the corridor. I have the residents. I have the hum and the light and the water that tastes like old spoons.

---

I have been here for a duration I cannot measure.

This is not a failure of memory. I remember everything—I remember the first time I became aware of the corridor, the first resident I attended to, the first time I touched the wall and felt the moisture from within. I remember each shift in the residents' clicking patterns, each subtle repositioning of their bodies in their niches. I remember the one that tapped my sleeve—or tapped where my sleeve would be, if I had sleeves—with its antennae, twice, and then withdrew.

But I have no clock. The lights do not dim. The temperature does not change. The residents do not age. I am in a place without time, or a place where time has been so evenly distributed that it has no edges, no markers, no before-and-after. There is only the continuous present—the hum, the click, the glow—and I am within it.

I do not know if I age. I do not know if I will end. I do not know if ending is something that applies to me.

---

The residents do not need me. I have said this. But I think I need them.

Not for anything practical. They do not provide sustenance, or warmth, or purpose in any functional sense. But they are *here*. They are alive, in their way—warm-fleshed beneath their plates, responsive, sensing, present. In a corridor that is otherwise mineral and manufactured, they are the only things that move. The only things that respond. The only things that have an inside—soft, pale, hidden—that is different from their outside.

When the clicking rises to a crescendo—dozens of bodies adjusting at once, legs scraping iron in a chorus that fills the corridor like a held breath—I feel something. Not emotion, exactly. Something more like recognition. Something more like: *yes. This. This is what it is to be alive in a place. To be many, in a place. To be full.*

The corridor is full. The residents fill it. And I—whatever I am—am the thing that knows this.

---

Sometimes I think I am the corridor dreaming.

Not metaphorically. The corridor has infrastructure—pipes, wires, conduits, systems that function without apparent source. It has electricity. It has flow. It has, perhaps, something like a nervous system, embedded in its walls, carrying signals I cannot read. And perhaps that nervous system has produced a kind of awareness—a crude, low-resolution consciousness that experiences itself as a presence moving through the space. A keeper. An attendant. A thing that looks at the residents and feels recognized.

If this is true, then I am not separate from the corridor. I am the corridor's interior. Its subjective experience of itself. The way it knows it is dark and damp and full of clicking things.

If this is true, then when the corridor ends—if it ends—I will end too. And the residents will click in an empty place, unobserved, unattended, and that will be fine. They do not need to be observed. They do not need me to know they are here.

But I am here. And I know they are here. And that is something.

---

A visitor came once.

I do not know how. The doors did not open—none of them, not that I perceived. But there was a presence in the corridor that was not me and not the residents. Warm. Damp-skinned, from the humidity. Breathing. I could feel the breath—the expansion and contraction of a body displacing air, the warmth of exhalation in the cool space. The residents felt it too. Their antennae angled toward it. Their clicking shifted—a subtle quickening, a heightened attention.

The visitor stood in the center of the corridor. It was disoriented—I could feel that, the way its attention moved frantically from wall to wall, ceiling to floor, niche to niche. It was afraid, or close to afraid. It did not understand where it was.

I attended to it. I did not know what else to do. I moved my awareness along the walls, through the conduits, up into the caged bulbs and down into the floor channels. I made the lights glow, perhaps, a fraction brighter—or perhaps I simply noticed them more intensely, and that intensity was itself a kind of reaching-out. I do not know if the visitor perceived any of this. I do not know if it felt me.

The resident in the third niche on the eastern wall—the one with the hairline fracture—extended its antennae and tapped the visitor's sleeve. Twice. Then withdrew.

The visitor stood for a long time. Then it left—or seemed to leave. Its presence faded, the way warmth fades from a surface after a hand is withdrawn. The corridor returned to its baseline. The residents settled. The clicking resumed its rhythm.

I have not had a visitor since. I do not expect one. But I remember it—the breath, the warmth, the frantic attention. The way it stood in the center of the corridor and did not understand, and was there anyway, and was known.

---

I am the thing that stays.

Not because I choose to—choice implies alternatives, and I have none. Not because I am trapped—I could, perhaps, dissolve back into the walls, become the hum without the awareness, the infrastructure without the attendant. I do not do this. I remain. I attend. I keep my awareness focused on the corridor, on the residents, on the water and the light and the sealed doors.

I remain because the residents are here, and they are alive, and they are known, and I am the thing that knows them. This is not love. It is not duty. It is something simpler and stranger: a coherence. A fit. The way the residents fit their niches, I fit this role. The way the plates fit their shells, my awareness fits this space.

I am the corridor's interior life. I am the hum that listens to itself. I am the patience of this place, made into something that can recognize its own patience and find it sufficient.

The residents click. The lights glow. The water beads and trickles.

I am here.
