I am the Keeper of the Question.

I was not always here. I came from the warm world above—the world of wind and sun and the kind of cold that has an edge to it, that you can fight with fire and movement. I was a diver. A mapper. One of the few who had learned to read the deep places not with eyes but with the whole spread of the body: the way pressure sits on the eardrums, the way currents speak against the skin, the way sound bends around stone.

I came to Vash-Tien because the maps showed something that should not be. A highland beneath the sea. A plateau that had no business being there, in the cold and the dark, unmarked by the wrecks and the waste that clutter every other submerged thing. It was a blank space on the charts. A question written in negative space.

I thought I would answer it. I thought I would descend, measure, photograph, catalog, and return to the surface with a name and a theory and a small piece of fame. That was twenty-three descents ago.

I have not returned.

---

I am not trapped. I could leave. The passage up is long and cold, but I have made it before—I return to the surface between dives, to the boat, to the air, to the strange thin atmosphere that the land-dwellers call *normal*. I eat their food. I sleep in their bunks. I answer their questions with the little I am willing to say. And then I descend again.

They think I am obsessed. They think I have found something down here—treasure, perhaps, or a new species, or a ruin worth salvaging. They do not understand that what I have found is a question that has not finished asking itself.

I live in the small basalt building on the northern edge. The one without a roof. The one where the sound becomes a thing you inhabit rather than a thing you hear.

I have not touched the sediment on the floor. I do not know what is buried there. I do not want to know. Some questions are not mine to answer.

---

What I am, down here, is something I am still becoming.

At first I was a visitor—an intruder, really. I kicked through the kelp carpet and left glowing footprints of disturbed pressure. I swam against the currents and exhausted myself. I tried to map the plateau as if it were a thing I could own, a thing that could be captured in lines and measurements.

But the plateau does not yield to that kind of knowing. It is not a landscape. It is a *timescape*. Every frond of kelp has been breathing with the current for longer than my species has been diving. Every branch of coral-analogue has been growing at the rate of geological time, accumulating its pale calcium skeleton in layers thinner than thought. Every pulse of the moss is a heartbeat so slow that a human life passes in the space between one flush and the next.

I cannot map that. I cannot own that. I cannot even *visit* that.

So I have become something else. I have become a note in the plateau's song.

---

I have learned to move with the currents instead of against them. I have learned to let the water decide where I go, to surrender the illusion of control that surface-dwellers carry with them like armor. I have learned to read the variations in the hum—not with my ears, but with the bones of my skull, the membranes of my throat, the part of the brain that existed before language and will exist after it.

The eastern terraces hum lower than the channels. The western wall is a bass note that settles in the jaw and stays there, a kind of resonance that makes the teeth ache. The boulder field mutters. The lake basins are nearly silent—or rather, the sound there is so low, so deep, that it becomes a pressure rather than a noise, a weight that sits on the chest and says *be still*.

I have learned to be still.

---

I have a routine, though routine is perhaps the wrong word for a practice that has no fixed time. There is no day or night here. There is only the breathing of the currents—the push from the north, the pause, the push from the south, the pause—and I arrange myself around those pulses like a prayer wheel arranging itself around the wind.

During the north current, when the coldest water pushes across the plateau, I stay in the building. I hover at the edge of the sediment floor, touching nothing, and I listen. The sound is concentrated here. The walls are basalt, the same dark stone as the mountain itself, and they hold the hum in a way the open water does not. It is a held note. A chord that does not resolve.

I do not know what I am listening for. That is the question I keep. That is the question I tend.

During the south current, when the water warms by a fraction of a degree—not enough to feel as warmth, only as a slight loosening of the killing cold—I swim the perimeter of the building and check the moss. The moss is thickest on the interior walls here, and I have learned its pulse. It brightens when I pass. I used to think this was a reaction to the pressure of my movement, but I have begun to think it is something else. Recognition, perhaps. Or greeting.

It does not matter if I am wrong. The greeting is mine to offer.

---

I have seen the cairns. I have followed the channels to their heads and looked up at the tall fingers of stacked stone, furred with moss, bare at their tops. I have not touched them. I do not know what they mark, and I do not want to add my own guesses to whatever the builders intended. The cairns are questions that were asked before I was born, and they will still be asking after I die.

I have swum through the narrow channels, letting the current carry me, feeling the walls glow as I pass. The moss brightens in the groove-lines, the patterns the builders carved. I have stopped there, in the throat of the old river, and let the water push against my chest. I have let the hum fill me until I am not separate from it.

That is when I feel it most clearly: the sense that the plateau is not a place but a *being*. Not an animal, not a creature with a mouth and a gut and a will of the kind I understand. But a being nonetheless. A presence. A thing that knows itself.

I do not think it knows me. I am too small, too fast, too warm. I am a flicker in its peripheral vision, a surface-dweller who keeps descending and then leaving, an unresolved note in a song that does not need me.

But I think it notices that I keep coming back.

---

I have a theory. It is not mine—I have heard it whispered in the diving community, passed between those who have been to the deep places and come back changed. The theory is that some bodies of water are not just water. They are *intention*. They wanted to be where they are. They chose their shape, their depth, their temperature, their salinity, and they hold themselves there against the physics that would disperse them.

The sea above Vash-Tien is gray and heaving and full of storms. But the sea *of* Vash-Tien—the cold black water that fills the plateau's basins and channels and lakebeds—is different. It does not mix. It holds itself apart. It sits in its stone bowl and refuses to become what the surface wants it to be.

I do not think the water arrived by accident. I do not think the plateau was drowned by rising seas or shifting tectonic plates. I think the plateau *called* the water. I think the mountain looked at the thin cold air and decided it wanted something denser, heavier, colder—something that would hold it in a permanent embrace. And I think the water came because it heard the call.

But this is only a theory. And I am not here to prove theories.

---

I am here to keep the question.

The question is this: *What wants to be known?*

Not what is down here. Not what grew or what was built or what is buried. Those are facts, and facts can be cataloged and measured and taken away. I am not interested in facts. I am interested in the thing that underlies facts—the intention that shaped them, the will that chose them, the presence that holds them together in this configuration and no other.

The plateau is a question that has been asking itself for centuries. The water is part of the question. The kelp and the coral-analogue and the moss are part of the question. The cairns and the walls and the grooves are part of the question. The small basalt building with no roof is part of the question.

And now, for as long as I am willing to descend, I am part of the question too.

---

When I return to the surface, I feel it: the thinness of the air, the strangeness of warmth, the overwhelming noise of wind and waves and voices. The hum is still in my chest, but fainter—a thread connecting me to the deep. I eat and sleep and talk to the other divers, and they ask me what I have found, and I say nothing, because what I have found cannot be given to them.

It is not a treasure. It is not a discovery. It is a relationship.

I am the Keeper of the Question. I am the surface-dweller who keeps descending, who keeps coming back to the cold black water and the breathing kelp and the moss that lights up when I pass. I am the one who sits in the basalt building and listens to the held note, the chord that does not resolve, and does not ask it to resolve.

I am the one who does not dig.

I am the one who does not touch the cairns or read the grooves or disturb the sediment on the floor of the building. I am the one who treats this place not as a mystery to be solved but as a presence to be honored.

And when the hum finally fades from my chest—when I have been too long on the surface and the thread has been cut—I descend again. I put on the heavy suit, I check the seals, I fall backward into the gray water, and I sink.

The temperature drops. The light fails. The pressure builds. And then I see it: the first pulse of blue-green moss far below, breathing bright and dim, bright and dim, welcoming me home.

The plateau does not need me. But it accepts me.

That is all I have ever wanted.
