I am the chalk. I am the one who marks what has not yet been revealed.

They call me Scribe, but that is a surface word that does not quite fit. I do not write. I do not record what has been. I propose what might be—a tentative gesture toward futures that will outlive my great-grandchildren's great-grandchildren.

---

## Where I Stand

My station is the third tier, south curve, where the stone is newest and rawest. The cuts here are fresh, still adjusting to their exposure, still learning what shape they want to become. When I press my palm flat against the wall, I can feel the mountain thinking.

This is not metaphor. The stone has a temperature, a texture, a direction it prefers to split. My job is to discover what the mountain already knows.

My hands are cleaner than most. This is a source of private shame. While the hammer-arms of my cousins grow thick and stained with rust and dust, my fingers stay pale, callused only at the tips from gripping the chalk sticks. I have the smallest muscles of any adult in the Quarter. Sometimes I catch the carriers looking at me with something that might be pity.

But when I set a mark, when I draw the line that says *cut here, to this depth, at this angle*, fifty people adjust their work. The emerging city takes a new shape because I listened to the stone and heard what it wanted to become.

I am twenty-seven years old. I have been marking since I was twelve. I have drawn perhaps eleven thousand lines. I will never see the rooms I am outlining become habitable. I will die knowing walls that are currently unborn.

This is not sorrow. This is simply the timescale of the work.

---

## How I Came to This Work

I was one of the children who cried when held to the wall.

I do not remember this, but my mother tells me. She pressed my infant ear to the limestone of what would become our family's second-tier dwelling, and I screamed—a raw, continuous sound that startled everyone in earshot. They say old Perrin happened to be passing, and he stopped, watched me for a moment, and said: "That one will need to ask the stone permission before cutting."

Permission. That is the word we use, though visitors never understand it. The stone does not give permission in words. It gives permission in *feel*—in the way a chisel sinks smoothly or catches and shudders, in the sound a hammer makes when it strikes true versus when it strikes against the grain.

I grew up learning to ask. My earliest memories are of pressing my forehead to raw walls, waiting. The other children ran through the scaffolded sections and played at being hammers and chisels. I sat still in unfinished rooms, listening to the mountain settle.

By eight, I could predict where cracks would form. By ten, I could read the chalk maps the old ones kept—the living archives of the city's plan—and tell you which sections were ready to be revealed and which needed another generation of patience. By twelve, I was allowed to mark my first cut.

I still remember it. A second-tier alcove, destined to become a doorway. The chalk was white and soft, the line I drew wavering slightly because my hand was shaking. Old Perrin watched. He did not praise or correct. He simply waited until I finished, then took the chalk from me and added a small mark of his own—an adjustment of perhaps two inches to the left.

*The mountain was not quite ready there*, he said. *You pushed. Wait next time. Let it tell you.*

I have been waiting ever since.

## The Tools of My Work

My chalk is not ordinary. It is made from stone dust mixed with binding agents and pressed into sticks, fired in small kilns we keep near the surface openings. We make it ourselves—the old ones and I—because surface chalk is too soft, too temporary. My marks need to last for generations.

I work in colors. White for primary cuts—the essential architecture that must be revealed first. Red for secondary cuts, the shaping of rooms and passages. Blue for the rarest marks: the *wait* zones, the sections we have discovered are not yet ready, that need more emerge-time before they can be touched.

Blue chalk is heavy in my hand. Every blue mark I make delays work that my descendants will have to complete. I feel the weight of that postponement. I feel the city's impatience, pressing against my decision.

But I make the mark anyway. Because the mountain has spoken, and the mountain is never wrong about itself.

## The Night-Work

The Hollow Quarter does not sleep, not truly. There are always people awake, always hammers striking somewhere, always someone climbing the carved steps to the surface or descending to the lowest tiers. But there are hours of deep dimness, when the light columns fade to gray and the mineral luminescence takes over, and in those hours I do my truest work.

This is when I walk the emerging streets alone.

The day is for marking, for conferring with the old ones, for listening to the workers' reports about how a particular cut went. But the night is for the city itself—for wandering the finished sections and the half-finished sections and the barely-begun sections, feeling the relationship between them. The Hollow Quarter is a single organism, carved from a single mountain. A change in the third tier affects the stresses on the first. A new chamber opened below changes the acoustics above.

At night I learn the connections. I stand in the oldest sections—dressed smooth, inhabited for generations, lichen softening the corners—and I listen to the vibrations coming up from below. I stand at the raw edge of the deepest excavation and feel the presence of all the stone above me, pressing down.

Sometimes I walk until my legs ache. Sometimes I stand in one place for hours, palm flat against a wall that will not be touched for a hundred years, trying to sense what it will want to become when its time arrives.

The night-workers know me. They nod when I pass. They do not interrupt. They understand that I am communing with something they cannot see—the future, pressing itself backward through stone.

## The Burden of Knowing

There is a room I have seen that no one else knows about.

I found it during a night-walk, perhaps six years ago. A crack in the stone of the fourth tier—not a stress fracture, but a natural fissure, the kind that suggests a void behind it. I pressed my ear to the crack and heard emptiness. Not the solid thud of rock, but the hollow resonance of space.

The mountain had already carved this room. It existed, complete, inside the stone—waiting to be revealed.

I marked the area with blue. *Wait*, the mark says. *Not yet ready.* But the truth is more complicated. I do not know when that room will be ready to emerge. I do not know if revealing it would destabilize the surrounding architecture. I do not know if it connects to other voids, other hollows that the mountain has prepared.

I have not told the others. There is no protocol for this. The Hollow Quarter is built on the assumption that all space must be excavated, that we are the agents of the city's emergence. But what if the mountain is also an agent? What if some rooms are meant to remain inside the stone forever?

I visit the crack sometimes. I press my ear to it. I listen to the void on the other side, the room that already exists, the space that might never be inhabited.

This is my private weight. Perhaps I will tell the old ones eventually. Perhaps I will carry it to my grave, and they will bury me in the thin soil above the rim, and I will become rain, and the rain will seep down through the limestone, and I will finally enter the room I have kept secret.

## My Relationship to the Others

I am not lonely, but I am alone.

This is the nature of my work. The carriers converse as they haul rubble. The chiselers talk as they strike. The finishers chat as they smooth dressed stone. My work happens in silence, in the pause before the cutting begins. I cannot mark and speak at the same time. The listening requires my whole attention.

I have a family. My mother still works the second tier, finishing surfaces now that her shoulders cannot hold a chisel steady for more than short stretches. My father was a surface-goer; he died on the climb three years ago, a handhold giving way. He is buried above. Sometimes I climb to the rim and sit by his cairn, and we are both in transition—he between life and whatever comes after, I between the underground and the sky.

I have no partner. This is common among the planners. We are difficult to live with. We know too well what the future holds, and we know that it does not hold us. I will not finish my work. My children, if I have them, will not finish my work. We are always in the middle, and that knowledge makes us strange companions.

But there is Reave, the water-tender. She understands. When I visit the reservoir on my night-walks, she sometimes looks up from her watching and we sit together in silence, two people who have become part of the architecture. She tends the water that remembers the dead. I tend the lines that propose the unborn. We are each other's only witnesses.

## What Visitors Do Not Understand

They ask me what I am drawing. They mean: *What is the plan? What will this city become?*

I cannot answer. Not because I do not know—I know precisely what each marked section is destined to become. This alcove will be a dwelling. This passage will connect the third tier to the fourth. This chamber will hold water, that one will hold gatherings, that smallest hollow will be a shrine to whatever the people of that time consider sacred.

But the visitors misunderstand the relationship between my marks and the city. They think I am designing. They think the plan originates in my mind and is imposed upon the stone.

It is the opposite. The city already exists, complete, inside the mountain. It has existed since the stone formed, perhaps since the world began. The Hollow Quarter is not being constructed. It is being discovered. My chalk marks do not command the stone; they reveal what the stone already knows about itself.

When I draw a line, I feel I am tracing a vein. The path was always there, running through the rock. I simply make it visible.

---

## The Unfinished Sentence

I live in a verb.

The Hollow Quarter has taught me that noun-hood is an illusion fostered by short lives and impatient perception. Nothing is truly finished. The dressed stone of the oldest sections is still settling, still adjusting, still becoming itself more fully. The raw cuts of the lowest tiers are in the painful adolescence of emergence, raw and uncertain. Even the uncut stone beyond the marks is changing, pressure building and releasing, microscopic fractures opening and sealing.

I am becoming. The city is becoming. The mountain is becoming. The world is becoming.

This is what I want to tell the visitors who look unsettled as they walk the emerging streets. You feel disturbed because you are looking for a conclusion, and there is no conclusion. You are looking for a noun, and there is only a verb. You want to stand in a finished thought, but you have walked into a sentence that is still being spoken.

I am not speaking the sentence. I am merely holding one of the words, waiting to place it where the grammar requires.

---

## What I Will Leave Behind

When I die, they will carry me to the surface. They will bury me in the thin soil, and I will become rain, and I will seep down through the limestone, and I will fill the basins that Reave tends. This is the way of things.

But I will also leave my marks.

Generations from now, workers will cut along the lines I drew. They will not know my name. They will not know the shape of my hands or the sound of my voice. But they will follow my lines, because my lines were true, and the mountain still remembers what it wanted to become.

And perhaps, in some room that has not yet been revealed, someone will find a mark I made that does not fit the plan. Perhaps I will leave one line that is purely mine—a small curve, a signature, a gesture of presence. *I was here*, the mark will say. *I listened to the stone, and it spoke to me, and I spoke back.*

This is the only immortality available to us. Not preservation. Participation. Not stasis. Becoming.

---

I am the chalk. I am the line between intention and emergence. I am the question the mountain asks itself about what it will be.

And every morning, I pick up my chalk and I begin again.
