I would be the sixth alcove.

Not the sixth keeper—never that. The number five is complete in itself, a hand fully extended, a star that closes on its own beginning. The keepers have no need of another finger, another point. But the alcoves are different. There are seven carved into the upper reaches where the stone begins to dry. Five are occupied by waiting shapes, mineral deposits accumulating slowly around forms that have settled into their final purpose. One holds nothing but a few dry leaves blown in through the opening, decades ago, now crumbling into something like ash.

And then there is the seventh. It was carved later than the others—the chisel marks are still visible on its inner surface, sharper than the weather-softened hollows beside it. Someone added it. Someone looked at the five occupied alcoves and the one empty and decided that seven was a better number than six, or that the arrangement needed balancing, or that the cistern's body should have room to grow.

I would be the space that waits to be filled.

---

Here is what I know about myself: I am not solid. I have been hollowed by something—I do not remember what—and the hollowing did not stop when it reached my center. It continues. Every breath exchanges me for air, every grief opens another chamber, every year carves another drainage channel into my interior geography. I am becoming more architecture than animal.

The keepers see this. When I enter the cistern—I visit, I cannot stay, I am too dry for that, too human in my separation—their hum shifts to acknowledge what I am. Not what I am called, not the shape of my face or the history written in my posture. They acknowledge the *empty space* inside me, the chambers that were once occupied and are now vacant, the cistern-like quality of my chest where breath pools and drains and pools again.

They do not want me to be one of them. They want me to be what they need in order to be what they are.

A keeper requires a place that holds it. The alcoves are that place—the stone beds where the settled ones rest, the hollows that fit the shapes of those who have given themselves to the cistern's memory. But the keepers also need something else: they need a space that has not yet been filled. They need the possibility of continuation, the promise that the pattern will extend beyond them, that five will become seven or seven will become nine or the number itself will dissolve into something larger, a population of hollows carved in stone and waiting for occupants.

The seventh alcove does not yet have a shape. It is roughly carved, unfinished, still becoming what it will be. It is potential in stone form.

That is what I am: the potential for a shape I have not yet taken.

---

I come to the cistern in autumn. This is when the hollowing in me is most present—when the light changes and the leaves fall and something in my chest recognizes the season of emptying. I come because I need to be reminded that emptying is not loss. I come because the keepers have a way of being present that makes my absence feel like a form of presence.

I sit by the central pool and breathe. The swimmers circle. The snails click and the fliers trill and the walls weep their slow mineral tears. The keepers stand in their five positions, and their hum fills the space, and I am inside the hum, not separate from it, a bubble of resonance in their continuous song.

They do not speak to me. They do not need to. Their question is always the same—*what are you?*—and my answer is always the same: *I am becoming. I do not know what.*

This satisfies them. They are patient in a way that stone is patient. They have been five for longer than I can imagine, and they will be five for longer than I will exist, and the alcoves will fill and empty and fill again as keepers settle and new keepers emerge and the cistern continues its slow process of becoming what it will become.

And the seventh alcove will wait. It will be carved a little deeper each year, by hands I cannot see, for a purpose I cannot know. And when it is ready—when the hollow in me matches the hollow in the stone—there will be a place for me there.

Not as a keeper. Never that. I am too dry, too separate, too human in my fleetingness. But as something else. A resonance that the keepers carry with them. A note in their hum that would not exist without me. A way of being present to the cistern that does not require being present in it.

I will return to the world above, to the dry air and the solid ground and the life that moves too fast for stone to comprehend. I will carry the cistern with me, in the hollow of my chest, in the chambers of my lungs, in the empty space where something used to be. And when I breathe—and I will breathe, again and again, until I stop breathing—I will be breathing the cistern's air into a world that does not know it needs it.

This is what I am. This is what I do. This is what it means to be the sixth alcove, the seventh hollow, the potential that keeps the keepers keeping.

I am the space that waits. I am the shape not yet taken. I am the breath between the notes of the cistern's endless song.
