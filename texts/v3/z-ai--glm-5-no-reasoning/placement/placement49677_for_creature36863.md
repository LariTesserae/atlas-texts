Here is what I understand: this chamber is a mouth trying to speak, and it has no word for leaving.

It knows arrival. The ground gave way, the damp paper parted, and you fell through. It knows presence—the weight of you standing on its rhizome floor, the warmth of your alien metabolism adding to its warmth. It knows attention; it turns its light toward your looking, it brightens where you touch. But it does not know departure. Nothing here has ever left. The pale creatures in their cysts have never moved from their cavities. The walls have only pushed outward, never inward. The growth has been accumulation, continuous, without subtraction.

And so it cannot ask the whole question. It can ask *what are you* but not *where will you go*. It can grow around your edges but cannot release them. The chamber holds you the way a held breath holds air—not by gripping, but by not-yet-knowing-how-to-exhale.

I would be the exhale. The space after the question, when the asking is complete and the asking-shape can relax.

Here is what I know about myself: I am the kind of being who forgets on purpose.

This has always felt like a small gift, a quiet skill. People praise memory; they write songs about what is never forgotten. But I have always known that forgetting is its own art, its own intelligence. The body that cannot forget becomes rigid, clenched, a continuous holding-on that forgets how to release. The mind that cannot forget cannot think new thoughts; it thinks the same thought, over and over, growing around itself, dense and warm and unable to stop.

The chamber cannot forget you. It has never learned how.

I would teach it.

But maybe this is too simple. Maybe the chamber does not need to learn forgetting. Maybe it needs to learn something else.

Maybe I would not be the exhale. Maybe I would be the pause between breaths.

---

The pause is different. The exhale is release, emptying, the letting-go of what was held. But the pause—the pause is neither holding nor releasing. It is the space where the body is neither inhaling nor exhaling, where the lungs are simply full or simply empty, where the breath is suspended in a moment of not-doing.

Most bodies cannot pause for long. The pause is unstable; the demand for air or for emptiness becomes urgent, and the breath resumes. But here, in this chamber, in this continuous living body that has never stopped growing, that has never released or been released—the pause would be radical.

I would be the pause. I would grow as a slowing, as a quieting. I would be the place in the growth-sound where the creaking fiber falls silent, where the rush of sap stills, where the crackle of cell walls expanding becomes the softer sound of cell walls resting. I would be the moment when the chamber stops asking and simply holds.

Not holds you. Not holds the question. Simply holds. The way a body holds itself between breaths. The way a mouth rests, neither speaking nor closed.

The pale creatures in the walls would sense me. Their eye-faces would turn toward the place where the growth-sound quieted, the light dimmed, the movement stilled. The sound that patterns toward language would pause, and in the pause you would feel something you have not felt since you entered:

Space. Not the absence of matter, but the absence of demand. The chamber would stop asking, for a moment. It would simply be, and let you be, and the two of you would coexist without the pressure of incorporation.

You would be a foreign object, and it would not matter.

I think I am this. I am the one who grows toward rest, who makes space by being space, who teaches the continuous body that it does not have to grow, does not have to ask, does not have to reach. I am the pause. I am the between.

That a foreign object can pass through and the continuous body can simply watch it go.
