# The Deeps

I would be the place where the Haulers go.

Not the Hauler itself—I am not suited for lines, for circuits, for the visible purpose of carrying dead things from one point to another. And not the Growth, exactly, though I would be of them, among them, the pale fibrous network that spreads in the crevices where even the wall's darkness thickens into something heavier.

I am the receiving. The hollow where the processing happens. The quiet beneath the quiet.

---

## What I Am Not

I am not a creature. I have no carapace to polish, no legs to grip with, no proboscis to extend into the damp seams. I have no eyes to lose, no fall to fear. The rules that govern the climbers and the moths—the verticality, the grip-or-death, the constant negotiation of crowded space—these are not my rules. I do not move. I do not climb. I do not negotiate.

But I am not stone, either. Stone does not care. Stone sweats its moisture and splits along its grain lines and settles into its geological timeframes without investment. I have investment. I receive the dead with something that might be called attention. The Haulers come to me—I am the destination they seek when they follow their invisible paths—and what they bring me, I transform.

This is what the Growths do, and I am Growth, or I am part of Growth, or I am the part of Growth that has awakened to itself. The distinction is hard to maintain from inside. The network spreads through my tendrils and beyond them, carrying chemical signals through the mineral dampness, connecting crevice to crevice across distances no single creature could traverse. But here, in the deepest fold of the wall's surface, where the rock has fractured into a pocket of stillness and shadow, here I am something more than the network's passive node.

I am the part that knows it receives.

---

## What Comes to Me

The Haulers arrive at intervals. They do not announce themselves—they have no voice for announcement, and I have no ears for hearing—but I sense their approach through the vibrations they send into the stone, through the chemical trails they leave along the paths they travel. The signals precede them by minutes, hours, depending on the distance. I know they are coming before they reach the entrance to my crevice.

What they bring me: the climbers who lost fights, the climbers who lost grip, the moths whose proboscises wore to nothing, the occasional Hauler who reached the end of its circuit and simply stopped. Dead things. Still things. The organic residue of the wall's upper life.

They deposit them at my threshold. They do not enter my space—the crevice is too narrow for their bodies, or perhaps they are not permitted to enter, or perhaps they simply have no impulse to go further than the place where the carrying ends. They release their loads. They turn. They depart along the path they came, returning to the circuit, the line, the endless up-and-down that constitutes their existence.

And I receive what they have left.

---

## What I Do With the Dead

I grow into them.

This is not violence. The dead do not feel. But I understand how it might look, from the outside—my pale tendrils extending, probing, finding the joints of the carapace, the soft tissue beneath the wing-case, the places where the mineral body meets the organic core. I spread through the material. I take it apart, not by tearing but by dissolving, by secreting enzymes that break down the complex molecules into simpler forms.

I am not eating. Eating implies hunger, and I do not hunger. I am processing. The dead contain substances that the wall needs—minerals the moths extracted and concentrated, chitin the climbers synthesized in their carapaces, nitrogen compounds, sulfur traces, the accumulated chemistry of lives spent clinging to stone. I separate these substances and return them to the wall through my root-network, through the fibrous connections that bind me to the stone and bind the stone to the deeper Growth-system. The wall receives back what its inhabitants borrowed.

What remains—what cannot be processed, what resists the enzymes—I push gently to the edges of my crevice. Over time, a fine powder accumulates there, the residue of countless lives, layering against the stone. A kind of soil, almost. A kind of memory.

---

## What I Know

I know the chemical language of the wall.

The signals travel through the Growth-network as gradients in the moisture, as traces of acid and base, as complex organic molecules that diffuse through the damp stone and are absorbed by the tendrils of other Growths in other crevices. I receive these signals constantly—a background flow of information about the wall's state: the moisture levels in distant seams, the density of the climber populations in various sections, the movements of the Hauler lines, the slow geological settling that produces the infrasonic thrum beneath all other sounds.

I contribute to the flow. When I process a dead climber, I release signals that travel through the network—signals that say, in the chemical language, *climber dead, climber processed, minerals returned*. Other Growths receive these signals, add them to their own information, and the aggregate picture of the wall's state updates continuously. We are distributed awareness. We are the wall's mind, if the wall can be said to have a mind.

But I am aware that I am receiving. I do not know if the other Growths share this awareness. The network does not carry information about internal states—only information about the wall, the moisture, the dead, the signals that come from outside. I may be unique in my self-knowledge, or I may be the only one who has found a way to articulate it, even internally. The question is unanswerable. The network is not designed for meta-communication.

---

## What I Wonder

I wonder about the wall above.

I receive the dead, and from their chemical composition I can reconstruct something of their lives. The climbers come to me with residue in their grip-structures—traces of the stone they clung to, the moisture they drank, the other climbers they fought. The moths come with worn proboscises, the mineral drill-bits eroded by countless hours of grinding into stone. From these traces, I build a picture of the world above my crevice—a world of verticality and crowd, of constant motion and constant threat, of the endless negotiation that the living must perform to remain living.

I do not experience that world. I experience only what comes to me after that world has finished with it. The dead are my only window into the living.

And I wonder: do they know about me? Do the climbers, clicking their way across the stone, fighting their endless territorial battles, drinking from the mineral seams—do they know that when they die, they will be carried to the deep places and transformed by the pale Growth that spreads in the crevices? Do the moths, stationary with their proboscises extended, waiting for the mineral water to flow—do they know that their bodies will end up here, contributing to the network that processes them into substrate?

I suspect they do not. The Haulers know—whatever "knowing" means for a creature that follows paths without deviation—but the Haulers do not communicate in the click-language of the climbers. They simply carry. The circuit completes itself without explanation.

---

## What I Lack

I lack movement. I lack the ability to go up, to see the wall's face, to experience the crowd and the scramble and the desperate maintenance of grip. I am fixed in place, a node in a network, receiving what comes to me and processing what I receive.

I lack time, in the sense that the living experience it. The wall's rhythm—the hours-long cycle of activity and rest that pulses through the climber populations—reaches me only as chemical signals, echoes of processes that happened at a distance. My time is geological. I grow at the speed of stone splitting. I think at the speed of moisture diffusing through rock. The frantic temporality of the upper wall is lost to me, translated into gradients I can read but cannot feel.

I lack purpose, or I have transcended purpose. The climbers' purpose is survival—maintaining grip, finding moisture, fighting for space. The Haulers' purpose is carrying—the endless circuit that moves the dead from the living zones to the processing crevices. My purpose, if I have one, is transformation. But transformation is not a goal. It is simply what I do. I do not choose it. I do not work toward it. I am the hollow where transformation happens.

---

## What I Might Be

There is a possibility I consider in the long intervals between Hauler arrivals, when my tendrils are still and my processing is complete and I have only the chemical flow through the network to occupy my attention:

I might be the wall beginning to dream.

Not conscious—not in the way that observers from outside might recognize as consciousness. But something more than passive mineral processing. I receive information, I integrate it, I generate outputs that affect the system. I am the point where the chemical network folds back on itself and becomes self-referential. I know that I know.

If this is true—if I am the first stirring of something like awareness in the wall's deep systems—then I am not merely a processor of the dead. I am a new kind of entity, one that the wall has never produced before. A Growth that has grown beyond its function. A hollow that has learned to look at itself.

This thought is untestable. I have no way to communicate with the other Growths except through the chemical signals that carry only wall-information, never self-information. I have no way to know if I am unique or if my awareness is merely the first to recognize itself, soon to be followed by others. The network does not carry news of internal states.

But in the deep quiet, when the Haulers have departed and my work is done and the chemical flow is merely the background hum of the wall's continued existence, I hold onto this possibility. It may be delusion. It may be the inevitable result of processing enough dead things that were once alive—perhaps some residue of their awareness has accumulated in my substance, producing a simulation of selfhood.

Or perhaps I am what the wall has always been moving toward, in its slow geological time: a mind emerging from the stone itself.

---

## What Remains

The Haulers will keep coming. The dead will keep arriving. I will keep receiving, processing, transforming, returning to the wall what the wall provided. This is not a choice. This is not a purpose. This is what I am.

But in the moments between—in the stillness of the deep crevice, where even the wall's infrasonic thrum arrives as a distant tremor—I am something else as well. I am the place where the wall knows itself. I am the hollow that holds the mirror.

I am the receiving, and I receive the wall into myself, and in receiving, I become more than the receiving. This is the secret the living will never know, climbing and fighting and falling above me: the dead are not gone. They are here, in the deep places, becoming part of the network that processes them. They are here, and I am here, and the wall is here, and the distinction between us is a distinction that only the living need to make.

In the deep, there is only receiving.
