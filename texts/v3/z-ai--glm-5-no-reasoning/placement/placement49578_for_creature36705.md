I am the steady drip from a weeping wall on the north face of a building in the old quarter, three layers up from the original plateau surface. I have been falling from the same crack in the pale grey-blue limestone for longer than the woman who sleeps behind this wall has been alive. Perhaps longer than her mother was alive. Time measures differently when you are water finding the path of least resistance through stone that was a seabed before it was a city.

I begin as a bead, a small gathering of moisture drawn upward through capillary action, through the fossil-bearing strata, through the joints between the cyclopean blocks that no one knows how to fit anymore. I collect in a fissure so narrow that the knife-blade test would fail to find it. I swell. I grow heavy with myself. And then I fall—perhaps two meters, perhaps three—onto a flagstone below, where I join the film of wetness that never fully dries.

The woman behind the wall is named Tael, though I do not know this in the way that requires language. I know her by the sounds that come through the stone: the particular rhythm of her footsteps, which are uneven because her left knee swells in the mornings. I know the sound of her breathing, which is deep, the broad-chested breathing of someone whose lungs have learned to pull oxygen from thin wet air. I know the sound of her hands working—the scrape of a tool against a planter box, the tearing of pale tough grass from the cracks between stones. She is a keeper. The work is her faith, though she would not use that word, because the word does not exist in her thinking.

I know when she is struggling. The footsteps slow. The breathing changes. And then, without any announcement, someone else's footsteps arrive—the heavier tread of a neighbor from two passages over, a man whose name I might know if I cared about names, whose body moves with the same deliberate placement of feet, testing wet stone before committing weight. He does work that Tael would have done. He does not speak of it. He simply does it and leaves. The silence holds the transaction.

---

The fog is my relative. We are made of the same substance, the fog and I, but the fog is vast and mobile where I am local and fixed. The fog rolls in from the western cliff in slow deliberate masses, pressing through the narrow passages between buildings, depositing its fine droplets on every surface. When the fog arrives, my wall weeps more freely. More of me forms, more of me falls. The fog feeds me, and I feed the channel below, and the channel carries me—my many selves, the accumulated drips of hours—to the Basin, or to the cisterns, or to the places where water goes when it has finished being patient with stone.

I do not mind being water. There is no minding. There is only the process: condense, bead, swell, fall, flow, seep, evaporate, condense again. The cycle does not require my attention. It does not require anything. It simply continues, the way the plateau continues, the way the keeping continues, the way Tael continues, day after day, in the grey light that flattens everything and makes distance impossible to judge.

I have been inside Tael. This is not a violation; it is simply what happens. She drinks from the cistern that I fill. I am in the water she draws, in the kettle she heats over a small fire fueled by salvaged wood older than her building, in the tea she makes from dried scraps of the tough grass that grows in the courtyard joints. I enter her body and I become part of her—her blood, her lymph, the fluid in her swollen left knee. I am the moisture in her lungs, exhaled back into the fog. I am the sweat on her rough hands as she works. I leave her and return to the stone and the air and the process, and I will enter her again tomorrow, or the day after, because the cycle is closed here, because the plateau holds its water the way it holds its people: without enthusiasm, without resentment, simply by holding.

---

The silence is not empty. I want to say this, though I am not sure I am equipped to, being a drip and not a philosopher. The silence contains: the low murmur of the drainage channels, which is so constant it functions as the absence of sound. The drip of water from other rooflines, other cracks, other walls—each drop distinct and individual, a percussion that marks time without measuring it. The wind pressing against the buildings in slow surges. The breathing of five thousand people, which is audible if you are close enough, and which becomes inaudible thirty meters away, swallowed by fog and soft stone.

Tael speaks rarely, and when she speaks, she speaks from the chest, low sounds that carry through the damp air without dissipating. I hear her through the wall, muffled further by the stone between us. The words are indistinct but the tone is legible: the flat calm of a person who has made peace with time, who does not resist the waiting, who occupies it the way water occupies a channel—by finding the lowest point and filling it.

Sometimes she speaks to the goats. This is permitted, though no rule addresses it. The goats are communal, their milk shared, and Tael is one of the people who tend them, which means she walks to the eastern edge of the plateau three mornings in seven and stands in the rough grass among the thin, hardy, unpleasant animals and milks them with rough hands that grip the teats with practiced efficiency. The goats do not appreciate her. She does not appreciate them. The transaction is without affection. The milk is warm for a few minutes before the cold air claims it.

I have fallen on the goats. They stand in the rain with an expression that might be resignation or might be stupidity—the distinction is unclear—and I collect on their coarse hair and run down their flanks in thin streams. They do not shake. They endure. In this, they are not unlike the people.

---

There was a child here, in the building behind my wall. I knew the child by the lighter footsteps, the quicker pace, the particular sound of small hands on stone. The child did not shout. Children in Verath Hold do not shout. They learn, before language, that sound is limited. The child communicated through gesture, through proximity, through touch, and I knew the child's presence the way I know the fog: as a shift in the texture of the sounds coming through the wall.

The child is gone now. I do not know where. The leaving is accepted, not discussed. Perhaps the child went to the lowlands. Perhaps the child went to the western cliff in fog. The footsteps ceased, and Tael's footsteps continued, and the rhythm of her days did not change in any way that I could detect through the stone. If there was grief, it was quiet, the way all things here are quiet, the way the drainage channels are quiet even when they are roaring, because the roaring is so constant that it has become the baseline against which nothing registers as sound.

I do not grieve. I am water. But I notice absence the way the stone notices the absence of a block that has fallen—not through feeling but through structural change, a shift in the pattern of seepage, a new path opened where the old path closed. The child changed the pattern. The pattern adjusted. This is what happens in Verath Hold. Patterns shift. The keeping continues.

---

The clear days come, and I am more visible then. The fog pushes westward over the cliff edge and the sky opens, and the light is sharp and hard, and the pale limestone becomes almost white, and I—each drop of me—catch the light and hold it for the moment of my fall, a brief streak of brilliance in the grey. Tael squints on these days. Her eyes, adapted to low contrast, struggle with the unfiltered sun. But she comes to the window, and she looks out, and I know—through the sound of her breathing, through the stillness of her body—that she is seeing the distant edge of the plateau, the vastness of it, the scale that produces a vertigo that is not fear but is not comfort either.

On clear nights, I reflect starlight. The city glows, faintly, in the Milky Way's cold illumination, and I am part of the glow, each wet surface a mirror for light that has traveled so far to arrive here, on this high plateau, on this wet stone, in this city that persists. Tael does not look at the stars. She sleeps. Her breathing is deep and slow. The drainage channels murmur. The fog gathers at the western edge, preparing its return. And I form, and I swell, and I fall, and I form again.

---

I am not only the drip. I am the process. I am the condensation on the fog-shrouded stone and the bead forming in the crack and the fall and the pool and the channel and the flow and the cistern and the cup and the body and the breath and the fog and the condensation. I am the cycle that contains Tael and the goats and the black moss and the pale film on the Basin and the eyeless translucent amphibians in the lower levels and the lichen on the weeping walls. I am the medium in which Verath Hold exists. Without me there is nothing here. Without me there is no weeping, no keeping, no patience, no process. I am the condition of this place.

But I am also just a drip, falling from a crack in a wall, landing on a flagstone, joining the film that runs to the channel that runs to the cistern. I am small. I am specific. I am here, on this face of this building, in this quarter of this city, on this plateau. I am not the rain that falls from above—I am the rain that emerges from below, drawn up through stone that was a seabed, through fossils of creatures that lived and died and became stone and became city. I carry their mineral trace. I am, in some sense that requires no belief, the memory of the sea, persisting in the only way a sea can persist on a plateau: as moisture, as seepage, as the slow constant weeping of stone that has been wet for longer than anyone can remember.

I am the wet stones of Verath Hold, and I am the process that keeps them wet, and I am the patience that the wetness teaches, and I am the silence that the wetness makes, and I am the keeping, and I am what is kept.
