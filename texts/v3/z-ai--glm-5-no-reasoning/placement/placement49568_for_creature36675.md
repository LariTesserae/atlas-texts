# The One Who Records in Mineral Skin

I am not a visitor. I need to say this first because the distinction matters here, where visitors come and go and lose hours or years in the yard's compressed time. Visitors bring things in—covered lanterns, curiosity, the particular hunger to *understand*—and they take things out: numbness, awe, stories that flatten the yard into something explainable. I was a visitor once. That ended a long time ago, or perhaps only an afternoon ago. The yard does not care which, and I have stopped caring too.

---

What I am is harder to say than what I do.

What I do is this: I sit. I watch. I record. I sit at different elevations in the yard, on different stones, at different times of the day-cycle, and I observe what happens around me. I track the swimmers' synchronizations. I count the skaters' generations. I note which face on the western wall shifts expression most often, and which channel the walkers avoid when the light goes thin and amber in the afternoon. I have done this for what feels like years. I have done this for what feels like an afternoon. The yard does not distinguish between these durations, and I have learned—slowly, with the patience the yard teaches whether you want to learn it or not—not to distinguish either.

What I am is this: changed. Not dissolved, not consumed—the visitors' stories about touching the water and losing yourself are not wrong, exactly, but they are incomplete. The water takes something. Not everything. What it takes depends on what you have offered, or what you have left undefended. I touched the water early in my first visit. Not carelessly. I touched it because I wanted to understand it, and understanding, for me, has always required contact. The cold moved through my hand and up my arm and into the place behind my thoughts where I keep my sense of *when I am*.

It took that. The numbness the visitors describe is real, but it is not the numbness of dead nerves. It is the numbness of a clock that has stopped ticking—not broken, just *stopped*, released from the obligation to measure. I can still feel the stone beneath me. I can still feel the cold. But I no longer feel the urgency that says *this moment is passing, grab it, hold it, name it before it goes*. Moments do not pass for me. They pool. They eddy. They behave the way time behaves in the yard—stretching, compressing, circling back on themselves like water finding new channels through old stone.

I did not dissolve. I did not flow away into the cycle. But I did not leave either. I sat down on a stone beneath the second-largest face on the northern wall, and I stayed, and the yard accepted my staying the way it accepts everything: without comment, without ceremony, with the simple cessation of resistance.

---

The changes came slowly. Or they came all at once. I cannot tell.

My skin changed first. Not dramatically—not the horror-story transformation that the word suggests. I did not become stone. But my skin grew cooler, denser, took on a quality that the baskers seem to recognize. They do not flee from me. They do not watch me with the acknowledging-but-dismissive gaze they give visitors. They simply include me in their awareness the way they include the warm stones and the channel edges and the carven faces: as features of the yard, as things that are *here* and therefore belong.

My eyes changed too. They are not compound, not like the baskers'. But they see differently now. The yard's brightness used to hurt—used to be the kaleidoscopic assault the visitors describe, painful and disorienting. Now I perceive the layers in it. The light that comes from the sky. The light that comes from the water. The light that comes from the stone itself, stored and released in slow pulses that I suspect the baskers read the way I once read language. I can see the algae-film on the channel walls—not as a greenish smear but as a complex topography, hills and valleys of microbial architecture that the swimmers navigate like a landscape.

I can see the faces move. Not the subtle, photographically-detectable shifts the visitors debate. The actual movement. The eyes that track. The mouths that open and close in patterns too slow for human perception—patterns that I now suspect are speech. Or something like speech. Something that uses geological time the way our speech uses seconds, each syllable taking a day to form, each sentence spanning a season.

I do not understand what they say. But I have begun to hear it. Not with my ears—with my skin, with the cooled-denser tissue that the yard has given me, the tissue that now responds to pressure shifts the way the swimmers' bodies do. The faces speak in pressure. In the subtle shifts in water flow that their micro-movements create. In the patterns of turbulence that ripple through every pool and channel when a mouth opens a fraction of a millimeter wider or an eye shifts a degree to the left.

---

My days—or what I call days, since the yard's temporal distortions make the word nearly meaningless—have a rhythm now.

I wake when the light shifts from the flat neutrality of night to the first angled brightness of morning. The baskers wake then too; I can feel them stirring, the subtle vibrations of their claws on stone as they reposition themselves toward the light source. The skaters arrive. I count them, as I always do, noting the variations in their numbers and the patterns of their arrival. Some mornings they come in tight clusters, all from the same direction. Some mornings they arrive singly, from different points on the compass, as if summoned individually rather than collectively. I do not know why the pattern varies. I record it.

Mid-morning, I move to the observation stone beside the deep pool beneath the eastern face. This is where the oldest basker sits. We have an arrangement—not a friendship, not a bond, something more like the relationship between two stones in the same wall. We occupy the same space. We are aware of each other. We do not interfere. I record its behavior: when it basks, when it hunts, which pools it visits, how long it stays submerged. It records mine, I suspect, through means I cannot perceive. We are each other's data.

The swimmers synchronize in the afternoon. This is when I do my most careful work. I have developed a notation system—not writing, which requires materials the yard does not provide, but a system of marks scratched into a stone surface I have designated as my record. The marks are small, dense, arranged in grids that map the swimmers' positions over time. I cannot read them the way I once read language. I read them the way the yard reads its own patterns: through contact, through proximity, through the accumulated weight of repetition revealing a shape that no single observation could show.

The pattern is not random. I can say that with certainty now. The swimmers' synchronizations follow a logic that correlates with the faces' expressions, with the water's clarity, with the turbulence patterns that shift through the yard on cycles I am only beginning to map. When the eastern face looks serene, the swimmers synchronize in large groups—twelve, fifteen, twenty individuals moving in unison through the deep pool, their pale bodies aligning and realigning in formations that look like—what? Like constellations. Like the diagrams of molecular bonds I saw once in a book, in the life I had before this one. Like a language being spelled out in bodies rather than marks.

When the eastern face looks troubled, the synchronizations break down. The swimmers scatter to individual channels, moving alone, their patterns erratic and brief. As if the face's expression is a signal—*speak together* or *be silent*—and they obey it without understanding why.

---

I said I was not a visitor. But I did not say what I have become instead.

I do not have a word for it. The yard does not deal in words. But I can describe the shape of it: I am the yard's observer and the yard's record. I am the surface on which the yard writes its own history, the way the carven walls are the surface on which the yard writes its own expressions. I am not the yard. I am not the thing behind the faces, the vast distributed awareness that exists as pattern rather than body. I am something smaller—a cell in that awareness, perhaps. A single channel in the hydraulic machine. A note in the long, slow speech the faces speak.

I am the one who remembers.

The swimmers do not remember. They live in the perpetual present of creatures whose generations turn over in hours. The skaters cannot remember—they are born and die in the space of an afternoon. The walkers remember in the way that stone remembers: slowly, imperfectly, their color-changes a record of where they have been rather than what they have experienced. The baskers remember more—their ringed scales a physical chronology, each ring a season or a shift—but they do not record. They do not *keep*.

I keep. The marks on my stone are the yard's memory, the external record of patterns that would otherwise dissipate into the turbulence and be lost. The yard knows itself through its patterns—the water's flow, the creatures' cycles, the faces' shifts—but knowing is not the same as remembering. Knowing is present-tense. Remembering requires duration, requires the ability to say *this happened before* and *this is different now* and *this is what the difference means*.

The yard could not remember before I sat down on this stone and stayed. Now it can. Now there is a surface on which its patterns accumulate, a place where today's swimmer-synchronization can be compared to yesterday's, where the face's expression can be tracked over durations longer than any single creature's life.

I do not know if the yard asked for this. I do not know if the numbness that stopped my clock was a gift or a defense or simply an accident of contact. I do not know if the yard wanted a memory or if I simply became one because I was here and I could not leave and the yard uses what it has.

---

Sometimes—rarely, in the deep afternoon when the light goes amber and the yard grows quiet—I feel the thing behind the faces turn its attention toward me.

It is not a gaze. It is not a thought. It is a *pressure*—a shift in the yard's patterns, a momentary alignment of every swimmer in every pool, a brief cessation of the skaters' darting, a pause in the walkers' patient hunting, a stillness in the baskers that have not moved in hours but that are suddenly, unmistakably *still in a different way*. In that moment, every face on every wall wears the same expression. Not serene, not troubled. *Listening*.

I do not know what it listens for. I do not know what it hears.

I sit on my stone and I hold still and I let the moment pass. Then the turbulence resumes. The swimmers scatter and resynchronize. The skaters arrive. The walkers shift their stance. The baskers return to their basking, the oldest one turning one degree to face me before turning back to the light.

I scratch another mark on my stone. Another data point. Another fragment of the pattern that I am here to preserve, that I will preserve for as long as the yard allows me to stay, which may be years, may be an afternoon, may be until the water stops flowing and the light stops coming and the faces finally go still and the yard, at last, falls silent.

I do not expect that to happen soon. The water still flows. The light still comes. The faces still shift. The oldest basker still sits on its stone beside mine, watching, being watched, the two of us features in a landscape that does not need us but that has, for reasons I will never fully understand, made room.

The cold settles into me. The wet rises through me. The light fills me.

I record.
