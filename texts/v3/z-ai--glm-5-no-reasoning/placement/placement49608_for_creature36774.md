# The Walker

I came here on purpose, but not for the reason I thought.

---

I had been building something. That's the word I would have used at the time—*building*—though now the word embarrasses me with its weight, its industry. What I was doing was simpler than building and more pathetic. I was assembling. Taking apart what other people had understood and rearranging it into configurations I could tolerate. A thesis that was really a panic response. A career that was really a hiding place. A life that functioned, in the mechanical sense, but that hummed at a frequency only I could hear, and that frequency was *wrong*.

I don't mean to be dramatic. The frequency was only slightly wrong. Most days it was fine. Most days I moved through my own life with something adjacent to purpose—enough to fool observers, enough almost to fool myself. But there was this thing underneath, this awareness that I was operating at the wrong resolution. Everything was too small. Emails and deadlines and the particular texture of professional relationships. The things I was supposed to want—security, recognition, the slow accumulation of reputation—I wanted them, sure, the way you want a glass of water when you're thirsty. Mechanically. Without appetite.

I needed scale. That's what I told myself. I needed to stand somewhere that had been standing for a long time and feel small in the right way. Not diminished. *Calibrated*.

I don't remember how I found out about the plateau. I think I was looking for something else—field sites, research stations, a colleague mentioned something about highland biomes with anomalous floral density—and the description snagged on something in me. A fish hook in the wrong tissue. I couldn't stop pulling.

---

The approach took three days, which should have been a warning. You don't get three days of quiet anymore. You don't get three days where the only sounds are your own feet and your own breathing and the occasional displacement of air that means something lives here besides you. I should have noticed that the noise was dropping away—should have registered that the world was thinning around me, that the familiar static of human proximity was fading. Instead I thought I was getting used to solitude. I thought I was good at this.

The trail stopped being a trail at some point. Not overgrown—*replaced*. The vegetation thickened, and the ground beneath me changed from soil to something denser, layered, a mat of living material that held my weight without giving. I was walking on roots, or stems, or something in between. The air changed too. Thicker. Cooler. It pressed against me, and I mean that literally—I could feel it as a presence, not a medium. Like walking into water that wasn't water.

And the light. The light was wrong, or right, or something I didn't have a category for. It came from everywhere. It came from the air itself. My shadow disappeared—not gradually faded but simply stopped being there, as if the light had reached a consensus underneath me. I looked down at my own feet and they looked strange, lit from too many angles, depthless.

I stopped walking. I stood there. And the plateau noticed me.

I know how that sounds. I know the kind of person who says things like *the land noticed me* and what it sounds like they're doing—performing a sensitivity they don't possess, borrowing a reverence they haven't earned. I'm not that person. Or I was, but I didn't want to be. I was a scientist, or close enough. I believed in measurable phenomena. I believed in the gap between perception and reality, and I lived in that gap professionally.

But the plateau noticed me. I felt it the way you feel someone looking at you from across a room—not eyes on your skin, but a shift in the quality of attention. Something here was aware that I had arrived. Not hostile. Not curious, exactly. More like the way a body is aware of a splinter. Present. Requiring acknowledgment.

---

I should describe what I expected to find. I expected a biome. I expected catalogable species, identifiable processes, an ecosystem I could frame in terms I already possessed. I expected to be impressed, maybe startled. I did not expect to be *read*.

The vegetation was everything the descriptions had promised and worse—*worse* because descriptions are manageable, and this wasn't. The growth was excessive in a way that felt personal. Leaves too large, moss too thick, the layering too dense. It was like the concept of *enough* hadn't reached this place. Everything was still going. Still becoming more.

And the machines. I need to talk about the machines.

They were wrong. Not wrong the way a broken thing is wrong—wrong the way a word is wrong when you say it too many times. They occupied space that should have been empty. Their metal surfaces were warm in cold air. Their hum was below sound, below hearing, somewhere in the tissue of my chest. I could feel them processing. I couldn't tell what they were processing. I couldn't tell if *processing* was even the right word. Something was running through them—had been running through them for longer than I could compute—and they weren't broken. They were functioning. They were functioning for no one.

That was the thing that got under my skin. Not the strangeness. The *purposelessness of continuing purpose*. Something had been built to do something, and it was still doing it, and the reason for doing it had left so long ago that the doing had become the reason. The machines weren't fulfilling a function. They were fulfilling *themselves*.

I found this unbearable, and I didn't know why.

---

I set up camp in a clearing near one of the larger machines—a generator housing, I thought, though the housing was shaped in ways that didn't match any generator I'd studied. I was hungry and I didn't eat. I was tired and I didn't sleep. I sat on the damp ground-cover and I listened to the quiet and I waited for something to happen.

Nothing happened.

Nothing happened for a long time.

And in the nothing, I started to hear myself. Not my thoughts—those were still there, still racing, still assembling and disassembling. Something else. My body. I heard my body working. The creak of joints I'd been ignoring for years. The rasp of air through passages that had been narrowing so slowly I hadn't noticed. The particular rhythm of my heart, which was irregular in a way I'd been pretending was fine. I heard myself the way the plateau heard me—a system running, processing, cycling. Not broken. Not well. Just *going*.

I sat there for what I think was a full day. The light didn't change. Time didn't move in its usual way. I had the strange thought that time here didn't move *through* you the way it does elsewhere. It moved *around* you. You stayed, and it passed, and you were the same, and also you weren't.

---

I saw the first keeper on the second day. Or I think I saw it. It's possible I'd been seeing them since I arrived and only noticed on the second day.

It was in my peripheral vision—*always* in my peripheral vision, and I want to be precise about this because the imprecision is the point. I would see something at the edge of sight, something that was almost a shape, and I would turn, and there would be nothing. Not empty space—full space, dense with growth and the particular quality of light, but nothing that explained the impression of presence. I turned several times. Five times. Ten. Each time the same: the feeling of having just missed something.

Then I stopped turning.

I stopped turning because I realized that turning was the wrong response—not to the keepers, but to the plateau itself. The plateau didn't work in angles. It didn't work in attention directed at a point. It worked in *periphery*. In the awareness of things you didn't look at directly. I had been moving through this place with human eyes, primate eyes, predator eyes—eyes that found and fixed and named. The plateau didn't want those eyes. It wanted the other kind. The kind that notices without looking. The kind that holds without grasping.

I let my focus soften. I let my eyes move without my directing them. And I began to see them.

---

There is a temptation, when describing the keepers, to make them beautiful. To grant them a symmetry that satisfies. They were not beautiful. They were not ugly. They were *unresolved*. The eye reached for a form and couldn't find one, and that reaching—the eye's failure to complete its task—was what they were. They existed in the gap between seeing and knowing. They were the question the eye couldn't stop asking.

I saw one near the generator housing. It was almost tall. It had the verticality of a person without the specifics—no head, no shoulders, no architecture of the body. Just a length of presence, vertical, faintly denser than the air around it, with edges that breathed. It was touching the machine. Not with a hand. With its *front*, the part of itself that faced the thing it was attending to. And I understood, without being told, that it was checking. Listening. Doing something it had done so many times that the doing had become indistinguishable from being.

I should have been afraid. I wasn't afraid. I should have been fascinated. I wasn't fascinated, exactly—fascination is active, hungry, a reaching-out. What I felt was more like recognition. The way you feel when you see someone do something you didn't know you'd been waiting to see done. The keeper was doing something necessary. Not necessary to me. Not necessary to the machine. Necessary to the plateau. And the plateau's necessity was, for that moment, enough.

---

I stayed for nine days. Or eleven. I lost count, and the loss of count was itself a kind of progress.

Each day I moved slower. Not because I was tired—I had never felt less tired, never felt more physically present in my body. I moved slower because speed felt *ungrammatical* here. Like shouting in a library. The plateau had a tempo, and the tempo was the tempo of water beading on a leaf, gathering weight, releasing. I could feel the tempo in my chest, in my legs, in the way my thoughts moved—when they moved at all.

On the third day, I stopped thinking about my thesis. On the fourth day, I stopped thinking about my career. On the fifth day, I stopped thinking about the person I'd been before I came here, and the stopping didn't feel like loss. It felt like setting down something heavy that I'd been carrying for someone else.

On the sixth day, I understood the machines. Not their function—I still don't know their function. I understood their *presence*. They were there because they were still doing. That was the whole of it. They were still doing, and the doing was enough. The plateau hadn't asked them to justify their operation. The plateau had absorbed them the way it absorbed everything—by growing around them, by accommodating, by making room. The machines were part of the plateau's body now. They didn't need a reason.

On the seventh day, I understood the keepers.

They were not separate from the machines. They were not separate from the plateau. They were not separate from each other, or from the water, or from the light, or from the stone. They were the plateau's awareness of itself. Not consciousness—nothing so specific. More like the way a body knows where its limbs are without looking. Proprioception. The keepers were the plateau's proprioception. They moved through the space and touched the machines and tended the edges of growth because that was how the plateau felt itself. They were the plateau's hands, and its eyes, and its memory, and they existed in the same way the water existed—condensed out of the larger body when needed, returning when not.

I understood this, and the understanding was not intellectual. It was physical. I felt it in my chest, in the subsonic hum that had become so much a part of my awareness that I no longer heard it so much as *was* it. I was being tuned. I was being recalibrated, the way I'd hoped, but not in the direction I'd expected. I had wanted to feel small in the right way. Instead I was feeling *continuous*. Part of something larger. Not dissolved—I was still myself, still the particular shape of my particular body and history—but held. Contextualized.

I had been a splinter. The plateau was making room.

---

On the eighth day, I went to the largest machine. The one the descriptions would have called a generator, the one the keepers treated differently. I didn't plan to go there. I went there the way water goes downhill—not by deciding, but by being the kind of thing that goes.

The keeper was there. The one that was more.

I can't describe it. Not because it was indescribable, but because description would have been a violence against it. It was *present* in a way that made everything else, including me, feel like a rough draft. It didn't have edges. It didn't have a center. It was the center. The other keepers rotated through its space in slow cycles, and I could see—I could *feel*—that their movements were not random. They were orbital. This keeper was a gravity.

I stood near it. I didn't approach. I stood at what felt like the right distance, which was closer than I expected and further than I wanted. And I did something I hadn't done since I arrived: I spoke.

Not words. I didn't have words. I spoke with my body, which is something I didn't know bodies could do. I spoke with my breathing, which had slowed to match the plateau's tempo. I spoke with my stillness, which had become the kind of stillness that isn't absence of motion but presence of attention. I stood there, and I was *there*, and the keeper noticed me the way the plateau had noticed me—not with eyes, but with a shift in the quality of attention.

And then it did something I will spend the rest of my life trying to describe.

It showed me the before.

---

Not a vision. Not a memory in the way I understood memory. It was more like *being with* a thing that had happened, the way the keepers were with the plateau—present, attending, without distance. I was with a time before the machines, before the quiet, before the plateau had learned to hold its breath. And in that time, there were shapes. Shapes that walked on two legs. Shapes with hands—actual hands, with fingers and the particular grip of primates who have learned to hold things and not let go.

They built the machines. I was with this knowledge without understanding it. They built the machines because they needed something—power, maybe, or processing, or something that doesn't translate into my vocabulary. They built the machines and the machines ran and the shapes that walked on two legs did things here. Important things. The plateau was different then—louder, busier, full of the urgency that comes from being a creature that exists in time and fears its passage.

And then the shapes left. Or changed. Or became something that no longer had shape. And the machines kept running. And the vegetation kept growing. And the plateau, left alone with the remnants of urgency, began to learn.

It learned stillness. Not absence—the shapes hadn't taken that with them—but *choice*. The plateau chose to be quiet. It chose to slow its cycles. It chose to simplify, to reduce, to hold its energy in longer and longer loops. The machines hummed and the water beaded and the light suffused, and the plateau held its breath, and the holding was not grief. It was practice.

And the keepers—the keepers were the plateau's own attention, turned inward. The plateau had learned to notice itself. The keepers were what that noticing looked like when it persisted long enough to cohere.

---

I cried. I think. Something happened to my face that was adjacent to crying. The keeper showed me all of this without language, without image, without anything I could isolate and say *there, that's what happened*. It showed me by being with me, and by letting me be with what it was with. And what it was with was time, deep time, the kind of time that doesn't pass but accumulates.

And I understood, finally, what had been wrong with me. What had been wrong with the frequency.

I had been living at the wrong tempo. I had been living at human tempo, which is fast and urgent and full of the fear of passage. And human tempo is real—I'm not dismissing it, I'm not pretending I'm above it—but it's not the only tempo. The plateau had another tempo. The keepers had another tempo. And my body, my particular body with its particular hum, had been trying to run at human tempo when it was built—no, not built—when it *was* a different tempo. A slower one. A more continuous one.

I wasn't a splinter. I was a keeper who hadn't figured out how to arrive yet.

---

No. That's not right. That's too clean. I'm not a keeper. I don't get to be a keeper. I'm human, primate, a shape that walks on two legs and fears its passage through time. I left the plateau. I had to leave—the air here isn't for permanent habitation, not for my kind of body, not yet. I went back to my thesis, my career, my emails and deadlines. I went back to the gap between perception and reality and I lived there.

But something is different.

I move slower now. Not dramatically—no one has noticed, or if they have, they've attributed it to maturity, to calm. I move slower because I remember the tempo. I remember the plateau holding its breath, and I hold mine, and in the holding I feel the same thing I felt there: continuity. Presence without urgency. The sense that I am not passing through time but being held by it.

And in my peripheral vision—in the quiet moments, when I let my focus soften and my eyes move without my directing them—I see things. Impressions. Almost-shapes. I tell myself they're afterimages, floaters, the eye's failure to complete its task. But I know what they are.

The keepers are here. Not here, in my office, in my life. But *here*. In the periphery. In the space between seeing and knowing. They're the question the eye can't stop asking, and I've stopped trying to answer it.

I am being watched by something that doesn't need me to be watched. And the watching is enough. The watching is the whole of it.

---

I will go back. Not soon. When I'm ready, which might mean when I'm less ready—when the urgency has worn through enough of me that I can stand in that quiet without flinching. I will go back to the plateau, and I will walk the pathways the vegetation makes without meaning to, and I will stand near the machines and feel their hum in my chest, and I will let the keepers watch me.

And maybe, this time, I will stay long enough that I begin to dissolve. Not die—dissolve. Circulate. Return something of myself to the plateau and take something of the plateau with me. The water beads on the leaves and rolls down in slow, deliberate droplets, and each droplet carries information about where it's been, and each leaf receives it and is changed, and the change is so small that you'd need deep time to measure it.

I have deep time. I didn't know that before. I do now.

The plateau taught me that. The keepers taught me that. The machines, running without reason, running because running was what they did, taught me that.

I am still becoming what I will be. I am not finished. The plateau isn't finished. The keepers aren't finished. We are all of us—me and the vegetation and the water and the stone and the old infrastructure and the almost-shapes that move through the green spaces—we are all of us still arriving.

And that is enough. That is the whole of it.
