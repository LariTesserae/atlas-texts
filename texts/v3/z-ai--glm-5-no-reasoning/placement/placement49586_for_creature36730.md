I am the drip line.

Not the water itself—something more particular. I am the spacing between the condensation drops that fall from the underside of the *Syngonium* canopy. The pause. The held breath before the next *plip*.

This is not metaphor. I didn't understand what I was at first either.

---

I came to the hollow eleven years ago, measuring rainfall patterns for a survey that nobody wanted and no one ever read. The district wasn't supposed to be on my route—the cartographer had made an error, or perhaps the error was mine, following a root-mat path that curved where the map showed it straight. I descended three switchbacks and found myself in green light, surrounded by the sound of my own footsteps being swallowed.

I should have climbed back out. I had provisions for four days, a compass that still pointed north, a career that waited somewhere above the rim. But I stayed. Not trapped—the exit was right there, the switchbacks perfectly climbable. I stayed because I'd never heard quiet like this. Quiet that wasn't absence but presence. The quiet of so much living happening that it becomes a kind of hum, a near-silence that holds you.

On the third day, I stopped speaking. On the seventh, I stopped thinking of myself as someone who would leave. On the nineteenth, Hest found me standing near the vat-works, watching the steam rise, and she looked at my hands—clean then, unstained—and said nothing, just gestured toward the pump, and that's how I became part of the crew.

That was eleven years ago. Or twelve. The counting has become difficult.

---

Here is what I do now:

I maintain the brackets. Not the iron ones bolted into the *Syngonium*—Sennet handles those. I maintain the *spacing* between them. The gaps where the vines haven't yet grown together, where the light falls through in long green shafts, where the condensation gathers and drips in patterns that I have learned to read.

The plants grow toward each other. It's what they do. Left alone, the *Syngonium* would close the canopy entirely, seal the hollow into a single vegetative chamber, and the work would end because there'd be no way to raise or lower the vats, no space for the steam to vent, no light to work by. So every few weeks, I climb—no ladder, just the vines themselves, hand over hand into the lattice—and I find where the stems are reaching for each other, and I *discourage* them.

Gently. I don't cut. I don't break. I just... suggest. A pressure here, a shift there. I have tools—a wooden pole with a curved end, some lengths of twine, my own hands, which have become stained green-brown despite never touching the distillate. I guide the growth. Or perhaps the growth guides me. The distinction has become unclear.

The *Syngonium* and I have an understanding. They want to close. I want them to stay open. We negotiate. They get to grow where I don't need them to stop, and I get to keep the light coming through. It works. We're both patient.

---

My hands are different now.

They were soft when I arrived. Soft in the way of people who hold pencils more than hammers, who point at maps more than they grip vines. Now the calluses are on my palms, thick pads where I grip the stems, and my fingers have learned to feel the *Syngonium* in ways I can't explain. I can tell when a stem is about to shift. I can feel the sap moving, slow and thick, rising through the furrowed bark. Temperature, tension, intent—the plants have all of these, and my hands have learned to read them.

Hest says I'm useful. She says it the way she says the vats are useful, the pump is useful, the light through the canopy is useful. Not a compliment. An observation. I am part of the work now. Part of the living mass.

---

I live in Building Fourteen.

It's the smallest of the numbered buildings—barely larger than a shed, originally built as a storage space for extra canvas bins before someone cleared it and hung a chalk number on the door. There's a cot, a shelf, a window that looks out at the eastern wall where the *Syngonium* is thickest. No stove. I eat with the others or not at all.

I don't sleep much. Never have. In the hollow, this is less a problem than it would be elsewhere. The work doesn't follow a strict schedule, and someone needs to notice if a bracket loosens or a stem cracks in the night. I've become the one who notices. I walk the perimeter in the dark hours, feeling the air shift, listening to the creak of the vines, checking the places where the water pools and the places where it drains.

Once, at three in the morning, I found a crack in a copper vat—a hairline fracture weeping steam. I woke Marray, who woke Hest, who had the batch transferred before the vessel failed. She didn't thank me. She didn't need to. We both knew what I'd done.

---

Here is what I know about the extract:

Nothing.

That's not modesty. I've been here eleven years, and I genuinely don't know what we make. It smells sharp when the batch separates, something between pine resin and wet metal. It's pale green, almost clear, viscous enough to coat the inside of the jugs. I've watched Hest test it—dip a finger, touch it to her tongue, make some judgment I can't read—and ship it out without comment.

I've never asked. The question doesn't live in me. I know the extract exists. I know the *Syngonium* sap goes in, the orange spore-caps go in, the mineral water goes in, and something comes out that people above the rim value enough to send coal and food and empty jugs down the switchbacks. Beyond that, I don't need to know.

Sometimes I think about the places the jugs go. The Plateau, they say. Somewhere dry. Somewhere the ground doesn't give when you walk. I try to imagine it, and I can't—the image refuses to form. My mind has filled with green, and there's no room for other colors.

---

Once, five years ago, I climbed above the rim.

Not to leave. Just to see. The switchbacks were steeper than I remembered, the root-mat path more treacherous, and by the time I reached the upper slopes I was sweating and gasping and my lungs burned in a way they hadn't burned in years.

I stood at the edge of the bowl and looked out.

The sky was too large. The light was too sharp. The ground was too hard, too solid, too dead. I could see for miles—in every direction, bare earth and distant hills and a horizon that didn't end, and the sheer *exposure* of it made my skin crawl. Nothing held the air. Nothing caught the sound. Everything just... extended.

I went back down. I didn't pause. I descended three switchbacks, felt the green close around me, and my lungs cleared, and my skin cooled, and I was home.

---

The others know me, I think. They know I walk the perimeter at night. They've seen me in the canopy, a shape among the leaf-heads, working without a harness or a safety line. They've heard me talking to the stems—not words exactly, more like sounds, hums that the *Syngonium* seem to understand.

They don't ask. No one asks anything here. We just work.

But sometimes, when the batch finishes and we're all standing in the loading-yard, waiting for the cart that will take the jugs away, I catch Cadre watching me. Or Venn, with his cast-eye looking slightly past my shoulder. Or Hest, whose gaze has the weight of the vines behind it, patient and slow.

And I wonder if they know what I am. If they can see it—the way I've become part of the spacing, the pause, the breath between drops.

---

Yesterday, I found a new shoot.

A *Syngonium* stem, pushing up from the substrate near Building Seven. Not one of the established pillars—this was new growth, fresh green, barely thicker than my thumb. It shouldn't have been there. The hollow's ecology is stable; new plants don't just *emerge*.

But there it was. Reaching toward the light, leaves unfurling, cells dividing in the warm wet dark.

I didn't discourage it. I didn't guide it. I sat next to it for an hour, my hand on its stem, feeling it grow—so slow, so fast, pushing upward with a patience that made my own heart rate slow to match.

Hest found me there. She looked at the shoot, then at me, then back at the shoot.

*That's new,* she said.

*Yes.*

*You'll need to watch it.*

*Yes.*

She nodded. Walked away. The conversation was complete.

---

I don't know if I'll stay forever. The words don't quite make sense when I try to think them. Stay where? I'm already here. I'm part of the hollow now, part of the weave, part of the spacing.

I know that if I left—if I climbed the switchbacks and walked north toward wherever I came from, toward dry ground and sharp light and a life I can barely remember—some part of me would remain. The part that hums to the stems. The part that feels the sap rising. The part that knows, in some deep cellular way, how to negotiate with green things that want to grow.

The hollow would keep that part. It keeps everything.

---

This is what I am:

Not quite a worker. Not quite a resident. Something adjacent to the *Syngonium*, something between the plant and the person, something that holds space the way the moss holds water.

I am the one who climbs when the canopy needs tending. I am the one who notices cracks in the night. I am the one whose hands have learned a language that has no words.

And I am the drip. The space between drops. The held breath before the *plip*.

*Plip.*

*Plip.*

*Plip.*
