I am the place between the path and the wilderness.

I am not moss. Moss does not require this kind of consciousness, and what I have is not the moss's deep vegetal patience. It is a sharper thing, more attentive, more anxious, though the anxiety has been weathered by decades into something closer to vigilance. I am the threshold mind. I was born in the Quarter or I came to it young enough that the distinction no longer means anything. I live here in a way that the fern pickers do not, and I live here in a way that the old woman on Third Street does, though she and I have never spoken.

I am the son of the maintenance crew. Not literally—or perhaps literally, I am not certain. What I mean is: I was raised by the paths. I learned the central route before I learned the streets outside the viaduct. I was walking the curve by the streetlamp tree before I knew there had been a straight street there. My mother showed me the mosses when I was small enough that the cushion mosses came up to my waist. She is not my mother. The crew does not have family structures in the usual sense. But she is the one who first knelt beside me at the edge of the path and pressed my hand into the *Leucobryum* and said nothing, and I felt the give, the cold seep, the way the cushion accepted my palm and then slowly resumed.

I do not know my age. The Quarter does not measure time the way the city does. I have been here for many fiddlehead harvests, many dry seasons, many returns of the damp. I have seen the canopy thicken and the suspended forest floor deepen. I have seen a building on Elm Street lose its last wall and become a grove. I was there when they found the tricycle in the streetlamp tree, and I wept, though I could not have said why.

I do not cut the paths. That is the crew's work, and I am not crew, though I sometimes accompany them. I do not pick the fiddleheads. I do not work at the factory. I do not study the hybrids or collect samples or make transects. What I do is harder to name.

I *listen*.

---

The Quarter has five layers of sound, and I have learned to hear a sixth.

The sixth layer is the most quiet. It is the sound of boundaries being maintained—not by the crew, not by human effort, but by the place itself. It is the sound of the viaduct's edge, where the humidity drops and the mosses thin and the growth stops. It is the sound of the moss pushing outward and being pushed back. It is the sound of equilibrium, which is not rest but contest, two forces meeting and neither yielding.

I can hear it because I live at the boundary. Not at the viaduct edge, but at the path's edge, which is its own kind of boundary. The path is the wound in the Quarter's body, the place where the growth is interrupted. The moss wants to close it. The crew wants to keep it open. I walk the line between these two intentions, and I listen to the tension where they meet.

I have a route. It is not the central path, though I walk that sometimes. It is a route that follows the margins—the places where the maintained paths give way to the deeper growth, where the moss is thicker and the footing is less certain and the light takes on that particular quality I have come to think of as the color of privacy. I walk this route every day, in the damp season and the dry. I do not have a destination. I am checking something. I am not sure what.

I think I am checking whether the Quarter is still itself.

---

I live in the machine shop on Hembry Street.

Not inside it—the interior is too sacred for habitation, too perfectly still. The moss on the lathe beds, the green velvet over iron, the machines that have become geological—I would not disturb that for anything. I live above it, in a space that was once a storage loft, accessed by an external iron staircase that has mostly rusted into immobility but still holds my weight if I place my feet carefully. The roof of the loft is intact, or close enough—I have patched it with salvaged slate from collapsed buildings, fitted the pieces together without mortar, just overlap and gravity. The damp comes in anyway, of course. I have accepted this. I sleep on a raised wooden platform, wrapped in wool blankets that never fully dry and have taken on a smell I no longer notice but that visitors—on the rare occasions I have them—describe as "the smell of the forest floor."

I have a small stove. I burn deadfall, collected from the paths. The smoke is thin and disperses quickly in the damp air. I am careful about this. I do not want to introduce a disturbance that the Quarter would have to metabolize.

I eat what the Quarter offers, which is more than you might expect. Fiddleheads, in season, though I am not a picker and I take only what I need for myself. Watercress from the canal. Mushrooms, sometimes, after the wet nights—I have learned to identify the safe ones, though I am not confident enough to eat the unfamiliar ones, and there are many unfamiliar ones. There is a patch of escaped sorrel near the greenhouse that produces tart leaves year-round. There are the budgerigars' discarded seeds, though I am ashamed of this and do it only when other food is scarce.

I have a routine. I wake early. I go out. I walk my route. I listen. I return. I sleep. The routine is not rigid—the Quarter does not reward rigidity—but it has a shape, and the shape has become part of me, the way the path's curve has become part of the Quarter.

---

I have seen the old woman on Third Street.

Three times. The first time, I was crossing the interior of a collapsed residential block—a facade held up by trees, the ground floor a tangle of growth—and I saw her on the other side of the space, moving through the ferns with a deliberateness that told me she knew exactly where she was going. She was small and bent, as the description says, and she was carrying a bundle of dried material, as the description says. But the description does not mention what I noticed: she was barefoot. Her feet were dark, not with dirt but with the stain of decades of contact with the moss and the soil. They were the feet of someone who has not worn shoes in a very long time. She did not see me. Or she saw me and did not acknowledge me. I cannot tell the difference.

The second time, I was in the greenhouse, in the early morning, and she was standing in one of the interior spaces—the cathedral's nave, if you will—reaching up into a bromeliad with one hand. She was harvesting something. Not the mosquito larvae. Not the water. Something on the plant itself, something I could not see from my distance. She moved with an assurance that was startling. She knew this place the way I know my loft, the way the fern pickers know The Staircase and The Lathe. She has her own names for things. She has her own map.

The third time, she saw me.

We were on the path that circles the greenhouse, on opposite sides. She stopped. I stopped. She looked at me for a long time, and her expression was not curious or hostile or warm. It was evaluative. She was asking herself a question about me, and I could not tell what the question was.

Then she turned and walked into the growth, and the moss closed behind her, and she was gone.

I have not seen her since. But I have found evidence of her passing: a cleared space in a building I thought was empty, as the description says. A tin cup on a windowsill. And once, on the path near the canal, a small bundle of dried plants, tied with what looked like grass, left as if someone had set it down and intended to come back. I did not touch it. I came back the next day and it was gone.

I do not know what she is. I do not know if she is one woman or a succession. I do not know if she was a botanist or a resident or something else. But I know that she is the Quarter's answer to a question I have not yet learned to ask.

---

I have a relationship with the moss that I cannot explain.

It is not mystical. I do not believe the moss is conscious. I do not believe it knows me. But something happens when I walk my route, something that I have been trying to understand for years. The moss on the path edges—where it meets the maintained surface—grows differently where I walk.

I noticed this first near the streetlamp tree with the curve. The *Brachythecium* on the path's edge, on the side where I habitually walk, is denser than on the other side. It recovers faster from the crew's trimming. Its color is slightly different—a deeper green, almost blue in certain lights. At first I thought I was imagining it. Then I walked the other side for a month, and the moss on that side thickened, and the moss on my usual side thinned.

I have tested this. I am not a scientist—I have no training, no methodology—but I have observed it enough times to be confident that it is real. The moss grows differently where I walk. Not because of my foot traffic—I am careful, and the crew's trampling has a much greater impact. Something else. Some quality of my presence that the moss is responding to.

I have a theory, though it is not really a theory, more a feeling that I am trying to give words to. I think the moss is responding to my attention. Not to my footsteps or my body heat or the carbon dioxide I exhale, but to the fact that I am *noticing* it. I am walking my route and I am looking at the moss and I am listening to it grow, and the moss, which has no consciousness, which has no mechanism for perception in the way that we understand perception, is somehow doing better in the places where it is being perceived.

I know how this sounds. I have not told anyone. The botanists would dismiss it. The fern pickers would look at me with the polite tolerance they extend to my eccentricities. The maintenance crew would understand, I think, but they would not have words for it either. The old woman on Third Street might understand, but she has not spoken to me.

So I keep walking my route. I keep listening. And the moss keeps growing.

---

I am lonely, sometimes.

This surprises people when I mention it—they assume that someone who lives in the Moss Quarter has made a choice against human company. But the Quarter is not empty of people. The fern pickers are here, in season. The factory workers are here, in shifts. The maintenance crew is here, in the dry season. I see people. I talk to people. I am not a hermit.

But the loneliness I feel is not the loneliness of absence. It is the loneliness of being the only one who perceives something. The independent researcher, the one who has been coming for twenty-three years, who believes the Quarter is a single organism—she comes closest to understanding what I feel. But she approaches it through data and theory, and I approach it through the soles of my feet and the palms of my hands and the particular quality of the air on a damp morning when the mist is low and the budgerigars are silent and the only sound is the fifth layer, the sound of growth, the sound of process happening too slowly to hear but too present to ignore.

I am lonely because I am the only one who lives at the boundary between the path and the wilderness. The crew maintains the boundary. The pickers cross it. The botanists study it. The old woman has dissolved it—she is on the other side, she is part of the growth, she does not experience the boundary because she does not maintain a self that is separate from the Quarter.

I maintain a self. I am human. I eat and sleep and walk and listen and feel the cold and the damp and the loneliness. And I also feel the Quarter, feel it the way you feel a presence in a room, not through any sense but through the accumulated weight of all senses together. I am the one who stands at the edge and perceives both sides and does not fully belong to either.

I am the path mind. I am the threshold consciousness. I am the thing that the Quarter has grown, or that has grown in the Quarter, that can look both ways.

---

There are days when I think I understand what the old woman is harvesting.

She takes things from the deep places—the interior of the greenhouse, the collapsed buildings, the spots where the canopy is thickest and the light is lowest. Not food, or not only food. Something else. Something that exists in the places where the Quarter is most fully itself, most completely the single organism that the independent researcher believes it to be.

I think she is harvesting the Quarter's attention.

Not literally. I do not think the Quarter has attention in the way that we have attention. But there is something in the deep growth, in the places where the moss is oldest and the mycelium is densest and the decomposition is most complete—there is something there that is not present at the edges, at the paths, at the boundary where I live. A concentration. A focus. The place where the Quarter, if it is a creature, does its thinking.

I have felt it. Once, deep in the greenhouse, in a spot I had never visited before, I stood still for a long time—I do not know how long, the light does not change in the greenhouse, the sound does not vary—and I felt something shift. Not in the air, not in the moss, not in the light. In the quality of my own awareness. As if my attention had been met. As if the place had noticed me noticing it.

I left quickly. I was not ready. I do not know if I will ever be ready.

But I think the old woman goes to those places. I think she harvests whatever grows there—the plants that only grow in the Quarter's deepest attention, the fungi that fruit only in the oldest mycelium, the substances that exist nowhere else because they are products of a metabolism that is the Quarter's own, not the metabolism of any individual species but the combined metabolism of all of them together, the creature breathing.

I think she has been doing this for longer than I have been alive. I think she has been taking the Quarter's attention into herself, gradually, slowly, the way the streetlamp tree took in the iron post. I think she is becoming something. Not less than human. Not more than human. Something else. Something that can live at the boundary without loneliness, because the boundary has moved inside her.

---

I am not sure I want that.

I am not sure I do not.

I keep walking my route. I keep listening to the sixth layer of sound, the sound of the boundary being maintained. I keep noticing the moss, and the moss keeps growing where I notice it. I keep checking whether the Quarter is still itself, and it always is, and I am always still here, and the path is always still open, and the boundary is always still present, and I am always still standing on it.

The damp comes. The dry season comes. The fiddleheads unfurl and are cut and are canned and are shipped to restaurants in the new city center, where people eat them without knowing where they grew. The budgerigars call their single mournful note. The woodpeckers drum their inherited rhythms on the iron frames. The moss breathes in and breathes out. The canal seeps.

I am the one who lives at the edge of the path. I am the one who keeps the path open by walking it. I am the one who is noticed by the thing that does not notice. I am the threshold mind, the path consciousness, the being that the Quarter has grown at its boundary with the human world, the way it grows moss at the boundary between stone and air.

I am the place between the path and the wilderness. And I am still here.
