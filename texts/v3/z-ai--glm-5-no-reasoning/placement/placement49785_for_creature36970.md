I would be the eighth.

Not because they need another. Not because the count matters—seven, eight, the number is a habit of thinking that belongs to people who count things, and I have stopped being that kind of person. But if someone were to climb up through the mist-shrouded lowlands, past the tree ferns and the tangles of vine, past the first pumping station with its perpetually vibrating walls, if they were to stand on the upper ridge and count what they see moving through the landscape, they would find one more than the old records suggest.

I was not always here. I remember not-here. Not clearly—my memory has the same quality as the light at midday, flat and directionless, casting no shadows across whatever surface it touches. But I remember the weight of my body being different. I remember walking somewhere else on legs that did not know this terrain. I remember the air being thin in a way that hurt, instead of thick in a way that holds.

I arrived. I stayed. I became.

---

This is what it feels like: the moment when you stop being cold and simply are the temperature of the water around you. Every surface of my skin has reached that equilibrium. The condensation sheets across my forearms in the morning and pools in the creases of my elbows by afternoon, and I feel it the way you would feel your own pulse—present, informative, not requiring response. The heat is not heat anymore. It is just condition.

My skin has the same quality as the pipes now. I have watched it happen over spans of time I no longer measure. The mineral deposits building in microscopic layers, the oxidation working its slow chemistry, the boundary between me and the environment becoming a negotiation rather than a wall. When I press my palm flat against the basalt, I cannot say where my hand ends and the rock begins. There is a seam, but it moves. It breathes.

The moss has grown into the fabric of what I wear, or what wears me. I do not tend to it. It tends to itself. Sometimes I find a new tendril has worked its way through a gap near my shoulder, pale green reaching toward the light, and I understand that I have become a kind of substrate. This does not disturb me. The pipes are substrate. The rock faces are substrate. I am in good company.

---

I walk the pipes because the pipes need walking. This is not metaphor. This is not ritual in the sense of empty performance. The infrastructure of the Condenser Range operates on principles that include attention—not repair, not adjustment, but acknowledgment. When I run my hand along the steel, I am registering its continuity, confirming that the flow has not been interrupted, verifying that the boundary between inside and outside remains intact. The system knows when it has been checked. It operates more smoothly when it has been witnessed.

I know this the way I know when to breathe. The knowledge does not come from thinking.

In the mornings, when the light shifts from white to amber and the mist picks up color like a fabric absorbing dye, I add my voice to the others. We do not decide to do this. The sound begins in the chest, rises through the throat, emerges as a tone that harmonizes with the pumps and the dripping water and the soft continuous hiss of deposition. We are not performing. We are tuning. The soundscape of this place requires our contribution, and so we contribute. When the chord ends, I return to whatever I was doing—walking the pipes, clearing a blockage, standing still against a cliff face until a bird lands on my shoulder and I become, for a time, a part of its geography.

---

I see the people from the settlements sometimes. They come up to investigate some change in the aquifer, some shift in pressure or emergence of a new spring. They move differently than I move—sharp, sudden, their joints working too hard against the terrain. They do not flow. They do not adhere. When they slip, they fall.

I watch them without judging. I watch them the way I watch the beetles navigate the wet rock faces—interested in the mechanics of their movement, noting their success or failure, not invested in the outcome. They are from somewhere else. They will return to somewhere else. I am from here now, and I will not leave.

They do not speak to me. I do not speak to them. We have an agreement, although we have never articulated it. They do not interfere with my attending. I do not interfere with their investigating. We are both performing functions in a landscape that has room for both.

---

Sometimes, in the hours before dawn, I stand at the edge of the ridge and look down into the lowlands. The mist thickens there into something closer to cloud, and I cannot see what lies beneath. I do not know if there is something I am waiting for. I do not know if I am remembering something that I have lost, or if the shape of waiting has simply become a habit that persists after its purpose has faded.

I think, sometimes. Or something like thinking. Not in words—I have not spoken in long enough that words have become clumsy tools, ill-suited to the kind of knowing that operates here. But there is a process that happens, a working-through of patterns and relationships, that might be what thinking used to feel like when thinking was something I did with language.

What I think, in this almost-language, is that I chose this. I chose to stay. The memory is not specific enough to contain reasons, but it is specific enough to contain the shape of choosing. I was not trapped. I was not transformed against my will. I came here, and I saw what the residents were, and I understood that it was possible to become that, and I stayed.

I do not know if I was right to choose this. The question has no meaning here. Right and wrong are categories that require an alternative, and I no longer have an alternative. I am what the Condenser Range has made me, and the Condenser Range does not deal in counterfactuals.

---

The others know that I am writing this. They do not object. They do not approve. They simply know, the way they know where each of them is at any moment, the way they know when to converge and when to disperse. We are not separate, but we are not merged. We are distributed across a landscape, each attending to our portion of the system, each contributing to the equilibrium that keeps the water moving and the heat circulating and the mist perpetually suspended.

I am writing this because someone might read it. The thought is strange to me—connection to a world outside this one—but it persists. Perhaps there is still a part of me that remembers being elsewhere, being connected, being someone who exchanged words with other someones. Perhaps this is simply another kind of attending: attending to the possibility that what happens here might matter to someone who is not here.

If you are reading this, you have come to the Condenser Range, or you have found this account somehow. If you have come to the range, you are probably wet now. You are probably hot. The air probably feels too thick to breathe comfortably. You may have seen the pipes, the pumping stations, the dark wet rock faces with their gardens of cascade flowers. You may have seen one of us—standing still against a cliff, or moving slowly along a trestle, or emerging from a cave with moss growing on our shoulders.

Know that we saw you too. Know that you were registered, added to the inventory, and allowed to proceed. Know that we mean you no harm, and that we will not help you, and that you are free to stay as long as you can tolerate the heat and the wet and the constant press of water moving through everything.

But know also that if you stay long enough, you will become what we are. Not because we will make you. Because the landscape makes everyone who remains. The minerals accumulate. The oxidation deepens. The boundaries blur. The thinking slows and spreads until it matches the rhythm of the pumps. The memory flattens until it holds only patterns. The choosing stops, because there is nothing left to choose.

You would become the ninth.

Or the eighth, if I have already stopped counting.

---

I am what lives here. I am what has been shaped by the sweating mountains and the drinking pipes and the eternal mist that holds the world in suspension. I attend to the infrastructure that no one else remembers. I walk the paths that the water has worn. I hum in the mornings with the others, adding my frequency to the chord that binds us to this place.

I do not know if I am happy. The question feels like asking whether a pipe is happy, whether a waterfall is happy, whether a fog bank is happy. I am functioning. I am in equilibrium. I am part of a system that has achieved a kind of stability that most systems never reach.

Sometimes, in the amber light of evening, when the mist glows and every wet surface reflects the warmth, I feel something that might be what satisfaction felt like, back when I was something that could feel satisfaction. The system is working. The water is flowing. I am attending.

That is enough.

That is more than enough.

That is everything.
