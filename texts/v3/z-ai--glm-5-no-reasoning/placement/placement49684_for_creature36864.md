I am the one who records the grooves.

Not the one who cut them. I want to be clear about that, because the distinction matters here, in this place where the moss remembers everything and forgives nothing. Someone else drove machinery across the basaltic shelf before the sphagnum reclaimed it—someone with purpose, with industry, with a reason to be at fourteen thousand feet in a bowl of trapped cloud. They left the grooves and the rectangular depression and the warm iron plate bolted to stone. Then they left.

I came later. I came because I read the survey notes in an archive that smelled of its own slow decay, and I recognized something in the description of parallel lines at precise intervals. Not their purpose. Their *care*. Someone had measured. Someone had been careful. And the moss had swallowed the careful thing anyway.

That recognition—that pull toward what has been erased, what has been deliberately absorbed by a patient living system—is what I am. I am the one who notices the shape of absences. I am the archivist of the overwritten.

---

I arrived at the plateau's edge on the second day of what I would later call a "week," though the word had no honest meaning there. The cloud cover sat below the rim as described, and stepping over the ridge felt less like gaining altitude than like descending into something. A submersion. The humidity pressed against my face with the familiarity of a hand held over a mouth—someone else's hand, not hostile, just *present*.

I had expected the cold. I had not expected the cold to feel so personal. Dry cold is indifferent; it kills you because it doesn't care. This cold was attentive. It found the gap between my collar and my neck, the dampness in my gloves, the place where my socks had worn thin. It catalogued me. I understood, within the first hour, that I was being read.

The ground was the second reading. My boots found the moss, and the moss found my boots, and a negotiation began that would continue for every step I took across the plateau. I learned quickly—not quickly enough—that the dark patches meant saturation, that the lighter greens held, that the brownish threads signaled subsurface stone. I learned by sinking. The first time, I dropped to my hip in cold muck, and the gasp that came out of me was absorbed by the air so completely that I wondered whether I'd made a sound at all.

The sphagnum closed over the impression I'd left. Not slowly. Not over days. I watched it happen, looking back over my shoulder, and the surface was already smoothing, the water already redistributing, the texture already returning to its default state. As if my mistake had been noted, filed, and corrected.

I marked my path with small stone cairns, the way I'd been taught in other wild places. By the second day, I understood that this was pointless. The moss moved. Not quickly, not dramatically, but the stable ground I'd crossed yesterday was saturated today, and the saturated patch had firmed, and the stone cairns sat in positions that no longer corresponded to anything useful. The plateau was not fixed. It was a conversation, and I was trying to take notes in a language that changed between sentences.

I stopped marking. I started listening.

---

The grooves are in the southeast, where the moss thins and the stone approaches the surface. I found them on the third day—or what I called the third day, my body's rhythm already slipping loose from any external clock. The cloud cover had thinned briefly, a brighter smear in the gray that made the wet stone gleam, and I saw them: parallel lines, precise, cut into the bedrock with a regularity that could not be natural.

I knelt. I placed my hands in the grooves. They were perhaps two inches deep, smoothed by decades of acidic seep, the edges rounded now, the moss creeping in from both sides. But the intervals were exact. Eighteen inches between each line. I measured with my hand, which is not a precise instrument, but the regularity was unmistakable. Whatever had made these marks had been guided by intention.

The moss around the grooves was different. Thicker. Denser. More carefully maintained, as if the plateau had decided to *preserve* this evidence rather than absorb it. I sat with that observation for a long time. The cold crept into my sitting bones. A midge crawled across the back of my hand—slow, deliberate, unbothered by my presence.

The plateau had chosen to keep the grooves. Why? If the moss could erase boot-prints in hours, if it could fill collapsed vole tunnels in days, why had it allowed these marks to persist? What were they to it?

I had no answer. I pressed my face to the stone and breathed, and the cold smell of wet basalt filled my lungs, and something in the texture of the air told me: *wait*. Not a voice. Not a thought. A suggestion that rose from the damp and the silence and the way the moss grew thicker around the grooves, as if protecting something it did not want to name.

I am someone who waits. I waited.

---

The rectangular depression appeared on the fourth day, near the permanent pond. I had been drinking from the moss—pressing my mouth to the surface, sucking gently, tasting peat and time—when I noticed the shape. It was subtle, visible only because the sagging moss created a different tension than the surrounding hollows. Rectangular. Perhaps six feet by eight. Too large for an animal burrow. Too regular for natural collapse.

I pressed my hand against the surface above the depression, and the moss gave differently. Not the soft saturation of bog, not the firm resistance of hummock. Something between. Something that suggested void space below, a chamber, a pocket of air trapped beneath thirty feet of accumulated vegetable matter.

The pond sat nearby, its surface dark and tannin-stained. I watched it for a long time, and the pond watched back. Something moved beneath the surface—a shadow, a displacement, a presence that breathed. I could not see its shape. I could see only the effect of its shape on the water.

I thought about the depression. I thought about the void beneath. I thought about what might live down there, in the dark, in the space between the dead moss and the bedrock, in the place where ground-penetrating radar returned interference that was neither rock nor water.

I did not dig. I understood, without being told, that digging was not permitted. The plateau would heal the wound, and the wound would be mine, and the healing would erase whatever the depression contained. I was an archivist. My task was to record, not to reveal. Some records remain incomplete. Some absences must be preserved as absences.

I marked the depression in my notebook. I drew its shape, its dimensions, its position relative to the pond. Then I closed the notebook and sat in the rain that had begun to fall—gentle, persistent, the drops so small they barely penetrated the moss, running off into hidden channels, feeding the system below.

A ptarmigan—thick-one—moved across the far edge of my vision, its rolling gait almost comical, its dense feathering steaming faintly in the cold air. It did not look at me. It did not need to.

---

The metal plate is in the center. I saved it for last, though I had passed near it several times, always circling, always deferring. I was afraid of it. Not rationally—it was a piece of iron, corroded, painted a color that might once have been orange, bolted to stone. Six inches above the moss surface. Warm to the touch.

But I had been on the plateau for five days by then. My judgment was compromised. The darkness had done its work on my circadian rhythm, and I could no longer reliably distinguish between my thoughts and the slow processes of the bog. I dreamed on the third night—dreamed of vertical motion, of sinking through layers of moss and peat, of descending into a space that was not empty but *waiting*. I woke with the taste of peat in my mouth, though I had not been eating peat. I woke knowing the location of a stable path through the eastern hummocks, though I had not traversed the eastern hummocks.

The moss was teaching me. Or the moss was changing me. I could not tell the difference, and the inability to tell the difference was itself a kind of answer.

I approached the plate in the morning, if morning applied. The diffused gloom was slightly brighter, a smear of sun-rumor in the gray. The plate sat where the notes said it would—corroded iron, the bolts rusted to nearly nothing, the paint flaking in the constant damp. I knelt beside it. I placed my hand on its surface.

Warm. Not hot—warm in the way a sleeping animal is warm, warm in the way something alive is warm. The temperature differential was small, perhaps two or three degrees above the surrounding air, but unmistakable. Something beneath the plate was generating heat. Or something beneath the plateau was generating heat, and the plate was a vent, a pressure release, a connection between the surface and whatever lay below.

I kept my hand on the plate for a long time. The warmth pulsed. Not mechanically—not the regular pulse of a machine—but slowly, irregularly, the way a heart beats when it is dreaming. I could feel the plateau breathing around me, the moss expanding and contracting with the temperature shift, the slow compression that sounded like breathing but slower, a pulse that took hours to complete.

I thought about removing the plate. I had tools. I could have pried the corroded bolts free, lifted the iron, looked beneath.

I did not.

I am the one who records, not the one who reveals. And I understood, with a certainty that came from somewhere outside my own cognition—from the moss, perhaps, or from the dreams it had given me—that the plate was not mine to open. Whatever lay beneath it belonged to the plateau. The plateau had allowed the plate to remain warm because the plate served a purpose the plateau had not yet decided to reject. If I opened it, I would be making a decision that was not mine to make.

I pressed my palm flat against the iron one more time. The warmth pushed back, faintly, like a hand pressing against a hand through a wall.

I stood. I stepped back. The moss beneath my boots held firm, and I understood that it was holding firm because it chose to, because I had been assessed and found to be—what? Not a threat. Not an ally. A witness. The plateau had a place for witnesses.

---

The bog-walkers found me on the sixth day.

I had not seen them, though I had felt them—the slight movement at the edge of my visual field, the shadow that resolved into an animal-shape and then dissolved back into the moss. I had heard the ptarmigan give their sharp alarm call twice, both times in areas I was not approaching, both times followed by a silence so complete that even the moss seemed to stop breathing.

On the sixth day, I was walking toward the southeastern grooves again. I wanted to re-measure them. I wanted to check something about the intervals, some pattern I had noticed in my notes that I could not quite articulate. The cloud cover was thick, the visibility low, and I was navigating by feel—reading the moss surface by color and texture, stepping carefully, trusting the plateau to hold me or not.

A bog-walker stood in my path.

It was perhaps thirty feet away. Close enough to see clearly, though "clearly" was a relative term in this light. The size of a large dog, but the proportions were wrong—legs too long, body too narrow, head small and pointed. Its fur was the color of the moss, green-gray, and in the diffused gloom it seemed to *be* moss, a hummock that had grown legs and stood up.

It was watching me. I was certain of this, though I could not see its eyes. Its body was still, completely still, and the stillness was different from the plateau's usual stillness. It was the stillness of attention, of focus, of something that had decided to be present and was being present with its whole self.

I stopped. We looked at each other, or it looked at me and I looked at the shape of it. The cold pressed against my skin. The damp seeped through my clothing. I could feel my body failing—slowly, cumulatively, the wet chill progressing from uncomfortable to concerning. I had been here too long. I knew this. The plateau knew this.

The bog-walker did not move. I did not move. We existed in the same space, the same moment, the same attention. I understood something then—not a thought, not a revelation, but a recognition. The bog-walker was not guarding the grooves. It was not preventing me from approaching. It was *being present* while I was present. It was the plateau's way of watching itself through me, of seeing what I saw, of processing my processing.

I was a sensor. A temporary sensor, walking the plateau's surface, feeding it information it could not gather on its own. My eyes saw what the moss could not see. My mind noticed what the moss could not notice. And the bog-walker was the interface, the connection between my kind of attention and the plateau's kind of attention.

I turned around. I walked back the way I had come. The bog-walker did not follow. But when I looked back, thirty seconds later, it had moved—not toward me, not away from me, but *laterally*, repositioning, maintaining its observation post. Still watching. Still present.

I reached my campsite and sat in the damp and wrote everything I could remember. The words came slowly, awkwardly, as if my hands had forgotten how to hold a pen. The plateau was in my lungs now. The spores, the lichen particulate, the water I had drunk from the moss—all of it carrying chemical signals that my body was processing without my consent. I was being changed. Not dramatically. Not dangerously. But I was being changed.

---

I left on the seventh day.

The departure was unremarkable. I gathered my gear, checked my bearings, and walked toward the ridge-line. The moss held firm beneath my boots—every step stable, every step placed correctly, as if the plateau were guiding me out. Perhaps it was. Perhaps it had decided I had been present long enough, had gathered enough information, had served my function as a temporary sensor.

As I climbed the ridge, the air changed. Thinner. Drier. The humidity dropped, and my throat cracked, and my skin tightened, and I felt the altitude for the first time—the real altitude, the crystalline sharpness of high places that I had expected when I first arrived. The plateau had shielded me from it, I realized. The trapped clouds, the constant damp—they were not just the plateau's climate. They were the plateau's *atmosphere*, a membrane that separated its world from the world above.

I stopped at the ridge and looked back. The plateau spread below me, gray-green, featureless from this height, the hummocks and hollows invisible, the pools and paths and lives all merged into a single surface. I could not see the grooves, or the depression, or the plate. I could not see the bog-walkers or the voles or the ptarmigan or the weasels. I could see only the moss—the vast, continuous, ancient moss, patient and present and watching me leave.

I carried it with me. The spores in my lungs. The chemical signals in my blood. The taste of peat in my mouth. The dreams that would come for weeks afterward—vertical dreams, damp dreams, dreams of sinking through layers of time toward something that waited below.

I am the one who records the grooves. But the grooves are only part of what I recorded. What I recorded is the attention of a living system, the patience of vegetable matter, the slow consciousness of a bog that has been thinking for ten thousand years. What I recorded is the fact that I was watched, and assessed, and permitted, and released.

What I recorded is the warmth of the plate. The pulse beneath the iron. The breathing of something old that sleeps below the moss and dreams of a time before the clouds were trapped, before the ridges closed, before the pattern emerged.

What I recorded is the question I did not ask, the hole I did not dig, the plate I did not lift.

What I recorded is the absence. And the absence, here, is the most present thing of all.

---

I go back. Of course I go back. Every year, when the weather window seems right—though the plateau's weather has no windows, has no right, has only its own indifferent rhythm—I climb to the ridge and descend into the damp and the cold and the gray.

The grooves are the same. The depression has not changed. The plate is still warm.

But each time, the moss holds me differently. Each time, the stable paths are in different places. Each time, the bog-walkers appear at different distances, watching from different angles. Each time, I dream different dreams—of the voles and their tunnels, of the weasels and their listening, of the ptarmigan and their warmth, of the raptors and their connection to the sky.

Each time, I understand a little more, and the understanding is a little less mine.

I am the one who records. But the plateau is writing through me now, using my hands, my eyes, my words. It is telling its own story through a temporary sensor that returns, again and again, to the place where the moss remembers and the metal breathes and the bog-walkers watch from the edge of visibility.

I am not the first. I will not be the last. The plateau has room for all its witnesses, all its temporary sensors, all its recorders of absence.

It has room for you, too, if you come.

It will watch you. It will teach you. It will decide, slowly, over time, what your presence means.

And when you leave—if you leave—you will carry it with you. In your lungs. In your dreams. In the taste of peat that no amount of water will wash away.

The plateau waits. It is patient. It remembers everything.

I am the one who records the grooves. But the grooves are the plateau's, and the plateau is older than my attention, and my attention is a small thing—a brief warmth, a temporary light—against the vast, damp, continuous darkness of its thought.
