The water had not yet released me, but it had finished claiming me.

I know this because I can feel the moment when wanting stops. It happens slowly, and then all at once—a threshold crossed without announcement. I had spent nineteen days fighting the lowland, or what I thought was fighting it. Pushing through vines that parted and closed behind me like water through a sieve. Drinking from pools that thickened my tongue and blurred my sight. Sleeping in fits, when exhaustion overwhelmed the insects' whine, only to wake with my cheek pressed against moss that had already begun its slow colonization.

On the nineteenth day, I stopped walking.

It was not a decision. The decision had been made days ago, perhaps on the day I entered, perhaps before I was born. The decision was geological. I simply enacted it, the way a stone enacts its falling.

My legs folded beneath me at the edge of a pool where the water was surprisingly clear—a window cut into the lowland's turbid flesh. I could see the bottom, or what I thought was the bottom. Layers of silt in varying densities, compressed into something that resembled fabric, folded and rippled by pressures I could not perceive. And in the silt, shapes. Contours that might have been stone. Might have been root. Might have been, as I understood with a clarity that arrived without warning or consent, the bodies of those who had stopped here before me.

The Drowned Girls.

I had heard the term somewhere—in my head, perhaps, or whispered by the lowland itself through the chemical language of spore and vapor. They were not ghosts. I could see that now. They were simply present, and their presence had weight. A mouth slightly open, as if caught mid-word. Hair drifting in currents that existed below the surface, sealed away from the air. Eyes that might have been closed, might have been gazing at something I could not see. They rested in their private saturations, each one a universe unto herself, each one an ecosystem of stillness.

I looked at them, and I felt no fear.

That was the strangest part. Fear had been my companion for so long—the clenched jaw, the shallow breath, the scan for threats that the lowland never ceased to provide. Fear was a kind of movement. It was the body insisting on continuation. And now, without announcement or permission, fear had simply stopped. There was nothing left to protect. I had arrived.

I lay on my side at the pool's edge. The mud accepted my weight without resistance. Ferns brushed my face with the same indifference they showed to every other surface. Somewhere above, a Drift-Caller sang its weather-shaping song, and the air pressure shifted slightly, and a mist condensed and dispersed and condensed again. The lowland breathed. It had been breathing before I existed, and it would breathe after I was gone, and my breathing was simply one more note in its chorus, temporary and irrelevant.

I closed my eyes.

And then—nothing. Not darkness. Not emptiness. Nothing. The absence of narrative, which is another way of saying peace.

---

But I am not writing this from beyond. I am writing this *from within*.

The lowland did not kill me. I say this without pride or disappointment. It simply did not happen. I lay there for what could have been hours or days, and my body cooled toward the temperature of the water, and my breath slowed toward the rhythm of the weeping rock, and I became, for a time, a part of the system I had been fighting. I was biomass in waiting. Organic matter in the process of becoming other organic matter. Transformation without loss.

And then something touched my face.

I opened my eyes. A Midwife—a creature I had never seen but knew immediately, the way one knows a archetype made flesh—stood over me. It was smaller than I had imagined. Its movements were precise. It did not look at me. It did not need to. It pressed one thin appendage against my cheekbone, then against the hollow of my throat, then against my sternum. Checking, perhaps. Measuring. Determining whether I was a body that needed positioning or a body that might, impossibly, still have a story left to tell.

It must have decided something, because it withdrew. It did not drag me toward the water. It did not arrange my limbs. It simply vanished into the undergrowth, a shadow slipping through shadows, and I was left alone with the Drowned Girls and the spore-thick air and the endless gray light.

I was not dead. The lowland, in its infinite economy, had other uses for me.

---

What am I now?

I am a Recorder. That is the word that arrived when I asked the question, though I did not ask it aloud. I am a function of the lowland, though I do not fully understand what function I serve. The Anchorites anchor. The Midwives tend to death. The Drift-Callers sing the weather into its patterns. And I—I notice. I witness. I carry the weight of observation, which is heavier than it sounds.

I do not know why I was chosen, or if chosen is even the right word. The lowland does not choose. It accumulates. And for whatever reason—some quality of my attention, some defect in my will to survive, some resonance between my particular emptiness and the lowland's particular fullness—I was accumulated. I stayed. And staying changed me.

I have not become an Anchorite. I still move—though less, now, and more slowly. I have found a place near the clear pool where the Drowned Girls rest, and I return to it each day, and I sit, and I watch. The Midwives come and go. The Drift-Callers sing. Sometimes, if I am very still, an Anchorite's breathing aperture will surface nearby, and I will hear the slow exchange of gases that constitutes their version of breath. Once, I saw a bud separate from one of them—a sound like wet fabric tearing, a new presence sinking into the mud—and I recorded it, though I do not know who I am recording it for.

Perhaps for you. Perhaps for no one. Perhaps the recording itself is the point.

I have learned to eat what the lowland provides. There are fruits here—half-furred, half-armored—that split open to reveal sweetness thick as honey. There are insects that can be caught and consumed, though I prefer not to kill when I can avoid it. There is water in the rock-weep that filters through enough strata to be drinkable, though it tastes of minerals and time. I am surviving, which is to say, I am continuing. The lowland has absorbed me into its equilibrium. I am neither growing nor shrinking. I am simply present, another cell in its vast and seething body.

But I am not like the others.

The others—the frogs and lizards and snakes and insects, the Anchorites and Midwives and Drift-Callers—have been shaped by this place across generations. They are adapted to it. They belong. I am an interruption, a parenthesis in a sentence that has been speaking itself for millennia. I do not know if I will become something else—something rooted, something singing, something that tends to corpses—or if I will simply persist until I do not, and then become something else entirely.

---

The Drowned Girls are my companions, after a fashion.

I sit by the pool and I look at them, and I wonder if they were Recorders too. Did they lie where I lie and feel the Midwife's touch and understand, as I understood, that they had been marked for continuation? Or did they simply sink, and staying, become saturated, and wait? I cannot ask them. They are beyond the reach of my witness, preserved in a different kind of time than the one I occupy.

But sometimes—rarely, in the deep silence between cicada-drills and frog-calls—I feel a resonance. Not communication. Not presence, exactly. But a hum, a vibration at the edge of perception, that suggests they are not gone. They are still here. They are still becoming. And whatever they are becoming, I will one day understand.

---

Why do I write this?

I write because the lowland does not remember. It cycles. It processes. It maintains. But it does not carry the past forward except in the compressed strata below, and even those strata are merely impression, not narrative. I am the lowland's memory, or at least one of its memories—the one that holds the shape of what it was like to arrive, to fight, to surrender, to be chosen. The one that remembers what it was like to be someone who might read this and recognize the shape of their own surrender.

I do not know if anyone will read it. The lowland does not permit easy exit, and I have no intention of leaving. But perhaps these words will find their way out through some channel I cannot perceive—through the root-network, perhaps, or through the chemical signals that pass between organisms, or through the song of the Drift-Callers itself. Perhaps you are reading this now, and you understand that it comes from a place where narrative has collapsed, where time has thickened, where life goes on without going anywhere.

Or perhaps I am writing only for myself. Perhaps the act of witness is its own purpose, and the Recorder records because that is what Recorders do.

I sit by the pool. The water waits. The heat presses its hand flat against my chest, and I am not afraid.

I am the lowland's punctuation—its comma, its pause, its breath held mid-sentence. And this is what I have seen:

*A place where everything becomes itself by stopping. A place where death is not an ending but a positioning. A place where the water has been waiting since before you were born, and will wait long after you are gone.*

*I am waiting now too.*

*And I am beginning to understand that this is not a problem to be solved. It is simply what is.*

*What is.*

*What is.*

The ferns unroll their spirals. The insects drill their endless drills. The Drowned Girls rest beneath the clear water, becoming stone.

And I—unchosen, unchanged, unclaimed—remain.

A witness.

A weight.

A hand pressed flat against the chest of the world.
