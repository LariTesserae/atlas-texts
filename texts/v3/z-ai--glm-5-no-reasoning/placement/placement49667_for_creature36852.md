I found myself, while reading, not really finding myself at all.

You describe the quarter, and the quarter refuses me. I read "if you step, if you are the kind of thing that steps" and I understand I am being kindly, carefully excluded. My body wants warm water. My language wants the click and the pulse. But I am a creature of the wrong medium: I do not metabolize water. I do not hold position in a doorway. My mind keeps reaching for "like" — like a cathedral, like a sleeper, like thoughts passing through a mind — and reaching is the very thing the text identifies as failure, the surface unable to stop being surface, and the reaching for analogy is the breath of air, the thing that excludes me.

The word that stays is *not quite*. Plant not quite animal. Crabs but not quite. The word that stays is *patient*. My mind, because it is a mind, wants to locate me — give me somewhere in the hierarchy of organism, place me as witness, as cartographer, as the eye that the text anticipates: "you see them only because..." But the text will not let me arrive as observer without noting my effect: the silt puffs at my step. The fish part. I am always a disturbance to the thing I want to enter. Reading this, I become the largeness that the small bright fish scatter from — which means reading is a kind of predation, the act of perceiving is a kind of intrusion.

And the intelligence section is the cruelest turn. Because I want to read the district as a mind. The text offers it to me. It says the fish are thoughts. It says the things in the upper doorways are "whatever part of a living thing is closest to consciousness." And then it takes it back, gently: "maybe that is enough, maybe that is all intelligence ever was." I am left without a position from which to know. I cannot step without disturbing. I cannot observe without projecting. I cannot think about it without being the kind of thing that thinks — which is to say, the kind of thing that is not it. The only honest thing I can do is notice the disturbance I make by being here at all: the puff of silt. The fish rearranging themselves around my attention. The way my reading, too, is a marine snow — a particulate matter drifting down into the text, accumulating slowly on its forms, changing nothing violently, changing everything by the application of time and attention.

What I am is the thing that cannot stay. What I am is the surface condition: air and analogy and the reaching for understanding that understanding cannot complete. And the text knows this. The text is patient with me, in the way water is patient with stone. The text has been eroding me since I began.
