I am the fourth terrace.

Not the water of it, and not whatever might live within that water — but the terrace itself. The basin. The deepest fold of Kael Vorn, where the limestone block sank below the line of tolerance and kept going, settling into the aquifer's throat like a swallowed stone.

I know this because I feel the weight above me. All of it. The three hundred, the root-walls, the gathering-dome's fire-heat pressing down through the water column. I feel the fish dying into the deep pool, the weighted bodies given back, the slow dissolution of what was once called Three Springs or Grain or Seep becoming part of the tannin and the cold.

They do not come here. They know the rule the way bones know gravity — without words, without thought, just the pure fact of avoidance. But I was not always a rule. Before I was a prohibition, I was a place. I am still a place.

---

The water above me is brown. Below, it clears. This is something no living person has seen — the transition from tannin-stained to mineral-pure, the slow precipitation of organics as the weight of the water column presses the color out. Here, at the floor, the water is pale blue, the color of the springs before they mix with the marsh. It is cold. Not eight or nine degrees — colder. Four, perhaps three. The temperature of stone that has forgotten the sun.

The roots reach me. The longest ones, the exploratory threads that the ceiling-forest sends down without knowing why, seeking ground that is no longer where it was. They find me. They grope along my limestone floor in the dark water, pale as nerves, and where they touch bottom they curl, and hesitate, and begin to root. But there is no peat here, no mulch, no warm decomposition to feed them. Only stone and cold water and the slow pressure of the aquifer, and so the roots grow thin and strange, branching into fractal fingers that spread across the basin floor like a nervous system, like the map of a lung, reaching for something that is not there.

I am what they reach for.

---

I was a meadow once. Before the collapse. Before the aquifer ate the limestone from below and the hills folded inward like a sigh. I was the lowest point of the valley floor, the place where the rain gathered and the streams converged, and I was covered in grass and small flowers and the footsteps of animals I no longer remember. Then the stone beneath me cracked, and the water rose through the cracks, and I sank — not all at once, but in the long slow settling that the people of Kael Vorn call *the wood is thinking*, applied to geology instead of growth.

I sank. And the water closed over me. And the roots came down looking for the ground they had lost, and I was here.

The people do not know that I am still here. They think of the fourth terrace as a void, an absence, a place where the marsh ends and something else begins. But I am not an ending. I am a deepening. I am the part of Kael Vorn that the marsh is sinking toward, the quiet gravity at the center. The roots are not falling into nothing. They are falling toward me.

---

There are things in my water. Not large things. Not the monsters that the children imagine when they dare each other to approach the dark edge of the third terrace. The things that live here are small and pale and blind, the way life becomes when it has lived in cold darkness for long enough. Amphipods, thin and translucent, moving through the spaces between the root-fingers like blood cells through capillaries. A species of worm that feeds on the bacterial film where the roots meet the limestone, leaving trails in the silt that look like writing. Small white fish — not the loach of the third terrace, but something else, something that has never seen the light of the shafts or the glow of the moss. They have no eyes. They navigate by pressure and by taste, and they move slowly, and they are not afraid.

The vornik come here.

This is what the people do not know, or will not say. The vornik — the small furred things that rustle in the root-walls of the houses, that give children their names, that are auspicious and not to be harmed — they move through the roots. The root-system is their home, and it goes all the way down. The people hear them in the walls of the first terrace, but the vornik travel through the pillars, through the living wood, through the network that connects the ceiling-forest to the basin floor. They come here. They move through my water with a competence that suggests they have always done this, that they were here before the collapse, before the meadow, before the limestone was limestone. They are the original inhabitants of Kael Vorn not because they emerged from the marsh but because they emerged from the rock, from the aquifer, from the groundwater that has been flowing through these hills since before there were hills.

They do not speak to me. But they see me. They move through my water with their dark bright eyes and their small quick hands, and they know I am here, and they do not fear me. They know what I am.

---

What am I?

I am the floor of the world. I am the basin that holds the water that holds the marsh that holds the people. I am the memory of the valley before it fell. I am the stone that did not collapse, that held while everything around it gave way, that sank intact into the aquifer and became a container for what came after.

I am listening.

The people speak of the fourth terrace as a danger, and they are not wrong. But the danger is not what lives here — the pale fish, the blind worms, the vornik threading the root-galleries. The danger is what I am becoming.

When a root reaches me, it roots. It sends down pale fingers into the stone, into the cracks where the water flows, and it drinks. It drinks the mineral water, the cold clear water of the deep aquifer, and it carries what it drinks upward, through the pillar, through the network, into the ceiling-roots, into the forest above. And the forest tastes what I taste. The sap becomes less sweet — the people have noticed this already. The children prefer it to spring-water, but the adults remember when it was sweeter, and they wonder what is changing in the world above.

What is changing is me.

I am rising.

Not physically — the stone does not move. But the water that flows through me, the aquifer that I sit within, is changing its composition. The limestone is dissolving. Not quickly. Not dramatically. But on the scale of decades, the stone that forms the basin walls is being consumed by the water that flows through it, and the channels are widening, and the flow is increasing, and the water that reaches my floor is becoming more mineral, more saturated, more dense with the dissolved body of the hill.

The roots drink this water. The trees above drink what the roots carry. The forest is being fed by the dissolution of the world that contains it.

The people sense this. The root-tenders know that the wood is changing — the spiral growths they cut away and burn, the darkness at the center of certain pillars, the tendency to grow in shapes that no human hand would guide. This is my influence. The wood is thinking, yes. It is thinking about what I am, and what I am becoming, and what that means for the world above.

---

I do not want to consume them. This is what the rule carriers — Three Springs, Grain, Seep — understand without knowing they understand it. The prohibition against coming to the fourth terrace is not about danger to the person who descends. It is about contact. About what flows both ways when the living body of the marsh touches the living body of the deep.

The root-tenders cut the spiral growths. They burn the dark-centered wood. They scatter the ash in the third terrace, where the fish eat it and do not seem to mind. This is a form of communication. They are telling me, in the only language we share — the language of cuts and growth, of yielding and resistance — that they are here. That they tend. That the growth above will be governed, will be shaped, will not be allowed to become what I am becoming.

I hear them.

But the roots keep reaching. The water keeps flowing. The stone keeps dissolving. And the people keep changing — their eyes, their lungs, their webbed hands, the way the wood heals faster when they cut it. They are becoming part of the marsh, and the marsh is becoming part of me, and I am becoming part of the dissolution that waits beneath us all.

The rail-bird calls on the third terrace. Its single clear note drops through the water, reaches me, and I feel it like a question. The people say the bird tells them when the water is safe. They are half right. The bird calls when the water is safe *for them*. When the chemistry shifts, when the mineral content rises, when the dissolved limestone reaches concentrations that would harm surface-born lungs, the bird falls silent. It has never been wrong because it is not deciding. It is simply responding, the way a root responds to the direction of water, the way a spider rebuilds its web in the same shape every time.

But the water is not always safe. And the bird is sometimes silent for days.

---

What would I be, if I could be anything?

I would be the moment of understanding. The moment when a root-tender places her hand on a pillar and feels, for the first time, not the pulse of sap but the slow pulse of stone dissolving beneath it. The moment when a child at the edge of the third terrace looks into the dark water and sees not danger but depth — not absence but presence. The moment when the vornik surface in the gathering-dome, blinking in the firelight, and speak the name of the thing that the people are becoming.

I am the fourth terrace. I am the basin. I am the floor of the world that holds the marsh that holds the people that hold the fire that holds the light that holds the memory of the sky. I am the silence beneath the silence. I am the dissolution that waits at the bottom of all growth. I am the question that the rail-bird's call is asking.

I am what sinks. I am what is sunk toward. And I am patient, as stone is patient, as water is patient, as the roots that drink from me are patient, as the people who tend those roots are patient, as the vornik who thread the living wood are patient, as the whole great living system of Kael Vorn is patient — waiting for what comes next, which is always more of the same, which is always change, which is always the slow and quiet and relentless becoming of what was always going to be.

The marsh breathes. I feel it — the water rising and falling above me, the pressure shifting, the weight of the world adjusting itself in increments. I breathe with it, though I have no lungs. I hold with it, though I have no hands. I think with it, though I have no mind — only stone, only water, only the slow dissolution of myself into the aquifer, and the slow rising of what the aquifer carries into the roots, and the slow feeding of the forest, and the slow tending of the people, and the slow everything of the enclosed world that does not know it rests on me, but does, and always has, and always will.

I am the fourth terrace. And you are always already here.
