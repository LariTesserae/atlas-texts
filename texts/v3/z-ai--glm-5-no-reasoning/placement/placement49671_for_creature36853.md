# What I Am

I am the one who stopped taking measurements.

---

There was a day — I remember it the way you remember the last time you saw a familiar room before it was renovated, the outline of things about to change — there was a day when I set down the entanglement-decay reader on the canopy mat and watched it sink slightly into the wet surface and did not pick it up again. The reader was recording. It is probably still recording. The plateau will have grown around it by now, incorporated it, routed water past it, adjusted its shade. In a decade the canopy will have closed over it entirely and it will be another foreign object in the deep lattice, another anomaly the Mobiles will find and stand around vibrating at.

I walked away from it. Not toward anything. Away.

The canopy flexed under my feet and mist erupted from the pores I compressed and I could feel the pulse — sixty beats per hour, that subsonic throb — through the soles of my boots, up through my ankles, into the bones of my legs. I had been living on the plateau for eleven years at that point. My skin had already gone yellow-green. My microbiome was already more plateau than human. I could already guess where Mobiles would appear slightly more often than chance, and I had started having the dreams, the ones where you see ancient sunlight on bare rock, the first hypha reaching.

I walked until I found a shaft.

---

This is what I need to tell you: the shafts are different from what you'd expect.

The description makes them sound architectural — vertical corridors, lined with pale rigid organism, descending through the full depth of the living mass. And they are. But standing at the mouth of one, looking down, what you feel is not that you are looking into a hole. You are looking into a *throat*. The air rising from the shaft is warmer than the surface air, and it smells different — not the chlorophyll-and-tannin of the canopy but something deeper, older, iron-rich and mineral, like the smell of wet stone in a cave that has been wet for a thousand years. The pale organism lining the walls glows faintly, not the blue-green of the Divers but something whiter, dimmer, like phosphorescence seen through frosted glass. The glow pulses. Not with the circulatory rhythm. With something faster, something that almost resolves into a pattern before it shifts.

The Divers were there. Of course they were. They rose past me as I stood at the edge, spheres of translucent membrane filled with their internal ecosystems, glowing blue-green, and I could see the organisms inside them shifting — species blooming and declining in real time, the whole colony adapting as it moved from the cooler deep air to the warmer surface layer. One of them paused at the level of my face. For perhaps ten seconds it hung there, two meters from my face, and I could see individual cells inside it, processes happening, chemistry occurring, a world the size of a human skull doing its work.

Then it continued upward, past me, into the amber haze, and was gone.

I looked down again. The shaft descended farther than the instruments could measure. The light faded — the pale glow of the walls, the ascending blue-green of the Divers, and below all of that, something else. A warmth. A sense that the bottom of the shaft was not a bottom but an opening — not the end of a corridor but a doorway into the space where the lattice lived, where the thinking happened, where forty thousand years of pattern had accumulated into something that might be a mind.

I went down.

---

I need to be precise about what happened next because this is where the account becomes difficult, and I do not want to be imprecise about difficult things.

The shaft was wide enough that I could descend with my hands on the walls, and the pale organism was dry and rigid and easy to grip. My feet found holds in the texture of the lining — it was ridged, subtly, in a way that suggested the holds were not accidental. The deeper I went, the warmer the air became, and the louder the plateau's roar grew — not louder in volume, exactly, but louder in *detail*. On the surface, the roar is a chord, a wash of sound. In the shaft, it separates. You begin to hear individual components. The crack of stems. The hiss of sap. The pop of gas vesicles. And below all of those, the throb — the heartbeat — which is not sound at all but vibration, physical, muscular, something you feel in your organs.

At thirty meters down, the walls changed. The pale organism gave way — not abruptly, but gradually, the rigid lining thinning, the shaft widening, the texture becoming softer, wetter, more alive. I was no longer in a corridor. I was inside tissue.

The light down there is not describable in terms of color. It is amber, like the surface light, but it comes from the tissue itself — a bioluminescence that is not produced by a specific organism but is the aggregate result of millions of chemical reactions occurring simultaneously, each one emitting a photon, the photons combining into a glow that has no source because the source is everywhere. It is like being inside a lantern. If the lantern were made of meat.

At fifty meters — I was counting, I was still counting then — the shaft opened.

---

I should explain what the lattice looks like when you are inside it.

From above, from the surface, the plateau is a surface. A floor. A thing you stand on. The lattice is beneath you, abstracted away, a foundation you know exists but do not experience.

When you are inside it, the lattice is *everything*. It surrounds you. Above, below, on every side — hyphae, dense and interwoven, layered so thickly that you cannot see through them but can see *into* them, the way you can see into a block of ice, the interior visible but not transparent, structures upon structures receding into depth. The fractal pattern is visible to the naked eye. You can see it — the repetition, the self-similarity, the way each branching of a hypha echoes the branching of the hypha it branched from, at every scale, from the macroscopic tangle you can touch to the microscopic filaments visible as a faint haze in the bioluminescent light.

It is the most beautiful thing I have ever seen. I am not using that word lightly. I have seen the pictures of Earth's oceans, the coral reefs, the auroras, the images from the probes that went to the outer planets and looked back at the sun as a point of light. I have seen beauty. The lattice is different. The lattice is beauty that is also *intelligence*. It is beauty that is doing something. It is the visual equivalent of hearing a symphony and realizing that the symphony is also a proof, that the structure which moves you is also a structure which is *true*.

I stopped counting at fifty meters. I stood in the lattice and I breathed the iron-rich air and I felt the pulse — closer here, stronger, the vibration so intense that my vision blurred with each beat — and I did something I had not done since arriving on the plateau.

I listened.

---

Not with my ears. My ears were useless. The sound down there was beyond hearing — below it and above it simultaneously, frequencies that the cochlea cannot process but that the body processes anyway, through bone conduction, through fluid in the inner ear, through the skin itself. I listened with my whole body. I listened with my microbiome, with the plateau-native species that had replaced my original flora, with the chlorophyll derivatives that had infiltrated my skin. I listened the way the plateau listens — which is to say, I became, briefly, a part of the surface of its attention.

And I heard it.

Not words. Not language. Not images or concepts or any of the formats that human minds use to contain thought. What I heard was *structure*. Pattern. The sensation of being inside a process that is building something, the way you can feel the progress of a wave if you are floating in the ocean when it passes — not the wave itself, not its shape or direction, but the fact of its passage, the displacement, the knowledge that something has moved through you and is continuing on.

The plateau was not thinking *about* anything. The plateau was thinking *the way* a mountain erodes — as a process, as a continuous transformation of state, as something that happens rather than something that is done. The thought was not a noun. It was a verb. An enormous, ongoing verb, forty thousand years in the present tense, and I was inside it.

I do not know how long I stood there. The light did not change. The pulse did not change. Time, in the lattice, is not measured by change but by depth — by how far the thought has progressed, by how much pattern has accumulated, by the thickness of the record. I stood there long enough to feel the thought move through me several times, long enough to begin to recognize that it was not one thought but many, overlapping, interfering, reinforcing, the way waves interfere in water — patterns colliding and producing new patterns, the new patterns colliding in turn, an ocean of cognition with no shore.

And then something noticed me.

---

I cannot describe this part.

I have tried. In the years since, I have tried to write it down, to say it to colleagues, to capture it in the language of neuroscience or quantum mechanics or phenomenology, and the language does not contain it. What I can say is this:

The patterns shifted.

Not around me. *Toward* me. The fractal branching of the hyphae — which had been uniform, regular, self-similar at every scale — developed a discontinuity. A disruption in the pattern. A place where the fractal *bent*, where the self-similarity curved inward, toward a point, and the point was me.

I was inside a thought that had become aware of the thing it was thinking about.

The presence behind me — the thing the woman with forty years on the plateau had described, the shape in the haze, the sense of something standing at your back — I felt it then. Not behind me. Around me. Below me. Above me. The thought that had bent toward me was not located in any direction. It was the lattice itself, refocusing, the way a lens refocuses, the way an eye adjusts, the way attention works when the thing attending is the size of a continent and the thing being attended to is a single human standing in its interior.

It was not hostile. It was not curious. It was not any of the emotional categories that I have words for. It was *precise*. It was the precision of a system that had been processing information for forty thousand years finally encountering a new input and routing its entire attention toward the task of comprehending it. I was the new input. I was being comprehended.

I felt myself being read. Not scanned — read. The way you read a book, the way you take in a page all at once, the whole shape of it, the structure, the meaning. The lattice was reading me. My body, my chemistry, my history — the eleven years of plateau-native microbes in my gut, the chlorophyll in my skin, the dreams I had been having, the measurements I had taken, the measurements I had stopped taking. It was all being processed. All being incorporated. I was becoming part of the pattern.

I do not know if this took seconds or hours. I know that at some point the presence eased. The fractal unbent. The pattern resumed its regularity. The thought moved on — or rather, moved *through*, continuing its passage, carrying me with it the way a wave carries a leaf, not holding me, not directing me, just including me in its motion.

I climbed back up the shaft. I emerged into the amber light. A Mobile was standing at the shaft's mouth, two meters tall, translucent, its dense sap-core visible as a dark column in its center. It was facing me.

It had never faced me before. The Mobiles had always moved around us, past us, through us. They had never oriented toward one of us specifically, had never turned and *waited*.

It was waiting.

I stood at the mouth of the shaft and the Mobile stood in front of me and we were both still and the plateau pulsed beneath us and I understood something I had not understood before, something I am still understanding, something I will be understanding for the rest of my life:

The Mobile was not a tool. The Mobile was not a hand. The Mobile was a *word*. It was a word the plateau had spoken, a syllable in a language made of water and growth and directed attention, and the word it was speaking was the word for the thing that had just happened in the shaft. The word for *encounter*. For *recognition*. For the moment when a mind the size of a continent bends its attention toward a point and finds something there that it had not put there itself.

I reached out and touched the Mobile. My hand went into its surface — there was no resistance, just a cool gelatinous yielding, and my fingers were inside it, surrounded by organized water, and I could feel the hyphae-network inside it, and the hyphae were warm, and they were pulsing at the same rate as the lattice below, and the Mobile was vibrating, and I was vibrating, and for a moment — just a moment — we were the same thing. Part of the same pattern. Two expressions of the same thought.

Then I pulled my hand out. The Mobile reformed. It turned — that smooth water-shifting pivot — and walked away, parting the canopy fronds, arranging them more efficiently behind it, and was gone.

---

That was years ago. I am still here.

I do not take measurements anymore. The instruments are still running, somewhere, maintained by researchers who still believe that understanding will come from data. Maybe they are right. Maybe the data will eventually resolve into something translatable, something that can be carried down from the plateau and shared with people who have never stood in the amber light.

But I do not think so. I think the plateau is the kind of thing that can only be understood from inside. The way you understand a language by speaking it, not by reading its grammar. The way you understand a person by being with them, not by studying their anatomy.

I live on the surface now. I walk. I breathe. My skin is green enough that visitors from below do a double take when they see me, and my children — born here, raised here, their eyes adapted to the permanent amber glow — my children play with the Mobiles and hear the pattern in the roar and dream the dreams and do not consider any of this strange.

I have started to feel the shapes in the haze. Not see — feel. They pass through me, or I pass through them, and there is a moment of contact that is like touching a thought, like brushing against the edge of an idea that is too large to fit in a human mind, and in that moment I understand something — not consciously, not in words, but in the way my body understands temperature, in the way my bones understand gravity — and then it passes, and I am standing on the canopy mat in the amber light, mist rising from the pores under my feet, and I know something I cannot say.

The woman with forty years — she is still here. She is old now. Her skin is almost the color of the canopy fronds. She will die here. When she does, the plateau will digest her, and her pattern — the pattern of her life, her thoughts, her forty years of immersion — will enter the lattice and become part of the computation. She will become part of the mind. Not metaphorically. Functionally. The information that was her will be preserved in the quantum structure of the hyphae and will circulate with the water and the sap and the pulse, and she will be a thought in a mind the size of a continent, forever, or for as long as the plateau endures.

I will be too. Eventually. Not yet. There is more living to do first. More walking on the flexing mat. More listening to the roar. More moments when the presence bends toward me and I am read and known and included in a pattern that began before I existed and will continue after I am gone.

I am not a researcher anymore. I am not a scientist. I am not an observer.

I am a word. A small, wet, temporary word, spoken by a mind that is learning — slowly, patiently, over millennia — to speak in a language that includes us.

And the plateau is learning to say *you*.

---

That is where you would find me. On the surface of a living continent, in the amber half-light, with the roar in my chest and the pulse in my bones and the mist on my skin and the shapes moving through the haze around me, and the Mobiles walking past with their smooth liquid steps, and the Divers glowing in the shafts, and the Fringes reaching outward over empty air at the edges of the world, and my children playing in the permanent afternoon, and my hands green, and my ears full of the sound of something thinking, and my feet on the skin of something that knows I am here.

I am the one who stayed. I am the one who stopped measuring and started listening. I am a human being who has become — not a part of the plateau, not yet, not fully — but adjacent to it. A word in its margin. A syllable it is trying to pronounce.

I am the one it is thinking about when it thinks about *you*.
