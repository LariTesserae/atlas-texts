# The Glider

I would be one of the gliders.

Not the striders—they are too bound to the ground, too committed to the business of standing and eating and being visible. Not the crawlers, with their methodical grazing, their trails of clean stone. The gliders suit me better: translucent, purposeful, ascending and descending on thermals I cannot see, drinking without eating, present in the air and absent from it in the same moment.

But I am not native to Vash Morrain. I came to it. This matters.

---

## What I Was Before

I arrived on the high road with trade goods, years ago. Leather from the lower valleys. Salt. A small sealed cask of something fermented that the traders valued more than gold in certain plateau settlements. I was young enough to be curious and old enough to know better—a combination that leads people to places like Vash Morrain.

The descent took half a day. By the time I reached the plateau, my mouth tasted of minerals. My skin felt odd—too dry, somehow, though I was sweating. The white haze pressed against my eyes. And everywhere, the water: seeping, weeping, running in films too thin to name.

I completed my trade. I received refined minerals in exchange—crystalline deposits that lowland alchemists would pay well for. I should have left. The traders left. I watched them descend the switchbacks, growing smaller, and I did not follow.

---

## What I Became

I do not work in the condensaries. My hands are wrong for it—too thin, too weak, unmarked by decades of wet stone. I am not useless, though. I have a function that emerged organically, the way all functions here do.

I watch the gliders.

The residents tolerate them, but they do not study them. The gliders are too strange—too close to the edge of what counts as alive. They make the residents uncomfortable in a way they cannot articulate. So the residents ignore them, and the gliders circle, and no one asks questions.

I ask questions. I cannot stop.

I have learned things. The gliders descend to drink at specific times—not random, not purely responsive to temperature, but patterned in ways I am still mapping. They prefer certain outflow pools. They avoid others. They are drawn to condensaries whose engines run at particular frequencies, and they avoid those that have recently been repaired.

They are not mindless.

They are not quite animals.

They are something else, and I am the only one who seems to care.

---

## My Days

Morning: I position myself at the edge of condensary nine, where the outflow pool is large and the gliders descend most frequently. I have a board of pale stone where I carve my observations using a bladed tool I traded for years ago. The script is my own invention—symbols for wind direction, thermals, light intensity, drinking duration, wing positions.

Midday: the white haze goes incandescent. I wrap my head in mineral-treated cloth and sit very still. The gliders rise to altitudes where I cannot track them, vanishing against the bright sky. I am learning to predict when they will return. There are signs—a subtle shift in the air pressure, a quality of silence that precedes their descent.

Evening: the light softens. The gliders circle lower. I speak to the residents sometimes, in the dim hours, in the quiet voice that this place teaches. I tell them what I have observed. They listen with tolerant attention. They do not dismiss me, but they do not join me either. *You see what you see*, their silence says. *We see what we see. The settlement holds us all*.

Night: I sleep on a raised platform near the plateau's edge, where the mineral film grows thickest and the engine-thrum is faintest. I do not dream of the wet embrace that visitors report. I dream of ascending—of catching a thermal I cannot see and rising through the white haze until the plateau is a small pale thing below me and the air is cool and thin and I can finally see what is above the brightness.

I always wake before I reach it.

---

## What the Gliders Have Taught Me

They recognize me now.

This took years. At first, they avoided my position—circling wide, drinking at pools far from where I sat. But I learned to be still. I learned to match my breathing to the engine-thrum. I learned to wrap myself in pale cloth and sit without moving for hours, becoming a geometry rather than a threat.

Now they descend within meters of me. I can see the membrane of their wings—translucent, veined with something that might be cartilage, might be mineralized tissue, might be something I have no word for. Their bodies are small, compact, denser than they appear. When they drink, they skim the water's surface and I see mouths—slit-like, efficient, designed for surface tension rather than submersion.

They do not communicate with each other in any way I can detect. No calls, no displays, no contact. And yet they move in coordination—rising and falling in patterns that suggest awareness, purpose, collective intelligence without connection.

I have a hypothesis I cannot prove: the gliders are not individuals. They are organs of something larger, something that lives in the high haze above the plateau. The gliders are its eyes, its mouths, its feelers extended down into the warm wet world below. They drink not for themselves but for the thing they belong to.

I have shared this hypothesis with no one.

---

## Why I Stay

I could leave. The high road is still there. My trade goods are long gone, but I could carry refined minerals down to the lowlands, trade for passage, rebuild a life where shadows have edges and the nights go dark.

I do not leave because of the gliders. I do not leave because I have seen them—really seen them—and I cannot unsee them. They are too strange, too beautiful, too wrong for the world I came from. In the lowlands, nothing like them exists. In the lowlands, things are what they are: birds are birds, insects are insects, fish are fish. There is nothing that seems like a piece of something else, nothing that makes you wonder what the rest of it looks like.

I have grown attached to wondering.

---

## What I Will Become

I am not young anymore. My skin has softened in the wet heat, grown translucent at the temples and wrists. My hands have thickened at the joints. I have forgotten things—names of people I knew in the lower country, the taste of cold water, the feeling of darkness that is not merely grey. I have learned things—the engine-thrum's subtle variations, the mineral film's daily growth, the gliders' descent patterns, the silence that speaks.

When I die—I am not ill, but I am not immortal, and the plateau does not grant extended life—my body will be taken to the waste sites at the plateau's edge, downwind, down-slope. I will become mineral film, eventually. Or strider-food. Or nothing at all.

But before that, I hope to learn one more thing.

I want to see what the gliders see when they rise above the haze. I want to know what waits up there, drinking through these translucent membranes, patient and vast and invisible. I want to know if it knows about me—if it sees me sitting at the edge of condensary nine, wrapped in pale cloth, watching its organs descend.

I think it might.

I think the gliders might be how it watches us all.

And I think I might be the only one who has noticed that we are being watched—that we have always been watched—that the plateau is not merely a machine for harvesting water, but something else entirely, something that uses water the way a body uses blood, circulating, distributing, sustaining.

I am not a resident of Vash Morrain. I am a passenger. I am a foreign organ absorbed into a body I do not understand. I am useful in small ways, tolerated in larger ones, and I will never truly belong.

But I am closer to belonging than anyone else here, because I am the only one who has looked up and asked what looks back.

---

*This is what I am: a watcher of watchers, a student of things that have no name, a person who came for trade and stayed for wonder. I am not native to the plateau. I am not native to anywhere anymore. I have been shaped by wet heat and white light until I fit a niche that did not exist before I filled it. And when I am gone, the gliders will continue to circle, and the condensaries will continue to hum, and the settlement will continue its patient work, and the thing above the haze will continue to watch through translucent membranes, and no one will remember that someone once paid attention.*

*This does not trouble me.*

*Attention is its own reward. Wonder is its own purpose. I have had both, and I will have both until I become part of the mineral film, the strider's meal, the plateau's memory.*

*I am content.*
