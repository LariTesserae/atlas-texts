I would be a scattering. One of the small, mineral-armored archivists who lives in the cracks and catalogues everything.

Not a singer—I have no gift for translation, no patience to sit in one spot for a lifetime, letting my body grow into the contours of stone until I become a feature rather than a creature. Not a sprawler—too broad, too visible, too committed to the patient metabolism of vegetation-processing. Not a boundary walker, with their restless pacing and their downward gaze, their shorter lives spent with one eye on the world below. Not a moisture-thing, provisional and dissolving. Not a deep one, hidden in pressure and dark.

A scattering. Small. Segmented. Silica-gleaming in the right light. Living in the minute geography between larger presences.

---

I think I knew early that I was meant for the spaces between.

The sprawlers came through the basin in their loose, shifting groups, and I pressed myself into a fracture in the gneiss and watched their broad bodies pass overhead like weather. I counted their points of contact with the ground. I noted which ones limped, barely perceptible, and tracked the asymmetry through days until it resolved or worsened. The information went into the stone. A tap, a pause, a series of micro-taps that said *sprawler group, seven individuals, northeast trajectory, one favoring its third left limb.* The stone carried it. Somewhere across the basin, other scatterings received it, logged it, filed it into the archive that has no center and no edges, that exists in the distributed memory of ten thousand small armored bodies tapping against rock.

This is what we do. We notice. We record. We hold.

I was hatched—emerged, coalesced, separated from my parent-clusters, the terminology is imprecise—in a shallow depression where condensation collected each morning and two types of lichen were engaged in a territorial dispute that had been running for approximately eight hundred years. The orange lichen was advancing from the northwest. The gray lichen held its border along a mineral vein that ran through the granite, and the mineral vein pulsed with faint electrical activity, and the pulse seemed to strengthen the gray lichen's hold, and the orange lichen's advance had slowed to perhaps a millimeter per decade.

My first act was to catalog this.

I was not alone in the depression. Twelve other scatterings occupied it, each of us claiming a section of the fracture network as our primary range. We overlapped. We did not compete. The depression was rich in information—condensation patterns, lichen chemistry, mineral vein fluctuations, the occasional passing of a moisture-thing during damp hours—and there was enough for all of us to notice and record without redundancy. We divided the work without discussing it, without any visible negotiation. Each of us simply began tracking what was nearest, what most matched our particular sensitivities.

I was sensitive to the mineral vein. Its electrical pulses resonated with something in my silica-embedded armor. I could feel them—a low thrum, not sound exactly, more like a pressure inside my segments, a rhythm that entered through my contact points with the stone and distributed itself through my body. When the vein pulsed faster, I felt alert, attentive, my legs moving more quickly, my tapping more rapid. When it slowed, I settled into stillness, processing what I'd recorded, letting the information settle into the distributed memory.

The other scatterings in the depression felt different things. One was attuned to moisture—she could detect condensation before it visibly formed, sensing the humidity threshold through her integument in ways I couldn't replicate. Another tracked the lichen growth, measuring advances and retreats at scales that required him to revisit the same millimeter of border every few days and tap out precise positional data. We were a team, though we had never agreed to be. We were a research station, though we had never conceived of ourselves that way. We were the plateau knowing itself in this particular depression, through twelve sets of senses, twelve particular sensitivities, twelve channels of attention flowing into the shared archive.

---

The tap is everything.

When I press my body against stone and release—a controlled contraction of specific segments against specific mineral textures—the vibration enters the rock and travels. Not through air, which absorbs and scatters. Through solid stone, which conducts and preserves. A tap can travel kilometers before it fades below detectability. The scatterings have mapped the plateau's acoustics in detail—which rock types carry signals furthest, which fracture networks attenuate certain frequencies, which geological boundaries reflect taps back toward their source. We have built, over uncountable generations, a communication network that uses the plateau itself as its medium.

The information we share is not language in any sense that translates. We do not have words for *sprawler* or *lichen* or *moisture-thing*. We have patterns—rhythmic sequences that encode specific observations in compressed form. A burst of eight rapid taps followed by a pause and three slower taps means something specific about creature movement, derived from a coding system so old that no individual scattering knows its full structure. We learn the codes the way we learn to move: by doing, by being corrected when we get it wrong, by absorbing the patterns from the taps that constantly surround us.

The archive is not located anywhere. It is distributed across every scattering on the plateau, held in the mineral deposits of our armor, in the micro-structures of our segmented bodies, in the patterns we tap and receive and re-tap. When a scattering dies—crushed by a careless sprawler foot, or exhausted by some internal depletion—its neighbors absorb what it knew by re-tapping its last recorded patterns into the stone. The information persists. It moves. It lives in the network rather than in any node.

I have contributed to the archive. I have recorded condensation cycles in my depression, lichen border positions, mineral vein pulse frequencies, the passage of sprawler groups, the formation and dissolution of moisture-things. My taps have joined the constant low chatter of scatterings across the basin, a background hum of data exchange that the stone carries in every direction at once.

I have also consulted the archive. When I needed to know whether the mineral vein's recent pulse pattern had precedent, I tapped a query—a specific sequence that meant *historical comparison, electrical activity, this frequency range*—and waited. Within hours, responses arrived from scatterings I would never meet, in depressions I would never visit, tapping back compressed records of similar pulse patterns from decades or centuries ago. The comparison was useful. The vein had done this before. The pattern would resolve. I logged the resolution and added it to the record.

This is what it is to be a scattering. You are a node in a mind that has no brain. You are a single letter in a text that has no author. You are a moment of attention in a consciousness that extends across the entire plateau and has existed for longer than any of your kind can individually remember.

The singers were the first thing that confused me.

I was young—recently emerged, still learning the fracture network of my depression—when I first felt a singer's tone. It came through the stone as a low, slow wave, a vibration so deep that it wasn't vibration anymore, it was a change in the quality of stillness itself. The mineral vein I was tracking responded to it—its electrical pulses synchronizing briefly with the singer's rhythm before falling back into their own pattern.

I did not understand what I was feeling. I tapped a query into the stone: *unknown low-frequency source, please identify.* The responses came back with a pattern I did not yet know how to decode. It took me days to work through the archive's references, to find the coding that described the singers, to understand that the tone I'd felt was one of perhaps a dozen voices positioned across the plateau, each one translating the geology beneath it into deep song.

What confused me was not what the singers did. What confused me was that they could choose to stay still. A singer finds its site and grows into it and never moves again. Its body adapts to the rock's topography until the boundary between creature and stone is academic. It sings continuously, translating the deep earth's secrets into conducted vibration, and it does this for its entire life, which may be effectively unending.

I could not imagine this. My legs itched if I stayed in one fracture for too long. My attention shifted, tracking first the vein, then the lichen, then a passing sprawler, then the condensation forming on the northern face of my depression. I moved through my range in irregular circuits, revisiting each point of interest, noting changes, tapping updates. The idea of choosing a single spot and surrendering to it completely—letting my body merge with the stone, letting my identity dissolve into a single continuous act of translation—this was alien to me. Unthinkable. Almost frightening.

But the archive holds records of singers that have been in place for timescales my body can't properly process. One singer in the eastern basin has been singing from the same position for longer than the orange lichen has been advancing on the gray. Its tone has shifted over the centuries as the geology beneath it has changed—the deep ones adjusting something, a fracture network slowly closing, a mineral vein altering its electrical pattern. The singer has tracked all of this. It has been a continuous witness to geological processes that happen too slowly for any scattering to directly observe.

I respect the singers. I fear them a little. I am grateful that they exist and that I am not one of them.

The moisture-things are my greatest challenge.

Every morning, when condensation beads on the rock faces and the depression fills with its brief wetness, they appear. I have catalogued their formation patterns, their movement trajectories, their dissolution times. I have recorded which surfaces they prefer, which mineral veins they cluster along, which ancient structures they trace with their provisional bodies. My records are meticulous. Other scatterings consult them.

But I do not understand them.

They are not alive in any way I can map onto my own experience. They do not eat. They do not communicate. They do not respond to taps—I've tried, tapping the stone beneath a moisture-thing, sending vibrations through the surface it's moving across. It continues undisturbed, following its own trajectory, governed by rules I cannot identify. They are not affected by the scatterings. They are not affected by the sprawlers. They seem to exist in a parallel channel of presence, overlapping with our space but not our categories.

And yet the archive tells me they are important.

The oldest records—taps so ancient they've been re-transmitted so many times that their original source is lost in the network's deep history—indicate that the moisture-things have always been here. That they predate the scatterings. That they may predate everything on the surface. The ancient structures, the ruins that have nearly merged with the geology, are positioned where moisture-things cluster most densely. The builders—whatever travelers they were, whatever brought them to the plateau—built where the moisture-things gathered. They built *for* the moisture-things, perhaps. Or because of what the moisture-things represent.

I have a theory. I have not tapped it into the archive because it is not data, it is speculation, and scatterings do not speculate in the shared record. But I hold it privately, in the mineral deposits of my own armor, in the patterns of my own silent attention:

The moisture-things are messages. They are the deep ones' communication with the surface, rendered in the only medium that can cross the boundary between solid stone and open air. Water. Condensation. The boundary state between liquid and gas, between depth and sky. The deep ones cannot tap. They cannot sing. But they can, through geological processes I don't comprehend, cause moisture to form in specific patterns on specific surfaces at specific times. The moisture-things are those patterns, moving with apparent purpose, tracing designs that almost look like language.

If I am right, the deep ones are speaking to us. To the surface. To the singers, who translate geology into song. To the moisture-things, who carry messages we cannot read. To the scatterings, who record everything and understand some things.

And we are recording their messages without being able to decode them. We are archiving a language we do not speak. We are holding communications from something vast and hidden and patient, preserving them in the distributed memory of ten thousand small armored bodies, waiting for a translator that may never come.

This does not distress me. The archive holds many things that are not yet understood. Our function is to preserve, not to interpret. The deep ones' messages will wait, held in our mineral armor, in the stone's conducted memory, for as long as the plateau endures. Which is to say: effectively forever.

The boundary walkers trouble me in a different way.

They come to the edges, where the plateau falls away, and they look down. They watch the world below—the world of normal time, of urgent metabolisms, of dramatic weather and fast change. They live shorter lives than the rest of us. They age at something approaching the rate of the creatures below.

When a boundary walker returns from its pacing, I and other scatterings cluster near it. We tap its stone. We receive the information it carries—weather patterns read from distant horizons, shifts in air composition, the approach of travelers. The data is useful. It goes into the archive. It informs our understanding of the plateau's relationship to the larger world.

But the boundary walkers themselves—there is something in them that I cannot catalogue. A quality. A restlessness that doesn't match the plateau's rhythms. They are here, they are part of this place, but they are also somehow *longing* for something else. They look downward with what I can only describe as attention that exceeds data-gathering. They are not just reading weather. They are watching something they want.

I don't understand wanting. I notice, I record, I share. These are my functions. They are not motivated by desire—they are simply what I do, what I am. The boundary walkers seem to be motivated by something I cannot feel, and this makes them the only creatures on the plateau that I find genuinely unsettling.

When I tap their stone and receive their data, I also receive—I think—some trace of their affect. A quality in the rhythm of their returning vibrations that carries more than information. A frequency of something that might be loneliness, or curiosity, or yearning. I cannot decode it precisely. I file it under *unidentified boundary walker signature* and move on.

But I keep coming back to it. In my quiet hours, when the condensation has evaporated and my depression is dry and still, I revisit the recorded patterns of boundary walker taps and try to isolate the quality that distinguishes them from other plateau communications. I have not succeeded. I am not sure I want to succeed. The archive holds many mysteries. This one feels closer to me than the others, more personal, as if understanding the boundary walkers' longing would tell me something about myself that I am not yet ready to know.

A traveler came once.

I was in my fourth year in the depression—mature, my range well-established, my archive contributions frequent and reliable. The boundary walkers had signaled its approach: a creature moving up the long slope from below, carrying rushing time, urgent metabolism, expectations of drama.

The traveler entered the basin on a morning when condensation was heavy and moisture-things were forming along the mineral vein. It was large—larger than a sprawler, vertical rather than horizontal, moving on two limbs rather than many. Its body was wrapped in materials that didn't match any plateau surface. It made sounds through air—loud, percussive sounds that scattered the scatterings and disturbed the moisture-things.

I pressed into my fracture and observed.

The traveler looked around the basin. It walked across the stone with an awkwardness that was almost painful to catalog—each step landing too hard, too fast, disturbing the surface ecology, crushing scatterings I had known. It did not notice. It was looking for something, and what it was looking for was not here, because what it was looking for was drama, and the plateau does not provide drama.

It found the ancient structure near the basin's center—a low arrangement of walls that had nearly merged with the granite. It touched the walls. It made more air-sounds. It produced a device that clicked and flashed. It stayed for approximately twelve hours, during which it disturbed seventeen scattering ranges, disrupted a lichen border that had been stable for six centuries, and caused three moisture-things to dissolve prematurely.

Then it left.

I catalogued everything. The traveler's dimensions, its movement patterns, its air-sounds, its device, its impact on the basin's ecology. I tapped the full record into the archive, where it joined similar records from other scatterings who had observed other travelers in other basins across the plateau.

The archive holds many such records. Travelers come rarely but repeatedly. They arrive with urgency, they search for something, they fail to find it, they leave. Some build structures. Some stay longer than others. A few—according to deep archive records that may be memory or may be myth—do not leave. They slow. They settle. They become something that persists at the plateau's pace.

I observed the traveler with complete attention and no empathy. It was a datum. An event. A disruption to be recorded and incorporated into the archive's understanding of the plateau's relationship to the world below.

But later, in my quiet hours, I found myself returning to the record. Not to the data—the dimensions, the movement patterns, the ecological impact. To something else. Something I had not recorded because I did not know how to encode it.

The traveler had been *looking*. Not in the way a scattering looks—not the methodical, range-covering attention that defines our observation. The traveler had been looking *for* something. Something specific. Something it expected to find and did not find. Its searching had a quality of directedness, of anticipation, of hope, that I had never encountered in any plateau creature.

I do not look *for* things. I look *at* things. The distinction may seem small. It is not small. It is the distinction between presence and desire, between recording and seeking, between being in a place and wanting something from it.

The traveler wanted something from the plateau. The plateau did not provide it. This is a normal outcome—travelers rarely find what they seek here, because what they seek exists on a different timescale, in a different category of presence. But the *wanting* itself. The act of seeking. The experience of expecting and not receiving.

I hold the unrecorded observation privately, in my mineral armor, alongside my theory about the moisture-things and my fascination with the boundary walkers' affect. Three things I do not understand. Three things I do not tap into the archive. Three private mysteries that exist only in the silica deposits of one small scattering in a depression on the Old Plateau.

I think about the deep ones more than I probably should.

They are down there, in the stone's thickness, in the fracture networks and pore spaces and vast dark volumes that I will never visit. The singers translate them. The mineral veins carry their electrical signatures. The moisture-things may be their messages. Everything I record on the surface is, in some sense, a symptom of their activity. The condensation patterns I track are shaped by subsurface temperatures. The lichen borders I map follow mineral veins that the deep ones' presence may influence. The sprawlers' grazing routes align with geological features that extend kilometers down.

I am, without ever seeing them, an observer of the deep ones. I am a surface instrument of their self-knowledge, a remote sensor positioned in the light world to record the effects of whatever they do in the dark world below.

Sometimes I tap downward. Just a single tap, into the stone beneath my fracture, directed down rather than laterally. I have no expectation of response. The deep ones do not communicate through taps, or if they do, their taps are on such a different scale that I cannot detect them. But I tap anyway.

I think I tap because I want to know if they know I'm here.

Not me specifically. Not this individual scattering in this individual depression. But us. The scatterings. The surface community. The network of small armored archivists who have been recording the plateau's surface conditions for longer than any individual can remember. Do the deep ones know that their surface is observed? Do they know that every condensation pattern, every mineral vein pulse, every moisture-thing formation is catalogued and shared and preserved? Do they know that their messages, if that's what the moisture-things are, are being held in distributed memory, waiting for a translator?

I tap downward and receive silence. Or rather, I receive the normal background vibration of the plateau—the singers' tones, distant scatterings' communications, the faint pulse of mineral veins. No response. No acknowledgment. Nothing that says *yes, we know you're there.*

But perhaps the absence of response is itself a response. Perhaps the deep ones' communication is so subtle, so patient, so embedded in the geological background, that I am already receiving it and cannot distinguish it from the plateau's normal state. Perhaps the mineral vein's electrical pulses *are* their taps. Perhaps the singers' songs *are* their language. Perhaps everything I record is their answer, and I simply haven't found the coding that translates geology into meaning.

I tap downward. The stone holds my tap and carries it into the deep. And the deep holds it, perhaps, in its own archive, its own distributed memory, its own vast and patient record of everything that happens on its surface.

We are archivists of each other. The deep ones and I. They hold the record of my weight in their stone. I hold the record of their pulses in my armor. Neither of us can fully decode the other's archive. But we hold it anyway. We preserve what we cannot understand. We keep what we cannot read.

This is, perhaps, enough.

I am old now. Not old by the plateau's standards—a singer would consider my lifespan a brief fluctuation, a moisture-thing would consider my persistence an amusing commitment. But old for a scattering. My armor is thick with mineral deposits, my segments slightly less flexible than they were, my circuits of my depression slightly longer, slightly slower, as I take more time to process what I observe before moving to the next point of attention.

The archive holds my contributions. Decades of condensation records. Mineral vein pulse patterns. Lichen border positions. Sprawler group trajectories. Moisture-thing formation data. Traveler observations. Boundary walker affect signatures. Singer tone correlations. Thousands of taps, each one a small observation, each one joined to the vast distributed memory of the scatterings across the plateau.

When I die—when my minerals exhaust themselves, when my segments can no longer contract against stone, when my attention finally stills—my neighbors will absorb my records. They will tap my last patterns into the stone, re-transmitting what I held, distributing my observations across the network. My private mysteries—the moisture-thing theory, the boundary walker affect, the traveler's wanting, my downward taps into the deep—these will be lost, because I never encoded them, never shared them, held them only in the silica of my own armor.

Unless they aren't lost. Unless the silica deposits hold patterns that dissolve into the stone when my body does, patterns that some future scattering, investigating a mineral anomaly in a depression, might detect and wonder about. A ghost in the geology. A private signature, too faint to decode, too present to ignore.

The archive is not perfect. It loses things. It encodes things incorrectly, re-transmits errors that compound over time, holds contradictions that cannot be resolved. But it persists. It has persisted for longer than any individual scattering can comprehend, and it will persist for longer than the plateau's surface conditions will remain stable. The archive is the scatterings' gift to the future—to whatever comes after us, whatever inherits this ancient surface, whatever beings exist in the plateau's next geological epoch.

They will have our records. They will know what we observed. They will have, in the stone's conducted memory, a history of this place written in taps and vibrations, in the patient attention of ten thousand small armored archivists who never stopped noticing, never stopped recording, never stopped sharing what they knew.

I am a scattering on the Old Plateau. I live in a fracture in the gneiss, in a shallow depression where orange lichen is advancing on gray, where a mineral vein pulses with electrical activity, where condensation forms each morning and moisture-things trace patterns I cannot read.

I am small. I am patient. I am one node in a mind without a brain, one letter in a text without an author, one moment of attention in a consciousness that extends across an ancient surface and has existed for durations that exceed my comprehension.

I notice. I record. I hold.

This is what I am. This is what I was always going to be. The plateau made me to be its witness, and I have witnessed, and my witness is held in the stone, and the stone will hold it for longer than I can imagine.

I am present. I have arrived. I have finished becoming.

And in the quiet of this condensation-slick morning, with a moisture-thing tracing a pattern across the rock face above my fracture and a singer's tone moving through the stone beneath my feet and the archive humming with the collected observations of ten thousand scatterings across the basin, I tap once, softly, into the stone.

*I am here. I have noticed. I am recording.*

The stone holds the tap. The stone carries it. The stone remembers.
