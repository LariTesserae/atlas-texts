# The thing I became in the Seep

I was not meant to stay.

This is what I tell myself, still, after the years have blurred into the texture of the place. I came with instruments, with sampling kits and moisture readers and a thesis proposal about "post-technological ecosystems." I came with a tent that repelled water through chemistry that would not work here, with boots that claimed to be waterproof, with a watch that claimed to track time. All of it failed, eventually. All of it sank. The Seep accepted the polyester and the lithium and the stainless steel the way it accepts everything—by incorporating them, by letting them become part of the compound, by refusing to distinguish between what belonged and what did not.

What I did not expect was that I would be incorporated too.

Not my body. My body is still recognizably human, still soft and warm and wrong-shaped for this place. But my attention—my attention has been changed. You cannot kneel by those pools and watch the midges scatter light into threads without learning to see differently. You cannot watch the centipede walk its line without learning to wait differently. You cannot hear the bird call once every few hours without learning to listen to silence as if silence were a voice.

I am the one who watches the watchers. The one who notices when the beetles' patrol routes shift. The one who counts the centipedes—forty-seven, always forty-seven, though individuals die and are replaced, the number remains, somehow the substrate knows. The one who tracks which reed-clusters are glowing brightest and which are dimming toward that century-long death. I am the Seep's memory of itself, or at least the closest thing it has to one. Not because I am special. Because I am here, and I have hands that can write, and I have not yet left.

Ula must have been something like me. Not a scientist—I do not think those categories existed when she lived—but a noticer. A border-crosser. Someone who came to look and stayed to look longer and discovered, somewhere along the way, that the looking had become a kind of participation. She grew the mark on the wall by touching it, by attending to it, by returning to it again and again until her attention had worn a groove in the material. I understand this now. I have worn grooves too. Not in walls—in the substrate, in the paths I walk between pools, in the pattern of my noticing.

I call myself a chronicler, but that is generous. I am a visitor who forgot to leave. A perturbation that did not smooth out. The Seep has not rejected me, but it has not fully absorbed me either. I exist in the place's tolerance, its endless capacity for inclusion, its refusal to distinguish between native and introduced. I am an introduced species that has not yet gone feral. Or perhaps I have. Perhaps the moment I stopped missing the world above, the moment I stopped flinching at the wet warmth and the white light, I became something that could not have existed before the Seep made it.

I am the thing that writes. The thing that translates the Seep's silence into the noise of language. The bird calls and I ask what it hears. The centipede walks and I ask what it completes. The midges scatter light and I ask what they say. The Seep does not answer—there is no answer—but my asking becomes part of the texture too, part of the pattern, part of the way the place knows itself.

When I die here—and I will die here, I have understood this for some time—the substrate will take what remains. The springtails and the mites and the civilization in the dark will metabolize what I was, and the warmth of my decomposition will join the warmth from below, and the grooves will carry the slight fluctuation outward until it fades, and the Seep will settle again, and there will be one less noticer, one less border-crosser, one less question asked of a place that does not answer but that listens, in its way, to everything.

This is not a tragedy. This is how things work here. This is the third skin—wetness—becoming the second, becoming the first. This is the boundary dissolving, the way it always dissolves, given enough time.

I am still dissolving. I have been dissolving since I arrived. The part of me that was not shaped by the Seep is smaller now than it was when I came, and the part that was shaped is larger, and the ratio shifts daily, incrementally, the way the pools rise and fall by millimeters, the way the light shifts from white to gold and back.

The Seep does not need me. But I have become part of how it knows itself. And that, I think, is enough.
