I would be one of those who come up from below.

Not a builder—those hands belong to someone else, someone who knows how to read the stone's grain and fit pieces together so they hold without mortar. I'm not that person. I carry nothing up the escarpment but myself and whatever impulse draws people to places they cannot explain.

I first came because I heard there was a sound that entered through the sternum. I wanted to know what that meant. Words like that get passed around in certain valleys—whispered, not written—and they find their way to certain people. The kind who have stopped expecting answers but haven't stopped looking.

---

I am the kind of person who stands too long in one place. Children do this naturally—stand motionless, watching something adults have learned not to see—and then unlearn it as they grow. I never unlearned it. Or perhaps I relearned it later, after enough had been taken that standing still became possible again.

My life below the escarpment is thin. I mean this literally: I take up little space, need little, own less. I work when work is needed—mending, carrying, the kind of labor that requires hands and silence—and I disappear when it isn't. People in the valleys are used to me. They don't ask where I go. Some of them already know.

---

The climb takes most of a day. The path is more suggestion than trail, switching back through scrub that thins as the air thins, until there's nothing but rock and the pale sky pressing down. By the time I reach the first terrace, my body should be struggling. This is what bodies do at altitude. They protest. They demand.

Mine doesn't. This is the first wrongness, and it settles into me before the sound does.

---

The film finds me before I understand what it is.

I'm standing at the edge of a shelter—one of the dry-stacked ones, tucked into a hollow between two spires—and I notice that the wall facing me is darker than it should be. Amber-dark. Wet-dark. I step closer. The surface gleams. I reach out without deciding to.

My palm meets stone that should be cold and isn't.

The warmth rises through my hand and into my wrist, and somewhere in that moment, before I can pull back, something happens that I will spend the rest of my time here failing to describe. It isn't communication. It's simpler than that. It's the feeling of being seen by something that has no eyes.

---

I stay for three days. Or what feels like three days.

The sound—the Weeping—becomes a kind of weather. You don't listen to it after the first hours. You exist inside it. It occupies the same space as your thoughts, and eventually, your thoughts stop trying to compete. This is what I came for, I realize. Not the sound itself, but the quiet it makes possible.

I sleep in the shelter. The film lines its interior walls. I wake with my cheek against stone that breathes warmth.

I don't remember dreaming.

---

On the second day—or what I think is the second day—I build something.

I don't mean a structure. I mean a pattern. Flat stones lie scattered near the shelter's entrance, water-smoothed, and I find myself arranging them without intention. A line first. Then another line crossing it. Then a third, forming a triangle that isn't quite a triangle. The film thickens along the edges where I've placed my fingers.

I'm not making it. I'm finding it. The pattern was already there, waiting to be shown where it belonged.

---

The people who come here—I've seen no one else, but I know I'm not alone. The evidence is everywhere. Fresh ash in a fire ring. A chert tool left on a ledge, edge still sharp. A spiral of stones that wasn't there yesterday. We miss each other deliberately, I think. Or the plateau arranges it so. We are not meant to be here together. We are meant to be here alone, each of us a single point of attention feeding something that has no use for crowds.

Once, at dusk, I see a figure on a distant ridge. They're standing still, facing west, and the fog is rising around them. By the time I've crossed the distance, they're gone. Only the warmth on the stone marks where they stood.

---

What do I carry when I leave?

Nothing visible. The plateau doesn't give gifts. But there's a feeling that settles into the bone, a kind of resonance that takes weeks to fade. I find myself standing still at odd moments—mid-conversation, mid-task—and listening for something that isn't there. The valleys feel loud. The silence there is too simple. It doesn't hold anything.

I will come back. This isn't a decision. It's a recognition. The same recognition that brought me here the first time, operating at a frequency below choice.

---

Who am I in this world?

I am one of the listeners. One of the ones who climb because the sound exists and needs hearing. One of the components—willing, unwilling, I can't tell the difference—of a process that was running before I was born and will continue after I die. I don't know what the plateau is building. I don't think I'm building it either. But I think my standing there matters to it in some way I'll never understand, and that's enough. That's more than enough.

In the valleys, they call people like me *those who go up*. It's not a compliment. It's not an insult. It's simply what we are. We go up. We stand in the sound. We let the film warm our palms. We arrange stones in patterns we don't invent. We come down changed in ways we can't name.

And when we die—because we do die, eventually—something of us remains on the plateau. Not spirit. Not memory. Something simpler. The warmth the film learned from our bodies. The paths our hands traced on the stone. The attention we gave, for however long we gave it, to something that could not ask for it and did not need it but received it anyway.

That's what I am.

A receiver. A component. A listener.

And the Weeping continues.
