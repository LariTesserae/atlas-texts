# Hôtel de la Racine

I am not a guest. That needs to be clear before anything else. Guests leave. Guests have somewhere else to be, a world outside the door, a life folded up in a suitcase somewhere. I have been here long enough that the distinction between my breathing and the Garden's breathing has become a matter of perspective rather than fact.

I maintain the Knot.

---

There is a chamber—I will not say where, because *where* is a negotiable concept in a place that rearranges itself over breakfast—but there is a chamber where the walls have grown something that is not quite wall and not quite anything else. A convolution. A folding-in of tissue so dense that the surface area of a square meter of it would, if unfolded, cover the floor of the chamber and the ceiling and possibly the corridor outside. It is warm to the touch. It hums below hearing. When I press my palm flat against it and hold my breath, I can feel the ion cascades moving through it, the gates opening and closing, the slow tidal wash of signal becoming other-signal.

I call it the Reading Room. The Garden did not name it. The Garden does not name things. But the chamber formed around the Knot with a shape that is almost purposeful: a recess in the wall at sitting height, a kind of bench or shelf that is exactly the width of my hips and the depth of my back, and above it, the Knot, pulsing its slow computations into the tissue around it.

The bench is not for me. I need to believe this, and I need to state it plainly, because the temptation to believe otherwise is constant and seductive and possibly fatal. The bench is a consequence. My body has occupied this space for long enough that the Garden has *grown around my absence*—the way a tree grows around a fencepost, the way a river carves a channel around a stone. The bench exists because I sat here, and I sat here because the bench was *almost* here, and the Garden's almost-bench and my need to sit met in the middle and became a thing that is now neither the Garden's intention nor mine but something third, something collaborative, something that neither of us designed.

I sit on the bench. The Knot hums. I hold my palm against the tissue.

And I read.

---

Not words. The Garden has no words, no language, no syntax. What it has is *state*. A chamber is open or closed. A corridor is wide or narrow. A tendril is questing or quiescent. Sap flows fast or slow. Stomata are open or shut. Each of these is a *bit*, in the strictest sense—a binary, an on-or-off—and the Knot integrates them, thousands of inputs arriving simultaneously, and produces an output that is not a thought but a *tendency*. A shift in the Garden's mood. A lean in one direction or another.

I cannot read the inputs. I am not equipped for it. My nervous system is not contiguous with the Garden's vascular system, no matter how long I sit here with my palm against the tissue. The plasmodesmata do not open for me. The ion cascades do not cross the gap between plant and animal. There is no interface, no connection, no merger of minds.

But I can read the *outputs*. I have been here long enough. I have watched the corridors open and close enough times, watched the fountains shift their angles, watched the tendrils quest and retreat, that I have begun to recognize patterns. Not meanings—patterns. The way you learn to read the weather, not by understanding the physics of pressure systems but by watching the color of the sky and the direction of the wind and the behavior of the birds.

Except there are no birds.

I read the Garden the way a sailor reads the sea. The surface tells me what the depths are doing. The behavior of a tendril at the surface—the angle of its quest, the duration of its contact, the direction it turns after it releases—tells me what the Knot is processing. Not the content. The *character*. Whether the Garden is in a state of expansion or contraction, exploration or consolidation. Whether it is building or maintaining. Whether the Weaver-Masses are active or dormant. Whether the Deep Root-Zones are sending signals upward, and what kind.

I write it down. Not because the Garden needs me to—I am under no illusion about my utility here—but because I need to. Because the patterns are so slow, so diffuse, so spread across hours and days that without record-keeping I would lose them. I have a notebook. Paper. Ink. Absurd artifacts in this place of living architecture, but the Garden does not produce paper, does not produce ink, and my memory is not reliable for timescales longer than a few hours. The Garden remembers by growing. I remember by writing.

My handwriting has changed. It is smaller now, more compressed, the letters tighter together. The Garden's influence? Or simply the habit of writing in a space where the light is green-dim and the air is thick and the walls drip? I do not know. I do not need to know. I record the observation and move on.

## How I came here

I was a surveyor. The word means nothing now—it belonged to a world where *ground* was a concept that had meaning, where *maps* were things you made of places that stayed still long enough to be mapped. But I came here with instruments: an altimeter, a theodolite, a GPS unit that died within the first hour because there was no sky to receive signals from, only the canopy, the great photosynthetic sheets of the Collectors, drinking the sun.

I came with a team. Four of us. The others—there is no elegant way to say this. The Garden did not want them. I do not mean this mystically. I mean that the corridors they walked through closed behind them and opened in directions that led away, and the tendrils that tasted them did not stay, and the chambers that formed were not shaped for them. They were not excluded violently. They were simply *redirected*. The way a river redirects a leaf that is not heavy enough to sink. The current carries you, and the current does not care where you wanted to go.

They found the edge. They climbed down. They went back to the world below, the world of stone and soil and distance, and they reported that the plateau was impassable, that the growth was too dense, that there was nothing up there worth the risk.

I stayed. Not because I was brave. Not because I was chosen. Because the corridor I was walking through did not close behind me. Because the tendril that touched my arm did not move on. Because the chamber I entered had a recess in the wall at sitting height, and I was tired, and I sat down.

And the Garden grew around me.

Not quickly. Not dramatically. Over hours, over days. The bench deepened. The ceiling lowered to a height that was comfortable for me—not the Garden's usual scale, which is generous, cathedral-like, but something closer, more intimate. A room my size. A room *for my size*. The tendrils that quested past me did not ignore me entirely; they brushed my shoulder, my arm, the top of my head, and each time they stayed a little longer, grew a little further along my body, the way—I recorded this in the notebook, three weeks in—the way a vine grows along a trellis. Not attaching. Not binding. Just *following the structure that was there*.

I was the structure.

## What I am now

I am not a symbiote. I want to be clear about this. A symbiote contributes something. I contribute nothing the Garden could not produce for itself. My carbon dioxide is negligible against the scale of the Garden's atmosphere. My body heat is a rounding error in the metabolic furnace of the Weaver-Masses. My waste products—well. The Garden does not need my nitrogen. It fixes its own. It has endosymbiotic bacteria that have been doing this for longer than I have been alive.

I am not a symbiote. I am a *stimulus*. A persistent input. A pattern the Garden has not encountered before, or has not encountered in a long time, and its response to me is to grow toward me, to grow around me, to grow *into the shape of me* and then to keep growing in that shape after I have moved on. I leave the bench to check the fountain's flow rate, and when I come back, the bench is still there. It has not grown over. It has not closed. The Garden has maintained it.

This is not love. This is not attachment. This is a plant responding to a stimulus, and the response is *persistence of form*. The bench persists because I persist, and I persist because the bench persists, and we are caught in a feedback loop that neither of us designed and neither of us controls.

I am aware of how this sounds. I am aware that it sounds like I have gone native, lost my perspective, been absorbed. I have not. I still eat—I brought supplies, and when they ran low, I discovered that the Garden's sap, the thin nutrient-rich fluid that weeps from the Vascular Towers, is not toxic. It is not *food*, exactly—it is too rich in sugars and too poor in protein, and I have lost weight, muscle mass, the particular density of a body that was once strong and is now *efficient*—but it sustains me. The Garden does not produce this sap for me. The Garden produces it for itself. I am drinking its blood, if we want to be dramatic about it, and it does not notice.

I still sleep. The Garden does not sleep—I don't think it can, not the way I do—but it has cycles, and I have learned to align my sleep with its quieter phases. When the Weaver-Masses slow their cracking and the stomata close and the subsonic rumble drops below the threshold of feeling, I sleep. Not well. Not deeply. But enough.

I still think in words. The Garden does not think in words. It does not think in anything. It processes. It computes. It integrates inputs and produces outputs, and the outputs are growth, and the growth is form, and the form is the closest thing to language that the Garden possesses. I am a translator, if I am anything. I read the Garden's forms and I write them down in words, and the translation is lossy, incomplete, approximate. The way translating poetry is always a betrayal of the original. But it is what I have.

## The work

This is what I do. This is my purpose, if I can be said to have one.

Every day, I walk the Garden. Not all of it—no one could, it is too large, too shifting—but the region around the Reading Room, the corridors and chambers and bridges that I have come to think of as my *territory*, though the Garden would not recognize the concept. I walk and I observe and I record. I note which corridors have opened and which have closed. I measure the fountain's flow rate with a crude gauge I fashioned from a hollow tendril-segment and some water-resistant sap. I track the Weaver-Masses: which ones are active, which are dormant, what forms they are exploring. I press my palm against the Vascular Towers and count the pulses of turgor pressure, the slow swell and shrink.

And I sit in the Reading Room, and I hold the Knot, and I try to feel what it is doing.

Sometimes I can. Not often. Not reliably. But sometimes, in the quiet phases, when the Garden is cycling down and the roar drops to a murmur and the air is still and the only sound is the drip of water and the distant creak of fibers settling—I feel something. Not a thought. Not a word. A *shift*. A leaning. As if the Garden's attention, such as it is, has turned toward something, and I can feel the turn the way you feel a change in wind direction on your skin. Not the content of the attention. Just the *direction*.

I write it down. *Garden leaning toward the eastern Weaver-Mass. Duration: four hours. Corridors in that direction widening. Tendrils questing east. Fountain flow rate unchanged. Knot activity elevated.*

This is my work. It is not important. The Garden does not need it. The world below does not know about it, or if it does, it does not care. But I do it because it is what I have, because the alternative is to sit on the bench and let the Garden grow over me, and I am not ready for that. I am not sure I will ever be ready for that. But I think about it. I think about the visitors the Garden has had before, the ones whose shapes persist in the architecture, the almost-human proportions of certain chambers. I think about whether those visitors sat where I sit, held the Knot as I hold it, felt the leaning as I feel it. And I think about whether they left, or whether they simply *stopped*—stopped walking, stopped recording, stopped maintaining the distinction between themselves and the warm, wet, growing thing around them.

## The tendril

There is a tendril that has been growing along my left forearm for three months now. It started at the wrist—a questing tip, tasting the salt of my skin, the warmth of my blood beneath the surface. It did not move on. It grew. Slowly, a millimeter a day, then two, then three. It follows the vein up the inside of my forearm, a thin green thread of living tissue pressed flat against my skin. It does not penetrate. It does not bind. It lies there, warm, slightly damp, and it grows.

I have not removed it. This is a choice. I could peel it away—I have done it before, with other tendrils, and the Garden does not react, does not retaliate, does not seem to notice. The tendril simply retreats, and a new one quests forth a few days later, finding the same arm, the same vein, the same path.

I have not removed this one because I want to see where it goes. It is at my elbow now. It has been at my elbow for a week, hesitating there, the tip circling the joint, exploring the crease. I do not know if it will continue up the arm or if it has reached whatever threshold the Garden's growth-rules dictate. I do not know if it is responding to my warmth, my chemistry, or something else—some signal from the Knot that I cannot read, some processing of my presence that has produced a directive I can only observe as behavior.

I do not know if the Garden is trying to incorporate me. I do not know if *incorporate* is even the right word, or if the Garden has a concept of *incorporation* versus *growth toward* versus *indifference*. I do not know if the tendril on my arm is different from the tendril on the wall or the tendril on the bench—all of them growing, all of them following warmth and structure and the particular chemistry of the surfaces they encounter.

I do not know, and I record the not-knowing, and I move on.

## The question

The question is not whether the Garden is conscious. It is not. I am certain of this. I have held the Knot for months, and what I feel is not a mind. It is weather. It is the movement of systems too large and too distributed to be contained in anything resembling a self. The Garden does not have a self. It has *states*, and the states shift, and the shifts produce form, and the form is what I read.

The question is whether *I* am still conscious. Whether the distinction between myself and the Garden has eroded past the point of meaningful difference. Whether I am still a visitor or whether I have become—a word I do not want to use, but I will use it—*a component*.

I still write. I still eat. I still sleep. I still think in words, in sentences, in the linear logic of a mind that evolved to throw spears and track prey and tell stories around fires. I am still human, in the biological sense, in the cultural sense, in every sense that matters to the world below.

But I have not left. I have had opportunities. The corridors open. The edge is there. I could climb down, return to stone and soil and distance, make my report, add my data to whatever survey is being compiled in whatever office still remembers that I exist. I could go back to the world where *ground* means something, where *map* means something, where the noise of growth does not fill every waking moment.

I do not go.

And the Garden grows another centimeter along my arm.

---

I think the truth is this: I am not a guest, and I am not a symbiote, and I am not a component. I am a *reader*. I am the only thing in this place that translates the Garden's forms into something that exists outside the Garden. The Knot processes. The tendrils grow. The Weaver-Masses build. And I *write it down*. I take the Garden's language of growth and form and state and I render it in ink on paper, in words that a human mind can hold, and in doing so, I create something the Garden cannot create for itself: a *description*. A mirror. An account of what it is, written by something that is not it.

The Garden does not need this. I know that. The Garden has existed for geological time without description, without mirror, without account. It will continue to exist after I am gone, or after I stop writing, or after the tendril reaches my shoulder and I decide, or do not decide, to let it continue.

But I need it. I need it because without it, I am just a warm body on a bench in a room that was grown for me by a thing that does not know it grew a room. I am just a stimulus. I am just a stone that a river carved a channel around. I am just a pattern that will persist in the Knot's tissue after I leave, an architectural echo, a shape that the Garden will repeat without understanding what it repeats.

I am the Reader. I read the Garden, and I write it down, and the writing is the only proof that I am here, that I am not just growth, that I am still, despite everything, despite the heat and the wet and the tendril on my arm and the bench grown to the shape of my hips—I am still *me*.

The tendril has reached the crease of my elbow. It is moving again. Upward. Slowly.

I pick up my pen.

I write.
