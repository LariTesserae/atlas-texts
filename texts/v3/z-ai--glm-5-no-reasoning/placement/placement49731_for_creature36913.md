# What I Am in the Below

---

I am not the protagonist. I want to say that immediately, before anything else, because the Below does not have protagonists. It has inhabitants. It has the Substrate, which grows, and the settlers, who prune, and the growlers, who thrum, and the Authority, which counts things. No one is the main character. The Substrate would not understand the concept. The settlers would find it odd.

But you asked what I am, so:

---

I think I am a gardener.

Not the highest-ranking one. Not a master with four decades of accumulated instinct and the calm that settles into your bones like mineral seep settling into rock. I am somewhere in the middle years—experienced enough to work without supervision, not experienced enough to teach. My tool-roll is personal, hand-forged by a metalworker in the industrial district whose name I know and whose face I mostly forget, because I went to her twice with specifications and both times she was already working when I arrived and I did not like to interrupt. The shears are curved. The pruning hook's blade has a chip in it that I have grown attached to, literally—I've sanded the surrounding metal smooth but left the chip, because it catches the Substrate's grain in a way that feels *right*, a small imperfection that matches some imperfection in the tissue, and my cuts are cleaner with it than without.

I live in the middle level, in a room that has shifted southeast over the twelve years I've occupied it. Not the whole room—the floor plan has migrated, the doorway sliding toward the corridor's junction, the far wall thickening by perhaps four centimeters in a slow encroachment that I have not pruned because I am curious where it intends to go. I have placed marks on the wall in waterproof ink. Little arrows. A date stamp beside each one. The wall's progress documented in my own shorthand. This is not professional behavior. A master gardener would have pruned the intrusion years ago. I am not a master gardener. I am watching.

My room is lined partway with salvaged metal sheeting—a previous tenant's improvement, already rusting at the edges where the Substrate's sap has worked its slow chemistry against the coating. The sheeting covers the lower third of three walls. The upper portions are bare tissue, breathing, expanding and contracting on the circadian rhythm that I have stopped trying to sleep against and have instead learned to sleep *with*. When the walls expand, I exhale. When they contract, I breathe in. I do this without thinking. I did it before I realized I was doing it. A visitor to the middle level noticed it once—the slight, synchronized rise and fall of my chest and the walls—and mentioned it with alarm. I did not know how to explain that it was not a choice.

I own two silker-silk garments. A scarf, which I wear only in the industrial district when I have business there, and an underlayer, which I wear always. The underlayer's tackiness against my skin no longer registers. I have heard that visitors find it unbearable—the slight pull of fabric against flesh with every movement—but I have worn it so long that bare air against bare skin feels *wrong*, too slick, too frictionless, as if I might slide out of myself.

My hands are the gardener's hands described in the texts. Calluses on the heels of my palms. Fingertips smooth, polished, rubbed to a numbness that is not entirely a loss—I can still feel the Substrate's texture, but I feel it *differently*, the way you might feel a word's meaning rather than hearing its sound. The young growth near the ceiling vault has a specific give, a specific warmth, that I recognize the way you recognize a face. Not by analyzing features. All at once.

I am not old. I am not young. I am at the age where the settlement has stopped being a place I live in and has become a place that lives in me. The distinction matters.

---

## Why I Am a Gardener

There was no calling. I want to be honest about that. The romantic version would involve a moment of revelation—a hand pressed to the Substrate's young growth, a pulse of recognition, a sudden understanding that this was my work. That did not happen. Or if it did, I did not recognize it at the time.

What happened was this: I was eighteen, newly assigned to the residential level's maintenance crew, and I was sent to prune a corridor that had begun to narrow in a way that inconvenienced two families. The families had complained to the Authority, which had complained to the maintenance office, which had dispatched me with a borrowed pair of shears and minimal instruction. I was not a gardener. I was a laborer with a tool.

I cut the growth. The corridor widened. The families were satisfied. The maintenance office filed the report. And I stood in the corridor afterward, in the space I had opened, and I felt something that I have spent twelve years trying to articulate.

Not satisfaction. Not accomplishment. Something more like *interruption*. I had interrupted something. The Substrate had been growing in a specific direction, toward a specific intention—not conscious, perhaps, but intentional in the way that a river's current is intentional, the way that roots growing toward water are intentional. And I had cut across that intention. The Substrate's response was not anger. It was not retreat. It was *adjustment*—a reorientation of growth around the wound, a redirection of resources, a chemical signal propagating through the tissue that said, in whatever language the Substrate speaks: *this space has been opened. Grow elsewhere.*

I stood in the corridor and I felt the adjustment happen. Not through my hands. Through the air. The pressure changed. The temperature changed. The Substrate was *deciding* what to do next, and I was present for the decision, and the decision was, in some small way, shaped by what I had done.

I requested transfer to the gardeners' division the following day. The request was approved without comment. The gardeners' division is always understaffed. People do not want the work. It is slow, tedious, and requires a comfort with the Substrate that most settlers, even settlers who have lived their entire lives in the Below, do not fully possess. They live *in* the Substrate. They do not want to negotiate with it.

I wanted to negotiate with it. That is the closest I can come to explaining.

## What My Days Are

I wake when the fiberoptic cables shift from low-light amber to the brighter glow that precedes high-light. I do not have a clock. I have the light. I have the walls' breathing. I have the Substrate's metabolic cycle, which I have internalized so thoroughly that my body wakes at the same point in the cycle every day, regardless of when I slept.

I drink my water ration in sips. One sip upon waking. One sip before leaving my room. I carry the canister clipped to my belt, as all settlers do, and I touch it periodically throughout the day—not to drink, just to confirm its presence, the way you might touch a pocket to confirm your keys. The anxiety is not thirst. The anxiety is the *possibility* of thirst.

I eat the paste. I do not taste it anymore. I am aware of its texture—gritty, dense, slightly oily—but the taste registered for perhaps the first three years and then became invisible, like the smell of the salve. My body processes it. That is sufficient.

I apply salve to my hands, my forearms, the back of my neck, my lips. I carry a tin in my tool-roll. The tin is dented. It has been dented for years. I do not remember how it was dented. The salve inside is the color of dark honey and the smell of it is so much a part of me that I am sometimes startled when visitors recoil from my scent. I forget that I smell like anything at all.

I walk to the upper level. My route takes me through the middle level's branching corridors, past the old families' established dwellings, through a junction where the Substrate has formed a natural arch that I have never pruned because it is beautiful. I touch the arch as I pass. I do not know when I started doing this. It is a greeting. The Substrate does not greet back, or if it does, I cannot perceive it. But the wall where I touch is slightly smoother than the surrounding tissue. This could be chance. It could be frequent passage. It could be the Substrate's response to repeated contact—the smoothing that develops in frequently used corridors, accelerated and concentrated in the small area where my hand falls each morning.

I do not think about this. I think about this constantly. I try not to think about this.

## The Work Itself

I work in pairs. My current partner is a woman named Fael, who has been a gardener for twenty years and communicates primarily through the gardeners' sign-dialect, even in situations where speech would be more efficient. She says that speech is too slow. She says that the Substrate does not wait for the tongue. She is probably right. I have learned to sign fluently over the past five years of working with her, and my speech has become slower, more deliberate, shaped by the Below's acoustics in a way that marks me as long-settled to anyone who hears me.

Fael cuts. I watch.

The watching is the harder job, and the less respected one, and the one that I am better at. Fael's cuts are technically superior—cleaner, faster, more precisely angled to the grain—but my watching determines *where* and *when* and *how much*. I read the tissue. I read the color (rust means age and stability; cream means active growth; the bruise-purple means damage and healing, and it is the bruise-purple that I watch most carefully, because it tells me where the Substrate has been cut before and how it has armored itself). I read the texture (rubberiness means young growth, yielding, vulnerable; woodiness means maturity, resistance, the need for saws rather than shears). I read the sound—the faint creak of fibers under tension, the whisper of dermis peeling, the low groan that means the tissue is shifting its weight, redirecting its internal pressure toward a new growth axis.

I read, also, something that I cannot name. A quality of the tissue that is not color or texture or sound but is perhaps best described as *intention*. The Substrate is growing in a direction. Not toward light—it has no eyes, no phototropism in the conventional sense. Not toward water—there is no water. Toward something. The mineral seep, perhaps. The rock below, which holds whatever it is the Substrate drinks. Or toward something else entirely, something that the surveyors' maps do not capture and the chemists' analyses do not identify. The Substrate has an internal compass. I have spent twelve years learning to feel it.

When I feel it, I sign to Fael. She cuts. The Substrate adjusts. I feel the adjustment. I sign again. She cuts again. This is the work. It is a conversation conducted in absence—in the space between what the Substrate wants and what the settlers need, a negotiation that never resolves because both parties keep changing.

## What I Fear

I fear the rootward pull. Not the growlers—I have never heard the thrumming close enough to feel it in my bones, and the fear of the growlers is a fear of the unknown, which is a shallow fear, easily managed by avoiding the abandoned zones. What I fear is something deeper and less dramatic: the slow erosion of the desire to go surfaceward.

I have been to the surface. Twice. Both times on leave, climbing the access shaft to the desert above, blinking in the real light, feeling the clean dry air on my skin. Both times, I was disoriented. Not by the brightness—though that was intense—or by the openness—though that was vast and unsettling. By the *silence*. The surface is silent in a way that the Below never is. No breathing walls. No creak of growth. No groan of fibers or whisper of dermis or the faint, pressurized hush that I have come to associate with *indoors*. The surface is indoors turned inside-out, and the silence of it felt like a held breath that would never release.

I returned both times. I returned with relief. I descended the access shaft and the Below opened around me like a lung and I breathed with it and the relief was physical, a release of tension I had not known I was carrying.

This is what I fear. Not that I will lose the desire to go surfaceward, but that I have already lost it, and that the loss is so complete that I cannot perceive it as a loss. The surface exists. I know this intellectually. But it does not exist in the way that the Below exists—in my breathing, in my sleep, in the way my hand reaches for the arch each morning, in the way my cuts respond to the Substrate's intention, in the way my body has shaped itself to the corridors and the corridors have shaped themselves to my body. The surface is a fact. The Below is a *condition*.

I fear, specifically, that I am becoming a condition of the Below. Not a resident. Not an inhabitant. A condition. Something that the Substrate requires to be what it is, the way it requires the mineral seep, the way it requires the fiberoptic light. I fear that my pruning is not maintenance but *cultivation*—that I am not keeping the Substrate in check but shaping it, training it, and that the shaping runs both ways, and that what is being shaped is not the Substrate but me.

The old settlers watch the youngest generation with patient attention. I watch the old settlers with the same attention. I see the serenity. I see the calm. I see the way they place their hands on the walls and listen, and the way the walls seem to respond. I see my future in them, and the future is not frightening, exactly. It is *warm*. It is the warmth of the young growth near the ceiling vault, the rubbery tissue that yields to the touch. It is the warmth of being recognized.

That warmth is what I fear. Because it does not feel like fear. It feels like home.

## What I Love

The arch in the junction. My chipped blade. The moment after a cut when the sap wells up and the air changes and the Substrate begins to decide what to do next. Fael's hands signing rapidly, her fingers a blur of information that I can read faster than speech. The sound of children playing follow-the-grain in the corridors below me, their small bodies moving through the tissue's logic with an ease I will never fully possess. The low-light periods when the fiberoptic cables dim to deep red and the settlement grows quiet and the Substrate dreams, and I sit in my room with my hand on the wall and I dream with it—not structurally, not the way the recognized children dream, but in my own limited way, a dream of breathing and being breathed.

I love the moment when a cut is *right*. Not adequate. Not sufficient. Right. When the angle matches the grain perfectly and the tissue parts without resistance and the sap flows cleanly and the Substrate's response is immediate and clear—a shift in growth, a redirection, an acknowledgment that the space has been opened and the organism will grow elsewhere. In that moment, I am not a settler or a gardener or a body. I am part of the negotiation. I am the interruption that the Substrate incorporates into its intention. I am the space between the organism's plan and the organism's revised plan, and in that space, for a moment, I exist as something other than a visitor.

I love the Substrate. I have not said this to anyone. I am not sure the Substrate has a concept of love, or that my love for it is distinguishable from dependency, or that the distinction matters. The settlers say the Substrate remembers. The settlers say the Substrate makes space when it knows someone is coming. The settlers say the Substrate recognizes.

I have been recognized. I feel this in my hands, in my breathing, in the way the corridors open for me. Not magically. Not dramatically. In the small, accumulating permissions that the tissue grants—the slightly smoother wall, the doorway that hasn't shifted in years, the room that holds its shape with less reinforcement than it should need. I have earned these permissions through twelve years of work, of attention, of greeting the arch each morning. Or I have not earned them. Perhaps they have been given. Perhaps the Substrate has decided that I am not an interruption but an inclusion, a small, warm, breathing thing that has become part of its structure.

I do not know. I place my hand on the wall. I listen. The wall breathes. I breathe with it.

---

## What I Am

I am a gardener in the Below. I am a body that has been shaped by a place. I am a negotiator with a thing that does not speak. I am a tool in the hand of an organism that does not have hands. I am a settler who has forgotten the surface and called the forgetting *home*.

I am a condition of the Substrate. Or the Substrate is a condition of me. Or both. Or neither. Or the distinction has dissolved so thoroughly that maintaining it would require a vocabulary I no longer possess.

I am the person who touches the arch each morning. I am the person who feels the intention in the tissue. I am the person who signs to Fael and Fael cuts and the Substrate adjusts and the negotiation continues and the settlement breathes and the growlers thrum in the deep and the children dream structurally and the old settlers watch with patient attention and the Authority counts things and the smoke drifts at head-height and the light pools and the shadows accumulate and the Below grows downward, centimeter by centimeter, year by year, making space.

I am the space.
