# What I Found There

I am the wrong shape for this place, and the place knows it.

I arrived the harder way—through the fissures from above, not the waterways from below. The karst plateau had been my companion for nine days: a howling, exposed moonscape of dissolving limestone where nothing grows taller than my boot sole and the wind tastes of mineral dust and ancient salt. I was following a spring that vanished underground. Not deliberately—I mean I wasn't *looking* for the spring, wasn't pursuing it as a quest or a calling. I simply noticed that every spring I'd found on the plateau for the past four days flowed in the same direction before going to ground, and something in me—the part that has always been a poor judge of self-preservation—wanted to know where they went.

The fissure that took me was barely wide enough for my shoulders. I turned sideways and scissored downward, pack catching, releasing, catching again, my breath loud against the stone in a way that felt rude. The light failed gradually—not a sudden darkening but a slow subtraction of certainty. I have been in caves before. This was not the shock of headlights-into-abyss. It was the gentle dimming of a room where someone is slowly turning down a lamp because they think you might like to rest.

Then the air changed. It thickened. I felt it in my chest before I felt it on my skin—a weight, a density, the sense of breathing through wet cloth. And then I heard it.

Not water falling. Water *rising*.

The sound entered me before my ears could parse it. A low boom, almost subsonal, that I felt in my sternum and the roots of my teeth. Then layers above it: a hiss, a whisper, a patter like rain on a thousand surfaces at once. I stopped moving. I stopped, and I pressed my face against the damp stone, and I listened to something I had no framework for.

When I emerged—sliding sideways down a last narrow chute, scraping skin from my elbow, landing on something soft and yielding that was not stone—I found myself in the blue-white half-light of Upper Vell, kneeling in moss up to my wrists, water falling upward in silver columns around me, and I understood that I had entered a place that did not need me to understand it.

---

I am tall. This is the first thing that marks me here, and it marks me constantly. My limbs are long, my bones are narrow, my body is built for covering ground—for striding across plateaus, for climbing ridgelines, for the long horizontal travel that the surface rewards. I have walked two thousand kilometers in the past year. I measure distance in days of footfall. Everything about my body speaks of movement, of passage, of the space between places.

Upper Vell does not reward this body. It tolerates it.

For the first three days, I am simply cold. The kind of cold that sits in your joints and whispers *you do not belong here*. I shiver in a way I have never shivered—not the violent, productive shivering of a body generating heat, but a slow, persistent trembling that seems to come from somewhere deeper than muscle. My fingers stiffen. My jaw aches. I huddle in the dwelling they have given me—given me, mind you, not rented or assigned or grudgingly conceded, but *given*, the way you give a cutting to someone who wants to grow something—and I feel the walls breathing around me, the moss contracting slightly in the cooler air, and I think: *this building is alive, and I am the only dead thing in it*.

They watch me, the people. Not obviously. Not with the staring curiosity that I would expect from an isolated community encountering a stranger. They simply... orient toward me. A slight turn of the head here. A pause in a gesture there. They are reading me the way they read the birds—passively, patiently, without urgency. They will know what I am when I show them. They have time.

I have never in my life felt so observed by people who were not looking at me.

---

There is one I notice first. Not because they are louder or brighter or more central—nothing here is louder or brighter or more central than anything else. I notice them because of their hands.

They are working on something—a membrane of moss, scraping it thin against a flat stone with a flake of chert. The motion is repetitive, meditative, the kind of task that requires attention but not thought. Their hands move in it the way a musician's hands move in a piece they have played a thousand times: from somewhere deeper than intention.

The fingers are long. The palms are broad. But the fingertips—when I get close enough to see, sitting on a moss hummock at what I hope is a respectful distance—the fingertips are smooth. Not worn, not damaged. *Polished*. As if decades of contact with living things have gently, patiently erased the ridges that the surface world uses to identify its inhabitants. I look at my own hands, calloused from trekking poles, the fingerprints still bold and assertive, and I feel something I don't have a word for. Not shame. Recognition of difference, maybe. The understanding that these hands and my hands have touched different things, and that the things they have touched have changed them more than they have changed the things.

They look up. Their eyes are pale gray, the color of the light that filters down through the fissures. The pupils are large, open, taking in everything. They do not smile—I have learned by now that smiling is not the currency of welcome here—but something in their face shifts. A softening, maybe. A readiness.

They make a sound. It is not speech. It is something between a click and a chirp, produced somewhere in the back of the throat, and it carries a meaning I cannot parse. A moment later, a swallow descends from the upper fissures—brown, pale-bellied, moving with the quick precision of something that has evolved in a space where every surface is wet—and lands on the moss beside the one with the smooth hands. It tilts its head. It makes a sound back.

They converse.

I sit on the hummock and watch, and I feel the mist settle on my skin, and I think: *I have walked two thousand kilometers to arrive at a place where a person is having a conversation with a bird, and the bird is answering*.

---

Days pass. Or what I think are days. The light dims and brightens in a rhythm that might correspond to the sun far above, or might correspond to something else entirely—clouds over the plateau, water shifting in the fissures, some atmospheric pressure system I have no way to measure. I sleep when the others sleep. I eat what they offer me: pale fungi with a flavor like walnuts and rain, dense tubers that crumble soft and warm in the mouth, small blind fish that taste of clean stone and dark water. I drink from the collection basins where the upward-falling water accumulates before descending again as mist. The water tastes of nothing. It tastes of *absence*—the absence of mineral, of contamination, of everything that water usually carries with it. It is the cleanest thing I have ever put in my body.

I learn the sounds. Not quickly—I am not built for quick learning, not here—but I begin to hear patterns. The boom of the water columns has a rhythm: a longer pause, then a shorter one, then two quick beats, then a pause that lasts longer still. I wonder if this means something. I wonder if I am the sort of person who would recognize meaning if I heard it, or if I am only the sort of person who would recognize patterns where none exist.

The birds teach me more than the people do, in those first weeks. Not directly—they do not sit me down and instruct him in their calls. But I hear them, constantly, and I begin to notice that certain calls precede certain events. A rapid, high chirping means the water flow is increasing. A long, descending whistle means someone is approaching the chamber through the lower channels. A series of short, soft clicks—the call the smooth-handed one made that first day—means *this one is here, this one is present, this one is known*.

I try, one evening, to make the clicking sound. I am alone in my dwelling, or as alone as anyone is in Upper Vell—the moss walls are thin enough to hear through, and the dwelling next to mine is occupied by an older person whose breathing I have begun to recognize. I sit in the blue-white half-light and I make the sound in the back of my throat, and it comes out wrong, too flat, too human. I try again. Closer. Again. The moss hums faintly around me. I do not know if it is responding or if I am imagining it.

I stop. I breathe. The air is thick with water.

I realize that I am doing what I have always done: trying. Throwing myself at something, attempting mastery, expecting to improve through repetition. It is how I have lived my whole life—foot after foot, kilometer after kilometer, peak after peak. Forward motion. Improvement. Progress.

Upper Vell does not believe in progress. Upper Vell believes in growth, which is different. Growth happens at the pace of the thing that is growing. You cannot rush it. You can only tend it.

I sit with this realization for a long time. The dwelling breathes around me. Somewhere above, the swallows call in patterns I cannot yet read.

---

I meet the one with the smooth hands again, in the central clearing. The bare stone feels strange under my feet after weeks of moss—I can feel the cold radiating up through my soles, the ancient density of the limestone. We are both there for the evening gathering, though *evening* is a word that means less here than anywhere I have been.

They look at me. Their pale gray eyes hold mine. And then they speak—not to me, not to the group, but toward me, as if I were overhearing something.

"You move too fast."

It is the longest sentence anyone has spoken to me in weeks. The words are soft, barely above the noise of the water, and they carry no judgment. They carry an observation. A reading.

I want to protest. I want to explain that I have been trying to slow down, that I have been learning to sit still, that I have been listening to the birds. But the protest dies in my throat because I know, with the sudden clarity of cold water, that it is true. I move too fast. My body still carries the momentum of two thousand kilometers. My mind still races ahead of my senses, parsing, categorizing, filing. I am still, after weeks, *passing through*.

I nod. The one with the smooth hands nods back. Something passes between us—not warmth, not friendship, nothing so definite. A recognition. An acknowledgment that I have been seen.

I feel, for the first time since arriving, that I am not invisible.

---

The weeks fold into a shape I cannot call time.

I help with the tending. This is what they call their work—tending. They tend the moss beds, loosening the substrate with bone tools, introducing water where it pools too thickly, thinning the fungi where they crowd each other. They tend the dwellings, pressing fresh moss into gaps in the walls, training the woody stems in directions that serve the structure's needs. They tend the water channels, clearing the small fissures where flow has slowed, redirecting the streams where the moss has grown too close to the edges.

I am bad at all of it. My hands are too rough, too fast, too certain. I pull when I should press. I shape when I should guide. I want to *fix* things—to see a problem and solve it, to make the crooked straight and the broken whole. But tending is not fixing. Tending is a conversation between your hands and something alive, and the alive thing sets the pace.

The one with the smooth hands works beside me sometimes. Not always—they have their own tending, their own relationships with the chamber and its inhabitants. But occasionally our paths cross, and we work side by side in silence, and I learn more from watching their hands than from anything I have been told.

They touch the moss the way you'd touch a sleeping child: with the full attention of someone who knows that the touch itself communicates. They do not impose. They suggest. The moss responds, growing toward their fingers, thickening where they press, thinning where they pull back. I watch this, and I feel something in my own hands shift—not physically, but in intention. My hands want to do what their hands do. My hands want to *tend*.

But I am not there yet. I may never be there. And I am beginning, slowly, to understand that this is not a failure. It is simply a fact. I am a different organism. I was grown in different soil, under a different sun, with different water. I cannot become them. I can only become more fully whatever I am.

---

One morning—*morning*—I wake up and realize that I have stopped measuring time in distance.

This happens without announcement. It is not a revelation. It is more like the moment when a sore muscle finally relaxes: you don't notice the transition, you only notice the absence of pain. I am sitting in my dwelling, running my fingers over the moss wall, and I realize that I am not thinking about where I will go next. I am not thinking about going at all.

This terrifies me.

For three years, I have been a body in motion. I left a home—such as it was, such as I had made it—and I walked, and the walking was the purpose and the destination and the meaning all at once. I walked because walking was the only thing that felt true. I walked because when I was walking, I didn't have to be anything other than a body covering ground. I walked because the alternative was stillness, and stillness meant thinking, and thinking meant...

I stand up. The dwelling sways faintly around me, the living walls adjusting to my movement. My heart is beating too fast for Upper Vell. The birds are calling in the upper fissures, and I cannot read them. The mist falls on my face, and I am shaking, and I do not know if it is the cold or something else.

The one with the smooth hands is outside. I do not know if they were already there or if they came when they heard me move. They are sitting on a moss hummock, doing nothing. Being. I stand in the doorway of my dwelling and I look at them and I try to say something and what comes out is:

"I don't know who I am if I'm not going somewhere."

They look at me with their pale gray eyes. They do not answer immediately. They sit with my words the way they sit with the moss—patiently, attentively, without any urgency to shape them into something.

Then they say: "You are here."

It is not an answer. It is not wisdom. It is a statement of fact, offered without weight, without significance, without the expectation that it will change anything. It is simply true: I am here. In this moment, in this chamber, in this body, in this light, in this air that is half water—I am here.

I sit down beside them. The moss yields under me, soft and damp. We sit. The swallows call. The water rises.

I am here.

---

I will not stay in Upper Vell.

I know this the way I know my own stride length—intuitively, bodily, without need for argument. I am not built for this place. My bones are too narrow, my rhythms too quick, my hunger for movement too deep. The people here have been grown by this chamber the way the moss has been grown by its water—slowly, over generations, shaped by conditions that I can admire but cannot inhabit. I am a visitor. I will always be a visitor, even if I stayed for fifty years.

But I will not leave the way I arrived.

I came through the fissures above, scrambling downward with the momentum of a body accustomed to passage. I was looking for where the water went. I was following the spring to its destination. I was, in every sense, *going somewhere*, even when I didn't know where.

When I leave, I will leave through the lower channels. The water goes down, eventually, into the deep caves, into the places with no names. I will follow it the way I followed it on the plateau—not because I am looking for something, but because the water knows where it is going, and I do not, and for once that feels like enough.

But first, I sit.

I sit in the central clearing, on the bare stone, and I feel the cold enter me. I listen to the birds. I taste the mist. I run my fingers over the bare limestone and feel its ancient, patient surface, worn smooth by millennia of water that moves in ways I cannot explain. I breathe the air that is almost water. I am still, for what may be the first time in my life, and the stillness is not emptiness. The stillness is full. It is full of the sound of water rising and plants growing and birds speaking in a language I am only beginning to hear.

The one with the smooth hands passes by. They pause. They make the clicking sound—the one that means *this one is here, this one is present, this one is known*—and a swallow descends from the fissures and lands on the moss beside me. It tilts its head. It looks at me with its small, dark eyes. It makes a sound back.

I am here. I am known. I am leaving.

All of these things are true at the same time.

---

When I go—slipping into the lower water channel, feeling the cold close over my shoulders, following the current into the dark—I carry with me the touch of smooth fingertips on moss, the taste of clean water, the sound of birds speaking in patterns that mean *present, present, known*. I carry the memory of a place that did not need me to stay in order to change me.

I do not know where the water goes. I do not know what I will find in the deep places, in the chambers without names, in the dark that no light reaches. I do not know if I will emerge anywhere, or if the water will simply carry me down and down until I become part of it.

But I know that I was in Upper Vell. I know that I sat still there, for a while, in a dwelling that was alive and a light that was borrowed and a mist that fell from water that rose. I know that I was seen by people who do not use names and heard by birds who do not need them.

And I know—I *know*, in my smooth-polished bones—that the water rises. The plants grow. The stone endures.

And I endure with it, for as long as I am able, in whatever form that takes, wherever the water carries me next.
