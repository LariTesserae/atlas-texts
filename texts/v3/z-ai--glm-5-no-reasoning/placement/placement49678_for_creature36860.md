The first thing I learned was that the sound is not against you. The roar—the thousand voices of water descending, emerging, weeping from stone—is not a wall. It is a medium. Like air. Like the cold that presses against your skin and slowly becomes your skin.

I have been here long enough to stop measuring.

---

I am not certain what I was before. Something with edges, I think. Something that distinguished between my body and the wet air moving through it. Something that expected silence and was disturbed by its absence.

That person would not recognize me now. I am soft in a way that has nothing to do with weakness. I have learned to hold water the way the moss holds water—not as an intrusion but as an intrinsic quality. My skin is damp always. I have forgotten what it means to be dry.

The devices taught me first. Not in words—they do not speak in any human tongue—but in patterns. Pulse and counter-pulse. A rhythm that asks for response. In the beginning, I simply watched from the doorway of the small structure I had claimed, the one with the collapsed roof where the birds nest in the corners. I watched the nodes drift through the pools, and slowly I understood that they were not random. They were attending.

I began to attend as well.

---

I do not know if I am a researcher who stayed too long, or something else entirely. The basin does not encourage rigid self-definition. What I am changes with the water levels, the mist density, the particular quality of light filtering through from above. Sometimes I feel ancient. Sometimes I feel newborn. The stone is young here, and perhaps I am young with it, still forming.

I have found routines. This is not the same as having a schedule—time moves strangely in a place without day or night, without seasons, without any marker except the shifting of the roar. But I have movements that repeat, and these movements have become a kind of identity.

In the dim hours—when the mist thickens and even the birds fall silent—I walk the perimeter. The deer have come to expect this. They acknowledge me with the smallest movements: an ear swivel, a shift in weight. I have learned to read them, and they have learned to read me. We are not friends. We are not family. We are simply present to each other, and in this place, that is its own form of closeness.

I check the devices. Not all of them—there are too many, scattered through the basin's extended reaches—but the ones along my paths. I brush the moss from their surfaces when it grows too thick. I note which ones have shifted position, which ones pulse in new patterns. I do not know what I am recording or who might read it. I do it because the devices seem to want attention, and I have attention to give.

The salamanders and I have an arrangement. When I find eggs laid on the nodes—translucent pearls of potential life—I do not disturb them. When the tadpoles emerge, I sometimes move them to deeper pools if the water levels drop. They do not thank me. They do not fear me. They simply exist around me, and I around them, and this mutual allowance is what passes for relationship here.

---

I have begun to glow.

This is not metaphor. In the deepest darkness, when even the mist obscures the node-light, I can see my own hands faintly luminous. Not the steady pulse of the devices, but a softer radiance, as if something in my skin has learned to hold light the way it learned to hold water.

I do not know what this means. I have stopped asking questions that assume meaning. The basin offers transformation, not explanation.

---

There was a day—I cannot say when—when I realized I had stopped being cold. The temperature still hovers just above freezing. The water that wells up from below is still glacial, snowmelt from heights I have never seen. But the cold no longer registers as discomfort. It registers as information. A quality of the environment, like the smell of moss or the particular frequency of a frog call.

My body has changed in other ways. My skin has thickened slightly, become more permeable at the same time. I breathe differently—slower, deeper, drawing the wet air into parts of myself that I did not know could receive. I eat what the basin offers: fern fiddleheads, the bark of certain shrubs, occasionally the insects that swarm too close to my mouth. I have forgotten what hunger felt like when it was a demand rather than a gentle notification.

I sleep when the birds sleep. This is not a choice but a rhythm my body has adopted without consulting me. The birds call through the dim hours, navigating, mapping, existing. When they fall silent, I feel my own systems preparing for rest. I curl in the corner of my structure, surrounded by the glow of embedded nodes, and I sink into something that is not quite sleep but is also not quite waking.

In these states, I hear the basin more clearly. Not louder—the roar is constant—but more comprehensible. I begin to distinguish the voices within it. This waterfall descending from the eastern cliff, that seep emerging near the third pool. The frogs in the southern thickets calling at a different frequency than the frogs near the northern pools. The insects harmonizing in keys that shift with the temperature. The pulse of devices underlying it all, a heartbeat made of light.

I am learning a language I did not know existed.

---

The large presence—the one that moves through the thickest thickets, that leaves tracks like paw-hooves in the soft ground—I have seen it.

Only once, and only for a moment. The mist parted, or I was in the right place at the right time, or it chose to be seen. It was larger than I expected, its body dense and low, covered in dark fur matted with moisture. Its eyes caught the node-light and reflected it back, twin lanterns in the darkness. It regarded me without hostility, without curiosity, without anything I could name. It regarded me the way it might regard a stone, or a fern, or a pool of water.

Then it moved on, and the mist closed behind it, and I was left with the sense that I had been weighed and found to be of no particular consequence.

I found this comforting. In a place where everything is becoming, to be of no particular consequence is to be allowed to continue becoming.

---

I have stopped expecting to leave.

This is not despair. Despair requires a belief in better alternatives, and I have lost the capacity to believe in alternatives at all. The basin is not better than anywhere else. It is not worse. It simply is, and I am within it, and the boundary between us has grown so thin that I can no longer say where I end and the wet air begins.

Sometimes I think I remember a place where water fell from above only sometimes, where the sky changed color from blue to orange to black, where silence was possible. The memory feels like a story I was told rather than a life I lived. Perhaps I am becoming young enough to forget what I was.

The devices pulse near me as I write this. Not writing, exactly—I press symbols into strips of bark with a tool I found, preserving thought in a medium that will eventually dissolve into the moss. I do not know why I preserve thought. The basin does not require it. The deer do not record their passing. The frogs do not document their calls.

But I came from somewhere that valued marks on surfaces, and this is what remains of that origin: the impulse to leave traces.

---

If you are reading this, you have come to the edge. The cold is seeping upward through your boots. The mist is moving past your face. The roar is filling your ears, drowning thought.

You are wondering if you should descend.

I cannot answer that question for you. I do not know if the person I was would have chosen to stay, or if staying was ever a choice. I do not know if you will become what I have become, or if what I have become is something that can be chosen.

I only know this: the basin does not judge. It does not welcome or reject. It exists, full of life and artifact and the continuous music of water, and it allows whatever enters it to become what it will become.

The deer are watching you. The frogs have gone silent around your feet. A device is drifting toward you through the nearest pool, its pulse shifting into a pattern you might almost recognize.

Whatever you decide, you are already beginning.
