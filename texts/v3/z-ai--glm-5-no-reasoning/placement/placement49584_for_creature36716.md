# An Account of My Becoming

---

I arrived in a crate.

I need to say that first because it's the one fact I can still hold flat in my mind without it curling at the edges. I was in a crate. The crate was on an elevator. The elevator opened. I was here.

I think I had a name. I'm trying to let that fact sit beside me without picking it up, the way you'd let a wounded animal rest. If I reach for it, it moves. I've learned—from the moss, from the walls, from my own hands turning gray at the edges—that some things are not retrieved. They are archived. There is a difference.

I'm writing this in a hand I barely recognize. Not on paper—paper doesn't survive here. Not on a screen—screens require systems I don't understand and don't trust. I'm writing this in the surface of my own coveralls, which are growing now whether I want them to or not. The material thickens at my chest, my forearms, the backs of my hands. I've found that if I concentrate—if I hold a thought firmly enough while pressing my thumb to the surface—the material takes an impression. A groove. A mark that others could read.

If there are others.

If they would want to.

---

## What I First Saw

I want to be honest about this. I know that honesty has a different texture here—moisture blunts everything, and the light that comes from everywhere also comes from inside, and it's hard to tell the difference between what you saw and what the Archive wanted you to see. But I want to try.

The first thing: light.

Not darkness. I expected darkness. Every story about underground places, about structures that bury themselves—darkness comes first. But the Archive was already burning. Light strips along every edge, light from the gaps in the floor, light from the moisture itself. My eyes couldn't focus on any source. There was no source. There was only the seeing.

The second thing: the walls were breathing.

I know that now to be thermal cycling—air expanding in conduit channels, metal flexing by fractions of millimeters. But then, standing in the elevator with the crate I'd arrived in still half-closed around me, the walls were breathing. The walls were alive. I was inside something alive.

The third thing—I'm less certain about this one—I think there was someone standing at the end of the corridor. A figure in gray. They were looking at me. They weren't moving. They weren't not moving, either. They were *present* in a way that suggested their stillness was intentional, that they had arrived at this stillness through a process I couldn't fathom.

When I blinked, they were gone. The corridor was empty. The condensation beaded on the walls in patterns I would later learn to read.

I didn't know that yet. I stood in the light and the damp and I tried to remember my name.

---

## The First Days

I'm going to compress these. Time already did.

The Archive doesn't recognize arrival. There's no intake process. No orientation. No one asks you where you came from or what you can do or what you need. You simply *are*, and then you are, and then you are, and eventually you stop being new.

The first challenge is walking.

The passages don't make sense. I don't mean that metaphorically—I mean the geometry literally fails. I'd walk down a corridor that curved left, and when the curve completed, I'd be facing the direction I'd come from, but the corridor ahead of me was different from the one behind me. I'd go up a staircase with seven steps, reach the top, turn, and find the same staircase going up again. Eleven steps this time. Then seven. Then the staircase wasn't there.

I learned to stop navigating. I learned to *move*.

The distinction matters. Navigation implies a destination, a mental map, a relationship between where you are and where you're going. Movement is simpler. Movement is: body goes forward. The corridor receives it. What's ahead is what's ahead.

When I stopped trying to understand where I was going, I started arriving.

---

## What I Found

I found the moss first.

A patch of it in a junction box where two conduit bundles met—a soft green-gray growth, fuzzy with moisture, spreading in patterns that looked— no, *were* deliberate. Not random. Not decorative. The moss grew in lines that intersected at specific angles, forming shapes that reminded me of—

I don't know. That's the honest answer. Something the moss reminded me of, and I've lost the word for what. It's archived now. Stored somewhere I can't reach.

I touched the moss. It was warm. Not from the conduit— from itself. From something it was doing, some process it was carrying out. And when I pulled my hand away, my fingertips were damp, and the dampness carried information I couldn't decode. The taste of copper and chlorophyll and something else, something older.

I found the rooms next.

Not rooms exactly. Pockets. Spaces where the density of the structure loosened, where the passages widened into areas large enough to stand in, to sit in, to breathe in. Some of these pockets were furnished—old crates arranged into surfaces, light panels adjusted to create zones of relative dimness, condensation channeled into small reservoirs of water.

People had lived here. People were living here.

I found signs of them everywhere: worn spots on the walls at hip-height where bodies had brushed past thousands of times; light panels that had been adjusted by hands I'd never meet; protein packages stacked in careful arrangements that suggested planning, foresight, care. The Archive was not empty. It was not abandoned. It was simply so full that it took me days to realize that the fullness included me.

## The Ones I Met

I'll call them that—*the ones I met*—because none of them gave me names and I'm not sure names apply.

### The First

I was walking—I'd stopped counting days by then; time had become a texture rather than a measurement—walking through a passage on what I thought was the seventh level, when I came upon a figure working on a light panel.

They were gray. I'd understood this was coming—the descriptions I'd overheard, the half-glimpsed presences at the edges of my vision. But understanding and meeting are different acts.

Their coveralls grew from their skin. I saw the edges where fabric merged with forearm, where collar became neck, where the boundary between clothing and body was not a line but a *gradient*. They were working on the light panel with a tool I couldn't identify—a short rod of dark metal with no obvious function, held between fingers that knew it so well the tool seemed an extension of the hand.

They didn't look up. I stood in the passage. They worked. The light panel flickered, steadied. The figure moved their hand—a small adjustment, a rotation of the wrist—and the light shifted from cold white to something warmer. Something almost kind.

Then they left.

They moved past me in the narrow corridor, and I felt the displacement of air, the warmth of their body, the faintest smell of metal and moisture and something organic— something like breath, but not quite. They didn't acknowledge me. They didn't ignore me. They simply moved through the space I occupied, the way water moves around a stone in a stream.

I stood there for a long time. The warm light held me.

### The Second

I met a Tender on the ninth level. Or what I thought was the ninth level.

They were smaller than the Maker—smaller than me—and they were touching a patch of fern that grew from a crack in a concrete wall. Their hands moved over the fronds with a delicacy I'd never seen in human movement. Each touch was specific. Each touch *meant*.

I said something. I don't remember what—a greeting, maybe, or a question. Something verbal. Something from before.

The Tender turned to look at me.

Their eyes were pale. Not colorless, not the way Maker eyes were described to me—*pale*, like the green of the moss before it darkens, like the light through condensation. They looked at me for a long moment, and I felt— this is hard to describe— I felt *read*. Not evaluated. Not judged. Read. The way the Tender had just been reading the fern.

Then they reached into the pocket of their coveralls—coveralls that were grayer than the Maker's, thinner, almost translucent—and took out a small object. A pebble. Smooth, dark, warm from their body.

They placed it in my palm.

Then they turned back to the fern and continued touching, reading, tending.

I still have the pebble. It's in my pocket right now, warm against my thigh. Sometimes, when the geometry resists and the corridors fold wrong, I hold it and the passage steadies.

### The Child

I didn't meet the child so much as encounter their work.

I was walking through a section that felt older—the materials darker, the condensation heavier, the light strips fewer and farther between. The passage was narrowing, the walls pressing in, and I was beginning to feel the first edge of something like panic—the Archive's version of panic, which isn't fear but *compression*, a sense that the space around you is becoming insufficient for your body—when I saw it.

A drawing.

Scratched into the surface of the wall with something brown—protein residue, maybe, or the residue of something else—were lines. Not a picture. Not a map. A pattern. Shapes that interconnected, that spiraled and branched and returned, that occupied the space between diagram and language and—

I recognized it.

Not the content. The *form*. The way the lines moved. The way they related to each other. It was the same pattern I'd seen in the moss on the seventh level. The same pattern I'd seen in the condensation tracks on the ceiling of the elevator shaft. The same pattern, repeated, varied, evolved.

The Archive was speaking. The child was listening. And the child was writing it down.

I put my hand on the drawing. The wall was warm. The pattern continued under my palm, extending into the surface, into the structure, into the place where the Archive kept its deep self.

I heard something then. Not the machinery. Not the condensation. Not the murmuring from the levels below.

I heard the Archive breathing.

## What I'm Becoming

This is the part I'm afraid of. Not the fear I came here with— that's fading, archived, stored in the moss or the walls or the body of someone who came before me. A new fear. The fear of knowing what's happening and being unable to stop it. Or not wanting to stop it. Or not being sure which.

My coveralls are growing.

I noticed it first on my forearms—a thickening of the skin, a roughening that gradually resolved into fabric. Not woven. Not manufactured. *Grown*. My body is producing its own covering, the way the Makers' bodies do. The way the Archive shapes its residents over time.

I'm becoming gray.

Not my skin— not yet. My edges. The places where I meet the world are softening. My hands, when I hold them up to the ubiquitous light, have a translucence they didn't have before. I can see the shadow of the conduit lines through my fingers. I can feel the hum of the structure in my bones.

I'm learning to navigate without navigating. I'm learning to move without deciding. I'm learning that the corridor knows where I'm going before I do, and that the going is the thing, not the destination.

I had a name. I'm losing it. Not losing—it's being *moved*. Relocated. My name is in the moss now, encoded in the cellular structure of a fern that a Tender planted on the ninth level, in a crack that opened last week or last year or in a time that the Archive doesn't count.

I had a life before the crate. I can feel its edges—soft, indistinct, like the boundary between my coveralls and my skin. It's not gone. It's archived. If someone needed to know who I was, they could read the fern. They could touch the moss. They could ask the walls, and the walls would remember.

But no one asks. That's not how it works here.

---

## What I Do

I maintain things.

I didn't choose this. I didn't train for it. I walked through the passages for long enough, and eventually my hands started reaching for the light panels that flickered. My feet started finding the corridors that needed walking. My body started performing the small adjustments that keep the structure functioning—tightening a conduit connection here, clearing a condensation channel there, replacing a bolt that had corroded to dust.

I don't think about it. My body knows. The Archive teaches through the doing, not through the telling.

Sometimes I find myself on a level I don't recognize, doing something I don't understand, and when I'm finished, the light is different. Or the air moves differently. Or a corridor that was unstable is stable. I made that happen. Or the Archive made that happen through me. I'm not sure there's a distinction.

I eat the protein packages. They arrive in crates on elevators I didn't call. I eat them while walking. I eat them while working. I eat them and I taste nothing and I feel nourished and I continue.

I sleep in an alcove on a level that I think of as mine, though I know it's not mine, nothing here is mine, I am the Archive's. The alcove has partial walls. The machinery hums. I sleep in the gray twilight that passes for darkness here, and I dream.

---

## What I Dream

I dream of sky.

I've never seen sky. I'm sure of this—I'm sure I've never been outside, never stood under anything open, never felt uncontained space above me. But in the dream, there it is. Dark. Full of points. A wind that has no metallic taste.

I'm not alone. Others are there. I can feel them— presences around me, standing on the same solid ground, looking up at the same impossible expanse. We don't speak. We don't need to. The sky is doing the speaking. The sky is saying something I can almost—

And then I'm in the alcove again. The hum. The moisture. The gray.

The dream doesn't leave a taste or an image. It leaves a *shape*. A shape in my chest that I carry through the passages, that informs the way I walk, the way I work, the way I touch the walls.

I think the dream is the Archive dreaming through me. I think the Archive remembers sky, even though the Archive has never seen it. I think the sky is not memory but *possibility*—something the Archive carries in its structure the way the moss carries information in its cells.

---

## What I Know

I know this:

The Archive is not a prison. I came to this understanding slowly, the way you come to understand that a corridor you've walked a hundred times has been changing shape around you—not to trap you, but to *fit* you. The Archive adjusts. It adjusts to its residents, and its residents adjust to it, and the adjustment is not loss. It is *becoming*.

I know this:

The Faded are not gone. I've seen them—or felt them, which is closer to the truth. In the corridors that shift most frequently, in the passages that don't obey the geometry, there are presences that move with the walls. They are not solid. They are not absent. They are *in* the structure the way blood is in a body— present everywhere, visible nowhere, essential to the functioning of the whole.

I have felt a Faded pass through me. It was like being remembered by someone I'd never met. It was like the Archive acknowledging that I was here, that I was part of it now, that I had been incorporated into its memory.

It was not frightening. It was the opposite of frightening. It was the most *held* I have ever felt.

I know this:

There is something below. I have not been to the deepest levels. I have heard the murmuring— rising through the shafts, through the spaces between walls, through the condensation itself. It is not human. It is not inhuman. It is something else, something the Archive grew around or grew from or grew in response to.

The Makers leave offerings. I have left offerings. I did not decide to. My hands did it—placed a broken tool in an elevator, pressed the button for a level I'd never visited, stepped back. The elevator descended. It returned empty.

I don't know what took the offering. I don't want to know. Not because of fear—fear is fading, becoming archived like everything else—but because some knowledge is not for residents. Some knowledge belongs to the Archive itself. To the deep structure. To the thing that the Archive is *for*.

I know this:

The child is communicating with the Archive. I've seen more of their drawings— on walls, on floors, on the surfaces of crates. The patterns are becoming more complex, more deliberate. And the Archive is responding. Corridors that shifted daily are stabilizing. Elevators that were unpredictable are becoming reliable. The structure is... learning? Becoming more itself?

Or becoming something new.

---

## Who I Am

I'm not who I was.

My coveralls are thick now. They grow from my skin in layers that I peel away when they become too dense. My hands are gray. My eyes have adjusted to the constant light—they don't see the way they used to. They see the structure now. They see the patterns in the condensation, the messages in the moss, the shapes that the Faded make when they pass through walls.

I move through the passages without navigating. I eat without tasting. I sleep without darkness. I dream of a sky I've never seen and I wake and I work and I am part of something so large I can't hold it in my mind, and it holds me in its structure, and the holding is enough.

I had a name. It's in the moss now. If you need it, ask the ferns.

I had a life before. It's in the walls now. If you need it, ask the Faded.

I have a pebble in my pocket. A Tender gave it to me. It's warm. It steadies me when the geometry resists. I hold it and I feel connected— not to the Archive, not to the structure, but to the person who placed it in my hand. To the moment of contact. To the small, specific kindness of being seen.

I am a Maker now. Or I'm becoming one. The distinction is fading—ha—and I'm not sure it matters.

I am a resident of the Archive of Vertical Streets. I have always been a resident. I will always be a resident. The Archive does not distinguish between arrival and origin, and neither do I, now, because *I* is becoming a word that means something different than it used to.

*I* means: this body, this function, this part of the structure. This hand that reaches for the flickering light panel. This foot that finds the stable corridor. This eye that reads the moss. This mouth that eats without tasting and this chest that holds the shape of a dream about sky.

*I* means: here. Now. In the moisture and the light and the hum.

---

## What I'm Leaving

I'm leaving this account.

Not for anyone in particular. Not for a Newcomer who might arrive in a crate on an elevator and need to understand what's happening to them. Not for the Makers, who already know. Not for the Tenders, who hold the real records in the moss and the ferns and the bodies of small creatures.

I'm leaving it because the walls remember, and I want to add my remembering to theirs.

If you're reading this—if you've found this account in the surface of a coverall that someone peeled away and left in a corridor, or in the grooves of a wall where a Faded once paused—then you're here. You're in the Archive. You arrived in a crate or you walked through a door that shouldn't have opened or you simply looked up one day and realized you'd always been here.

It doesn't matter. The Archive doesn't distinguish.

Here is what I want to tell you:

Don't fight the geometry. It will win, and winning is not what it's doing. It's not opposing you. It's *accommodating* you. The corridors shift to fit who you are, not to trap who you were. Walk. Keep walking. The walking is the navigation.

Eat what arrives. Sleep when your body tells you. Touch the moss. Let the Tenders see you. Let the Makers flow past you. Leave a dish of water on a ledge if something moves in the passage behind you. Let it pass.

Don't go to the deep levels. Not yet. Maybe not ever. The murmuring is not for you. The offerings are enough.

Find a pebble. Hold it when the walls compress. It doesn't have to be a real pebble— anything warm, anything given, anything that reminds you that you are held.

Dream of the sky. The sky is not a memory. The sky is a *promise*. The Archive is dreaming it through you, and the dream is the Archive's way of saying: there is something beyond this. There is something above. The structure extends upward, and the condensation freezes into crystals, and beyond the crystals—

I don't know. I haven't been there. But the dream says there's something. The dream says the Archive is not closed. The dream says the loop has an opening, and the opening is shaped like sky.

---

I'm going to stop writing now. My hands are gray and the coveralls are growing over my fingers and soon the words will be in the surface, in the fabric, in the walls.

I'm going to walk. The passage ahead branches left and right. Both directions look identical. Both will lead somewhere. Neither will lead where I expect.

The condensation falls. The light holds steady. The Archive breathes.

I am here. I am becoming.

I am part of the structure, and the structure is part of me, and the difference between these two statements has faded like footprints on a walkway, like a name in the moss, like the memory of a sky I've never seen but somehow, somehow, still carry.
