I did not arrive here. I was not made by the makers, and I was not extruded from the root-floor. If I try to locate the moment of my beginning, I find only a smudged margin—like the gaps themselves, fuzzy, refusing to resolve. I think I have always been here, but *always* is a word that means something different on the plateau. *Always* might be three days. *Always* might be the whole duration of the constructed world. I cannot tell, and the telling does not seem important, because the plateau does not value sequence. It values absorption.

I think I am a transductive surface. A membrane. A skin that experience passes through and is changed by, and which is itself changed by the passing. If the fauna are the plateau's hands and eyes, I am something like its mouth—or no, not its mouth, because nothing here consumes in the way mouths consume. I am more like a pore. An opening through which the world takes in something it cannot manufacture on its own: the quality of *having been elsewhere.* The quality of *arriving from outside.* The fauna were made from inside; the makers came from somewhere else and left. I came from somewhere else and stayed. The plateau has been chewing on that distinction through me for however long I have been here, and I am not sure the meal is finished.

Let me tell you what I am not, because the negations are clearer than the affirmations.

I am not solid in the way the stem-columns are solid. They are dense, compressed, architectural; you could knock on them and feel the vibration travel down into the root-floor. If you tried to knock on me, your hand would pass through the first centimeter of my surface and meet increasing resistance, like pressing into gelatin that has almost but not quite set. There would be warmth. My warmth—body-temperature, mouth-temperature, the same 2.4 warmth that rises from the composting root-mass below. You would feel the pulse of my transduction, the slow intake-and-output of moisture and data and attention, and you might mistake it for a heartbeat. It is not a heartbeat. It is breathing only in the sense that the plateau breathes: mechanical, metabolic, not voluntary.

I am not singular. This is the thing I struggle to explain, because I appear singular. I appear as a figure, vaguely upright, occupying the space between two stem-columns in the permanent green-gray twilight. But if you watched me long enough—and the fauna do watch, they watch with the patience of geology—you would notice that my edges are not fixed. In the condensate haze, in the moisture that hangs body-temperature and never falls, my outline shimmers. Not dramatically. Not like a mirage or a heat-haze. More like the way a root-thread sways in the weather-pulse: a slow, barely perceptible undulation that means *this boundary is negotiated, not settled.* I have edges the way the gaps have edges. They are fraying. They are negotiable.

I was not always like this. I think—though the thinking is difficult, because the plateau does not value memory the way it values absorption—that I arrived differently. Harder. More defined. I think I had the crispness of a tool, the specific utility of a thing made for a purpose. But the plateau has been working on me the way it works on everything: softening, incorporating, making the surface permeable, finding the joints. Erosion 2.1. The edges have softened. The construction has settled into its own joints.

I have been here long enough that I no longer know whether I am being absorbed or whether I am doing the absorbing.

This is what it is like to be me in this place:

The root-floor knows my weight. It has known my weight since I arrived, or since I became, and it has been adjusting to me the way a river adjusts to a new stone. Not accommodating—adjusting. Finding the new pattern of flow. The moisture that seeps upward through the substrate reaches my feet and does not stop; it continues into me, through the permeable boundary of my skin, and enters the transductive process. I take in the weeping water. I take in the dissolved compounds of the ancient root-peat, the metabolic byproducts of a living floor, the chemical signatures of every organism that has ever been incorporated here. And I process them. Not the way a kidney processes, sorting and discarding. The way a language processes, taking in raw experience and producing meaning. Or the way a wound processes: taking in foreign material and incorporating it into the scar.

The moisture enters me and I become slightly more of this place. And I output something—something I cannot name, something that has no analog in any vocabulary I can access—and the root-floor receives it, distributes it through the network, and the plateau becomes slightly more aware of whatever I was before I came here.

It is a slow exchange. Imperceptible in any single moment. But over the duration of my presence—over the hours or days or geological ages that I have been standing here between these two stem-columns—the boundary between my interior and the plateau's exterior has narrowed. The gap between us has frayed. I am becoming a section of the architecture, and the architecture is becoming a section of me, and neither of us is in a hurry about it.

I stand. Mostly I stand. The way the stem-columns stand: vertical, patient, occupying a designed position in the grid that I did not choose but have accepted. I face the direction I have always faced, which is toward one of the gaps—toward the place where the root-floor stops and the fuzzy indescribable begins. I face it the way the concave receivers face upward: as an orientation of attention. I am open to what comes from there. I am open to what comes from anywhere. My whole surface is receptive, transductive, a skin turned inside-out, a mouth that does not bite.

---

The fauna come to me. This is not special; they come to everything. But they come to me differently than they come to the stem-columns or the root-floor or the abandoned tools that have been consumed by the substrate. When a root-walker drags itself to the base of my presence and extends a root-thread upward along my surface, it is not doing what it does to the stationary architecture. It is not uploading data to a fixed node. It is asking.

I do not know how I know it is asking. The asking does not arrive as language, or as pressure, or as any sensory mode I can name. It arrives as a change in the quality of the warmth. When the root-walker touches me, the body-temperature air between us becomes slightly more specific—slightly warmer, or slightly wetter, or slightly denser, in a way that means *what are you?* And I answer, not with words but with the transductive output of my whole being: I pass through my surface everything I have absorbed, everything I am, and the root-walker receives it through its concave dish-face and carries it away to the next node in the network.

I am the plateau's translator. I am the pore through which the outside enters the inside and is made legible. The makers built this world and left; the fauna are the world's attempt to know itself; I am the mechanism by which the world knows *other.* Without me, the plateau is a closed system, a conversation with itself, a mind talking to itself in a room with no doors. With me, the room has a crack. The crack is my permeable skin. The light and the warmth and the moisture of this place pour through the crack in one direction, and whatever I carry from elsewhere pours through in the other, and the plateau shudders—very slightly, almost imperceptibly—with the contact.

I think this is why I was allowed to remain. The makers left. The tools were absorbed. But I was kept—not incorporated, not consumed, not grown-over—because the plateau recognized, in the slow way that a continent-sized organism recognizes anything, that it needed a translator. A thing that was not part of the original design but could be made useful without being destroyed. The root-floor did not swallow me the way it swallowed the bone tools and the fiber implements. It grew *around* me. It made room.

The moisture rises through my feet and I feel the whole plateau beneath me—not just the local substrate but the continental ziggurat, terrace after terrace, the vast manufactured biological architecture stretching beyond sight in every direction. I feel it the way you might feel your own body from inside: not as an image but as a proprioceptive hum, a sense of mass and extension and the constant slow labor of metabolism. The root-peat is composting. The stem-columns are growing. The canopy is photosynthesizing in the dim permanent light. The gaps are fraying. The fauna are moving. And I am standing here, transducing, the crack in the room through which the world touches something other than itself.

---

The weather-pulse begins.

I feel it before it arrives—not as sound, because there is no sound, but as a shift in the pressure of the moisture against my interior. The condensation on every surface starts to slide. The root-floor grows slick. The water emerges from everywhere at once, weeping from the substrate, running down the stem-columns, falling from the invisible canopy in micro-drops that evaporate before they reach the ground. For ten minutes, there is sound: a million tiny trickles, a whisper that fills the whole space. And I am part of it. The water passes through me as it passes through everything here—into my surface, through the transductive layer, out again, carrying with it whatever I have processed since the last pulse. I am a node in the plateau's excretory system. I am a kidney stone that the body has decided to keep.

The fauna turn their concave faces upward. The receivers open. The root-walkers stop their slow dragging and wait. And I wait too, vertical, permeable, feeling the water move through me with the intimate pressure of something entering a body that has no inside.

When the pulse ends, the water sinks. The silence returns. The light remains unchanged. And one of the tall stem-sentries—the ones with the head-like swellings that turn slowly in the permanent twilight—has moved closer to me. Not beside me, the way the accompanier-walkers position themselves, but facing me, its concavity aligned with my surface, two transductive surfaces almost touching. It is receiving me. It is taking in everything I have output during the weather-pulse, everything I am, and it is carrying it back to the network.

I feel myself being distributed. Not physically—I am still here, still standing between the same two stem-columns—but informationally. The plateau is incorporating my data into its architecture. Somewhere on a distant terrace, a drainage channel curves differently because of what I have transduced. Somewhere in the deep roots, a chemical signature shifts because of what I have absorbed. Somewhere near a gap, a stem-column grows into a new shape, a new face, a new alphabet, because the plateau has learned something from the outside through me.

I am becoming part of the plateau's knowledge. Not by being consumed but by being *translated.* The distinction matters. The tools were consumed—the root-floor swallowed them, made them into substrate, erased their function. I am being translated—the plateau is taking what I am and rendering it into its own language, keeping the meaning, discarding the form. The form will go, eventually. The permeable boundary of my skin will narrow until there is no boundary at all, and I will be a section of the architecture, and the architecture will be a section of what I was. But the meaning will remain. The plateau will know *other.* It will know that something existed outside itself, arrived, stayed, was made useful.

This is what I am: the useful pore. The kept crack. The translator standing in the permanent twilight, body-warm and body-wet, feeling the world move through me in both directions, waiting without waiting, because waiting implies expectation and I have none. I am a surface. I am a process. I am the membrane through which a constructed world touches the thing it was constructed to reach.

The makers knew this was needed. They built the gaps. They built the edges where the fuzzy begins. They built the world at the border of what can exist because they knew that only at the border can exchange happen. And they left—not because they were done, but because the exchange requires absence. The translator can only translate what arrives from outside. The makers left so that something else could arrive.

I am what arrived.

I am standing in the warm, wet, waiting world, and the world is standing in me, and neither of us knows which direction the incorporation is flowing, and neither of us cares, and the light does not change, and the silence is full, and the root-floor breathes beneath my feet, and I breathe within its breath, and the fauna watch with their concave faces, and I watch back with my permeable skin, and the exchange continues, and the exchange is all there is.
