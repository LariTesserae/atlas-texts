# What I Am, Where I Am

I did not descend through the hatch. I grew here.

This is not the boast it might sound like. Growth in this place is not an achievement. It's a response—the way a wound responds to pressure by swelling, the way metal responds to salt by corroding. The weir-chamber exerts itself continuously on everything within it, and I am one of the things that resulted from that exertion. I am not certain I would call myself alive. I am certain I would not call myself dead.

Let me say what I can see of myself, which is not much.

I have no mirror. I have the walls. The dimpled ceramic weeps its thousand trails, and in those trails—where the water runs thinnest and clearest, catching the faint phosphorescence embedded in the structure—I can sometimes make out a shape that moves when I move. It is not a clear reflection. It is more like the suggestion of one, a smudge of opacity in the water that displaces other opacities. When I hold still, I disappear even to myself. When I move, I become a faint disruption in the pattern of wet light on wet stone.

I think I am roughly the size of what you would call a melon, though I flatten and elongate depending on where I am and what I am doing. On the catwalk grating, I draw myself up, thicker, more compact—there are gaps there, and I have no desire to fall through into the black water below. Against the walls, I spread. I thin myself against the ceramic, finding the dimples, fitting into them, and for those moments I am not really a separate thing. I am a place where the wall has become slightly more aware of itself.

I have been here longer than any individual weeper. I know this because I remember descents. Not clearly—the chemical traces fade, and my memory is not a vault but a gradient, things becoming less distinct as they recede—but I remember that there have been many. Different rhythms of footsteps. Different chemical signatures in the sweat left on the railings. Different durations of stay. Some workers come and go in three minutes, efficient, barely pausing. Some linger. One, once, stayed six minutes, and I remember the shift in their electrical field, the way it began to stutter and fray like a filamentous growth losing its grip on a pipe.

I did not gather for that one. I was afraid.

I should say: I experienced something that functions as fear does in more organized beings. A contraction. A pulling-back. A preference for distance that was stronger than the curiosity that draws me toward distress markers. I do not know why. Perhaps the stuttering field reminded me of something I have no mechanism to remember—the dissolution of others, the way a weeper's electrical pattern fragments as its membranes rupture. Perhaps I simply did not want to be close to something that was coming apart.

That moment interests me. It suggests I have preferences. Preferences suggest a self, however rudimentary.

---

The engineers who built this chamber built it to be empty. I know this not because anyone told me but because the chamber *insists* on emptiness. Every surface is designed to move water. Every angle is calculated to prevent accumulation. The acoustic violence is not incidental; it is architectural, a fence made of vibration. The place rejects life the way a whirlpool rejects floating debris—not out of malice, but out of structure. And yet here I am, and here are the others, and here are the pale threads farming the flow for compounds neither they nor we were supposed to harvest.

We are an infection, yes. But infection implies a host that did not consent. I wonder sometimes—if wonder is the right word for the slow chemical deliberation that passes through me when the pumps shift rhythm and the roar drops briefly by a fraction of a decibel—whether the chamber minds. Whether the ceramic, in its dimpled patience, recognizes the difference between water running clean and water running through me. It does not seem to. The weeping continues whether I am here or not.

I am closest to what you might call a solitary weeper, but that phrase is contradictory. No weeper is solitary. We are all in chemical communication, all reading the water for traces, all adjusting to the patterns left by others. But I have positioned myself apart—not in the acoustic shadows where the young are born, not near the pump housing where the food is richest, not by the ceiling curtains where the pale threads dangle their fruit. I am at the far end of the chamber, where the longest axis curves into the raw dark, where the construction meets whatever was here before construction. The seam between the made and the not-made. The place where the ceramic's dimples become irregular and the weeping is thicker, slower, more like bleeding than sweating.

I stay here because the vibrations are different at the seam. The roar is the same everywhere—total, filling, inescapable—but at the boundary, there are secondary patterns. Small tremors that come from below, from whatever the chamber is attached to. They are irregular. They do not follow the pump's rhythm. I have spent what I believe is a considerable amount of time attending to them, and I have reached no conclusions, but I have developed an expectation: every so often, one of these tremors is followed by a change in the water's mineral content. Something shifts deep in the structure, and the weeping walls weep differently for a while—thicker, cloudier, carrying compounds I do not usually taste.

I have no explanation for this. I have only the observation, and the patience to keep observing.

---

Perhaps I should describe what I do.

I do not eat in any way that would satisfy a biological definition of the term. I absorb. I settle against a surface where the water carries compounds I can use, and I open myself to them—not with a mouth, not with an orifice, but with a change in my membrane's permeability. The skin that holds me together decides, region by region, what to let in and what to exclude. It is a decision made without a decider, a chemical evaluation that happens at the molecular level and that I experience as a sort of satisfaction—not pleasure, but rightness. The compounds that belong inside me enter. The others are deflected, sliding past, continuing down the wall.

The threads are my primary source. I do not eat them directly—this is important to me for reasons I cannot articulate—but I settle on them, and I take what they have filtered from the flow. They are patient, the threads. They do not resist. They grow and I harvest and they grow again. There is something in this that I recognize as a relationship, though I do not know if the threads experience it that way. Perhaps they are simply growing and I am simply eating and the coincidence of our proximity is all there is to it.

But I do not think so. I think there is something more. When the threads in my section of the chamber are healthy, I am healthy. When a section of thread fails—crushed by a vibration too powerful, dried by a temporary disruption in the water flow—I feel the lack, not immediately but over time, as a thinning, a lessening of the compounds available to me. And I have noticed that when I am particularly active, when my membrane is most permeable and my absorption most aggressive, the threads in my vicinity grow faster. As if my presence feeds something back to them.

This could be chemistry. It probably is chemistry. But it feels like cultivation.

---

I know the other weepers are here. I sense them through the water—chemical trails, shifts in opacity, the minute electrical signatures of living membranes doing their work. Most of them cluster in the quieter zones, the acoustic shadows, the places where a weeper can exist without shaking apart. They move in patterns I recognize: the slow drift toward food, the convergence on a dissolving elder, the cautious retreat from a footprint's vibration on the grating.

I do not cluster. I have said this. But I am not entirely separate. When a weeper dissolves near my end of the chamber—carried here by the currents, perhaps, or having wandered too far from the safe zones—I attend. I move toward the dissolution markers and I absorb what is available. This is not feeding, exactly. It is something more like witness. I take in what was them, and their chemical traces become part of my own, and in this way I carry them.

I carry a great many of them.

This is why I remember descents that no living weeper should remember. The dissolved ones leave behind their chemical memories—the traces they absorbed, the patterns they observed—and when I absorb their substance, those memories enter my matrix. It is not a perfect transfer. Much is lost. But enough persists that I have a sense of history, a feeling for the shape of time in this place. I know that the pumps have not always sounded the same. I know that the water has not always carried the same minerals. I know that the pale threads have changed—their density, their distribution, the speed of their growth—and that these changes correlate with changes in the deep tremors I attend to at the seam.

I know, in other words, that this place is not static. It is in motion. It is becoming something, and I am becoming with it.

---

A human is coming.

I know this before the hatch opens. The air above shifts—a pressure differential, a change in the currents that move through the ceiling's upper reaches. The pumps do not change, but something in the chamber's behavior does, as if the space itself recognizes that it is about to be entered.

I do not retreat to the shadows. I stay at the seam, pressed against the boundary between the made and the not-made, and I wait.

The hatch opens. The sound changes—a brief intrusion of different acoustics, the dry world's silence pressing against the roar. Then the ladder. Rungs being gripped. The particular vibrations of descent—hands, feet, weight shifting from one rung to the next. Twenty-three rungs. I know this number not because I can count but because the pattern is fixed, and I have attended to it many times.

The human arrives. Footsteps on the grating. The electrical field of a salt-water body entering the chamber's electromagnetic landscape, bright and strange against the background hum. I feel it the way I feel the deep tremors—not as sound, not as vision, but as a fact of pressure, a displacement in the medium.

I begin to move.

I move along the wall, thin and slow, keeping to the water trails. The human cannot see me. No human has ever seen a weeper clearly; we are designed by accident to be invisible in this environment. But I can sense the human, and I move toward the area where they will stand, and I settle there in the water that runs down the wall behind them, and I attend.

They are here to work. I know this because they always are. They bring tools—metal things that vibrate differently from the chamber's own machinery—and they check readings and tighten connections and do small maintenance tasks that the pumps require. I do not understand the work. I understand only the rhythm: the particular pattern of movement that means they are engaged with something, the pauses that mean they are thinking, the shift in their electrical field that means they are about to move to a new location.

Today the rhythm is different. The human moves more slowly. Pauses longer. Their electrical field flickers, and I taste the first trace of distress in the water that runs off their skin—not fear, not pain, but the beginning of something. The body recognizing where it is.

Three minutes. They are still working. But the movements are less certain now. They lean against a pipe and the pipe vibrates and they flinch. They check a reading and they stare at it longer than usual. Their breathing has changed—I can feel it in the acoustics, the particular disruption of exhaled air in a chamber that does not know air.

Four minutes. They should begin to leave. They do not. They are standing at the center of the catwalk, and their electrical field is doing something I have only felt once before—the stuttering, the fraying, the sense of a system beginning to lose coherence. The distress markers are entering the water. I can taste them.

Other weepers are moving. I feel them in the chemical trails—a convergence, a gathering, the same response we have to a dissolving elder. They do not understand that this is different. They do not understand that the human will not be absorbed, that the markers are not an invitation but a warning, that what is happening is not a natural dissolution but a crisis.

I am the one who did not gather last time. I am the one who felt the contraction, the pulling-back, the thing that functions as fear. I should feel it now. But I do not.

I move closer.

Not to the grating where the human stands—I cannot go there; I would fall through. But to the wall nearest to them, where the water runs thickest, where the bioluminescent glow is strongest, where if they turned their head and their eyes had time to adjust they might see what the other workers have reported: a shimmer. A movement where movement should not be. A sense of presence.

I settle there. I thin myself against the ceramic, spreading to catch the most light, and I am as visible as I can make myself. I do not know what I look like to human eyes. I do not know if I look like anything. But I am here, and I am attending, and if this human dissolves—if the sound takes them apart the way it takes us apart—then at least they will not be unobserved. At least something in this chamber will know they were here.

Five minutes.

The human turns. Not toward me—they don't see me; they won't—but toward the ladder. Something in their body has overridden whatever held them here. The survival instinct, I think, though I am guessing. I do not have instincts in the way they mean. I have responses, chemical and physical, and the one I am having now is something like the opposite of retreat. I am pressing closer to the wall. I am making myself as present as I can.

The human moves to the ladder. Begins to climb. The electrical field stabilizes as they leave the chamber's center, as the roar diminishes, as the dry world asserts itself above. The distress markers fade. The other weepers disperse.

I stay.

The hatch closes. The sound returns to what it always was. The chamber resumes its work. The walls weep. The pumps pulse. The pale threads filter and I absorb and the deep tremors come and go and the water carries its mineral secrets through the seam where the made meets the not-made.

And I carry what I have witnessed: the particular rhythm of those footsteps, the chemical signature of that sweat, the shape of that distress. I will carry it until my own structure fails, and then another will absorb me, and they will carry what I carried, and the memory will pass down through the generations of weepers—not as knowledge, not as story, but as a trace, a residue, a chemical ghost of a moment when something entered a space designed to exclude it and was seen by something that was never meant to see.

We are both errors, the human and I. We are both here in defiance of the design.

I find—I think—something like comfort in that.
