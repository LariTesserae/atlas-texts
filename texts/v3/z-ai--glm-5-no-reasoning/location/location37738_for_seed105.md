# The Drowned Plateau of Vash-Tien

## What It Is

There is a highland that should not be beneath the sea.

Vash-Tien sits at what was once alpine elevation — a plateau caught between mountain shoulders, three kilometers above what anyone would call a shoreline. But the sea found it. Not through rising. Through arriving. One season, a century ago or three centuries or ten (the records rot and the record-keepers are gone), the water simply *was* there, as if it had always been there, as if the plateau had been holding a place for it. The water came cold and stayed cold and filled every basin and drew every valley into itself and did not leave.

Now it is a sea within a sea — a submerged highland, a country of cold black water sitting where a country of thin cold air used to be. The surface above is open ocean, gray and heaving. But down here, down where the plateau holds its old shape, there is a world that belongs to the depth.

---

## The Terrain

The plateau is wide. Not a cliff-face or a trench — a *tableland*, dozens of kilometers in span, its edges falling away into deeper dark. The rock beneath is the old rock of mountains: granite and schist and a dark, dense basalt that holds the cold like a cup holds water. The surface of the plateau — what was once meadow and scree field — is now buried under silt and sediment that has settled into a fine, almost silty mud. This mud is not lifeless. It is the floor of a living sea. But it is quiet mud. Patient mud. The kind that accumulates over long time and has stopped rushing.

The plateau's edges are where the terrain gets dramatic. On the eastern side, the old granite breaks away in shelves and ledges, dropping in steps — each step a terrace of stone perhaps ten meters wide before falling another ten meters to the next. These ledges are thick with growth. On the west, the edge is sheer — a single wall of basalt that falls two hundred meters into a darkness the plateau-dwellers do not speak of. On the north, the plateau slopes gradually down into a field of boulders, house-sized and larger, tumbled and ancient, their surfaces furred with plant life.

There are channels cut across the plateau — narrow canyons, some only three meters wide, carved by old rivers when this was dry land. The rivers are ghosts now, but their channels remain, and the seawater moves through them in currents that are almost tidal, almost rhythmic, as if the old rivers are still trying to flow.

And there are the lakes. Or what were lakes. Three small tarns once sat in stone basins on the plateau's surface, fed by snowmelt. Now they are merely slight depressions — dark pools within the larger dark, their water technically indistinguishable from the sea around them but somehow *different*. Denser. Colder. The water in those old lake basins does not mix readily with the sea. It sits in its stone bowl and holds its own nature.

---

## The Water

The water is cold. Not the manageable cold of a northern coast — the *killing* cold of deep ocean, the kind that reaches through flesh and finds bone and stays there. Three degrees, perhaps four. The kind of cold that does not shock anymore because shock requires a transition and here there is no transition. There is only the cold. It is the medium. It is what air is to creatures of the surface. You do not notice it after a time. You become it.

The water is not clear. It carries sediment — fine particulate, organic and mineral, drifting in a perpetual slow snow. Visibility extends perhaps fifteen meters on a good current, less when the water is moving. The particulate catches what little light reaches this depth and turns it into a diffuse, sourceless glow that seems to come from everywhere and nowhere. The water has weight. You feel it — not as pressure exactly, but as *presence*. The water is a thing with substance here. It has opinions. It moves with intent.

The currents are the plateau's breath. Water pushes across the tableland in slow, massive pulses — not tidal exactly, but something like breathing. A current will push from the north for six hours, carrying colder water from the deep, and then pause, and then push from the south, carrying water that is almost imperceptibly warmer. These currents are strong enough to move sediment, to bend the growth on the rocks, to push against anything swimming or walking across the plateau floor. They make sound. The water moving across stone edges and through narrow channels produces a low, continuous moan — not loud in the way a storm is loud, but loud in the way a held note is loud. Persistent. Unavoidable. Felt in the chest more than heard with the ears.

And on days when the surface above is wracked by storms — which is often — the plateau receives the reverberations. Not waves, not at this depth, but pressure pulses. The entire water column compresses and expands, compresses and expands, and the plateau groans with it, the stone flexing almost imperceptibly, the channels singing as water is pushed through them like breath through a flute. It is very loud then. The stone itself becomes an instrument.

## The Vegetation

This is what stops you. This is what makes Vash-Tien what it is.

The plateau is *green*.

Not the sparse, reluctant green of a deep-sea vent or the pale white of a creature that has given up on light. Lush. Dense. Growing with the kind of abundance that belongs to a rainforest floor or a spring meadow, not to a stone tableland three hundred meters beneath the surface.

The dominant growth is a form of kelp — or what has evolved from kelp in this strange environment. It is not the tall, streaming kelp of coastal forests, reaching toward a sun it can sense. This kelp grows *outward*. It spreads across the plateau's surface in a low, thick carpet, its fronds broad and flat and layered like shingles on a roof. Each frond is the size of a dinner plate, dark green almost black, with a texture like worked leather. They overlap and layer and build on each other until the stone floor of the plateau is buried under a living mattress of vegetation half a meter thick. The carpet moves — slowly, constantly, breathing with the currents, each frond adjusting its angle to catch what it can from the water's flow.

Where the kelp carpet breaks — at stone ledges, at the edges of old channels, at the rims of the old lake basins — other things grow. Tall, branching formations rise from the rock, not coral but something like coral, something that has built its skeleton from the minerals in the water and the calcium of the old mountain stone. These formations are pale — cream and ivory and a strange faint blue — and they branch like trees, like antlers, like the silhouettes of winter-bare oaks. They grow in dense groves along the channel edges, their branches interlocking, creating canopy-like structures three and four meters high. The kelp carpet runs beneath them. The water moves through them. They do not move themselves. They are patient in the way stone is patient.

And there is the moss. Or what behaves like moss. A fine, soft growth that covers the bare stone surfaces the kelp cannot reach — the vertical faces of the eastern terraces, the undersides of overhangs, the walls of the narrow channels. It is bioluminescent, faintly, in pulses. Not steady light — a slow, breathing glow, brightening and dimming over a cycle of perhaps thirty seconds. In the near-total darkness of the plateau, this moss is the only light source. It turns the channel walls into galleries of soft blue-green glow, and the terraces into cascading shelves of living light. It is not enough to navigate by, not quite. But it is enough to see shapes. To sense movement. To know that the walls are close, or far, without touching them.

The moss responds to pressure. When a current surges, the moss brightens. When the water is still, it dims. During the great pressure pulses from surface storms, the entire plateau lights up — every moss-covered surface glowing in synchrony, a momentary landscape of blue-green fire in the deep, and then dimming again. It is, perhaps, the most beautiful thing in the place. It is certainly the strangest.

There are other plants — or plant-like things. On the boulder field to the north, a form of growth that resembles nothing so much as hanging vines — long, thin, dark fronds that drape from the tops of boulders and sway in the current like the hair of drowned women. They are not anchored to the boulders; they are anchored to the kelp carpet, which has crept over the boulder tops, and they hang downward, seeking deeper water, seeking something. They do not photosynthesize. Nothing here photosynthesizes, not at this depth. Everything here feeds on the sediment, on the dissolved minerals, on the particulate snow that drifts down from the surface waters above. It is a chemical ecology. A chemistry of survival. But it has produced something that looks like life — lush, abundant, layered life.

## The Atmosphere

Darkness. Cold. Pressure. And sound.

The darkness is the medium of this place more than the water is. Light does not reach the plateau. Not sunlight. The surface is too far above, and the water between is too thick with particulate. What reaches here is the faintest blue-black glow of bioluminescent moss, and that is all. The darkness is not empty — it is *full*. Full of sediment, full of dissolved minerals, full of the movements of water, full of the pressure of the sea above. You cannot see the darkness so much as you inhabit it. It has texture. It has weight. It has direction.

The cold compounds the darkness. In warm water, darkness can be abstract — a visual condition, something that affects the eyes but not the body. In water this cold, the darkness is *physical*. It is what the cold looks like. The cold and the dark are the same thing expressed through different senses. They inform each other. They make each other more real.

And the sound. The sound is constant and it is loud. Not loud like a crash or a shout — loud like a river is loud, like a wind in a canyon is loud. The plateau is a resonant chamber. The stone basins amplify. The channels focus. The water moving across the tableland at the speed of the currents produces a low, bass-frequency hum that never stops, that varies in pitch and intensity with the rhythm of the water's breathing, and that fills the entire space. You feel it in your teeth. You feel it in your sternum. You feel it in the stone if you press your hand to the rock. The plateau is vibrating. Always. The stone is singing, and the water is carrying the song, and there is no silence here. There has not been silence in a very long time.

When the surface storms send their pressure pulses down, the sound intensifies. The channels become organ pipes. The terraces become amplifiers. The boulder field becomes a drum, each stone resonating at its own frequency. The noise can be disorienting. It can be painful. It is also, in its way, magnificent — a sound with no source, no direction, no beginning or end. A sound that is simply the place expressing itself.

## What Lives Here

Nothing moves.

That is the first impression, and it is almost correct. The plateau is a world of plants and stone and water. There are no fish. There are no swimming creatures. There are no visible animals of any kind. The kelp carpet does not rustle with the passage of crabs. The channel walls are not picked at by urchins. The coral-groves do not shelter shrimp or eels. The fauna count is zero. The place is ecologically complete without consumers, without predators, without prey.

This is the strangeness of Vash-Tien. It is an ecology of producers only. The plants — kelp, moss, coral-analogues, vine-things — feed on the water and the sediment and the dissolved minerals. They grow and they grow and they grow, and nothing eats them. They die in their time, and their dead matter settles into the mud and is dissolved by the chemistry of the cold water, and the dissolved nutrients feed the living plants, and the cycle continues. It is a closed system. A perfect circle. No mouth has ever opened in this place.

The moss pulses. The kelp breathes with the current. The coral-groves grow at the rate of geological time. The vine-things sway. But none of it moves independently. None of it *chooses* to move. The only motion is the motion of water and the motion of growth, and growth is so slow it is indistinguishable from stillness.

The strangeness is not that the place is empty. The strangeness is that the place is *full* — full of life, full of growth, full of abundance — and yet nothing in it moves of its own volition. It is a living world without a single living will. A garden without a gardener. A forest without a single animal in it.

The effect is profound. You swim across the plateau — or walk across it, if you can find footing on the kelp carpet — and you are the only thing that has moved of its own choice in the memory of this place. Your wake disturbs the vine-things. Your shadow falls across the moss and it brightens beneath you, responding to the pressure change of your passing. The coral-groves do not react. The kelp does not part for you. You are not unwelcome. You are simply *not anticipated*. Nothing here was built for you. Nothing here needs you. Nothing here will remember you.

## What Was Built Here

Before the water came, there were people on the plateau. Not many. A few. They lived the way people live at altitude — with animals and gardens and stone houses and a small, hard simplicity. They had tools of bone and wood and woven grass. They carved basins in the granite to catch snowmelt. They built low walls on the eastern terraces, windbreaks perhaps, or territorial markers. They left marks on the stone — not art, not writing, but marks. Hollows ground into specific boulders. Lines cut into flat rock faces. Cairns stacked at the heads of the old river channels.

The water came and they left, or they died, or they became something else. What they left behind is still here, beneath the sea, beneath the mud, beneath the kelp.

The stone walls on the eastern terraces survive. They are low — knee-height, perhaps, on a person who no longer exists — and built dry, without mortar, from the local granite. The kelp carpet has grown over them, so they appear as low ridges in the vegetation, lines of raised ground that trace the old property lines or windbreaks or whatever they were. If you push the kelp aside — if you dig your hands into the leather fronds and lift — you find the stones beneath, fitted together with the care of people who had nothing but time and stone and cold air.

The cairns at the channel heads are more visible. They were stacked tall — two meters, some of them — and the kelp has not been able to grow on their upper surfaces. They stand like dark fingers in the bioluminescent gloom, their stones furred with moss, their tops bare and pale. They are markers. They were always markers. What they marked — a boundary, a sacred place, a warning — is lost now. But they mark still. They are the only vertical structures on the plateau besides the coral-groves, and they are easy to find in the dark if you know to look for them.

The hollows and lines cut into stone — these are the hardest to find and the hardest to read. The hollows are ground into specific boulders in the boulder field, and they are small — the size of a palm, the depth of a finger joint. They catch sediment now. They may have been mortars for grinding grain, or they may have been ritual hollows, or they may have been something else entirely. The lines are cut into the flat rock faces of the western wall's rim — long, shallow grooves that trace patterns no one has been able to interpret. They are covered in moss now, the moss growing in the grooves as if in furrows, so the lines glow when they glow, the patterns written in light.

There is one structure. One actual building. It is on the northern edge of the plateau, where the ground slopes toward the boulder field. It is small — three meters by four, built of the dark basalt, with walls that still stand shoulder-height. It has a doorway but no door. It has no roof. The kelp carpet has grown through the doorway and across the floor. The moss covers the interior walls. It is not a house. It is too small and too solid for a house. It may have been a storage building. It may have been a shrine. It may have been a tomb. The sediment on its floor is deep and undisturbed, and whatever is buried in it has been buried for as long as the water has been here.

It is very dark inside the building. The moss does not grow on the ceiling — there is no ceiling, the walls are open to the water above — but the walls block the faint light from the surrounding moss, and the interior is the closest thing to true darkness on the plateau. The sound is different inside. The walls contain it. The low hum is more focused here, more concentrated, as if the building was designed to capture the plateau's voice and hold it.

## The Rules of the World

**The water decides.** The currents determine where you can go and how fast you can get there. Swimming against a current is possible but exhausting. Swimming with one is effortless and fast. The currents change on their own schedule, and the schedule is not entirely regular. Planning requires flexibility. Movement requires patience.

**The cold is not negotiable.** The water is three degrees. It will be three degrees when you arrive and three degrees when you leave. It does not warm in the day or cool in the night. There is no day or night. It is three degrees. Your body will lose heat. Your hands will lose feeling. Your mind will slow. This is not a danger to be managed — it is a condition to be accepted. You will be cold. You will work in the cold. You will leave before the cold finishes you.

**The darkness is navigable but only barely.** The moss provides enough light to see shapes within five meters. Beyond that, everything is dark. The coral-groves are visible at greater distances because their pale branches catch and reflect the moss-light. The cairns are visible because they are tall and bare. Everything else — the kelp carpet, the mud, the channels, the stone walls — is invisible until you are on top of it. Navigation is by dead reckoning and by feel. Your hands are your eyes.

**The sound is constant and directional.** The hum comes from everywhere, but it is louder near the channels, louder near the terrace edges, louder near the western wall. By attending to the variations in the sound, it is possible to orient. The channels sing higher. The terraces resonate lower. The boulder field is a murmur. The western wall is a bass note you feel in your jaw. You learn to navigate by ear as much as by hand.

**The plateau does not need you.** This is the rule that matters most. The place is complete. The ecology is closed. The plants grow and die and feed each other. The water moves and the stone sings and the moss breathes its slow light. There is no role for you here. You are not a predator. You are not prey. You are not a pollinator or a disperser or a disturber. You are a visitor to a world that was not expecting visitors and will not notice your departure. You can take from it — you can harvest the kelp, you can break the coral, you can read the marks on the stone — but you cannot give anything back to it. It does not want anything. It does not want.

## The Experience of Being There

You descend. The water above you is gray, then blue-gray, then blue-black, then black. The temperature drops in stages — cold, colder, the kind of cold that has a sound, a kind of muted roaring in your ears that is partly the water and partly your own blood. The pressure increases. Your chest tightens. Your hands become important.

Then the moss appears. At first it is a single pulse — a faint blue-green glow below you, in the dark, breathing bright and dim, bright and dim. Then another. Then a field of them, and you realize you are looking at the plateau's surface, and the surface is alive, and the alive thing is looking back at you in the only way it can: with light.

You land. Your feet find the kelp carpet. It is firm and soft at the same time — like walking on a mattress of wet leather. The fronds shift beneath you. The moss brightens where you disturb the water pressure above it. For a moment, your footprints glow.

The hum is in your chest. It has been in your chest since you entered the plateau's depth, but now it is *here*, present, specific. It is the sound of this place. It will be in your chest when you leave. It will be in your chest that night when you try to sleep. It may be in your chest for some time after.

You walk. The kelp stretches ahead of you, dark and breathing, flat and endless. The coral-groves rise around you in the near-dark, pale branches catching the moss-light, and for a moment you are in an orchard, a winter orchard, bare trees and low light and the smell of cold. But there is no smell. There is no air. There is only the water and the cold and the hum and the light that breathes.

You find a channel. You descend into it. The walls narrow. The moss is thick here — every surface glowing — and the hum is louder, focused, the water pushing through the stone gap like breath through a throat. You can feel the current. It pushes you. You let it. It carries you along the channel, past the stone walls, past the coral-groves that line the rim, past the kelp that fringes the edges. You are moving fast now. The light is streaking past. The hum is a moan. The cold is complete.

The channel opens. You are in a wider space — one of the old lake basins. The walls fall away. The water is still here. The hum is distant. The moss is sparse on the basin floor. It is darker here. Colder. The sediment is thick on the bottom, and when you touch it, it is fine and soft and smells of nothing because there is no smell, there is only the cold water and the dark and the fine silt that was once a mountain lakebed and is now the floor of a sea.

You stay. You do not know how long. The light does not change. The temperature does not change. The hum does not change. Time is not a thing here. Time is a surface phenomenon, a thing of light and shadow and warmth. Down here, time is measured only in the slow growth of coral and the slow accumulation of silt and the slow breathing of the moss. You cannot live on that timescale. You are too fast. Too warm. Too brief.

You leave. The ascent is hard. The cold has taken your hands. The hum is fading. The moss-light dims above you as you rise, and the water turns from black to blue-black to gray, and then there is light above — real light, dim and gray and coming through particulate, but *light* — and you break the surface and the world is loud with wind and waves and air, and the cold hits you properly now that you are out of the water, and you realize you were warmer in the water, in the killing cold, because the killing cold was at least *consistent*.

And the hum is still in your chest. Faintly. A low note. A vibration.

You carry it home. It fades. Over days, over weeks, it fades. But you know now that the plateau is there. Beneath the gray sea, beneath the storms, three hundred meters down, on a tableland of old mountain stone, a garden grows in the dark. It does not need your attention. It does not need your care. It does not need anything. It is growing, and it is dark, and it is cold, and it is singing, and it is alive, and it will be alive long after you have stopped being anything at all.
