# The Ferrous Quarters

The first thing you notice is the humidity. Not the clean kind that smells of earth and growing things — this is metallic, warm, carried through corridors of riveted iron and stale air ducts that have been breathing the same moisture for what looks like decades. Every surface is wet. Not dripping, not running, just *wet* — covered in a thin film of condensation that never fully evaporates because the air is always nearly saturated. The temperature sits somewhere between uncomfortable and inescapable, the kind of heat that settles into your clothes and stays.

## The Structures

The Quarters were built in a low basin — barely above sea level, maybe below it in places, hard to tell when everything is so flat and the horizon is obscured by stacked construction. The architecture is industrial without being elegant. Corrugated walls, exposed structural framing, pipes running externally along facades because the builders kept adding systems after the initial construction and never bothered to integrate them properly. Five-story tenement blocks of riveted steel and poured concrete alternate with longer, lower structures — fabrication halls, pump stations, what appears to be a sorting facility connected to a series of bins by an overhead conveyor system that still operates intermittently, its chain dragging and clicking through a cycle that nobody seems to have initiated.

The buildings have been here long enough to have settled into themselves. Rust has established its presence permanently — not the fragile, flaking kind, but the stable, dark variety that has become almost a second skin on the metal surfaces, stopping where it stops, neither advancing nor retreating. Paint survives in patches, mostly on the trim around door frames where someone, at some point, cared about appearance. That person is no longer present.

Mud-brick additions fill some of the gaps between the original steel structures. These look newer, though "newer" is relative — they've weathered enough to lose any freshness. Their walls absorb the ambient moisture and darken at the base, a tidemark that rises and falls with the season. Some have cracked, and the cracks have been left unrepaired. Vegetation — actual vegetation, the leafy, rooted kind — is entirely absent. Nothing green grows here. Whatever once might have taken root in the packed earth between buildings is consumed or outcompeted.

## The Water

Water is everywhere in this place without ever forming proper streams or pools. It emerges from the ground in slow seeps along foundation walls. It collects in the depressions of the packed-earth yards between buildings, forming shallow, mineral-scummed puddles that look old. The drainage system — a network of open concrete channels running alongside the paths and roads — is clearly functional but overwhelmed. Fine silt has accumulated in the channels, and water moves through them in a slow, reluctant fashion, as if it has somewhere to go but isn't in any hurry.

Every morning, condensation beads on every available surface and then takes most of the day to clear, only to return again at dusk. The air itself feels heavy, not quite rainy but never quite dry. The local word for this, if there is one, probably means something like *the wet that doesn't end*.

## The Inhabitants

And then there is the chittering.

There is no other word for it. It's not a sound you hear constantly, but it surrounds you in its near-constant absence — a low, rustling, clicking ambience that comes from everywhere and nowhere, rising and falling in cycles. The creatures are everywhere.

They're some kind of arthropod — hard-shelled, segmented, roughly hand-sized, though the sizes vary considerably. They have too many legs to count comfortably and a set of mandibles that look built for crushing rather than biting. Their shells are dark — rust-brown, iron-grey, occasionally almost black — and they blend into the industrial palette of their environment so well that you don't notice them until they move. And they are always moving.

The ground is thick with them. Not a carpet, not a swarm — more like a population that has found its equilibrium at a very high number. They climb the walls. They occupy the interiors of the abandoned structures. They teem in the drainage channels, where they appear to feed on the mineral residue and organic sediment that washes through. When the conveyor system cycles on, they swarm the discharge points, climbing over each other to reach whatever the bins have deposited.

At first glance, they look like vermin, and the Quarters look like a settlement that was abandoned because the infestation became unbearable. But on closer observation, the relationship is more complex than that.

## The Operating Logic

The machinery is old but functional. Pumps cycle at long intervals, drawing water from somewhere below and moving it to somewhere else — not always clear where. The conveyor operates on a timer that nobody has set recently. Exhaust fans in the fabrication halls still turn, though slowly, pushing warm, damp air through vents that have become stained with years of mineral output.

And the creatures interact with all of it.

They gather at the pump outlets when the water flows. They clear the drainage channels — consuming the sediment that would otherwise block them entirely. They cluster around the conveyor discharge and process whatever material comes through, carrying it away in their mandibles to locations unknown. The interior of one of the tenement blocks has been colonized almost completely — floors, walls, ceilings covered in shells and movement — but the structural integrity of the building seems unaffected. If anything, the way they bind organic material into their own structures — nest-webs, or whatever the correct term is — seems to reinforce rather than degrade the metal framework.

This is not a ruin being eaten. This is a machine being operated, by hands that aren't hands.

## The Sound

For all the activity, the Quarters are quiet. The creatures make a constant low clicking and rustling, but it fades into background noise almost immediately, like the hum of electricity or the sound of wind. The machinery cycles infrequently and without urgency. The pumps make a low thrumming when they run. The conveyor clatters. But these sounds are isolated events, not a continuous roar. Most of the time, there is just the wet, the heat, the dimness, and the chittering.

Footsteps, too, are absorbed — by the damp earth, by the layers of organic material that have built up on the ground, by the rust and the mud-brick and the condensation. The Quarters muffle everything. They feel insulated, enclosed, despite being open to the sky. And the sky, what you can see of it between the buildings, is uniformly grey — overcast, not dramatically so, just featurelessly and permanently grey, as if the clouds have settled in and taken up residence.

## What This Place Is

The settlement appears to be a mining or processing facility — extracting something from the ground, sorting it, moving it on. The tenement blocks housed workers. The fabrication halls held machinery. But there are no people here now, and there haven't been for some time.

Whether they left or were driven out is unclear.

What's clear is that the machinery kept running, and the creatures kept multiplying, and at some point, the two systems merged into something that still functions — still pumps, still sorts, still processes — even though no one is at the controls. The creatures aren't intelligent in any recognizable way, but their population and behavior have adapted to the rhythms of the infrastructure. They respond to the cycles of the machines, occupying spaces when they're idle, moving to discharge points when they activate, clearing blockages, maintaining flow.

If this is infestation, it is also, somehow, maintenance.

The Quarters are not abandoned. They are simply occupied by something else, something that doesn't require the buildings to look any different than they did when the human tenants left. The same iron walls. The same wet air. The same low light filtering through the same dirty windows. The same sounds of industry, just slower, older, and operated by smaller hands.

The heat sits. The wet persists. The chittering rises and falls. The pumps cycle on, then off. Something is being produced here, moved through pipes and channels to a destination you cannot see from within the settlement's boundaries.

The Quarters are still at work.
