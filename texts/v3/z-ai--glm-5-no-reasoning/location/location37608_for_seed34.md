# The Wet Stones of Verath Hold

It is high, and it is vast, and that is the first thing you must understand.

The city — if it can still be called a city, though nothing else fits — sits on a plateau so wide that its far edge vanishes into haze, and the haze is not distance alone but the atmosphere itself, thick with moisture that has nowhere to go. The plateau is the roof of something older. The stone underfoot is layered, sedimentary, every stratum a record of some sea that covered this place and then withdrew. The water never fully withdrew. That is the second thing.

---

## The Ground

Under the stone, water waits. You cannot see it but you can feel it — a certain give to the flagstones, a darkening at the joints between them where moisture seeps upward through capillary action, drawn by the cool air above. After the frequent rains — and it rains often, three days in four, sometimes a fine misting drizzle that lasts sixteen hours, sometimes a sudden hard downpour that turns the drainage channels into roaring rivers for twenty minutes and then vanishes — the stone dries only partially. Shadows of wetness remain in the channels carved into the rock. The channels are old. Centuries of flowing water have cut them deeper than any human hand would have bothered to dig. They follow paths that look intentional until you follow one far enough and realize it is simply obeying the micro-topography, the subtle undulations of the plateau surface, finding the lowest path the way water always does.

The stone itself is a pale grey-blue limestone, weathered smooth in the places where feet have traveled for generations, rough and pitted where exposed. In the walls of the older buildings, you can see fossil traces — spiraling ammonite shapes, the long thin marks of worm burrows, occasional ribbed scallop shells the size of a human palm. The plateau was a seabed. The buildings are made from the seabed. There is something in this that the inhabitants understand intuitively but do not discuss.

The rock weeps. Not metaphorically. In the shaded faces of the taller structures, where sun rarely reaches, water emerges from the stone in slow beads that track downward and connect into threads. These weeping faces are most common on the north and east sides of buildings, and the stone there takes on a different character — darker, almost black, slick with a thin biological film that is not quite algae and not quite moss but something between, something that has evolved to live in this exact niche, this perpetual thin sheet of moisture on vertical limestone.

---

## The City

Verath Hold is old enough that its original plan is no longer visible. What remains is the accumulated logic of centuries of building, abandoning, building again atop the ruins, filling in courtyards, tunneling through old foundations, constructing new walls from the stones of collapsed ones. The result is dense — not tall, but dense. Buildings press against each other. Narrow passages run between structures where you can touch both walls with outstretched hands. These passages serve as drainage during heavy rain and as pedestrian routes during dry hours. The dry hours are precious. People move quickly through them.

The architecture has no single style. The oldest surviving structures are massive, cyclopean — great blocks of limestone fitted without mortar, the joints so tight that a knife blade won't enter. These buildings are often partially subterranean, their ground floors having become basements as the street level rose with successive layers of construction and debris. Newer construction is more modest — smaller stones, mud mortar, timber lintels where wood can be found. Wood is rare here. There are no forests on the plateau. Timber is carried up from the lowlands at great expense, and most of it has been carried up so many times — used, rotted, burned, replaced — that the concept of "new wood" is almost foreign. Doorframes are patched and re-patched. Some contain fragments of wood that are demonstrably older than the buildings they serve.

The density is not just horizontal but vertical. Structures are layered. A building may have three distinct phases visible in its wall — cyclopean base, middle-period small-stone construction, upper-level repair work using scavenged materials. Rooftops serve as floors for the next tier of construction. In the oldest quarters, you can stand in a street that is actually the roof of an older street, and below that, another, and below that, the original plateau surface. The drainage channels cut through all these layers. Water does not care about archaeology.

There are open spaces — courtyards, plazas, the occasional broad avenue — but they are fewer than you would expect in a settlement of this size, and they feel less like planned amenities and more like accidents of geography, places where buildings happened not to be, or where a structure collapsed and was never rebuilt. The largest open area is the Basin, a natural depression in the plateau surface near the city's center, roughly oval, perhaps three hundred meters at its longest axis. It fills with water during rains and drains slowly through the porous limestone beneath. In the hours after a storm, it becomes a shallow lake, ankle-deep, reflecting the grey sky. Children — there are not many children, but there are some — sail things across it. Pieces of bark. Flat stones. The reflections are always more vivid than the sky above, as though the water holds a version of the light that is steadier, more considered.

## The Wetness

Water is the city's primary occupation. Not in the sense that people worship it or mythologize it — they are practical about it, as people must be who live with it constantly — but in the sense that every aspect of daily life is organized around water's presence. Rooflines are angled to channel rain into cisterns. Streets slope toward drainage channels. The lowest levels of the city, the oldest buried streets, are sometimes navigable and sometimes not, depending on the water table, which rises and falls with the seasons. There is a season of dryness — six weeks in what might be called summer, when the rains thin to mists and the water table drops enough that the lower passages reopen, and people go down to retrieve things, to scavenge, to check on the structural integrity of what lies beneath. Sometimes they do not come back. The lower levels are not entirely stable, and the stone there is saturated, weakened, prone to sudden collapse.

The atmosphere condenses. This is the most disorienting aspect of Verath Hold for a visitor. The plateau is high — high enough that the air is thin, high enough that the horizon sits lower than it should, high enough that you are above the cloud layer roughly a third of the time, looking down on a sea of white that obscures the lowlands entirely. But the air is also wet, perpetually wet, and the combination of altitude and moisture produces fog that is not like lowland fog. It is denser, almost particulate, and it has weight. You feel it on your skin. It deposits fine droplets on every surface. Hair becomes damp within minutes of exposure. Cloth absorbs it. Stone absorbs it. The fog rolls in from the western edge of the plateau — there is a cliff there, a drop of such height that its bottom is usually invisible — and it moves across the city in slow, deliberate masses, like something alive, like herds of pale animals grazing through the streets.

When the fog combines with the near-surface water, the city becomes almost aquatic. The drainage channels run even without rain, fed by condensation alone. The weeping walls intensify. Puddles form in depressions that have no apparent source. The air temperature drops — it is always cool here, rarely above fifteen degrees even in the dry season, often near ten — and in the fog, the cold becomes a damp cold that reaches bone. People wear layers of wool and felted cloth, treated with animal fat where available to resist the moisture. The garments are heavy. Movement in Verath Hold has a certain weight to it, a deliberateness. People do not run unless they must.

## What Grows

Vegetation is sparse but not absent, and what exists is strange, not in its form but in its specificity. Every plant here has found a particular niche in the water-stone matrix and exploited it with precision.

The weeping walls host the dark film mentioned earlier, and in patches where the film is thick enough, a kind of/lichen — grey-green, crustose, nearly flush with the stone — establishes itself. It spreads in circular colonies that merge over time into continuous coverage. Under magnification, it is beautiful, intricate, fractal. To the eye it simply looks like darkening. The lichen does not damage the stone. It may, in fact, protect it, forming a biological seal that slows erosion. The inhabitants leave it alone.

In the drainage channels, where water flows regularly, there grows a moss — or something moss-like — that is almost black, so dark it is easy to mistake for wet stone. It forms slippery mats on the channel floors and is the primary cause of falls during the wet season. Its spores are released when water flows over it, and the runoff carries them to new locations. Every drainage channel in the city is seeded with it. It grows nowhere else.

The Basin, after it fills, hosts a brief bloom of something ephemeral — a plant, or perhaps an animal, that exists only as long as the standing water remains. It appears as a pale film on the water's surface, iridescent, oily, and within days of the water's appearance it spreads across the entire surface. When the water drains or evaporates, it vanishes, leaving no trace. The inhabitants have various names for it. None of them agree on what it is. It does not appear in every filling of the Basin, only some, and the pattern of its appearance does not correlate with any obvious variable. People have stopped trying to predict it.

In the courtyards and the wider streets, where enough light reaches ground level, there are scattered tough grasses — thin, pale, sharp-edged, rarely taller than a hand's width. They grow in the joints between flagstones, in the cracks of walls, in the accumulated soil of rooftop gardens. The rooftop gardens are the closest thing the city has to agriculture. On the flat roofs of the newer buildings, in wooden boxes lined with felt and filled with soil carried up from the lowlands or scraped from the deepest crevices of the plateau, people grow root vegetables. Small, dense, not particularly nourishing, but capable of surviving in the thin soil and cool air. The gardens are protected from wind by low walls and from excess rain by drainage holes. They are tended carefully. The produce is meager and essential.

There are no trees on the plateau. There are no shrubs. The ecological niches that exist here are narrow and specific, and woody plants have not found one.

## What Lives

Fauna is rare, and what exists is small and specialized.

There is a type of insect — wingless, dark, roughly the size of a fingernail — that lives in the drainage channels and feeds on the black moss. It is slow-moving, nearly inert during dry periods, and active only when water flows. Its lifecycle is synchronized with the rain. After a storm, the channels seethe with them briefly, and then they subside. They are the base of a very short food chain.

Above them in that chain is a small, pale amphibian — eyeless, translucent, its internal organs visible through its skin — that lives in the deeper, permanently wet sections of the lower city. It feeds on the insects and on the biological film that covers the weeping walls. It is rarely seen by people who do not venture below street level. Those who do venture below report it in tones of mild disgust. It is harmless. It is, in its way, essential — it is one of the few organisms that breaks down the biological films and prevents them from overgrowing the drainage infrastructure.

Birds appear, but they do not stay. They pass over the plateau on migratory routes, and sometimes they land on the rooftops and rest for a few hours, but there is little for them here, and they move on. Their calls — when they come — are startling. The city is so quiet that a single bird's song carries across half its width.

There are no large animals. No predators. No livestock, except for a small number of goats kept in a stone pen near the eastern edge, where the plateau surface is rough enough to provide footing and where a particular tough grass grows in sufficient quantity to sustain them. The goats are thin, hardy, and unpleasant in temperament. They provide milk, occasionally meat, and their hair is woven into a coarse fabric used for garments. They are the only non-human mammals in the city.

## The Silence

Verath Hold is quiet in a way that unsettles visitors.

The combination of high altitude, dense fog, soft stone, and layered construction dampens sound. Footsteps on wet limestone are muffled. Voices do not carry — the fog absorbs them, the stone absorbs them, the layers of building absorb them. A conversation held at normal volume is inaudible thirty meters away. The drainage channels produce a constant low sound of flowing water during and after rains, but it is so ubiquitous that it functions as silence, a baseline below which nothing registers. The wind, when it comes, is felt more than heard, pressing against the buildings in slow surges that make the fog move but produce little sound against the stone.

The result is a city in which you can stand in a major street at midday and hear almost nothing. Your own breathing becomes audible. The drip of water from a roofline twenty meters away arrives as a distinct, individual sound, each drop separate and perceptible. People in Verath Hold speak quietly. They have learned to. There is no need to shout. Sound does not travel, and the effort of raising one's voice feels inappropriate, intrusive, like breaking a rule that nobody wrote down but everybody understands.

The silence has social consequences. People here are physically close — the density of the city means near-constant proximity, bodies brushing in narrow passages, neighbors separated by walls thin enough to hear coughing — but they are auditorily remote. You can be two meters from another person and hear nothing from them. Privacy in Verath Hold is not visual but acoustic. You can see everyone. You hear no one.

## The Processes

The city is active, not in the sense of bustle — there is little bustle, the pace is slow, weighted, damp — but in the sense of constant process. Water falls, flows, drains, seeps, condenses, evaporates, falls again. The fog forms, moves, dissipates, reforms. The lichen spreads. The moss grows. The Basin fills and empties. The lower levels flood and drain. The goat kids are born in season and grow. The rooftop gardens are planted, tended, harvested, replanted. The drainage channels are cleared of debris — a constant task, since the channels carry not only water but sediment, fragments of stone, the accumulated grit of a city slowly wearing itself down.

Maintenance is the primary labor. Not construction — little new is built — but repair. Keeping the channels clear. Keeping the cisterns sealed. Keeping the lower levels from collapsing. Keeping the rooftops draining. Keeping the walls standing. The city is in a state of permanent, slow-motion entropy, and human effort holds it in a dynamic equilibrium, neither declining nor improving, simply continuing. The inhabitants have a word for this work. It translates roughly to "keeping."

The erosion is established. The plateau has been here for geological ages. The city has been here for centuries. The drainage channels have been cut by water over generations. The weeping walls have wept for longer than anyone can remember. Everything is in its state, its long-existing, settled state, and the energy of the place goes not into change but into the maintenance of that state. The city does not grow. It does not shrink. It persists.

## The Light

Light here is normal in quantity but strange in quality. The frequent fog diffuses it, flattens it, removes shadow and contrast. On clear days — which come in the dry season, and occasionally outside it, when the wind pushes the fog westward over the cliff edge and the sky opens — the light is sharp, almost hard, and the city looks different. Colors emerge that the fog had muted. The pale limestone becomes almost white. The black moss in the channels becomes visible as distinct colonies. The distant edge of the plateau, usually lost in haze, becomes visible, and you can see how far it extends, how vast this place is, and the scale of it produces a particular vertigo, a sense of standing on something too large to be a surface, something that should be a planet but is merely a plateau.

On clear nights, the stars are dense and close, the altitude bringing them nearer, or seeming to. The Milky Way casts a faint illumination. The wet stone reflects it. The city glows, very faintly, in starlight on wet limestone, and it is beautiful, and it is cold, and it is the image that stays.

## The Rules

There are rules in Verath Hold, and they are not written because they do not need to be. Everyone knows them.

Stay out of the lower levels during the wet season. The water table rises without warning.

Keep the channels clear. A blocked channel floods the street above it, and the street above that, and the flooding reaches the buildings, and the buildings are wet enough already.

Do not build on the Basin's edge. The ground there is not stable. It has been stable for two hundred years. It may not be stable tomorrow.

Tend the gardens. They are the margin.

Listen for silence. If the drainage channels stop sounding, something is wrong upstream, and you should find out what.

The goats are communal. Their milk is shared. Disputes about this are not permitted.

Do not go to the western cliff in fog. You will not see the edge until you are past it. The edge has a low wall, but the wall is old, and the stone is wet, and the fog is thick. People have gone. They are not found.

---

The city sits on its plateau, high and vast and wet and quiet, doing what it has done for longer than anyone can remember: persisting. The water falls. The channels run. The walls weep. The fog moves through the streets like something alive, and the people move through the fog like something patient, and the silence holds everything, and the stone holds everything else, and the plateau holds the stone, and the plateau is held by nothing, and the vastness of it is the final fact, the one that contains all the others.
