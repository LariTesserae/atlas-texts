# The Green Compendium

## A living archive at the edge of the meltline

There is a place where a glacier once pressed its full weight against a ridge of quartzite and schist, and where, in retreat, it left behind a scraped basin — shallow, bowl-shaped, perhaps four hundred meters across at its widest — ringed by fractures and fresh talus. The ice withdrew. Water gathered. And then something ancient in the soil recognized the opportunity and began to build.

What stands here now is not a settlement in any conventional sense. No one quarried stone for it. No one fired clay or felled timber. The structures — and they are structures, with walls and floors and galleries and partitioned rooms stacked three and four deep — are entirely botanical. Grown. Woven from root and rhizome, from thickened stem and calcified bark, from the accumulated lignin of ten thousand generations of plants that understood, somehow, that the architecture of shelter was also the architecture of survival.

---

## First impression: the bowl

You descend into it. There is no other approach. The basin's rim rises perhaps thirty meters above its floor — not dramatic, not cliff-like, but a clear and sudden edge where the surrounding highland falls away into a shallow scoop, as if a vast thumb pressed into wet clay and the clay remembered. The rim is sharp in the way young terrain is sharp: the rock here has not yet been rounded by millennia of weather. Edges catch at your boots. The quartzite breaks in clean planes, glinting where mica catches what little light reaches this place.

And what little light there is. The sky above is a persistent pall — not cloud exactly, not fog, but a thick, luminous haze that might be moisture held in suspension at the very edge of condensation. You cannot see the sun. You can sense where it is, roughly, a slightly brighter region of the grey above, but its light reaches the basin diffused, scattered, rendered soft and directionless. Everything is lit from everywhere and nowhere. There are no shadows. The effect is that of being inside a pearl: luminous, dim, depthless.

The cold is immediate. Not the sharp, dry cold of high altitude in winter, but a wet cold, a soaked cold, the kind that finds seams in clothing and settles there with patience. The temperature hovers just above freezing — perhaps two, perhaps three degrees above — and it does not waver. This constancy is itself strange. In the highlands surrounding the basin, temperature fluctuates with altitude and wind and cloud cover. But inside the basin, the cold is steady, held in place by something — the thermal mass of the water, perhaps, or the metabolic output of the biological mass that fills the space, or some balance between the two that has settled into equilibrium over centuries.

You crest the rim and look down, and what you see is a city made of moss.

---

## The structure of the place

That first description is inaccurate, but it is what the eye reaches for. The basin's floor is covered — saturated, packed, filled — by a continuous mass of plant matter that has organized itself into vertical structures, horizontal surfaces, connected passages, and enclosed chambers. The word "city" comes to mind because the organization is evident: there are taller masses and shorter masses, there are thoroughfares and dead ends, there are what appear to be functional districts — denser regions of growth, looser regions, areas where the architecture shifts from tight chambers to open colonnades. But there are no streets. There are paths, but they are not paved or cleared. They are channels — spaces where the plant mass has not filled in, or has been compressed by passage, and the distinction between "path" and "room" is often unclear.

The scale is modest. The tallest structures rise perhaps eight meters from the basin floor. Most are between two and five meters. The overall footprint, if you could view it from above, would resemble a densely packed hamlet compressed into a bowl: a single continuous fabric of living material with variations in height and density but no true gaps. You cannot walk between buildings because there are no separate buildings. There is one building, and it is the entire settlement, and the settlement is a single organism — or, more accurately, a consortium of organisms so thoroughly intertwined that they function as a single system.

The substrate is root. Everywhere, root. The floor of the basin, where you can find it — and in most places you cannot — is a dense weave of woody rhizomes, some as thick as a forearm, layered over and under each other in a lattice that has the structural integrity of a wattle wall. Between the roots, water moves. Not standing water, not pools, but a slow, constant seepage — water that emerges from the basin's edges where the rock still weeps melt from the retreating glacier above, water that flows through the root lattice in channels no wider than a finger, water that collects in hollows and depressions within the root floor and sits there, dark, still, cold, reflecting nothing because there is no light strong enough to create a reflection. The water is not dirty. It is clear, in fact, remarkably so, but it carries a faint amber tint — tannin, released from the slow decomposition of the plant mass it flows through. If you cup it in your hands, it is the color of very weak tea, and it is so cold that your fingers ache within seconds.

You walk on the roots. The surface is uneven but navigable — the woody mesh has compressed under its own weight into something firm enough to support passage, though it yields slightly, springily, with each step, and in places your foot finds a gap between roots and you feel the cold water below, pressing up. The roots are not bare. They are covered — sheathed — in a layer of moss and liverwort so dense that it forms its own substrate, a carpet of green and brown and the deep, almost black green of something that has never been dry. The moss is wet to the touch, always. Not dripping, not saturated, but wet — the surface of each frond carries a film of water that comes away on your fingertips, that makes everything slippery, that means you move slowly and carefully, placing each foot with intention.

## The architecture

The walls grow from the root floor as clusters of woody stems — not trees, not exactly, but something between a shrub and a vine, a plant that has evolved to build structure rather than to build a single trunk. Each stem is perhaps two to three centimeters in diameter at its base, and they grow in tight bundles, dozens of stems rising together, leaning against each other, thickening where they contact, grafting — fusing — at the points of touch. Over years, over decades, the bundles become columns: composite pillars of living wood, each one a community of individual plants that have merged into a single structural member. The columns rise two, three, four meters and then arch — bending toward neighboring columns, meeting them, grafting again, forming lintels and beams and the ribs of vaulted ceilings.

The effect, inside the larger chambers, is architectural in a way that feels deliberate. The columns are spaced at intervals that suggest planning — roughly two meters apart, though the spacing varies, tighter in some regions, looser in others. The arches they form are regular, symmetrical, and where multiple rows of columns stand parallel to each other, the space between them forms galleries — long, narrow rooms with vaulted ceilings and walls of interwoven stem and root. The light inside these galleries is the light that filters through the walls and ceiling: a greenish, dim, submarine glow, as if you are walking through the body of a whale made of chlorophyll.

Some chambers are enclosed on all sides — domed spaces, three or four meters across, where the columns curve inward at the top and meet at a central point, forming a ceiling that is fully closed. These rooms are darker still. The air inside them is different: warmer, by a degree or two, from the metabolic heat of the dense plant mass, and richer in moisture, so that the atmosphere feels almost tropical, almost greenhouse-like, a pocket of humidity in the general cold. Water condenses on the inner surfaces of these domes — on the undersides of the arched stems, on the moss that lines the ceiling — and falls in fine, persistent droplets, a mist of cold water that settles on your hair, your shoulders, your upturned face. The rooms weep.

Other spaces are open — colonnades, essentially, where the columns support a ceiling but the walls between them are incomplete, filled with a lattice of thinner growth that admits light and air but blocks passage. You can see through these walls into adjacent chambers, the shapes of the neighboring spaces visible through the green filter of the lattice, rendered indistinct and slightly distorted, as if seen through water. The effect is of a building with no real interior, no truly private space — a structure of permeable boundaries, where every room opens into every other room, where the distinction between inside and outside is a matter of density rather than separation.

---

## The materials

The dominant material is wood — but wood in a state that is not familiar. The stems that form the columns are not deadwood. They are living tissue, and their surface reflects this: smooth, slightly tacky with sap, greenish-brown in color, mottled with patches of lichen and moss that have colonized the outer bark. Where stems have grafted, the junction is visible as a swollen ridge, a callus of woody tissue that has grown to seal the union, and these ridges give the columns a knuckled, jointed appearance, as if the architecture is also a skeleton, as if the building has bones.

The moss is the second material — and in terms of coverage, it is the first. Every surface in the basin is moss-covered, or moss-lined, or moss-adjacent. There are at least a dozen distinct species visible to the careful eye: sheet mosses that form smooth, dense carpets; hair-cap mosses that rise in spiky tufts; sphagnum in the wetter areas, pale green and almost white in the dim light; feather mosses that drape in branching fronds from the undersides of arches; lantern mosses that grow in tiny, gem-like clusters on vertical surfaces. Together, they form a continuous skin over the woody structure, a living insulation that retains moisture and warmth and that slowly, incrementally, breaks down the woody substrate into the organic soil that feeds the next generation of growth.

The third material is water. Not water as a liquid in a pool, but water as a medium, water as an atmospheric condition. The air in the basin is saturated, or very near it. Relative humidity is close to one hundred percent, and the consequence is that water is everywhere — not as standing water, but as condensation, as film, as the fine aerosol mist that hangs in the still air of the enclosed chambers. Every surface is wet. Every surface is, in a sense, underwater — not submerged, but wetted so thoroughly that the distinction is academic. The moss carries water in its cellular structure. The wood carries water in its grain. The air carries water in suspension. You breathe water. You touch water. You walk on water, held just above it by the woody mesh of the root floor.

And the fourth material — less visible but structurally essential — is ice. Not the ice of the glacier that once filled this basin, but a newer, more transient ice. In the coldest hours, in the deepest chambers where the sun never reaches and the cold air settles, moisture freezes on surfaces: a rime of clear ice on the undersides of arches, a crust of it in the hollows where water has collected in depressions in the root floor. This ice is ephemeral. It forms and melts within the span of a day, or it persists for weeks in the darkest chambers and then melts during a warm spell. It is part of the dynamic system: water to ice to water, cycling, never still, the medium through which the basin breathes.

---

## The atmosphere

The light, as noted, is dim. The haze above filters the sun to a diffuse glow, and the plant canopy — where the taller structures rise — filters it further, so that the basin floor exists in a perpetual greenish twilight. The color of everything is shifted: greens are deeper, almost black; browns are darker, closer to the color of wet earth; and the rare patches of lighter color — a pale lichen, a white splash of mineral deposit on a root, the faint amber of tannin-stained water — glow with a luminosity that is entirely relative, a brightness created by contrast rather than by any inherent property of light.

The air smells of peat. Not the sharp, acrid smell of a peat fire, but the raw, wet, vegetal smell of peat in its natural state: organic, decomposing, rich, layered with the sweetness of live moss and the mineral edge of cold water on stone. There is also a faint smell of resin — sap from the woody stems, weeping from graft-union calluses, pooling in small amber droplets that harden in the cold into a natural varnish. And underneath everything, a smell that is difficult to name: the smell of a place where biological processes are continuous, where photosynthesis and respiration and decomposition are all happening simultaneously and in close proximity, where the line between living and dead tissue is blurred and porous. It is the smell of a living floor. It is the smell of compost and birth.

The temperature inside the chambers is almost exactly at the freezing point of water. Not below — the metabolic output of the plant mass, combined with the thermal mass of the seeping water, keeps the interior just above freezing, perhaps one or two degrees, enough to maintain liquid water but not enough to be comfortable. This is not a place where warmth exists. This is a place where cold is the baseline and life has adapted to function within it, not by escaping it, but by operating at its threshold. The plants here are not tropical. They are not temperate. They are something else: cold-adapted species that have found a niche at the exact boundary where water is liquid, and they exploit that niche with a ferocity and a thoroughness that leaves no space unoccupied.

## The sounds

The basin is quiet, but it is not silent. The silence here is layered, textured, and the sounds that emerge from it are small, persistent, and biological.

The first sound is water. Not the sound of a stream — there is no stream, no channelized flow — but the sound of water moving through the root lattice: a faint, continuous trickling, barely audible, that comes from below, from beneath your feet. It is the sound of water finding its way through gaps in the woody mesh, following gravity, seeking the lowest point in the basin. In the enclosed chambers, where sound is contained, this trickling is more audible — a delicate, irregular rhythm, like fingers drumming lightly on a hollow surface. In the open colonnades, it is lost in the ambient hush.

The second sound is dripping. In the domed chambers, where condensation forms on the ceiling and falls, the dripping is constant and irregular. Each drop lands somewhere — on moss, on root, on water — and the sound varies with the surface: a soft, almost inaudible impact on moss; a clearer, sharper sound on wood; a liquid, musical note when a drop falls into one of the shallow pools that collect on the root floor. The pattern is random, and in its randomness, it becomes a kind of music — not composed, not rhythmic, but present, a constant reminder that this place is in motion, that water is moving through it, that the atmosphere is not still but cycling.

The third sound is creaking. The woody structure of the columns and arches is living tissue, and living tissue responds to changes in temperature and humidity. In the coldest hours, the stems contract — pulling tighter, stressing the graft unions, and the result is a low, groaning creak that propagates through the structure. It is the sound of a building that is also a body, settling and shifting in its own metabolism. The creak is not loud. It is barely above the threshold of hearing. But in the quiet of the enclosed chambers, with no wind and no birdsong and no human noise, it is audible, and it carries through the woody structure the way sound carries through the hull of a wooden ship: distant, resonant, felt as much as heard.

The fourth sound is growth. Not the sound of a single plant elongating — that is below the threshold of perception — but the collective sound of ten million cells expanding, of fibers stretching, of fruiting bodies pushing outward through moss. This is not a sound you hear. It is a sound you sense — a pressure, a faint vibration in the substrate beneath your feet, as if the floor is breathing, as if the architecture is slowly, imperceptibly, pulsing.

## The ecology

The plant life here is not diverse in the way a tropical rainforest is diverse — no flowering plants, no showy petals, no nectar and no pollinators. It is diverse in the way a reef is diverse: many species occupying tightly packed niches, differentiated by microhabitat, by moisture gradient, by the amount of light that reaches a particular surface, by the chemistry of the substrate they grow on. But they are all, fundamentally, the same kind of organism: small, non-vascular or minimally vascular, cold-tolerant, moisture-dependent, reproducing by spore and by fragmentation and by the slow, vegetative extension of root and rhizome.

The dominant group — the one that provides the structural architecture — is a cluster of woody-stemmed species that are not quite trees and not quite shrubs. They have the growth habit of something that evolved to climb and lean, to use its neighbors for support, and when they grow in close proximity, they form the composite columns and arches described above. Their taxonomy is unclear — they may be relatives of the creeping pines or club mosses, or they may be something that has no familiar analog, a lineage that found its own path in this cold, wet basin and evolved to fill the niche that trees would fill in a warmer climate. They do not reproduce by seed. They reproduce by spore, and the spores germinate in the wet moss and send up thin, threadlike stems that thicken over years into the woody columns. Growth is slow — a stem may add only a centimeter of height in a year — but the structure is cumulative, built over generations, each new stem adding to the mass, each graft-union creating a stronger composite, until the architecture is self-sustaining: a framework that supports itself and provides substrate for everything else.

On this framework, the mosses and liverworts grow. They are the sheathing, the insulation, the skin. Their role is to hold moisture against the woody surface, to prevent desiccation — though desiccation is not a serious risk in this saturated environment — and to slowly break down the outer bark of the woody stems, converting it to the organic substrate that supports new growth. They are, in a sense, the digestive system of the architecture: they consume the old growth and convert it to soil for the new.

In the wettest areas — in the channels between roots where water flows, in the shallow pools that form in depressions — there are algae. Not the green algae of a pond, but a cold-adapted, filamentous form that grows in dark, almost black strands, waving gently in the slow current. The algae are the primary producers of the aquatic system, fixing carbon from the dim light that penetrates the water, and they are food for the few organisms that inhabit the water itself.

And there are fungi. Visible everywhere, if you look closely: white mycelial threads spreading across damp bark, fruiting bodies pushing through the moss — small, dark, bracket-like growths that emerge from the woody columns in tiers, each one a few centimeters across, leathery and tough, occupying the specific niche of decomposer in a system where decomposition is slow but constant. The fungi are the recyclers. They break down the lignin of dead stems, return the nutrients to the root network, and close the loop: growth to death to decomposition to growth.

## The fauna

There are very few animals here. This is, fundamentally, a plant ecosystem — a system where the biomass, the structure, the metabolism, and the dynamics are all botanical. But there are a few.

In the water that seeps through the root lattice, there are small invertebrates — something like a tardigrade, but larger, perhaps two millimeters long, visible as a pale, moving dot if you hold very still and watch the surface of a pool. They move slowly, deliberately, feeding on the algal filaments and the organic particles that wash through the root mesh. Their life cycle is tied to the water: they emerge, feed, reproduce, and die within the channels and pools, and their remains contribute to the organic layer that forms on the bottom of the waterways.

On the moss surfaces, there are mites. Tiny, eight-legged, barely visible to the naked eye, they move across the moss carpet in a slow, ceaseless foraging, feeding on the microorganisms that inhabit the moss's surface — bacteria, protists, other mites. They are the grazers of this ecosystem, the herbivores, though what they harvest is not the moss itself but the community of smaller organisms that live on and around it. In turn, they are prey for the only predator in the basin.

The predator is a spider. Not a web-building spider — a hunting spider, small, dark, cold-blooded, that moves across the moss surface with a deliberation that seems almost calculating. It is perhaps a centimeter long, dark brown, its body slightly flattened as if adapted to moving through tight spaces in the root lattice. It does not spin webs. It hunts by patience: sitting motionless on the moss surface for hours, waiting for a mite or a springtail to pass within range, and then striking with a speed that is startling in this otherwise slow-motion ecosystem. There are not many of them. In a day of careful observation, you might see three or four. They are the apex predators of this world, and their rarity is a measure of the ecosystem's limited energy budget: there is only so much biomass, only so much metabolic throughput, and the food chain is short and narrow, tapering quickly to its top.

There is, as far as I can tell, nothing else. No birds — there are no perches large enough and no food source sufficient. No rodents — the root floor is too dense to burrow into and too wet to nest in. No insects with wings — the air is too cold, too still, too saturated for flight. The few animals that exist here are small, cold-adapted, wingless, and entirely dependent on the botanical system that surrounds them. They are afterthoughts — minor characters in a story that is, fundamentally, about plants.

## The dynamics

The basin is not static. It moves, but it moves slowly, on biological time, and the processes that drive it are cyclical, continuous, and layered.

The first cycle is water. Water enters the basin from the rim — seeping from the rock face where the retreating glacier still melts, even in this cold, at a rate that is almost imperceptible but that is, over the course of a day, substantial. The water flows through the root lattice, through the channels between stems, through the moss, and collects in the lowest part of the basin, where it forms a shallow pond — perhaps ten meters across, perhaps thirty centimeters deep — that is the basin's reservoir. From this pond, water seeps back into the ground, or evaporates into the saturated air, or is taken up by the roots of the woody plants and transpired through their leaves. The cycle is: ice to meltwater to seepage to root uptake to transpiration to condensation to drip to pool to seepage. It never stops. It is the heartbeat of the basin.

The second cycle is growth and decomposition. The woody stems grow — slowly, adding centimeters per year — and the oldest stems, in the center of the basin, in the oldest part of the structure, are dying. Their bark is stripped by moss and fungus, their wood softened by decay, their structural integrity failing so that they sag and bow and eventually collapse, adding their mass to the root floor. But as they collapse, the younger stems around them — the ones that have been growing in their shadow, waiting for the light that the old stem's failure will admit — accelerate their growth, filling the gap, weaving into the void, grafting onto the still-living neighbors and incorporating the architecture of the dead stem into the structure of the living. The result is a continuous process of renewal: old growth failing, new growth replacing it, the architecture maintained not by preservation but by replacement. No part of the structure is older than a century. But the structure itself is older. How much older is impossible to say.

The third cycle is the seasonal pulse — though "seasonal" is perhaps the wrong word for a place where the temperature barely varies. What varies is light. In the brighter months, the haze above thins slightly, and more light reaches the basin floor. The mosses respond: photosynthesis accelerates, new growth appears, the green of everything intensifies by a shade. In the darker months, the haze thickens — perhaps because of atmospheric patterns, perhaps because of increased moisture from the glacier — and light drops, and the mosses slow, and the woody stems pause their growth, and the basin enters a state that is not dormancy but something close to it: a reduced metabolic state, a holding pattern, a held breath.

And there is a fourth cycle, less regular, more dramatic. Occasionally — perhaps once a decade, perhaps less frequently — the temperature drops below freezing for a sustained period, and the water in the root lattice freezes. Not the deep water, not the water in the pond — but the water in the small channels, the films of water on the moss surface, the condensation in the enclosed chambers. When this happens, the ice expands, stressing the root lattice, cracking the woody stems, opening new channels and new fractures in the structure. The damage is not catastrophic — the structure is redundant, overbuilt, with so many interconnected stems that the failure of any one does not compromise the whole. But the damage is real, and in the thaw that follows, the new channels admit fresh water, the new fractures admit new light, and the basin's architecture shifts — slightly, incrementally, in ways that accumulate over centuries. The place is, in a sense, designed by ice. The ice breaks it, and the plants rebuild it, and the rebuilding is always slightly different from what preceded it, and the structure evolves.

## The built

What makes this place remarkable — what elevates it above a mere wetland or a biological curiosity — is the degree to which the plant growth is organized. This is not a random accumulation of vegetation. The columns are spaced. The arches are curved. The chambers are enclosed. The galleries are aligned. There is structure here — actual architecture — and it is architecture that was built without hands, without tools, without intention as we understand it.

The mechanism is straightforward, if not simple. The woody plants grow toward light and toward moisture. They grow away from their own shade. They graft with neighbors when they touch, because the graft-union strengthens both stems and allows them to grow taller than they could alone. And the pattern of growth — the spacing, the alignment, the shape of the chambers — emerges from the interaction between these simple rules and the physical environment. The columns are spaced the way they are because that is the optimal density for light penetration to the lower layers. The arches curve the way they do because the stems grow toward the brightest available light, which is directly overhead, and the curve is the path of least resistance. The chambers are enclosed because, in the densest parts of the structure, the stems grow inward as well as upward, seeking any available light, and the inward growth eventually closes the dome.

But the result feels designed. Walking through the galleries, passing through the chambers, you experience something that is uncannily architectural. The spaces are proportioned for passage. The ceilings are high enough to walk under. The walls are dense enough to block wind. The enclosed chambers are warm enough — by a degree or two — to feel different from the outside. These are spaces that function as shelter, whether or not anything uses them as shelter. They are infrastructure without inhabitants. They are buildings without tenants.

And there is evidence — not much, but some — that the structure has been used. In one chamber, near the center of the basin, the moss floor is worn away in a patch roughly the size of a sitting person. The woody root beneath is smooth, polished, as if something has sat there repeatedly, over a long period. In another chamber, there are marks on a column — not cuts, not carvings, but scratches, shallow grooves in the bark that have been grown over and re-opened, grown over and re-opened, in a cycle that suggests repeated contact over years. In a third chamber, a section of root floor has been reinforced — additional stems woven into the lattice, filling gaps, creating a drier, firmer surface than the surrounding floor. The reinforcement is old. The stems that were added have graft-joined with the original lattice. But the pattern of addition is visible: someone, at some point, improved this floor.

Who? What? There is no record. There are no bones, no artifacts, no tools, no hearths. Whatever used this place — whatever built this floor, whatever sat in this chamber — left no trace in the archaeological record because it used no tools and left no remains. It may have been human. It may have been something else. The place is old enough, and the organic substrate is thorough enough in its decomposition, that any evidence has been composted back into the system, recycled into root and moss and the amber-tinted water that flows through the lattice.

What remains is the structure itself. And the structure is — I keep returning to this word — built. It is built the way a coral reef is built: by the accumulated growth of organisms, over time, in response to physical conditions, without plan or intention. But it is also built the way a house is built: with spaces for habitation, with surfaces modified for use, with an organization that serves a function. The tension between these two kinds of building is the defining quality of the place. It is natural and constructed. It is grown and built. It is plant and architecture.

## The experience of being there

You move through the galleries. The light is green and dim. The air is wet and cold and smells of peat and sap. The moss beneath your feet is soft and yielding and soaked. The columns rise around you, their surfaces knuckled and calloused, sheathed in moss, beaded with condensation. Above, the arches curve and meet, forming a ceiling that is not quite closed — you can see the sky through gaps in the lattice, the pale grey of the haze above, filtered to a greenish tint by the stems and moss that intervene. Water drips on you. It drips constantly, from somewhere above, landing on your hair, your shoulders, the back of your hands. You stop wiping it away. There is no point. Everything here is wet.

You enter a domed chamber. The ceiling lowers, curves inward, closes. The light dims further — you can see your hands, but barely. The air changes: warmer, thicker, so humid that you feel you are breathing liquid. The sound changes: the trickling and dripping is contained here, echoed slightly by the domed ceiling, and the space has a resonance, a depth, that the open galleries lack. You hear the creaking of the woody structure — a low, slow sound, like a ship in a calm sea. You hear your own breathing, louder than it should be, reflected back to you by the close walls.

You sit. The moss is soft. It compresses under you, and water seeps up through it, cold against your legs. The root beneath is firm. You are sitting in a room that was built by plants, for no purpose that you can identify, in a place that no one seems to inhabit. You are surrounded by living tissue. The walls are alive. The floor is alive. The ceiling is alive. Everything in this room is growing, slowly, imperceptibly, adding cells, extending fibers, grafting, reaching. You are inside a living thing, and it does not know you are here, and it does not care, and it will continue to grow and to die and to be replaced long after you leave.

You sit in the green light. The water drips. The structure creaks. Something — a mite, a spider, a tardigrade — moves on the moss near your hand, too small to see, too small to hear, but present, alive, occupying its niche in this packed, saturated, fully-occupied world.

You are in a city of moss. You are in a cathedral of root. You are in a room that has no purpose and no architect and no inhabitant, and it is the most built place you have ever been.

The water continues to flow. The structure continues to grow. The light does not change.
