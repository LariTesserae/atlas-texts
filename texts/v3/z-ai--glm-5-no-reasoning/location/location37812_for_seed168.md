# The Growing Floor

High altitude carries a different quality of silence. Up here, where the air thins to barely a breath, sound dies before it can travel. Not the dead silence of vacuum—something else. A held silence. The kind that makes you aware of your own heartbeat.

The plateau stretches in every direction. Kilometers of it. The ground underfoot is warm, almost hot to the touch, radiating from below. Not volcanic. Something slower. Something patient. Heat rising through stone that has not yet decided what stone wants to be.

They call it the Growing Floor in the settlements below, though no one lives up here. Nothing lives up here. No animals. No insects. No birds crossing the sky. Just the green and the heat and the mineral churn.

---

The vegetation covers everything. Knee-high, thick-stemmed, rubbery to the touch. Not quite plant—more like the idea of plant, the template before nature filled in the details. Leaves without veins. Stems without nodes. The same three species repeating in mathematical patterns: the spiral-cluster, the fan-array, the vertical-cord.

Lush does not capture it. Lush implies fullness, abundance. This is *saturation*. Every square meter holds the same density of growth. The same height. The same color—that deep blue-green of photosynthetic tissue evolved for a light spectrum Earth never intended.

Walk through it and you feel the resistance. The stems bend and rebound. They don't break. They don't bruise. You leave no trail. Within minutes, the growth has erased you.

No water. Not a drop. No streams, no dew, no moisture in the air you can taste. The plants don't drink. They process something else through their rubbery stems—minerals pulled directly from the stone below, converting substrate into tissue through a chemistry that skipped the aqueous phase entirely. Dry photosynthesis. Mineral metabolism. The slowest kind of life, but persistent.

---

Stand still and you notice the erosion parameter—or its absence. The plateau has no features because features haven't formed yet. No valleys cut by ancient water. No ridges weathered by wind. The land is still becoming, still cooking, heat rising and cooling in cycles too slow to feel. The rocks beneath the vegetation are soft, almost chalky. Not sedimentary, not igneous. *Undifferentiated*. The raw material of geology before pressure and time choose its form.

In a million years, this will be mountains. In a million years, water will find it and carve it. But water has not found it yet. This is the world's embryo, the crust before it learned to be crust.

The scale works against you. You can see too far. The horizon sits lower than it should because the plateau is so high, and nothing interrupts your vision. No trees. No buildings. No haze. Just kilometers of blue-green carpet under pale sky, extending until the atmosphere's edge blurs the horizon into light.

You feel exposed. Not to predators—there are none—but to *space itself*. The emptiness presses. A landscape this vast and this empty makes you aware of your own volume, the space you displace, the anomaly of your presence.

---

No built structures. No ruins. No evidence that anything has ever stood here. Not even cairns, not even trails. The indigenous peoples of the region consider this place taboo—not for spirits, but for *absence*. You don't go where nothing has ever been. You don't walk on ground that hasn't finished becoming ground.

And yet—

The industrial signature persists.

It took months for the surveyors to understand what they were measuring. The instruments kept registering electromagnetic fields consistent with large-scale industrial operations. Generator hum. Transformer buzz. The magnetic anomalies of power distribution grids. But there were no grids. No generators. No structures.

The vegetation, it turns out, is the industry.

Each stem contains conducting tissue. Each leaf is a solar collector. The pattern of growth isn't random—it's a circuit, kilometers of parallel and series connections, power generation distributed across the plateau at a scale that makes human factories look like hobby projects. The heat from below drives a thermal differential. The mineral substrate provides conductor material. The light powers the whole apparatus.

Something planted this. Or someone. Or something else entirely, some process we lack the category for. Not agriculture. Not engineering. Some third thing, a kind of biological infrastructure that grows its own power grid and maintains it without water, without fauna, without intervention.

The plateau is a machine that learned to grow.

---

The quiet is the strangest part. Industrial zones hum. Power stations whine. Electromagnetic fields produce audible frequencies. But here, at altitude, the air is too thin to carry sound properly, and the vegetation's electrical activity operates at frequencies below human hearing.

Stand with your eyes closed and you feel it instead—the subsonic thrum of current moving through millions of stems, the warmth radiating from below, the faint static charge that makes the hair on your arms stand up. Not sound, but *presence*. The landscape is working. Processing. Converting stone to energy in patient, silent increments.

Some surveyors report headaches after long exposure. Others describe a meditative calm, a clarity they can't find at lower altitudes. The brain, deprived of its usual sensory anchors—no animal sounds, no wind through leaves, no water noise—starts to fill gaps with its own products. You begin to hear things that aren't there. Or maybe you begin to hear things that *are* there, just outside your species' bandwidth.

---

The plateau never changes. This is what disturbs the surveyors most.

Return after a year and the vegetation stands at the same height. The same three species. The same patterns. No seasonal variation—there are no seasons up here, just the constant warmth and the constant light and the constant hum of the power-grid-forest, growing in place without growing, processing without consuming.

Equilibrium, but the wrong kind. Most equilibrium is dynamic—populations rising and falling, weather shaping and reshaping. This is *static* equilibrium. Frozen. The vegetation doesn't die and doesn't reproduce. It simply *persists*.

Agricultural scientists have taken samples. The stems contain no seeds, no spores, no reproductive structures. This isn't a population. It's an installation. A system placed here and left to run.

---

The warming stations at the plateau's edge employ technicians who monitor the output curves. They've learned to predict solar flares from fluctuations in the vegetation's electromagnetic signature. They've learned to forecast earthquakes from subtle shifts in the thermal pattern rising from below.

The plateau doesn't care about any of this. It processes. It persists. It waits.

For what, no one knows. Maybe nothing. Maybe the system is complete as-is, a self-maintaining power infrastructure that requires no maintenance, no fuel, no attention. Maybe the plateau is a leftover from something else, a tool that outlived its makers, running its program on automatic.

Or maybe it's not finished yet. Maybe the Growing Floor is still growing, still cooking, still becoming whatever its designers intended. In another million years, when the water finally finds this place and the erosion begins and the stone learns to be stone—what will the plateau be by then?

The technicians don't speculate. They monitor. They record. They keep their stations at the edge and never walk far into the blue-green silence.

Some things, they've learned, are best left to finish themselves.
