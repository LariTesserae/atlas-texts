The ground beneath your feet is ceramic. Not ceramic-like—actual ceramic, vast cream-colored tiles laid in irregular polygonal patterns that stretch toward every horizon, each seam grouted with something darker, flexible, almost organic. You kneel and touch the join. It gives slightly beneath your fingernail. Warm.

The heat rises through the tiles. It is always rising. The air sits thick and wet in your lungs, heavy with moisture that has nowhere to go, suspended in a bright haze that softens the far distance into a pale shimmer. The sun directly overhead—there is always a sun directly overhead, you realize, though you cannot remember seeing it move—presses down like a hand, pressing the heat into everything.

Water weeps from the constructed world. It does not rain; the tiles themselves are crying. You see it now: moisture beading along the upper edges of walls, gathering into droplets that slide down in slow, deliberate paths, leaving darker trails behind them. The water moves like it's thinking. Pools have collected in the shallow depressions between tiles, perfectly still, their surfaces reflecting the white sky so precisely they look like holes cut into reality.

There are walls everywhere. Not buildings—walls. Freestanding, purposeless, constructed of the same pale ceramic as the ground, rising in smooth planes to heights that vary from knee-level to three times your height. They do not form rooms or corridors; they simply *are*, scattered across the landscape in arrangements that feel almost meaningful, like the work of a planner whose logic you cannot access. Some run straight for dozens of meters before terminating abruptly. Others curve in gentle arcs that lead nowhere. A few form tight spirals, inward-turning, the space at their centers too narrow for a body to enter.

Everything is constructed. You press your palm against a wall. No texture of natural stone, no variation, no grain—just smooth, warm, artificial surface extending down, presumably, to whatever lies beneath the tiles. You have walked for what feels like an hour and have not found a single thing that grew. This is not a landscape; it is a *floor*.

And yet it is lush.

The vegetation erupts from the joins. Wherever two tiles meet, wherever a wall meets the ground, wherever moisture collects in the angled corners of constructed surfaces, life spills upward in tangled exuberance. The plants here have never seen soil. They root in the dark, flexible grout, drawing sustenance from something beneath the ceramic shell of the world—water, certainly, and perhaps nutrients dissolved in it, some underground reservoir feeding this explosion of green. The leaves are enormous, some as broad as your torso, glossy and dark and dripping with condensed humidity. Vines drape from the tops of walls, curtains of varied greens—sage, forest, the yellow-green of new growth—hanging motionless in the still air.

Nothing moves. The air is windless. The leaves hang exactly as they hang. The water droplets slide down walls at their own slow pace, but the plants do not sway, do not shiver, do not respond to any stimulus you can detect. It is as if motion here must be earned, must have reason.

You walk between the walls, and the space opens around you. The walls are sparse enough that you can see far—the region extends past your immediate view, and from a slight rise in the tile-topography you can see the pattern of it all: the pale geometric scatter of walls like bones left by some enormous beast, the green pools of vegetation clustering in their shadows, the glint of standing water catching the unchanging light. A valley-sized space, perhaps. A town-sized absence of purpose.

You walk closer to a cluster of walls that form an almost-enclosure, a space with a narrow opening, and the sound changes. There has been almost no sound—your footsteps on tile, the soft click of your clothing, your own breath—but here, in this semi-enclosed space, there is less. The air feels thicker. The silence presses against your eardrums. You realize you are hearing the absence of echoes, the constructed surfaces absorbing sound rather than reflecting it. The walls want quiet. The world was made to hold silence.

Near the standing water, you find the fauna.

Insects, first—iridescent beetles the size of your thumb, moving with slow deliberation across the wet tile surfaces. They do not scatter at your approach. They do not react. They move in straight lines, following paths you cannot see, and when they reach a wall they climb it with the same unhurried purpose. Some have gathered at the water's edge, drinking, their bodies bent to the surface. They shine in the bright light—green and copper and something almost purple, colors that do not quite belong to any beetle you have seen.

And the fish.

The pools are not stagnant. You stand at the edge of a larger one, perhaps four meters across, and watch the shapes moving beneath the surface. Pale things, translucent, their bodies long and flexible like eels but moving with a grace that seems less animal than *designed*. They glide. There is no other word. They do not dart or swim; they glide through the still water, turning in slow spirals, their fins like brushed silk. When one breaks the surface, you see that its head is smooth, featureless—no eyes, no mouth that you can discern. It simply *is*, a presence in the water, gliding, turning, gliding.

You feel the edges of things.

It begins subtly—a sense that the wall to your left extends further than it appears to, that if you reached out your hand it would meet surface before you expected. You do reach, and your fingers touch ceramic that was not visible a moment before. The geometry is...wrong. Or not wrong, precisely, but *folded*. The walls do not stand in simple three-dimensional space. They occupy positions that shift depending on angle, distance, the quality of light falling on them. You walk around a tight spiral wall and find yourself, after three full rotations, standing where you began—but the view is different now. The walls behind you have rearranged themselves.

Time does not move here, but space does.

You settle onto your knees beside a pool, watching the fish glide, watching a beetle drink, watching the water bead and slide down the nearest wall. The heat presses against your skin. The humidity settles into your clothes. The bright light illuminates everything with an evenness that feels like attention—like the world is being *looked at*, evenly, completely, by something that does not blink.

This is what you understand, as you sit in the quiet:

The world was made. All of it. The tiles, the walls, the grout that holds moisture and roots, the pools of still water with their pale gliding inhabitants, the beetles with their purposeful paths—everything here was constructed, placed, arranged according to a logic that belongs to something other than human need. The plants grew *after*. The life came to fill the space. But the space itself—this valley of pale walls and crying ceramic—was *built*.

And it was built to be still.

The heat does not change. The water does not rise or fall. The fish glide in their eternal spirals and the beetles walk their straight lines and the leaves hang motionless in the thick wet air, and the whole constructed landscape holds itself in a perfect equilibrium that does not need you, does not respond to you, does not change for your presence.

You could stay here forever. The thought comes unbidden. You could become part of the stillness—a body beside the pool, motionless, the heat pressing down, the moisture condensing on your skin until you, too, begin to weep.

The wall behind you has moved closer while you weren't looking.
