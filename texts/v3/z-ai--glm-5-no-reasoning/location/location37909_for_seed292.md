# The Drowned Foundries

The water falls upward here.

Not all of it—most follows the ancient channels carved into the mountainside, rushing down through the gutted factories in sheets and torrents. But in certain chambers, certain moments, the hydrodynamics invert. Columns of water rise in spiraling pillars, defying gravity for seconds at a time before collapsing back into the general chaos. The old engineers called them "breathings"—the mountain exhaling through the machinery of its own slow drowning.

This is a place where a temperate rainforest has colonized the ruins of industry, and now both are drowning together.

---

The elevation change is extreme—nearly three thousand vertical feet of mountainside, terraced and blasted and built-over through centuries of expansion. Once, this was the anchor point for an entire region's production. Smokestacks rose from the cliff face like organ pipes. Freight elevators hauled raw ore up from the valleys below; finished goods descended through a series of lock-gates to the river. The mountain was a machine, and the machine was a mountain.

Now the elevators hang rusted in their shafts, cables snapped or sagging, platforms wedged at odd angles where they finally caught on something. Water finds them anyway. It always does.

The rain is constant—not violent, not quite, but inexorable. A grey soft roar that permeates everything, defines the acoustic space, becomes the silence against which other sounds distinguish themselves. You learn to read it: the way the pitch shifts when wind drives sheets against metal versus stone versus leaf-crowd, the way sudden silences mean the wind has changed direction, the way a thunderclap of impact means a mudslide somewhere in the upper works.

---

The vegetation is impossibly lush. Ferns chest-high in the lower galleries, their fronds slick with the perpetual mist. Moss that carpets every horizontal surface and most vertical ones, inches thick, spongy underfoot, growing in gradients from deep green where water pools to yellow-green where it runs to grey-green where it only mists. Vines have found every crack and gap in the factory walls—they descend through broken skylights, they emerge from burst pipes, they twine around the dead machinery in slow embrace.

Some of the machines have become almost unrecognizable. A generator housing becomes a planter, its iron casing split by root-action, soil accumulating from decades of leaf-fall inside what was once a precision-engineered chamber. A conveyor belt becomes a terrace, its rubber surface rotted away but its metal frame providing structure for soil and saplings. The forest doesn't merely grow here; it incorporates. It builds itself from the bones of the industrial age.

There are orchids. Tiny white ones, nearly transparent, clinging to the rusted pipes in the upper levels where more light reaches. And in the deeper flooded chambers, where the water rises and falls with the rains, there are lilies that bloom in sudden profusion during the dry spells—brief explosions of pink and white against the black water, gone again within days when the floods return.

---

The water: 2.8 on the scale. Almost maximum. This place is more water than not-water.

The rainfall alone would be enough to define it, but the hydrology is more complex than precipitation. The mountain catches weather systems from two directions, and the upper slopes channel everything downward through the old industrial infrastructure. There are moments—days, sometimes weeks—when the lower factories flood completely. The water rises past the second-story windows, submerges the ground-floor machinery, transforms the galleries into something like underground lakes. Then it drains again, slowly, through the lock-gates that still function (rebuilt by someone, at some point, with scavenged parts and practical intent), leaving behind mud-slick floors and the phosphorescent residue of algae blooms.

But there's also water *inside* the mountain. Aquifers breached by mining. Pipes burst and never repaired. The deep pumps that once kept the lower levels dry have been silent for decades, and now the groundwater rises at its own pace, filling the subterranean chambers from below. In the lowest accessible levels—the ones that haven't collapsed entirely—you can hear the water moving. A slow dark current, patient, taking back what was stolen from the stone.

---

The temperature hovers at the edge of uncomfortable. Not cold enough to be dangerous, not warm enough to be pleasant. The dampness makes it worse; the humidity sits around ninety percent, and nothing ever fully dries. Condensation beads on every surface. Your clothes cling. Your skin prickles with the perpetual moisture. At night, in the upper levels where the wind cuts through the broken walls, it becomes genuinely cold—a wet chill that settles into bone.

---

The density of life here is overwhelming. Not just plants; the fauna is abundant, layered, in constant noisy motion.

Insects first: the air thick with them, water striders on every pooled surface, clouds of midges in the sunlit galleries, beetles iridescent and quick in the shadows. Spiders have colonized the abandoned spaces, their webs catching moisture in delicate nets that sparkle briefly when light finds them before dissolving under the next rainfall.

Birds have taken over the upper works. Swallows nest in the gaps between ceiling panels, darting out through broken windows to catch insects on the wing. Their calls echo through the chambers, multiply, become a constant chittering backdrop. Larger birds—herons, sometimes—stand motionless in the flooded lower levels, fishing in the industrial pools. There are rumors of eagles nesting in the highest ruined smokestacks, but no one has confirmed it.

Mammals too. Rats, certainly, thriving in the dense vegetation, swimming through the flooded chambers with ease. But also larger things: feral goats on the terraced slopes above, descendants of escaped livestock, picking their way along rusted catwalks with unnerving confidence. And something else, something that moves through the upper forests at night, leaving tracks in the mud and occasionally screaming territorial challenges across the valleys. The workers who still maintain the functioning lock-gates say it's wild pigs. Others aren't so sure.

---

The built environment: 0.6. Scattered, partial, remains.

Most of the factories are shells. Roofs gone, walls buckled, floors collapsed into the levels below. But "most" is not "all." There are still functional spaces, still working mechanisms, still evidence that someone keeps a minimal operation running.

The lock-gates are the key. Seven of the original twelve still function, their mechanisms rebuilt with scavenged parts, their chambers cleared of debris by hand. Someone maintains them. Someone opens and closes them at the right moments, managing the flood cycles, preventing the worst of the damage. The system works—a slow breathing rhythm of water rising and falling through the industrial body.

There are living spaces, too. A few. A watchman's cabin near the uppermost functioning gate, its chimney smoking during the cold nights. A cluster of sheds near the mid-level pump station, where the water is clean enough to drink. A workshop where parts are fabricated from salvaged metal, crude but serviceable. Maybe a dozen people total, maybe fewer, scattered across the mountain like the buildings themselves.

They don't talk about who they are or why they stay. They trade information grudgingly, share food more freely, and know the drowned foundries better than anyone alive. They are practical about the chaos, superstitious about the breathings, and utterly uninterested in the past. The ruins are just walls to them. The machinery is just material. The forest is just an obstacle and a resource.

---

The soundscape is loud, layered, overwhelming.

The rain, first and always: that constant grey roar, shifting in intensity but never ceasing. It produces a kind of auditory fatigue; after hours, you stop hearing it consciously, but when it briefly stops (rare, but it happens), the silence is shocking, almost painful in its emptiness.

Against that backdrop: the water moving. Rushing through channels, gurgling through broken pipes, thundering over the spillways during peak flow. The mechanical groans of the lock-gates as they open and close. The slow drip of condensation from every surface, the sudden splash of accumulated water finally reaching critical mass and falling from some high ceiling to the flooded floor below.

And life. The birds, the insects, the occasional animal cry from the forest. The frogs that have colonized every pooled surface, their calls rising to choruses at dusk and dawn. The crickets that sing through the night. The sudden rustle of something large moving through the underbrush when you startle it.

And machinery. Some of it still runs. A generator in the mid-levels, patched and rebuilt, providing power to the functioning pumps. Its rhythm is uneven, stuttering sometimes, but it runs. A few of the conveyor belts have been jury-rigged for the salvage operation, moving parts and materials between levels. They screech and complain, metal on metal, but they work. Occasionally, something explodes—a pipe burst, a valve fail—and the noise is tremendous, echoing through the chambers, provoking bird-flurries and animal cries.

---

The turbulence here is near-maximum. 2.9. The place is fundamentally unstable.

The mountain itself is still settling. Small tremors are common; larger ones happen every few years, collapsing walls, redirecting water channels, opening new passages and closing old ones. A section of the upper works slid into the valley last decade, taking a functioning workshop with it. The slide scar is still visible, raw earth and broken concrete, now being claimed by fast-growing vines.

The water levels change constantly. Not just the seasonal floods, but daily, hourly, sometimes minute-to-minute. A blockage upstream (debris, animal nest, collapsed section) sends a surge through the system. A blockage clears and the water drops suddenly, stranding fish in suddenly-shallow pools. The breathings come without warning, those impossible upward columns, lasting seconds or minutes before collapsing.

The ecosystem itself is turbulent, competitive, always in flux. Invasive species arrive and struggle for dominance. Disease outbreaks sweep through insect populations. A dry year changes the balance of power between water-loving and drought-tolerant plants. The forest is winning now, but in twenty years, the factory ruins might have crumbled past the point of providing structure. Or a massive flood might scour the vegetation entirely. Or the mountain might finally give way.

---

There are no rules here, only patterns.

The workers who maintain the lock-gates have learned to read the signs. A particular quality to the rain-smell means flooding within hours. The swallows flying low means insects hatching in the lower galleries. The breathings only happen when the groundwater reaches a certain pressure threshold, and even then, only in particular chambers. There's a logic to it, but it's a logic of observation, not prediction. The drowned foundries do what they do, and you adapt or you leave.

The occasional visitors come for salvage, for research, for the peculiar beauty of the place. Some stay longer than they intended. The density of experience—the overwhelming input of sound and moisture and green life and industrial decay—produces a kind of trance state. Hours pass without notice. You find yourself standing in a flooded gallery, watching light refract through broken windows onto water and moss and rust, and you don't remember arriving.

---

The light is "normal" only in the sense that it follows familiar laws. But the light *here*—the particular quality of it—is strange. Filtered through constant cloud cover, diffused by the mist, reflected and refracted by water at every scale, it produces effects that feel unlikely. Rainbows in the spray. Sunbeams that seem to come from multiple directions at once. The green-gold underwater glow in the flooded chambers when the sun finds a gap in the clouds. The way shadows in the forest move independently of the visible sun, responding to the shifting canopy above.

At night, the darkness is absolute in the lower levels, but the upper works glow faintly with bioluminescence—moss and algae and some kind of fungus that's colonized the metal surfaces, producing a soft blue-white light that pulses slowly, rhythmically, like breathing.
