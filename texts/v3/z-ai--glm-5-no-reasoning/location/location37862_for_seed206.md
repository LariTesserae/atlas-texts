# The Engine Hollow

It exists inside a hill. Not built into it—*inside*, the way a cyst exists inside tissue. A pocket. A chamber that shouldn't be there, sealed and discovered rather than constructed.

---

The entrance is a root gap. You crawl through it, hands pressing into bark that gives like wet leather, and then you're inside and the sound hits you.

*Sound* isn't the word. This is *presence*. A roar so constant it has texture. It presses against your eardrums, fills the hollow behind your sternum, settles into your bones until you understand: this is what your own heartbeat would sound like from the inside, if you could hear it properly.

The chamber is small. Thirty feet across, perhaps. Dome-shaped. The ceiling presses low enough that you could touch it with your fingertips if you stood on toe-point. But the walls aren't walls. They're root mass. Hundreds of roots, maybe thousands, each one thick as a thigh, pale as the flesh beneath bark, interlaced in a weave so dense it might as well be solid. They curve and intersect, forming the dome. They radiate from a central point somewhere above you, where they all meet in a knot you can't quite see.

The roots are wet. They're always wet. They *weep*—slow tears of clear, warmish fluid that slides down the pale surfaces and collects in the creases where root meets root. The air is thick with it. Not fog, exactly, but *saturation*. Your skin becomes damp within seconds. Your breath fogs if you exhale slowly. The condensation beading on your arms catches the light.

And there is light. Normal light—the kind you'd find under a forest canopy on an overcast afternoon. Green-tinted, diffuse, sourceless. It seems to emanate from the roots themselves, or perhaps from the lichen that coats them in patchy, living velvet. The lichen is pale sage, dusty silver, occasionally flushed with rust where something in the air touches it differently. It's soft to the touch, like the short fur on a moth's wing.

---

The floor is water. Not *water* as you'd think of a pool or a puddle—this is water that has become substrate. It's shallow, barely ankle-deep, and covered in a living mat of duckweed so dense it reads as solid ground until you step on it and sink through the green surface into the warmth beneath. The duckweed is the smallest layer. Underneath it, filamentous algae in hair-fine strands, floating suspended. Underneath *that*, the true bottom: a mat of decomposing plant matter, leaves and stem fragments and the soft debris of seasons, compressed into something approaching solidity. You walk on it and it yields. Each step sends ripples outward through the duckweed, tiny displacements that travel to the walls and bounce back.

The water is warm. Body temperature. It smells like green things living and green things dying—chlorophyll and decay in equal measure. Neither smell is unpleasant. Together they become something else, something you stop noticing after a minute because it's just *air*, just *here*, just the smell of the place where you are.

---

The sound. The sound is the engine.

You can't see it at first. You have to wade to the center of the chamber, duckweed parting around your shins, and look down through the water. There, beneath the organic floor, something else.

Metal. Iron, specifically, dark with rust and age. A housing. A shape that might once have been cylindrical but has been reshaped by pressure and time into something organic-seeming. Pipes emerge from it in four directions, burrowing into the root-walls where they connect to something beyond this space. And it turns. Slowly. Relentlessly. A rotation so steady it has clearly been happening for longer than you can imagine—a patient mechanical heartbeat, driven by something that needs no fuel you can identify.

The engine makes the roots hum. The vibration travels through them, through the water, through your body. It's not unpleasant. It's like being held. The sound is a chord, layered over itself, tones so low they're felt rather than heard, overtones that shimmer and resolve and shimmer again.

It never changes. The chord is constant. The engine's rotation is constant. This moment and this moment and this moment are the same moment, repeating forever. Time here is a circle, or perhaps doesn't exist at all.

---

The animals don't care.

There are so many of them. This small space, this pocket-world, holds more life than the forest outside by orders of magnitude. Amphibians dominate—frogs of a size you've never seen, their bodies the size of dinner plates, their voices part of the engine's chord or perhaps harmonics of it. They sit on the roots, suction-cup feet adhering to the wet surfaces, throats pulsing in rhythms that feel synchronous. They're green-brown-gold, mottled, perfectly camouflaged except when they move. Which they rarely do. They're waiting for something that never comes, or that's always coming, or that's already here.

Salamanders thread through the water. Bright orange, electric blue, colors that seem impossible in this filtered light but persist anyway, like they're lit from within. They're curious. They approach your ankles, your fingers, taste your skin with tiny tongues, decide you're not food, move on. They've never seen a human. They have no fear. They've never learned it.

Insects carpet every surface that isn't underwater. Water striders dimple the duckweed's surface. Beetles with shells like polished bronze navigate the root-walls, feeding on the lichen. Moths the color of old paper cling to the dome's upper reaches, their wings slightly open, drinking the humidity. Dragonflies patrol, fierce and iridescent, landing on the engine's exposed housing to rest before resuming their circuits.

And there are things that don't have names. Isopod-like creatures, pale as the roots, segmented and leggy, that move through the decomposing mat beneath you. Something like a snail but without a shell, translucent enough to see its internal organs working, that leaves a luminescent trail on the surfaces it crosses. A life-form you can't categorize—plant? animal? something else?—that anchors to the root-walls and extends feathery feeding structures into the water, drawing nutrients from the duckweed mat. It pulses. When you touch it, it retracts, waits, extends again.

---

The chamber was not built. This is the crucial fact. The engine does not belong here, but it didn't come from outside. It was *grown around*. The roots incorporated it. The water adapted to it. The ecosystem stabilized into this arrangement—the engine provides something, warmth perhaps or vibration or some quality to the water that life finds essential, and life in turn maintains the chamber, keeps the roots interlaced and the water flowing and the organic mat thick enough to insulate the machinery beneath.

It's a partnership. Or a parasitism. Or something for which no word exists because no word was needed—the engine and the wetland are the same entity now, have been for longer than the trees outside have been growing, will be for longer still. The factory that made the engine is gone. The species that built the factory is gone. The language they spoke is gone. But the engine remains. The chamber remains. The chord sounds.

---

Nothing changes. This is not stasis—stasis implies something preserved against its nature. This is *equilibrium*. Perfect, dynamic, alive. The engine turns at the same rate it has turned for millennia. The water stays at the same depth, fed by the weeping roots, drained by channels you can't see. The life-forms birth and die and birth again at replacement rate. The lichen spreads and recedes in patterns that repeat annually, though you'd need to watch for months to see them. The frogs sing their part of the chord. The salamanders taste your skin and move on. The duckweed closes over your footprints.

You could stay here. That's the dangerous thought. You could sit on one of the root-ledges, let the water warm your legs, let the chord fill you until you stopped hearing it as sound and started feeling it as *self*. The air would keep you hydrated. The water would sustain you somehow—you sense it, that there's something in it, some quality that feeds more than hunger. The animals would accept you. You'd become part of the equilibrium, another element in the pocket, another voice in the chord.

But that would be wrong. Not morally—there's no morality here, nothing so human. Wrong in the sense of *incorrect*, a note out of place. You're not part of the system. You entered through the root gap. You can leave through the root gap.

You think this, and you turn, and you do.

---

The sound stays with you for hours afterward. A hum in your bones. A warmth in your chest. The sense, never quite fading, that you're still inside, still held, still part of the equilibrium that has no need for you but tolerated you anyway.

The forest outside is quiet by comparison. Too quiet. You find yourself listening for something that isn't there.

Days later, you'll dream of it. You'll wake with the taste of warm water in your mouth, duckweed fragments on your pillow, and you'll understand: the chamber isn't a place you visited. It's a place that took a sample of *you*.

You don't know what it's growing from that sample.

You're not sure you want to know.
