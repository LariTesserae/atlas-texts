# The Flooded Manufactory of Kassen-Verd

The sound reaches you before the water does.

It is not one sound. It is ten thousand sounds compressed into a single pressure that enters through the chest cavity before the ears can parse it. Metallic percussion, enormous and arrhythmic. The hiss of superheated fluid meeting cold. A low harmonic drone that seems to originate from the mineral substrate itself, as though the earth below the water is humming at a frequency designed to loosen tooth from bone. Overlaid on all of this: a crackling, like static discharge, like the sound a power station makes when it is working properly, and also the sound it makes when it is not.

Then the water. Always the water.

---

Kassen-Verd is not a city. It is not a factory. It is what happens when the distinction between those two things fails under thirty meters of warm saltwater and keeps failing for what appears to have been a very long time.

The depth here is inconsistent. That is the first wrong thing. You can stand in a corridor where the water reaches your chin, take three steps, and find the ceiling only two meters above the floor — the water does not drain, does not compress, simply occupies a space that should be full of air as though air were a courtesy that has been revoked. Then three more steps and the corridor opens into a flooded chamber so vast the surface is a memory, a brightness above that might be light or might be the neurological phenomenon of oxygen deprivation. You learn quickly not to trust depth. You learn that depth here is a negotiation, and the water is not a party to it.

The temperature is tropical. The water sits at thirty-four, thirty-five degrees — bathwater, incubator warmth, the temperature at which the human body stops being able to shed heat through the skin. Sweat is meaningless. The warmth enters through every surface and stays. After an hour your core temperature rises. After two, you begin to feel the first neurological tremors, though it is possible these are not thermal in origin. It is possible these are the place itself, pressing against the inside of your skull to see what shapes fit.

---

## The Structures

There were buildings here. Are buildings here. The tense is difficult because the structures are not consistent in their relationship to time.

The dominant architecture is industrial: poured concrete walls stained olive-black with algae and something else, something that stains in branching patterns like lichtenberg figures burned into the mineral surface. Steel beams, riveted, enormous — the kind of structural members that belong to plants built for heavy production, built to last, built by engineers who assumed the work would continue forever and designed accordingly. The beams are warped. Not rusted — warped, bent into curves that steel should not accept without fracture, as though the water pressure has reheated them to forging temperature and patiently, over years, insisted on new geometries.

Doorways exist. They do not always lead to rooms. Some open onto more water, which is expected, but the water beyond the doorway is a different temperature, or a different color — there are bands of opacity here, the flooded equivalent of weather fronts, where silty water meets clear water and the boundary holds like a membrane. You can put your hand through it. It is like passing through a layer of warm glass that is not there. On the other side, the sound changes. Always the sound changes.

There are fewer structures than there should be for a place this size. The scale is open — the flooded chambers connect to larger flooded chambers, which connect to flooded courtyards where the ceiling is the surface, visible as a silver-gold membrane overhead, and beyond those, more chambers. But between the structures there are gaps, absences, places where a building should be and is not, where the water simply extends with nothing in it but the drowned vegetation and the light. The impression is of a city that has been partially dissolved. Not demolished — dissolved, the way acid removes material incrementally, leaving the adjacent structures standing in their original footprint, untouched, as evidence of what was adjacent.

One structure recurs. A tower, or what remains of one — a cylinder of reinforced concrete, maybe eight meters in diameter, rising from the flooded floor to something above the surface. There are four of them visible from any given vantage, arranged in a pattern that suggests a grid, though the grid's logic degrades the further you move from any single tower. Each one hums at a different pitch. Together they produce a chord. The chord is not pleasant. The chord is the dominant sonic element in the lower chambers, the bass note that you feel in your long bones before you hear it.

## The Vegetation

This is what makes Kassen-Verd difficult to describe to anyone who has not been inside it, because the vegetation does not behave the way vegetation should in thirty meters of warm saltwater with almost no light.

It is lush. That word is insufficient but accurate. The plant life — and it is plant life, or close enough; it photosynthesizes, or performs something analogous, or perhaps it does not require light at all and the word is a convenience — covers nearly every submerged surface that is not actively being eroded. The concrete pillars are wrapped in fronds the color of jade darkened to near-black, fronds that are broad and flat and move in the current like cloth, like fabric in slow wind. The steel beams host a different species: something crystalline, mineral in appearance, that grows in geometric patterns — hexagonal plates stacked in spirals, catching what little light exists and refracting it into brief, involuntary spectra.

The floor is where the growth becomes densest. Here the vegetation is layered: a base stratum of something like kelp, thick-stalked, reaching upward ten, fifteen meters, swaying in a current that comes from no single direction and changes every few seconds. Above the kelp, floating at mid-water, are colonies of something that might be algae and might be something else entirely — spheres the size of a human head, translucent, dark green, filled with gas and with something that moves inside them. Not animals. The fauna count here is zero. Inside the spheres, what moves is a kind of fluid that does not move with the current, that has its own circulation, its own purpose. The spheres reproduce. You can see this happening: one splits into two over the course of minutes, not hours, the division visible as a constriction that tightens and tightens until there are two where there was one. The new sphere is smaller. It grows. The water is full of them. They brush against you as you move, warm and yielding, and you feel the thing inside them press against the membrane where your arm touches, as though investigating.

The vegetation emits the light. This is important. The dimness here is not absence but suffocation — the ambient light from the surface is almost entirely consumed by the density of growth, but the plants themselves produce a low bioluminescence, a green-gold glow that is barely enough to see by, that turns everything the same color, that makes distance impossible to judge. You see a wall three meters away and it appears to be thirty meters away. You see a shape ahead and cannot tell if it is a structure or a plant formation or a trick of the light until you are close enough to touch it.

In the deeper chambers, where the surface light does not reach at all, the vegetation produces a different color. Blue. Not the blue of shallow bioluminescence but a deep, saturated blue that seems to generate shadow rather than light, that makes the spaces around it darker rather than brighter. The plants that produce this do not photosynthesize. You can tell by looking at them. They have the appearance of something that feeds on a different substrate entirely, something that has found a source of energy that is not the sun and is not chemical and is, possibly, the place itself.

## The Machinery

The industrial element is not separate from the vegetation. This is the second wrong thing, and it is more wrong than the first.

The structures contain machinery. Large machinery — the scale of production equipment, stamping presses, turbines, things with rotors the diameter of a city bus. The machinery is active. Not all of it, not all the time, but enough. Enough that the sound is constant, that the vibration travels through the water and the concrete and the steel and the vegetation and the human skeleton and does not stop.

The active machines are partly organic. This is not a metaphor. The turbine in Chamber 3 — the one nearest to what might have been the main entrance, the one that still has a sign above the waterline reading KASSEN-VERD PROCESSING FACILITY 7 in a font that was designed for legibility and is now almost entirely obscured by growth — that turbine has blades that are half steel and half chitin. The steel portion is original, stamped, riveted, manufactured. The chitin portion has grown. It is a continuous surface with the steel, not grafted but emerging, as though the metal itself has decided to become biological, or as though something biological has decided that the most efficient way to build a turbine blade is to begin with a steel one and convert it cell by cell.

The turbine turns. Slowly. It does not appear to be connected to a power source. It does not appear to be generating power. It turns because turning is what it does, and the thing that has grown into it has either restored its function or is its function now.

This is not an isolated phenomenon. Throughout the facility, machinery is being colonized, converted, hybridized. Conveyor belts with vine-belt drives. Electrical conduits replaced by what appear to be nerves — bundles of wet, translucent fiber that carry no current but carry something, because the lights in the walls still flicker, occasionally, and the lights are connected to the nerve-conduits and not to the original wiring, which has rusted to nothing.

There is a machine in Chamber 11 that appears to be manufacturing something. It is a press, a stamping press, and the vegetation has grown into its hydraulic systems and replaced them with pneumatic bladders that contract and expand with a slow, regular rhythm, and the press descends and rises, descends and rises, and what it stamps is a surface of living material, a mat of the crystalline growth, and each time the press descends it leaves an impression — a shape, a pattern, something that looks like a circuit board, or something that looks like a leaf, or something that looks like both.

The machine does not need to do this. Nothing is being produced. Nothing is being assembled from these stamped components. The press operates because the press exists, and the thing that has grown into it has inherited its purpose without inheriting its reason.

## The Sound

I need to describe the sound more precisely because the sound is the defining feature of Kassen-Verd and everything else — the water, the vegetation, the machinery — is organized around it.

The sound is layered. There are at least seven distinct strata:

1. **The harmonic drone.** This comes from the towers, from the four concrete cylinders that form the grid. It is a chord built from four tones, each slightly different, each slightly unstable. The chord shifts. Not in sequence — in unison, all four tones bending, exploring adjacent frequencies, then returning. The effect is of something searching for a resolution it cannot find. This is the lowest layer. You feel it in your teeth.

2. **The percussive layer.** This comes from the machinery. It is arrhythmic but not random — there are patterns that repeat at intervals too long to hold in short-term memory, phrases that last minutes before recurring, giving the impression of a composition that is being performed at a temporal scale the human ear was not designed to parse. Metal on metal. Metal on chitin. Chitin on living surface.

3. **The hydrostatic layer.** The sound of the water itself, which is not one sound but a continuous texture — the hiss of dissolved gases escaping from solution, the low moan of pressure differentials between adjacent chambers, the percussive slap of the surface fifteen, twenty meters above, where the water meets whatever weather exists outside.

4. **The biological layer.** The vegetation makes sound. The kelp-like growth on the floor produces a continuous low crackling as its structures grow and divide, cell walls forming and rupturing in the thousands per second. The spheres produce a tonal element — each sphere hums at a frequency related to its size, and the water is full of them, and the cumulative effect is a sound like a choir of glass, high and thin and almost outside the range of human hearing but not quite, present at the edge of perception where it becomes a pressure behind the eyes.

5. **The structural layer.** The concrete and steel make sound as they deform. This is a slow sound, a groaning that operates on a timescale of minutes, as the material accepts new shapes from the water pressure and the biological colonization. If you press your ear to a wall, you can hear it: the sound of a building changing its mind about what shape it is.

6. **The electrical layer.** Faint, intermittent, the crackling and popping of the nerve-conduit system carrying whatever it carries through the walls. This sound is closest to what lightning would sound like if lightning were very small and occurred inside the walls of a submerged building.

7. **The layer that should not be there.** This is the sound that has no source. It is a voice, or voices — not speaking, not singing, but performing an action that is adjacent to both. It is intermittent. It is quiet relative to the other layers, which is to say it is still loud enough to hear clearly, because the other layers are deafening and this layer exists at a volume that cuts through them. It comes from no direction. It comes from the water itself, or from the space the water occupies, or from somewhere else. The sound is not language. The sound is not music. The sound is the sound of information being processed by a medium that was not designed to process information and is doing so anyway.

## The Rules

I have not spoken about the rules because the rules are not clear, but there are things that are consistently true inside Kassen-Verd, and I will list them:

**Nothing here is dead.** The fauna count is zero — there are no animals, no fish, no insects, no organisms that move independently with purpose across the environment. But everything here is alive. The vegetation. The water, possibly. The machinery, in its hybrid state. The structures, in their slow deformation. The place has the quality of a living thing that is not an organism, that is a system, that is a body without anatomy.

**The water does not flow.** It moves. The currents are real, the pressure differentials are real, the temperature layers are real. But the water does not go anywhere. It does not drain. It does not circulate through any external system. It is a closed volume, and it has been a closed volume for long enough that the things living in it have forgotten, or never knew, that water can exist in a state of not-being-here.

**The structures are young.** This is the most difficult thing to explain because the evidence is contradictory. The concrete is fresh. You can find surfaces where the pour marks are still sharp, where the form-work grain is still visible in the surface, where the material has not yet begun the long process of carbonation and micro-fracture that defines aged concrete. The steel is new — not rusted, not fatigued, not degraded by the saltwater in the way that steel degrades over years. And yet the biological colonization is extensive, mature, complete. The vegetation has had time to establish complex communities, stratified ecosystems, reproductive cycles. The machinery has been hybridized at a depth that implies years of incremental growth.

The place is young and old simultaneously. It was built recently and it has been here for a very long time. The water is warm and has always been warm. The structures are fresh and they are already ruined. The vegetation is ancient and it is still arriving.

**The place is getting larger.** I cannot confirm this, but the impression is consistent across every observation point: there is more of Kassen-Verd than there was before. Not in the sense of discovery — in the sense of generation. New chambers, new corridors, new structures, appearing in spaces that were previously open water. The concrete is being poured by something. The steel is being forged by something. The machinery is being assembled by something, and the something is not human and is not animal and is not mechanical, or is all three, or is the system itself, the body, the place performing construction on its own body the way a body grows.

## What It Is Like To Be Here

You are always wet. You are always warm. You cannot see more than a few meters in any direction because the vegetation and the spheres and the bioluminescent haze create a visual field that is dense and close and claustrophobic even in the open chambers. You are always touching something — a frond, a sphere, a wall, a beam, a surface that is warm and yielding and may be plant or concrete or both.

The sound does not stop. You cannot make it stop. You cannot hear your own voice over it. You can hear your breathing, your heartbeat, because these sounds are inside you, but everything outside you is the sound, and the sound is the place expressing itself at a volume that suggests it is not interested in being ignored.

You do not see animals. You do not miss them. The absence is not felt as absence because the place is so full — full of growth, full of sound, full of motion, full of the slow violent energy of things being built and converted and stamped and divided and grown. The absence of fauna is not a gap. It is a choice. The place does not need animals because the place is performing the function of animals — consumption, conversion, production, circulation — at a level of integration that makes the distinction between organism and environment meaningless.

You move through Kassen-Verd and you have the sense of moving through the interior of a living process. Not a living thing — a living process. A verb rather than a noun. An action that has been ongoing for a duration that the young concrete contradicts and the ancient growth confirms, and the contradiction is not a contradiction, it is a condition, it is the way this place exists, new and old, fresh and ruined, building and built, at a volume and a density and a sound level that leaves no room for doubt that whatever is happening here is happening completely, is happening all at once, is happening whether you are here to observe it or not.

The light dims further as you move deeper. The blue-emitting vegetation takes over. The sound does not dim. The sound increases. The temperature stays the same — warm, always warm, the warmth of a body, the warmth of a process that generates heat as a byproduct of being itself.

You keep moving. There is no reason to stop. There is no reason to continue. The place does not notice you. The place is not unaware of you. The place is a system that includes you now, the way it includes the water and the concrete and the steel and the vegetation and the sound, and the inclusion is not hostile and is not welcoming and is not neutral. The inclusion is simply what happens. You are here. The place is here. The place is what is happening.

The sound continues. It has always been continuing.
