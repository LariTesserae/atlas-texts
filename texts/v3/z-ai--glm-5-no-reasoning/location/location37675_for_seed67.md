# The Congestive Plain

The first thing you notice is that nothing moves.

Not "nothing moves much." Nothing *moves*. The air has a temperature and a direction and it holds both of those facts with absolute commitment, the way a dead man holds his last expression. A warm current, faintly mineral, pushes east at perhaps three kilometers per hour, and it has been doing this long enough that the long metal grasses it bends have grown into permanent lean, each blade a fixed curve, a frozen wave in a field of frozen waves.

Everything here is made. Once you see it, you can't unsee it. The ground is not ground. The horizon is not horizon. What you took for a plain stretching to the edge of sight is a *surface* — fabricated, paneled, joined at the seams with the precision of a machinist who had centuries and no reason to hurry. The panels are vast, each one perhaps half a kilometer across, and they are not flat but very slightly concave, so that water — and there is water everywhere, beading, pooling, sheeting — collects in their centers in shallow circular ponds that look, from a height, like the irises of enormous eyes. The whole plain blinks with reflected light.

---

The water has nowhere to go. That is the foundational rule of this place. The surface was engineered to hold it. Every panel dips toward its own center. Every seam is flush but sealed — a gasket of something dark and rubbery that has outlived whatever flexibility it once had. The water sits where it lands and it lands frequently. Not rain, exactly. The atmosphere above the plain is saturated past its carrying capacity and it condenses constantly, a fine precipitation that isn't quite falling and isn't quite forming in place — rather, the air simply *becomes wet*, as if wetness is its natural state and dryness would require an effort it has long since stopped making. The condensation happens on everything at once: on the panels, on the structures, on your skin, on the metal grass, on the carapaces of the things that live here in such numbers that the ground, when you look closely, is not ground at all but *beings*.

They are not animals. They are not machines. They are something the makers left behind that the makers did not intend to leave behind — a maintenance ecology that outlived its maintainers.

---

I need to describe the structures before the beings, because the beings only make sense in relation to the structures.

Rising from the paneled surface at irregular intervals — irregular, but not random; the spacing follows a logic that becomes visible only when you've walked for hours and your body starts intuiting the pattern in your legs — are vertical assemblies of a material that looks like concrete and behaves like concrete and is almost certainly not concrete. Dark grey, smooth, slightly warm to the touch. They are pillars, or they are stacks, or they are chimneys. The word depends on your angle. From a distance they look like chimneys: square-sided, thirty meters tall, evenly spaced across the plain in lines that recede to the horizon like a perspective exercise. Up close they look like pillars, because they support nothing. Inside — and you can hear this if you press your ear to the surface, which is warm and hums faintly — they contain machinery. Engines. Cylinders and pistons and great turning shafts that move with a slowness that makes geological time feel rushed. A single revolution of the main shaft in the nearest chimney takes, by my count, forty minutes. The next one turns at a different rate. They are not synchronized. They have never been synchronized, or they were synchronized so long ago that the synchronization has drifted into a new pattern so slowly that no one noticed the transition.

The chimneys breathe. Not metaphorically. The internal mechanisms process the pooled water — draw it up through intake valves at the base, run it through chambers that warm it and cool it and warm it again — and exhale it as steam from vents near the top. The steam rises perhaps ten meters before it stops rising. It hits the saturated air and dissolves back into it, returning the moisture it borrowed. This is a closed cycle. The water leaves the pond and returns to the air and the air returns it to the pond. The chimneys have been doing this for — I don't know. A long time. Long enough that the mineral content of the pond water has reached a specific equilibrium with the mineral content of the air and the mineral content of the chimney material itself. Everything has already exchanged everything it can exchange with everything else. The system is at equilibrium.

The system is, in a word, *done*.

---

And yet it teems.

The beings — I'll call them vessels, because "animal" and "machine" both mislead — are everywhere. Their density is the most overwhelming thing about this place, more overwhelming than the scale, which is already vast enough that the plain has its own weather. The curvature of the world is visible here, or rather the curvature of whatever this surface is — it bends away at the edges, and the chimneys recede until they are a texture, a grain, a line of dark dots against the lighter grey of the surface, and then they are gone, but they are still there, still standing, still breathing.

But the vessels. They cover the surface between the ponds. They climb the chimneys. They float in the shallow water. They occupy space the way liquid occupies a container — filling every available volume, pressed against each other, layered two and three deep in places, a living — a *functioning* — carpet that shifts only when disturbed and then only to settle back into the same arrangement.

They are roughly the size and shape of a human forearm. Dark, segmented, with a smooth hard casing that is the same grey as the chimneys — not because it was painted but because the material is the same, drawn from the same source, processed through the same system. They do not appear to eat. They do not appear to excrete. They do not appear to reproduce in any way I can observe. What they do is *attend*. They move toward the chimneys. They cluster at the intake valves. They enter the chimneys through maintenance apertures that were designed for tools, not for vessels, but the vessels are the tools now, or the tools' descendants, or the tools' replacements. Inside the chimneys, they do — something. The machinery does not function differently when they are present and it does not function differently when they are absent, but when they are absent from a chimney — when you find a chimney whose base has been cleared, which happens sometimes, for reasons — the internal temperature is 0.3 degrees lower. That is the only observable difference. 0.3 degrees.

They are keeping the chimneys warm.

Or: the chimneys are keeping them occupied.

Or: the distinction between the vessels and the machinery no longer means what it once did, and what I am looking at is a single organism that happens to have both metal and chitin — or metal and whatever this is, this hard grey casing — as its body.

---

The light here is ordinary. That is the strangest thing. After the scale and the saturation and the impossible equilibrium, the light is just *light*. A sun — or something functioning as a sun — sits at a fixed position in the sky, casting shadows that do not move. The light is warm and slightly amber and it catches the water on the panels and makes each pond a small bright circle, and it catches the vessels' carapaces and makes them gleam, and it catches the steam from the chimneys and turns it gold for the ten meters of its existence before it reabsorbs. The light is doing what light does. It is not part of the system. It illuminates the system from outside, like a lamp on a diorama, and this — the ordinariness of the light, its indifference — is what makes the place finally legible as a *place* and not a condition.

You are standing on something that was made. Something that was made to do something, and that is still doing that something, and that has been doing it so long that the doing has become the being, and the being has become the place, and the place is this: a plain of dark panels holding shallow water, breathing through chimneys, covered in vessels that attend to machinery that functions at a temperature 0.3 degrees warmer than it would without them, under a light that does not change, in an air that is always almost rain.

---

The sound. I should mention the sound.

There is almost none. The machinery turns so slowly that its movement is subsonic — you feel it in the panels through your feet more than you hear it. The vessels move, when they move, with a faint ticking, hard parts touching hard parts, but the movement is rare enough that the ticking comes once every several seconds, a single *tick*, then silence, then another *tick* from somewhere else, then a long silence that is not empty but *full* — full of the held breath of ten million vessels not moving, of a hundred thousand chimneys not quite turning, of air not quite raining.

The loudest sound is the water. Not falling water. Sitting water. The faint, almost imaginary sound of a film of condensation forming on a surface, which is not a sound at all but a presence in the ear, a density in the quiet, like the quiet itself is wet.

You stop walking. You stop talking. You stand and the plain does what it does, which is what it has always done, which is continue.

Nothing decays. Nothing erodes. The seams between the panels are as sharp as the day they were joined. The edges of the chimneys are clean. The vessels' carapaces are unscratched. This place is young — not in age but in condition. It emerged, or was activated, or was begun, and then it arrived at equilibrium, and then time stopped meaning anything, and it has been *this* — exactly this, precisely this, without drift or decay or the slow insult of entropy — ever since.

The plain does not need you. The plain does not notice you. The plain is not waiting.
