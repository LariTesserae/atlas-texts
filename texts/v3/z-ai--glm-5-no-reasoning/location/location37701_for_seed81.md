# The Condensate City of Vashunn

The first thing is the heat. Not the dry, forgiving heat of desert or kiln — this is greenhouse heat, Turkish-bath heat, the heat of air that has nowhere to go and knows it. It presses against the skin like a warm, damp hand. The second thing is the light: a sallow, directionless amber, as though the sun has been filtered through glass stained by decades of tobacco smoke and mineral dust. There *is* a sun, presumably, but you wouldn't know where. The sky is a low ceiling of iron-colored cloud, uniform and sagging, and the light that makes it through arrives exhausted, already half-spent.

---

## The City

Vashunn occupies a broad shelf of basalt high on the western flank of the Tsaari Ramp — a great lava terrace that climbs in steps from the lower badlands toward a ridgeline lost in cloud. The elevation would be bracing if the air weren't so thick. As it is, the altitude only means the clouds are *close*, almost tactile, dragging across the upper surfaces of the tallest structures like felt dragged across stone.

The city is old. Not ancient — not ruins — but *settled*. It has existed long enough to have found its shape and stopped reaching for others. The buildings are low and heavy, built from the basalt they stand on, fitted without mortar in the older districts and mortared with a pale calciumite paste in the newer ones. The walls are two meters thick at ground level, tapering to a meter at the rooflines. Few structures exceed three stories. The effect is a city that looks poured rather than built — dark, low, organic, as though something viscous had been allowed to settle and harden here.

The streets are narrow and covered. Not roofed, exactly, but bridged — the upper stories of facing buildings lean toward one another, connected by enclosed walkways and pipe runs until the sky is visible only in thin strips, like veins in dark marble. This was not aesthetic. This was to keep the rain off the machinery.

There are districts, or what were once districts. The eastern quarter holds the engine halls — long, shed-roofed buildings whose corrugated iron cladding has gone the color of old blood from the mineral-laden water that runs down them in constant thin films. The western quarter is residential, or was. The northern quarter is processing: kilns, separators, vats, most of them cold now, some still warm. The southern quarter is the reservoir district, and it is here that the city's reason for existing becomes apparent.

---

## Water

Vashunn is a city built to drink.

The Ramp is wet. Not wet on its surface — wet *in* it. The basalt is porous, vesicular, riddled with bubbles and fissures from its formation, and somewhere in its interior, at a depth no one has been able to map, there is water. A great deal of it. Not an aquifer in the traditional sense — the geology doesn't support a contained reservoir — but a saturated zone, a stratum of rock so impregnated with heated water that it might as well be a slow underground river. The water is thermal, mineral-heavy, faintly sulfurous, and under pressure. It finds its way upward through every fissure and vesicle in the basalt, emerging on the Ramp's surfaces as seeps, weeps, springs, and — in the places where the fissures are widest — as steady, steaming curtains that run down the rock faces year-round.

The city was built on one of the widest of these curtain-seeps. The southern quarter is a collection of cisterns, settling tanks, and distribution pipes that capture the water as it emerges, let it cool, let the minerals settle, and route it to the engine halls and the residences. The system is industrial in scale — iron pipes thick as a man's torso, flanged and bolted, running through trenches cut into the basalt and covered over with stone slabs. The pipes sweat. Everything sweats.

The atmosphere condenses. This is the third thing you notice, after the heat and the light: the moisture. Every surface in Vashunn is damp. The walls are damp. The streets are damp. The ceilings of the covered walkways bead with water that falls in fat, irregular drops — *tlock, tlock* — into puddles that never fully dry because the air is always at the dewpoint. The locals call it the Weep. The city weeps. The stone weeps. The iron weeps. The pipes weep. It is not rain — nothing is falling from the sky — but it is a wetness that comes from everywhere at once, an exhalation of the earth itself, and it means that nothing in Vashunn ever becomes fully dry. Clothes stay limp. Leather molds within days. Iron rusts in weeks. The basalt alone seems indifferent, holding the water on its surface without absorbing it, going dark and slick as oiled skin.

After rain — and it does rain, briefly and intensely, several times a day during the wet season — the Weep intensifies. The rain doesn't so much fall as condense out of the sagging clouds onto every available surface, and then the surfaces shed it, and the city becomes for an hour a place of running water, every street a shallow stream, every gutter a torrent, every pipe overflowing. Then the rain stops, and the Weep resumes its slower, constant work.

## What Lives Here

Almost nothing.

This is what makes Vashunn strange — not its architecture, not its heat, but its *emptiness*. The city was built for a population that is no longer present. The residential quarter has four thousand rooms. Perhaps three hundred are occupied. The streets are built for foot traffic and cart traffic, and you can walk for ten minutes without seeing another person. The engine halls, which once held six working engines, now have two. The others stand in their housings, massive iron things — reciprocating beam engines, scavenger-style, driven by the thermal water itself — cold and still, their brass fittings gone green, their cylinders housing nothing but damp air.

The people who remain are not caretakers. They are not mourners. They are *residents*. They live here the way the city lives here — because it is where they are, and moving would require a reason, and reasons have been in short supply for a long time. They work the two remaining engines. They maintain the pipes. They check the cisterns. They eat fish from the lower pools — a pale, eyeless thing, the only vertebrate that seems to have adapted to the thermal water, brought up in buckets and grilled on iron plates over the engine exhausts. They eat a type of mat — a fibrous, layered growth that appears on the basalt in the permanently wet zones, not plant, not fungus, something between, something that belongs to a kingdom that was never properly classified. It has no name. They call it *mat*. It tastes like wet chalk and sustains them adequately.

There are dogs. Three of them, lean and hot, that sleep in the engine halls and follow the workers on their rounds. They are not anyone's dogs. They are the dogs of Vashunn, as much a part of the city as the pipes.

There are insects — small, black, wingless things that live in the mineral crusts on the pipe flanges and emerge at night to walk on the wet walls. They do not seem to eat anything visible. They seem to eat the minerals themselves, scraping the calciumite deposits with mandibles adapted for exactly that purpose. They are endemic to the Ramp and found nowhere else. Nobody has named them. Nobody has needed to.

## The Machines

The two working engines are called Westfall and Greylag, names whose origins are unrecorded. They are beam engines — twenty-foot cast-iron beams rocking on massive pedestals, driven by thermal water flashed to steam in iron boilers, condensing in great cold-water jackets fed from the cisterns. The rhythm is slow: a four-second cycle, *shhhhh-CLUNK-shhhhh-CLUNK*, the beam rocking, the piston descending, the condenser hissing. The sound is the dominant sound of Vashunn. Not loud — the engines are well-muffled by their housings, and the thick stone walls damp them further — but pervasive. You feel it in the basalt under your feet before you hear it. A pulse. A heartbeat. The city has a heartbeat, and it is two iron beams rocking in two stone halls, and when one of them stops — which happens, which has happened, which will happen again — the silence is not peaceful. It is *wrong*. Like a body with one less heartbeat than it should have.

The engines do work. What work? The pumps. The pumps move water from the lower cisterns to the upper reservoirs, and from the upper reservoirs to the distribution pipes, and from the distribution pipes to the few occupied buildings and the few functioning workshops. The system is overbuilt for its current demand — the pipes could serve ten thousand people, and they serve three hundred — but the pumps run because the engines run, and the engines run because the thermal water provides pressure, and the thermal water provides pressure because the Ramp is saturated, and the Ramp is saturated because the geology of this place is what it is. The system is a closed loop of dependency: water feeds engines, engines feed pumps, pumps feed pipes, pipes feed people, people maintain engines. It is not complex. It is not fragile. It is *durable*. It has outlasted its builders, its purpose, and most of its population, and it continues because there is no reason for it to stop.

## The Sound

Mostly quiet. The engines pulse. Water moves — the steady *shhhh* of flow in pipes, the irregular *tlock* of condensation falling, the lower, almost subliminal gurgle of water in the cisterns, the hiss of steam escaping from gland seals that have been weeping for decades. Wind moves through the covered streets in slow, warm currents, carrying the smell of sulfur and iron and wet stone. The dogs breathe. The insects, if you put your ear to the wall, can be heard scraping — a tiny, rhythmic rasping, like filing.

People speak quietly. Not from secrecy or sadness — from acoustics. The stone corridors and covered streets amplify and echo, and conversation at normal volume becomes oppressive, bouncing back from every surface. So the residents of Vashunn have developed a speaking register that is barely above a murmur, lips barely moving, sound concentrated in the lower frequencies that carry without echoing. To an outsider, it sounds like everyone is sharing secrets. To the residents, it sounds like home.

## The Weirdness

There is a room in the northern quarter, in a processing building that has been cold for thirty years, where the mineral deposits on the walls have formed patterns. Not geometric patterns — not quite — but *almost*-patterns. Almost-maps. Almost-script. Lines and whorls and branching structures that look deliberate, that look *intentional*, that look like they might mean something if you could find the cipher. The residents know about this room. They do not go in. They do not talk about it. When asked, they say the room is "just deposits" and change the subject, and the way they change the subject — quickly, smoothly, with a redirect to engine maintenance or cistern levels — is itself a kind of answer. The room is not dangerous. Nothing has happened in it. But the patterns are *wrong*, in the way that a face is wrong when the proportions are almost right but not quite, and the residents of Vashunn have learned, over generations, that wrongness is worth avoiding even when it is harmless. Especially when it is harmless. Harmless wrongness is the most unsettling kind, because there is no action to take against it. You can only not go in the room.

There is also the matter of the third engine.

There were six engines. Two run. Three are cold and dead in their housings, rusting. That is five. The sixth is gone. Not removed — gone. Its housing is intact, its pedestal is intact, the bolt holes where it was mounted are empty but not stripped — the bolts are simply absent, as though they were never there. The residents do not remember the sixth engine. They remember five. The records — what few exist, in a clerk's office in the eastern quarter, water-damaged and barely legible — list five. But the housing is sized for an engine, and there are five housings total, and two are occupied and three are empty and that makes five, and five is the number everyone agrees on. There is no sixth housing. But if you count the spaces between the housings and calculate the spacing, there is room for one more. Or there isn't. It depends on how you measure. The residents do not measure.

And there is the matter of the fish.

The eyeless fish in the lower pools are the right number. They are always the right number. No one has ever counted them, but the buckets come up full, and the buckets have always come up full, and the population never seems to grow or shrink. This could be ecology. This could be balance. But the fish are all the same size. Every one. Exactly the same size, to the millimeter, as though stamped from a mold. And they do not appear to age. And they do not appear to die, except when they are caught, and when they are caught, they do not struggle. They come up in the bucket, lying still, and they are grilled, and they are eaten, and the next day the buckets come up full again, and the fish are the same size, and nothing has changed.

The residents do not find this remarkable. The residents have never found this remarkable. The fish are the fish. The engines are the engines. The room is the room. Vashunn is Vashunn. It is not a place that invites questions. It is a place that *is*, thoroughly and completely, in the way that stone is stone and water is water, and the strangeness of it is not something layered on top — it is something *structural*, something baked into the basalt and the thermal water and the iron and the low amber light, something that you can feel in the pulse of the engines under your feet, in the *tlock* of condensation on wet stone, in the quiet murmured conversations of three hundred people living in a city built for ten thousand, eating identical fish from pools that never empty, tending engines that run because they run, avoiding a room where the minerals almost make sense.

---

## Evening

The light does not change much. The clouds thin slightly in the late hours, and the amber deepens to a copper-bronze, and the wet surfaces of the city — the pipes, the walls, the streets, the iron roofs of the engine halls — catch it and hold it, and for an hour the city glows, darkly, like a forge seen through smoke. The engines pulse. The water moves. The dogs sleep. The insects scrape. Somewhere in the residential quarter, a door closes — a stone slab fitting into a stone frame, the sound flat and final, *tock*, and then nothing, and then the *tlock* of condensation, and then the *shhhh* of the pipes, and then the pulse, and then nothing, and then the pulse again.

The city breathes.

It does not need the people. It has not needed the people for a long time. But it has them, and they have it, and neither has anywhere else to be, and so they remain — the city and its three hundred residents, its two engines and its three dead engines and its five-or-six housings, its identical fish and its unnamed insects and its three lean dogs and its room of almost-patterns — all of them held in the hot, wet, dim, quiet space between the basalt and the clouds, weeping and pulsing and not quite making sense, and continuing, and continuing, and continuing.
