The plateau sits above the cloud line, a shelf of sharp, unweathered granite that was thrust upward in some ancient tectonic gasp and has not softened since. The rock here does not round. It sheers. Cliffs drop away on three sides into the white immortality of fog, and the stone underfoot still holds the striations of its emergence—fine, parallel grooves as if the mountain remembered being squeezed through a mold. Everything is angular. Even the wind seems to have edges.

It is cool, a persistent mildness that never deepens to cold nor relents to warmth. The air tastes faintly of iron and wet dust. Moisture comes not as rain but as condensation: each dawn, a fine beading of water appears on every exposed surface—stone faces, carved lintels, the broad leaves of the plateau's stubborn plants—only to evaporate slowly over the course of the stillborn day. There are no streams. No springs. Only this respiratory cycle of appearance and disappearance, the landscape inhaling dampness and exhaling it back to the air.

Between the stone, life persists with a tenacity that borders on aggression. Vegetation fills the horizontal surfaces in patches of startling green—a green that seems almost theatrical against the gray, a painted stage-flat green. Low shrubs with leathery, water-storing leaves cluster in the crevices where dust has accumulated sufficiently for roots. Mosses and lichens race across the fresh rock faces in slow-motion conquest, their colors shifting from silver-green to deep emerald depending on the angle of light. Tall, reed-like grasses grow in scattered tufts, their stalks rigid and vertical, each one a tiny spear asserting life against the mineral dead. The effect is lush but selective: abundance squeezed into available niches, abundance that knows exactly where it belongs and nowhere else.

And among this life, the city.

It is not a city by the standards of flat places. No grid organizes it, no central square focuses it. Instead, the buildings spread across the plateau like a settlement that grew from the rock itself—structures of fitted granite blocks, mortarless, their walls leaning at angles that follow the natural fracture-lines of the underlying stone. Some are small, single-room shelters barely distinguishable from boulders. Others rise two or three stories, their upper floors cantilevered out over the cliff-edge on brackets of cured wood, hanging in the cloud-air as though daring the void to claim them. There are hundreds of them, spread across the flattened ridge-top in a pattern that seems sparse from within—much space between neighbors, much empty granite visible between constructions—but resolves into intention when viewed from the paths that connect them.

Those paths are the city's true infrastructure. Cut into the rock at depths of a handspan, grooved routes mark the passages between buildings, their floors worn to a faint polish by generations of feet. They follow the natural contours of the plateau, dipping into shallow depressions, rising over ridges of uncut stone, splitting and rejoining at intersections marked by cairns of stacked pebbles. At intervals, larger stones have been set into the path-floors—stepping stones across the areas where morning condensation pools longest, allowing passage without soaking through rawhide sandals.

The paths lead to the cisterns. These are the settlement's masterpiece: deep channels cut into the granite at the lowest points of the plateau's subtle topography, funneling the morning dew into covered reservoirs that hold water through the dry hours. Each cistern is capped with a flat stone Lid carved with a single finger-hole for access. The water inside is cold and mineral-sharp and perfectly still. Some cisterns are communal, their Lids worn smooth by constant handling. Others belong to specific lineages, marked with chiseled sigils that record family histories in a script of angular, jointed characters—the same shapes, it must be said, as the fracture-patterns in the surrounding rock. As if the families had not invented their names but discovered them.

The people here—I have not seen them, but their presence permeates every surface—are primitive in craft and sophisticated in adaptation. Their tools are stone and bone and wood, worked with patience into forms that suit the available materials: granite hammers with rawhide grips, obsidian blades mounted in antler handles, needles carved from the ribs of something large. Their fabrics appear to be woven from the fibrous interior of the tall reed-grass, processed and spun into thread that takes dye from lichen—rust-red, pale yellow, a blue so dark it reads as black until the light catches it. They work with what the plateau provides, and they waste nothing.

No metal. No fired ceramic—there is no clay on the plateau, no fuel for kilns. Cooking vessels are hollowed stone, heated by dropping fire-rocks into water. The fire-rocks themselves are a minor puzzle: dense, porous stones that seem to retain heat far longer than geology should allow, found in a particular crevice on the plateau's eastern edge and replaced in that same crevice after they cool. The people do not question this. The rocks are hot. They are left in the crevice. They become hot again. This is the order of things.

And the order of things does not change.

This is what strikes the visitor most forcefully, after the height and the green and the angular stone: the stillness. Not silence—there is sound, but it is the sound of a system in equilibrium. Wind around the cliff-edges, a constant low whisper. The drip of condensation falling from leaves in the mid-morning. The grunt-hoots of the plateau's fauna, calling to each other across the gaps between structures. The scrape of sandals on worn stone. These sounds repeat. They have always repeated. There is no crescendo, no diminuendo. No morning chorus swells and fades; each species calls at its appointed time and stops at its appointed time, and the total sound remains level throughout the day.

The animals are abundant and unafraid. Small goat-creatures with too-many joints in their legs navigate the cliff-faces with unsettling ease, their hooves finding purchase on inclines that appear vertical. They come onto the plateau to graze the grass-tufts, moving between buildings with the confidence of residents, stepping around cistern-Lids and over path-grooves as if they had helped design them. In the crevices, insects carapace-click and scatter at vibrations, then resettle in the same positions. Birds—stout, flightless, their wings reduced to decorative vestiges—waddle along the path-edges and peck at the morning's moisture. They have no predators here. Or rather: they had predators once, and the system reached equilibrium, and now the predators take only the weak and the old and the unlucky, and the birds accept this arrangement as they accept the granite and the fog and the condensation that arrives each dawn like a promise kept.

There is a quality to this place that resists articulation. Not wrongness—nothing here is wrong—but *offness*. A sense that the plateau operates on principles slightly adjacent to the expected. The way the morning dew appears: not condensing gradually but arriving all at once, as if someone had brushed the world with a damp cloth while the inhabitants slept. The way the fire-rocks hold their heat. The way the city's paths match the rock's fracture-patterns. The way the building-layout mirrors the plant-distribution, scattered and purposeful, with the same logic of abundance-in-available-niches. As if the people had learned their urbanism from the vegetation. As if they were another species of cliff-plant, rooted here, drawing sustenance from stone and fog and the strange evenness of days that do not differ from each other.

Time does not move on the plateau. This is not literally true—the sun traces its arc, the condensation comes and goes, births and deaths occur in the private interiors of the granite buildings—but it is functionally true. Nothing accumulates. The city is not growing. It is not shrinking. No new buildings rise; no old ones collapse. The erosion of the paths proceeds at exactly the rate of their maintenance. The population holds at the number the plateau can support. The vegetation greens and persists. The animals call and graze and are culled. The fog sits below the clifftop like the floor of a room that has no floor, and the people walk their grooved paths and draw water from their cisterns and cook with heated stones and sleep in rooms that their great-great-great-grandparents carved from granite that was young when the world was young.

It is not stagnant. Stagnation implies change that has stopped. This is something else: a place that achieved its shape so perfectly that change became unnecessary. A system that found its equilibrium and held it, not through effort but through the simple logic of each component fulfilling its role without friction. The plateau does not resist change. It simply has no occasion for it.

Standing at the cliff-edge, looking out over the cloud-sea that stretches to every horizon, the visitor might feel a strange disorientation—not of the body but of the sense of time. How long has this city perched here? How many generations have walked these same grooves, drawn from these same cisterns, watched these same fog-horizons? The granite offers no answer. It is young stone, geologically speaking; its edges have not yet softened. But the city feels ancient, not in years but in resolution. As if it had always been this way. As if it could never be any other way.

The condensation is beginning to form. The visitor should find shelter soon, or at least a path-groove to walk. The fog does not rise, but the dampness settles into everything—cloth, skin, the porous surfaces of borrowed stone. The city goes about its evening. The goat-creatures return to the cliff-faces. The insects click into position. The fire-rocks, cooling in their crevice, begin their patient re-heating.

Tomorrow will be like today. This is not a prediction. It is a description.
