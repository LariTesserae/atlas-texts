# The Cistern of Unmaking

You find it behind a false wall in the lower maintenance corridors — a hatch you were not supposed to open, set into a door that had been painted over so many times the handle was barely functional. The hinge resists. Then it gives. Sound hits you first.

Not a sound. A *saturation* of sound. A mechanical roar so total and so constant that it stops registering as noise and becomes instead a physical condition — a vibration in the teeth, a pressure behind the eyes, a low thrumming in the sternum that your body learns to accommodate within thirty seconds because there is no alternative. It is not painful. It is simply *everywhere*. The air itself seems to hum with it.

---

The space beyond the hatch is small. Embarrassingly small. You expected something grand behind a sealed industrial door — a vault, a gallery, a cathedral of machines. Instead you get a room perhaps four meters across, roughly circular, with a ceiling that a tall person could touch. The walls are riveted steel, the floor cast-iron grating, and everything — everything — is wet.

Not dripping. Not damp. *Wet* in the way that the inside of a sealed boiler is wet, condensation forming on every surface in a constant sheen that gathers into beads and runs in thin parallel rivulets down the walls, pooling in the channel between the grating and the baseboards, flowing somewhere you cannot see. The air is close and warm — not hot, not tropical, just warm in the way of a room that has been breathing its own exhalation for a very long time. You are below grade here. You knew that from the corridors leading to the false wall, the way the floor tilted downward, but the room *feels* below grade — feels buried, feels interred.

The light comes from glass-arm fixtures mounted in brackets along the ceiling, industrial caged bulbs with filaments visible behind frosty glass. They throw a yellowish, even, unremarkable light. The kind of light that makes everything look like it belongs in a maintenance manual. Nothing dramatic. Nothing atmospheric. Just *illumination*, flat and functional.

---

At the center of the room stands the machine.

You say *machine* because you have no better word, but the thing does not resemble any machine you know. It is perhaps two meters tall, a column of dark iron — not cast iron like the grating but something denser, older, the metal almost black where it is dry and amber where the condensation has streaked it. Its surface is segmented into vertical fins, like the vanes of a radiator, and between each fin there is a gap perhaps a centimeter wide through which you can see — barely — the suggestion of interior mechanisms. Gears. Or teeth. Something that interlocks and moves with a slowness that seems geological.

The machine is running. This is the source of the sound — or rather, the room *is* the sound, and the machine is the room's throat. The roar comes from everywhere and nowhere, but the vibration is strongest at the column's base, where the iron meets a circular plinth set into the floor. The plinth is bolted down with twelve heavy bolts, each one the size of a fist, each one corroded to a patina of dark green. The plinth does not vibrate. The machine does not vibrate *noticeably*. But something inside it is moving, and the movement generates a harmonic that fills the room the way water fills a cup — completely, to the brim, with nothing left over.

You put your hand on the wall and the vibration travels through the steel and into your bones. It is not unpleasant. It is the opposite of unpleasant. It feels like standing inside a heartbeat.

---

The vegetation should not be here. You note this with the part of your brain that still categorizes things as possible and impossible, though that part is getting quieter. Moss — a dense, dark moss the color of forest shadow — grows in the seams where the wall panels meet. Not in tufts or patches but in continuous lines, as if seeded deliberately into the joints, following the geometry of construction with a precision that seems either cultivated or inevitable. The moss is wet, of course; everything is wet; the moss holds water in its capillary structure the way a sponge does, and when you brush it with your knuckle, water squeezes out in a thin line.

Higher up, where the wall meets the ceiling, something fern-like has taken hold — not a true fern, you think, but something that has decided to be fern-shaped because fern-shape is the shape that works in this environment: curled fronds, unrolling downward, seeking the light with the blind patience of organisms that have never known a different condition. They are small. The largest frond is perhaps fifteen centimeters. They grow from the gaps between the rivets, which have widened over the years — not through rust, exactly, but through the slow mineral action of water that has been running over iron for longer than anyone can remember.

How long has this room been here? How long has the machine been running?

The answer, you begin to suspect, is *longer than the building above it.*

---

There is almost nothing else here. The room is four meters across and contains one machine, one grating, one set of walls, one ceiling, one hatch (the one you came through), and one other door — sealed, bolted, leading somewhere you cannot determine. The emptiness is striking. In a building full of stored materials, stacked crates, routed pipes, and hung cables, this room contains nothing but itself and its function. It is a pocket of pure purpose. The machine does not produce anything you can identify. The pipes that enter the wall behind it — two copper lines, corroded to green, one warm to the touch and one cool — carry something in and something out, but the something is invisible, and the room gives you no indication of what it might be.

The sound never changes pitch. Never changes volume. Never pauses or stutters. You stand in the room for what feels like ten minutes and the sound at second one is identical to the sound at second six hundred. There is no cycle, no rhythm, no variation. Just a single sustained chord of mechanical operation that has presumably been sustained since the machine was first activated, whenever that was. You try to imagine the day it was turned on — some worker, some engineer, throwing a switch or turning a valve — and you cannot. The machine seems to predate intention. It is not *on*. It simply *is*. It has been *is* for so long that the distinction between running and existing has collapsed.

The condensation never changes either. The beads form at the same rate. The rivulets trace the same paths. The water collects in the same channel and flows, presumably, to the same destination. You watch a single bead form on the ceiling fixture, swell, detach, and fall. You watch another form in the same place. You watch another fall. The process is regular, measured, metronomic. The room keeps its own time.

---

You notice the moss again. You notice it because it is *everywhere*, now that you look — not just in the seams but on the machine itself, growing in the gaps between the cooling fins, a dark green thread of life that has colonized the iron column the way lichen colonizes stone. The machine does not seem to mind. The moss does not seem to suffer. The vibration that fills the room is felt by the moss as a constant, a given, a condition of existence no more remarkable than gravity. The moss grows toward the water. The water flows over the moss. The machine runs. The room hums. The light shines. The temperature holds. Nothing is changing. Nothing has changed, perhaps, for decades. Perhaps for longer.

There is a word for what this room is, and the word is *equilibrium* — but equilibrium implies a balance reached after imbalance, and this room does not feel like it ever struggled. It feels like it was born settled. Like someone designed it to be exactly this: warm, wet, loud, empty, green in the crevices and dark in the corners and humming, always humming, the kind of humming that you stop hearing after a while because your body decides it is not sound but texture, not texture but *weather*, not weather but *home*.

You could stay here. That thought arrives unbidden and you examine it with some alarm. You could sit on the grating with your back against the riveted wall and the moss touching your shoulder and the condensation falling on your forehead and the vibration in your chest and you could stay. Not comfortably. Not happily. But *contained*. The room would hold you the way it holds the machine — without opinion, without interference, without any change in its endless, even, deafening song.

---

You leave, eventually. You close the hatch. You replace the false panel. You walk back up the corridor, and the ordinary sounds of the building — footsteps, distant machinery, the ventilation system — seem thin and provisional after what you have come from. The room behind the false wall continues without you. The machine runs. The water beads. The moss grows at the speed of centuries. The sound goes on, filling a space no one visits, performing a function no one remembers, in a room that was perhaps never meant to be found and does not care that it was.

It will be there when the building above it is gone. You are almost certain of this.
