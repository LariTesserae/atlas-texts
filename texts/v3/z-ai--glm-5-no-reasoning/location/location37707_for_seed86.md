# The Lower March

The plateau drops away. Not gradually but suddenly—a shelf of granite shearing downward into a vast underground expanse that opened, geologically speaking, only yesterday. Perhaps a century ago. Perhaps two. The stone still holds the shock of it, raw-faced and unweathered, its breakage surfaces sharp enough to cut careless hands. In another million years, these walls will be rounded and familiar. Now they are abrupt, angular, almost violent in their newness.

You descend through switchbacks that aren't paths, not really, just the least impossible route down. The air changes as you go. Not warmer—this place doesn't do warm—but wetter. Condensation beads on the fresh rock faces, collects in the angle of each ledge, drips from overhangs in a slow, irregular rhythm. The dampness isn't rain. It's breath. The whole enormous cavity exhales moisture, condenses it against its own cool surfaces, drinks it back. A living system, in a way, though nothing here lives in any sense you'd recognize.

The scale reveals itself in stages. First you think: a cavity. Then: a cavern. Then, as the light fades and your eyes adjust to the dimness filtering from some unseen source above—you think: no, this is a hollow world. The ceiling disappears into darkness perhaps three hundred meters up, and the floor of the thing, when you finally reach it, extends outward in every direction, a plain of fractured stone stretching toward distant walls you can barely make out. The shape is roughly circular. A bowl? A crater? Something punched through from beneath and then stopped, leaving this vast absence where solid rock used to be.

Sparse doesn't mean empty. Sparse means each thing that exists matters more because of all the nothing around it. Here and there across the stone plain, mineral-rich water has pooled in shallow depressions—tarns of pale blue, green where the condensation has leeched copper from the walls, red where iron has bled downward from above. They reflect nothing because the light is too weak for proper reflection. They simply sit, still as glass, undisturbed.

The vegetation comes in bands. At the edges of the pools, where the dampness is most consistent, pale threads of something fungal push up through cracks in the stone—not quite moss, not quite lichen, some intermediate kingdom that hasn't been named. They form thin rings around the water bodies, perfect circles, no wider than your hand. You might not notice them at all if the light were any dimmer, and the fact that you can see them makes them extraordinary. Life, here, has to work very hard to exist.

Above the waterline, the stone goes bare. No plants, no algae. Just rock, and the marks of its breaking—stress fractures radiating from the walls, plunge pools where water has begun, just barely begun, to think about erosion. A few more thousand years and this place might have proper caves, proper formations, stalactites and stalagmites, all the slow architecture of dissolution. Right now it has only potential. It has the feel of a room prepared for a guest who hasn't arrived.

And then you hear them.

At first it's just texture in the silence—a roughness, a static. But the static has rhythm, has harmonics, has too many layers to count. You stop walking. You listen. And the sound resolves itself into what it always was: wings.

Millions of wings.

The walls, you realize now, aren't smooth. They're ribbed, vertically, with shallow crevices and deeper shelves and thousands upon thousands of small hollows carved by nothing more than the pressure of bodies returning to the same places, year after year, decade after decade. And in each hollow, in each shelf and crevice and fold of the stone, there are birds.

Not birds. Bats? Also not quite bats. Something between and beyond, adapted to this place, to this light, to this specific combination of dampness and stone and the endless, echoing dark. They are larger than you expect—most of them asleep, or whatever passes for sleep in a world without day or night. Their wings fold against their bodies in ways that seem anatomically improbable. Their fur, where you can see it, is pale, almost white, adapted to the cold, to the damp, to a life spent pressed against condensation-slick rock.

The sound they make isn't song. It's the sound of a million small bodies making small adjustments—clicks and chirps and the rustle of wing membranes against stone, the scratch of claws finding purchase, the constant low murmur of a population too vast to ever truly quiet. It echoes off every surface, multiplies, folds back on itself until the air itself seems to vibrate with it. Not loud like machinery. Loud like an ocean. Loud like weather. A volume you feel in your chest more than hear with your ears.

A few of them are awake, moving between the walls and the pools, dipping to drink, to hunt the small pale insects that breed in the mineral water. They move without collision, each aware of the others through some sense you can't perceive, navigating by the echoes of their own voices in a system too complex to have evolved by accident. They must have been here before the hollow opened—living in smaller cracks, deeper fissures, populations isolated from each other until the rupture brought them all together into this single vast colony. Now they own this place. You are the intruder. The anomaly. The thing that doesn't belong.

You walk slowly across the plain, and they adjust around you, parting in your wake like water around stone. A few come close enough to feel the displacement of air from their wings, the particular cool-damp of their breath. None touch you. None startle. They have no natural fear of anything your size, because nothing your size has ever come here before. You are an event without precedent. They treat you with something that might be curiosity, might be indifference, might be the simple acknowledgment that you exist and then, just as simply, the acknowledgment that your existence changes nothing.

The light, such as it is, comes from above—from the opening where the plateau sheared away, letting in a distant gray illumination that could be dawn or dusk or an overcast afternoon. But filtered through three hundred meters of humid air, through the haze of condensation and the particulate haze of a million flying bodies, it becomes something else. Flat. Directionless. A glow that seems to emanate from the air itself rather than from any particular source. You could read by it, barely. You could see your own hand held at arm's length. Beyond that, darkness—the darkness of depth, of distance, of a world larger than any single vantage point could comprehend.

And the place does not change. This is the strangest thing. You walk for an hour, two hours, and the landscape does not shift. The pools appear at the same intervals, the pale fungal rings around them identical in size, in shape, in color. The walls maintain the same distance, the same texture, the same density of sleeping bodies. The condensation beads and drips at the same rate. The sound maintains its volume, its texture, its endless unchanging murmur. You could walk for days, you realize, and arrive at... more of this. The same this. An environment so thoroughly itself that variation has become impossible.

It is not dead. Far from it—there is more life here than in any forest, any grassland, any ocean surface. But the life has found its equilibrium, its perfect balance, and it holds that balance with a stubbornness that feels almost aggressive. Nothing new will grow here until the old grows old enough to make space. Nothing will change until the change itself becomes inevitable. The Lower March, you think—this is what it must be called, or something like it—is a world that has decided what it is. It will not negotiate. It will not evolve. It simply is, in all its overwhelming, sparse, damp, living permanence.

You could stay here. You could become part of the equilibrium—a source of carbon dioxide, a slight displacement of air, a body that breathes and sleeps and eventually stops breathing and becomes part of the stone, the water, the slow mineral leaching that might, in a thousand years, produce a new kind of fungus, a new shape of pool, a new fold in the endless unchanging song.

Or you could climb back up, through the switchbacks that aren't paths, toward the plateau and the world above—where things change, where time moves, where the weather happens and the seasons turn and nothing, nothing stays the same.
