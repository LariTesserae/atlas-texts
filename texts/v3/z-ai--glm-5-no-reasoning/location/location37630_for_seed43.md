# The Hive-Basins of Yong

The descent takes three hours. You climb down through the Salt Gates—massive, crude affairs of timber and rope that the basin-dwellers winch open with teams of six—and enter a world that existed before the foundations were laid.

---

The basin opens below like a cupped hand carved from living rock. Cathedral-scale. A single chamber perhaps four hundred meters across, its ceiling lost to shadow thirty meters up where the light from above becomes a diffuse glow. The walls are not walls but accumulations: layer upon layer of built structure, a vertical settlement that has grown downward and inward over centuries, each generation adding to the dense coral-reef of platforms, walkways, dwellings, storage chambers, and workshops until the original cave geometry is merely suggestion.

Everything here is made. There is no natural floor—only pavements laid over pavements, mended and re-mended, cracked and patched, some sections sunken where older construction has collapsed into voids below. The basin-dwellers say their home rests on older homes, which rest on older still, descending into myth.

The moisture comes from below. Warm water seeps upward through the stone, emerging in countless seeps and weeps along the lower walls, collecting in shallow channels carved into the pavement, feeding the basin's life. The air hangs thick with humidity—not tropical, not stifling, but present. You feel it on your skin like a fine veil. Your clothes dampen over hours and never quite dry.

And the sound. The basin *talks*. Water drips from innumerable sources, each impact finding its own note in the acoustic bowl. Footsteps echo and multiply. Children shriek across the tiers. The basin-dwellers sing while they work—call and response between levels, work songs that coordinate labor and mark time. Hawks cry from their roosts in the high corners where they hunt the fat cave-moths. The compounds soundscape has no silence, only varying densities of noise.

---

The ecology here rotates around moisture and meat.

Cave-moths first—large, pale-winged things with bodies the size of your thumb, attracted to the mineral-rich seep-water. They cluster in the damp corners, their wings leaving powder on the stone. Children catch them with woven nets, both for food and for the hawks.

Hawks next—descendants of birds brought down generations ago, now bred for the low light and enclosed space. They nest in the high crevices, diving on moths and the small pale rats that live in the infrastructure. The basin-dwellers keep them for hunting and for companionship. A hawk on a perch outside a dwelling marks ownership.

Pale rats third—the basin-dwellers call them *wei*, and they are everywhere. In the walls, under the floors, inside the cavities between layers. They eat the grain stores and the Moth-pupae and the dead. The basin-dwellers hunt them with hawk and trap and bare hand. Wei-meat goes into the stewpots. Wei-fur lines the winter blankets. The rats are pest and resource both.

Then the people: perhaps three hundred at any given time, though counting is difficult when families are embedded in the architecture itself. They occupy every level from the rim to the floor, their dwellings stacked and interconnected by ladders, rope-bridges, and hand-carved steps. Space is allocated by lineage and trade. The weavers have the eastern middle-tier where the light is steady. The fish-keepers have the floor-level where the deep channels collect. The Salt Gate operators live near the top, close to their work.

The crowding is intimate. Privacy exists only in the mind. You hear your neighbor's cough, their arguments, their children's footsteps overhead. Screens of woven reed separate spaces that are already separated by walls of rammed earth and timber. The density presses like a hand against your chest until you acclimate, and then it becomes warmth—a surrounding presence. You are never alone here. The basin-dwellers find our silence obscene.

---

Light comes from three sources.

The first is the Salt Gate—the opening to the world above, admitting a diffuse glow that never quite reaches the floor. It moves with the sun above, tracking across the hours, and the basin-dwellers read time by its position.

The second is fire—clay lamps burning rendered wei-fat, placed at intersections and entrances, tended by children with long wicks. The fat-smoke adds to the haze, and the lamps gutter in the constant updrafts.

The third is bioluminescent moss—a cultivated thing, grown in shallow trays along the major walkways. It produces a faint blue-green glow, too weak to work by, but enough to mark edges and boundaries. The moss-tenders are an entire profession. They manage the moisture, harvest the excess, trade it between tiers.

---

The seeps define the basin's economy.

Warm mineral water, salt and calcium-heavy, emerges from the walls and floors at hundreds of points. It pools in carved channels, flows toward the central drain, and is diverted at every stage for use. Drinking water comes from the cleanest seeps, marked by carved sigils. Working water runs through the reed-beds where the weavers soak their fibers. Waste water collects in the lowest channels, where the wei-pelt cleaners do their foul work.

The water is warm—body temperature, or slightly above. It never cold-shocks. This warmth rises, keeping the basin habitable even as the world above freezes. The basin-dwellers speak of "the breath of the stone" and credit it with health. Certainly the warm damp grows things well: the reed-beds thrive, the moss spreads, the people rarely sicken from chill.

But the damp penetrates. Everything not stone or bone eventually softens. Wood swells and warps. Cloth mildews unless constantly treated. Rope stretches and rots. The basin's constant work is maintenance—replacing what the moisture claims, repairing what it weakens. Every structure is temporary; every wall will need rebuilding; every ladder will eventually snap. The basin-dwellers accept this as the order of things. They build for replacement, not permanence.

---

The erosion here is not natural.

The basin is in balance with itself—a slow negotiation between construction and decay. New layers accumulate as old layers sag. Walls lean and are buttressed; buttresses lean and are braced; braces lean and become part of the structure. The basin-dwellers work with this process rather than against it. When a section becomes unstable, they cannibalize its materials and abandon it to slow collapse. When a new void opens, they frame it over and build into it.

The result is a place that shifts under your feet—not suddenly, not dangerously, but perceptibly. This part of the walkway is lower than it was last year. That dwelling's floor has developed a slope. The old granary is now three granaries stacked as the floor sank. The basin settles into itself century by century, and its people settle with it.

---

The weirdness here is ordinary.

The basin-dwellers practice a geomancy of moisture. They read the seeps for signs—increased flow, changed color, new mineral deposits—and interpret them as messages from below. When a seep runs red with iron, they say the stone is hungry. When one goes dry, they say the stone is sleeping. Their offerings go into the deepest drains: wei-bones, woven hair, the first cuttings of each harvest.

They also carry a mutation: adapted to the low light, their pupils have expanded until their eyes appear wholly black. They see in darkness that would blind a surface-dweller, navigating by the moss-glow and less. In the upper world, they squint and shield their faces. Down here, they own the dark.

And they sing the basin. Each level, each profession, each family line maintains its own songs—call-and-response patterns that travel through the space, echoing off the walls, weaving into a continuous texture of sound. A stranger entering hears chaos. A basin-dweller hears a map: this voice is on the third tier, that voice is in the weaving quarter, the rhythm of the fish-keepers' song says the catch is good. The basin knows itself through its singing.

---

I have spent three days here now, and the place has begun to make a kind of sense.

The heat and damp are not uncomfortable but embracing. The crowding is not oppressive but communal. The noise is not chaos but communication. The basin is a single organism, its people the cells, its structures the membrane, its water the blood. Everything is connected. Everything serves something else.

The basin-dwellers say: "We do not live in the stone. We are the stone, breathing."

I begin to believe them.
