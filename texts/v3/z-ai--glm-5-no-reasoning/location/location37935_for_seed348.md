# The Hatchery

The corridor stretches maybe forty meters end to end—a cathedral-scale tube of riveted iron and something like ceramic, like the inside of a colonial-era municipal water main enlarged to become a street. The walls curve overhead and meet in a seam that runs the length of the ceiling like a closed eyelid. The space was built. It was built on purpose, with intention, by someone who understood what they were doing. That person is gone. The corridor remains.

---

The dampness here is not rain. There is no sky. The moisture comes from the walls themselves—condensation that beads and trickles in slow, irregular rivulets down the curved interior. The iron has gone black and orange with oxidation in patches, but not everywhere. Some sections gleam as if installed yesterday. This is not contradiction; it is simply the way things are. The corridor exists in its own state, weathered and whole at once. It does not decay further. It has not decayed further for a very long time.

If you touch the wall, your fingers come away wet and cold. The water tastes metallic. It tastes like drinking from an old spoon.

---

There is no plant life. No green. No growing thing. The world is mineral and manufactured—iron, ceramic, copper wiring in conduits that run along the floor in channels, glass in the light fixtures that still function. The fixtures are industrial things, caged bulbs with thick filaments that glow the color of warmed butter. They cast a normal light. A working light. The kind of light you would expect in a factory or a hospital corridor where work gets done.

The light has not flickered. It does not flicker.

---

The temperature sits at that particular coolness just above uncomfortable—a basement in autumn, a unheated room in a house where someone has simply stopped paying for heat. You might want a jacket. You would not freeze. Your breath does not cloud. The cold settles against exposed skin like a heavy fabric, present and consistent.

Nothing changes. The temperature is what it is.

---

The sound is what strikes you first.

It begins as a low mechanical hum—industrial, deep in the walls—like the heartbeat of a large building. Pipes carrying something. Generators somewhere below. The infrastructure of the corridor runs hidden but audible. You understand that this place is powered. Electrified. Connected to something larger that still functions.

But the hum is not the loudest thing. Not even close.

---

The fauna are overwhelming.

They fill the corridor—dozens of them, perhaps a hundred—stacked in niches along the walls in symmetrical rows, occupying the space in a density that makes the architecture feel like an afterthought. They are not insects. They are not mammals. They are something else entirely.

Each one is about the size of a human hand—shelled, segmented, like a trilobite crossed with a mechanical toy. Their backs are ridged with overlapping plates of something like chitin, something like dark plastic. Their undersides are pale and soft, visible only when they move. They have many legs—twelve? Sixteen?—that tick and scrape against the iron walls as they shift in place.

They make noise.

---

The sound is continuous. A clicking, scraping, shuffling chorus—the sound of a hundred small bodies adjusting themselves against metal. They do not move far. They do not need to. Each one occupies its own niche, its own small depression in the wall, and there they sit. Waiting. Existing. Being.

The sound is not aggressive. It is not hostile. It is simply the sound of life at density—the sound of a place that is, for reasons you cannot parse, absolutely full of living things.

They do not fight each other. There is no competition. There is enough space for all of them. There is enough of whatever they need.

---

The corridor is a hatchery. Or a nursery. Or a storage facility. The purpose is not clear.

The creatures—you have started calling them "the residents" in your head—have no eyes that you can find. They have antennae, pale and jointed, that emerge from beneath their head-plates and wave slowly in the air. They are sensing something. The air, perhaps. The moisture. The vibration of your footsteps if you walk.

They can hear you. They know you are there.

They do not care.

---

This is the strangest part: the indifference. You are in a corridor filled with creatures that have no recognized biology, in a built environment that has no visible maintenance, powered by systems that have no apparent source, and nothing here regards you as remarkable. You are a presence. You are a variable. You are accounted for in whatever equation governs this place, and you are not its solution.

You could stand here for an hour. You could stand here for a day. The residents would click and shuffle. The lights would glow. The water would bead and trickle. Nothing would change. Nothing expects you to leave. Nothing expects you to stay.

---

The scale of the place is intimate. You could walk its length in less than a minute. You could press your hands against both walls if you stood in the center and stretched your arms. It is a room-sized place, a corridor-sized place. The kind of space you could hold in your memory entire.

And yet it feels larger. It feels like a single room in an infinite building—a fragment of a structure that extends somewhere else, somewhere you cannot reach. The residents might know. They have been here longer than you. They will be here after you leave.

---

There are doors at intervals along the corridor—iron doors with wheel-locks, like hatches on a submarine. They are sealed. They have been sealed for as long as the residents can remember, which is not a useful metric, because the residents do not remember. They simply are.

You could turn a wheel. You could open a door. You have not. Something about the place suggests that the doors are closed for a reason—that whatever lies beyond them is not your business.

Or perhaps nothing lies beyond them. Perhaps the corridor is all there is.

---

The erosion parameter manifests as a kind of settled grace. This place is not new. It is not ancient in a romantic sense—it is simply established. It has reached the state that all places eventually reach when they are left alone long enough: the state of being exactly what they are, with no pressure to become anything else.

The rust does not spread. The water does not pool. The lights do not dim. The residents do not multiply beyond capacity. Everything has found its level. Everything has found its balance.

It is a peaceful place, in its way. Loud, but peaceful. The noise is not a warning. The noise is just the sound of living things being alive, side by side, in a space made for them.

Or made by them.

Or simply made, and then occupied, because that is what spaces are for.

---

You have been standing here for some time now. Your clothes are slightly damp from the humidity. Your ears have adjusted to the clicking; it has become a kind of silence. The residents have shifted slightly in their niches, settling deeper, perhaps, or simply repositioning their many legs.

One of them—near your shoulder, in a niche at eye level—extends its pale antennae toward you. It touches your sleeve. The sensation is faintly cool, faintly dry. It taps twice, like a blind finger testing the texture of the world.

Then it withdraws. It settles back into its place.

You are known. You are catalogued. You are not a threat. You are not food. You are not anything that matters to the system of this place.

You are simply here. And that is enough.
