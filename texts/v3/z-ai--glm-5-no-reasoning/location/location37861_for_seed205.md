# The Great Respiring

The heat arrives first. A thick, wet blanket of it that presses against your skin before you've taken three steps into the open. Not the dry heat of deserts or the baking heat of sun-stone—but the heat of greenhouses, of compost piles working, of a billion small bodies metabolizing in close quarters. The air tastes green. You understand this is not metaphor.

---

The landscape has no horizon. This is not poetic license—the plant mass extends far enough, rises high enough, that distance becomes a green haze rather than a line where earth meets sky. We are high, terribly high, on what might be a plateau or might be the accumulated bodies of ancestors. The elevation creates its own weather. Clouds form *within* the growth, not above it.

Rain comes and goes without pattern. Not storms—just the structure above deciding to shed its condensation. You feel it as a sudden brightening of the air's moisture, a quickening of drip through layers, and then rainfall that lasts anywhere from four minutes to four hours. The plant mass drinks what it needs. The rest finds its way down through channels that might be accidental or might be purposeful architecture. The water that reaches walking level is warm, faintly sweet, and leaves a film on the skin that tingles.

---

There is no ground.

This requires a moment to absorb. You walk on root, on rhizome, on the compressed fibers of what grew before. The "surface" yields slightly with each step, and if you stand still too long in one place, you begin to sink. The plants are patient digesters. Movement is not just locomotion—it is survival. The sparse spacing between significant landmarks exists because anything stationary eventually gets incorporated. Paths exist only because enough feet have passed through. They close behind you within hours.

---

The structures are rare and strange. A few hundred paces apart, if you're lucky to find them—woven shelters made from living branches trained into rough domes, their interiors hollowed out by hands that knew which stems to cut and which to leave. The cuts heal over within days, scar tissue bulging into new growth that must be trimmed again. Living architecture requires constant negotiation. The builders—whoever they were, or are—understood that they were not constructing homes but proposing temporary arrangements with something that would outlast them.

Some shelters show signs of recent use. Fronds bent inward to create shade. Sections of wall thinned to admit light. Others have been wholly swallowed, their doorways sealed by new growth, their occupants either migrated or consumed. It's impossible to tell which.

---

The sound never stops.

A low, rhythmic thrumming that you feel in your spine before your ears register it. This is the place's heartbeat—or its breath. The combined metabolism of uncountable organisms, the friction of leaf against leaf in the upper layers, the movement of water through tissue, the creaking of massive root-systems shifting in their slow search for purchase. It's not loud in the way of machinery or thunder. It's loud in the way of oceans, of stadiums filled with murmuring crowds.

Your own thoughts become difficult to sustain. The noise doesn't drown them—it weighs on them. Thinking requires more effort here, as if consciousness itself must push against a medium thicker than air.

---

And then there are the animals.

"Insects" is the wrong word, though they might have started as something analogous. The term implies smallness, and small is not what you're dealing with. The creatures here range from hand-sized to half-your-height, their bodies built on plans that evolution never proposed on any continent you know. Six legs, eight, twelve—the numbers vary, but the purpose doesn't. They move through the plant mass like water through a sponge, their legs evolved to grip and release, to find purchase in gaps between fibers, to navigate a world where solid ground is a fiction.

They teem. This is the only word for it. In any cubic meter of visible space, at any moment, you can find movement—scuttling, crawling, climbing, hovering. The ones with wings create a constant low drone in the upper layers where the plant mass thins toward light. The ones without wings traverse the vertical spaces with a speed that makes your breath catch. They don't swarm like locusts—they go about their business with the focused industry of commuters, each one following paths laid down by generations before.

Most are herbivorous in a sense—eating the plant substrate, tending sections of growth, pruning and processing. But not all. You see the evidence of predation in the scattered remains, the sudden commotion of a chase that ends too quickly to track. The food web here is dense, complex, and operating at a scale that defies your ability to map it.

They don't fear you. They don't seem to register you as something distinct from the plant mass—perhaps because your body heat blends with the ambient warmth, perhaps because their sensory systems evolved for different parameters. You can walk among them, feel the breeze of their passing, the clicking of their limbs against surfaces near your skin. This is more unsettling than aggression would be. You are simply... irrelevant. A temporary obstruction. Something to be climbed over or routed around.

---

Higher up—the rare clearings where the plant mass opens, creating platforms of intertwined growth—the light reveals itself. Not direct sunlight; that exists somewhere far above, filtered through dozens of layers until it becomes a diffuse green-gold glow. But enough light to see by, to rest in, to observe the strange weather of this place.

Clouds form within the canopy. Mist thickens and thins without reference to any external sky. You realize, standing in one of these clearings, that you have no idea what time of day it is—whether the sun above is morning or evening, whether night ever truly falls here or whether it's all just varying degrees of dimness. The interior spaces below the clearings don't receive even this muted light. They exist in a permanent green twilight where bioluminescence—faint, blue-white, emerging from fungal patches and the underside of certain leaves—provides the only illumination.

---

The heat peaks in what you decide to call afternoon, though the term means nothing here. Sweat doesn't evaporate. It beads and runs, or gets absorbed by the humid air before it can fall. Your clothes become a second skin of damp fabric. Dehydration is a constant risk—the water all around you isn't potable without treatment, thick with spores and microorganisms that your gut isn't built to handle.

But the plant mass provides, for those who know where to look. Certain gourds, cut open, yield liquid that's almost water, filtered through layers of tissue that strain out most of what would harm you. It tastes vegetal, faintly bitter, and it leaves a residue of sugars on your teeth. Enough to survive on. Not enough to thrive.

---

You find evidence of others. Not often, not clearly—but fragments. A tool left behind, its handle rough-hewn from some woody stem, its edge worn to a curve that suggests specific use. A path that's too deliberate, cleared with more precision than the random navigation of animals would produce. Twice you hear sounds that might be voices, might be the settling of massive growth, might be the calls of creatures you haven't yet catalogued.

The builders live here somewhere, or pass through. Whether they're permanent residents or seasonal migrants is impossible to determine. You suspect they've adapted to conditions that would kill an outsider within days—have learned the rhythm of the place, the cycles of rain and heat, the safe paths through the animal throngs. They've certainly learned how to navigate without ground, how to read the subtle signs of stability in the shifting root-mass.

Perhaps they worship this place. Or perhaps they simply understand it.

---

The scale of the Great Respiring becomes clear only when you climb—when you find a vine-ladder or a natural stair of compressed matter and ascend through layer after layer after layer, each one its own ecosystem, its own weather, its own animal population. Hours of climbing. And still the green extends above and below, horizon replaced by green haze in every direction.

This is a continent of plant, or something larger. A plateau that became a mountain of accumulated life. It has its own gravity, its own atmospheric systems. The wind that moves through the upper layers doesn't come from anywhere else—it's generated here, thermal currents rising and falling, creating a slow respiration that gives the place its name.

You are inside an organism, or a superorganism, or a collaboration of organisms so vast that the categories stop mattering.

---

Night, when it comes (or something like night), doesn't bring silence. The sound shifts—certain frequencies dropping out, others rising. The diurnal creatures settle, the nocturnal ones emerge, and the constant background hum takes on different harmonics. The temperature doesn't drop much. If anything, the plant mass seems to exhale heat at night, releasing what it stored during the dim-lit hours.

Sleep is difficult. Your body isn't built for this. Every position feels wrong, the yielding surface beneath you more disturbing than solid ground would be. You dream of green, of being digested, of roots growing through your chest cavity. You wake more tired than when you closed your eyes.

And the Respiring continues.
