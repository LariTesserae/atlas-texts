# The Rattle

There is a pocket in the world — not the world you'd recognize, but a world nonetheless — where the stone has been ground so fine and so long that it has forgotten it was ever stone. You enter through a fissure that narrows to a shoulder-width crawl and then opens, without ceremony, into a chamber perhaps thirty meters across and eight high. Low ceiling. Close walls. The kind of space that makes you aware of your own breathing because the air has opinions about displacement.

The floor is the first thing. Not flat — never flat — but worn into a topography of shallow ridges and furrows, like the grain of old wood, like wind on water frozen mid-ripple. The material is mineral. Something between chalk and ochre, pale yellow-gray, compressed by ages into a hardness that belies its dusty appearance. It has been here for an unreasonable span of time. You can feel that in the way it holds your weight: not yielding, not welcoming, but *resigned* to being stood on. Every surface carries the same patient erosion. The walls curve inward where something — water, perhaps, in some forgotten epoch when this place was not sealed — spent geological patience smoothing them into whale-belly curves. The ceiling is the same material, stained darker in branching patterns that might be mineral deposits or might be the fossil ghost of something that once grew here and is now part of the architecture. You cannot tell. There is no one to ask.

There is no water here. The air is dry in a way that registers immediately in the back of the throat and the corners of the eyes — not desert-dry, not wind-dry, but *sealed*-dry, the dryness of a container that was closed long ago and has not been opened since. Whatever moisture once existed has been locked into the stone itself or has departed entirely. The chemistry of the place runs on minerals and air, and the air is stale but not dead. It moves. Slightly. In currents too small to feel but large enough to carry sound.

And the sound — the sound is the second thing, because it does not stop.

It is not one sound. It is a thousand small sounds compressed into a continuous rustling drone, like static, like rain on loose gravel, like the collective whisper of an audience that has been told to be quiet but cannot quite manage it. The chamber is *full*. Not of stone — of life. And the life is not what you would expect.

---

They cover every surface. Every surface. The floor, the walls, the ceiling, the furrows and ridges, the darker stains, the curved transitions between plane and plane. They are small — the largest perhaps the length of a finger, the smallest barely visible — and they are in constant, ceaseless motion. Arthropodal is the closest word, but it is not quite right. They have segmented bodies, yes, and legs — many legs, in varying numbers — and shells of a pale, almost translucent material that might be chitin but might be something the local mineralogy has produced on its own. They are the color of the stone, mostly, which is why the chamber does not at first appear to be alive. But when light moves across the surfaces — when your lamp shifts, when you turn your head — the refraction catches and the walls *shimmer*, and you realize that what you took for texture is population.

There are species here. Many. The dominant form is a flat, oval creature with a ridged back and twelve legs that moves in a flowing, almost liquid gait, like a wave passing through a crowd. They travel in columns, following chemical traces laid down by their predecessors, and the columns intersect and merge and split in patterns that look, from a distance, like the circulation of a single organism. Among them are smaller, rounder forms — rapid, erratic, seeming to navigate by collision — and larger, slower creatures with elongated bodies and powerful forelimbs that they drive into the substrate, gouging shallow tracks as they go. These last are the loudest. Each impact of their digging limbs produces a click, and the clicks overlap into a rhythmic undercurrent beneath the general rustle, like the heartbeat of the chamber itself.

And above them — or rather, *among* them, because in a space this crowded the concept of above and below begins to lose meaning — are the fliers. Not true fliers. They have no wings. But they have long, frond-like appendages that catch the air currents and allow them to drift in slow, controlled arcs from wall to wall, and they are everywhere, thousands of them, each no larger than a thumbnail, each drifting with a purpose you cannot parse. They make a sound like paper tearing, very quietly, and when several pass close to your face the aggregate sound is a dry, papery rushing that adds another layer to the din.

The noise is remarkable. It fills the chamber completely, bouncing off the close stone surfaces, amplified and softened in turns by the irregularities of the walls. Standing in the center, you are immersed in it. It is not silence, this place. It is the opposite of silence. It is the sound of life compressed into a container and left to run.

---

There are structures here. Or rather, there are the remnants of structures — built things, made things, objects that did not grow from the stone but were placed here by hands or by processes that intended a specific shape. They are sparse. Scattered through the chamber in no clear pattern, half-buried in the substrate, overgrown — if that word applies — by the constant passage of the crawling fauna. There is a metal frame near the eastern wall, rusted to a deep brown, about the height of a knee, that might have been a stand for an instrument or a bracket for a pipe. The pipe, if there was one, is gone. The frame remains, and the flat-backed creatures use it as a bridge, their columns flowing over it in unbroken lines, their feet wearing smooth grooves into the metal where the traffic is heaviest. There is a housing of some kind — a box, rectangular, the size of a large book, bolted to the ceiling at a point where two of the darker stain-branches meet. Its surface is corroded but intact, and a cable emerges from one end and runs along the ceiling for a short distance before terminating in a connector that has greened with oxidation. It hums. Faintly. Whether it is still powered or whether the hum is a mechanical resonance produced by the vibration of the creatures moving across its housing is impossible to determine without touching it, and touching it would mean reaching through a thick curtain of drifting fliers and standing ones.

There are marks on the walls. Not many. A series of vertical scratches in groups of three, cut into the stone at a height that suggests they were made deliberately, by a tool, for a reason. They are old. The edges have been softened by the same erosion that has smoothed everything else, and the creatures have worn paths across them so that they are more felt than seen — you have to run your fingers over the stone to detect them. Someone was here. Someone who counted something, or marked a level, or simply recorded their presence. They are the only evidence of intent in the chamber, and they are nearly gone.

---

The chamber is not dead, and it is not dormant. It is *in motion*. The motion is constant and multidirectional and involves processes you can observe and processes you cannot. The columns of the flat-backed creatures are the most visible — they flow like rivers across the floor, converging at certain points and diverging at others, and the convergence points are always near deposits of a darker mineral that seams through the pale substrate. They are mining it. Or eating it. Or doing something to it that serves the same function. The smaller, rounder creatures follow the columns, and the larger diggers follow them, and the fliers drift between all of them, occasionally landing, occasionally being consumed — you see this happen once, near your foot, where a flier settles on a flat-backed creature's shell and is gripped and folded inward in a motion so fluid it looks like the larger creature has simply changed shape, and the flier is gone, and the column moves on.

There is waste. There is reproduction. There is territorial dispute — you hear a sudden increase in the clicking, a flurry of motion where two columns intersect, and a brief, violent engagement between two flat-backed creatures that lasts less than a second and results in one turning away, slightly damaged, and the other continuing along its path. There are corpses — pale, curled, small, scattered through the substrate like punctuation, and the smaller creatures move among them, and the smaller creatures are also moving among the living, and you realize that some of the smaller creatures are juveniles of the larger forms and some of them are something else entirely, something that occupies a different branch of the local taxonomy, and the whole system is so dense and so interconnected that you cannot find the edge of it. It does not have an edge. It has surfaces, and the surfaces are all the same surface, and the life covers them the way lichen covers a rock, and the life *is* the surface, and the stone beneath is only the substrate on which the life writes itself.

You crouch. You listen. The sound does not change. It has not changed since you arrived and it will not change after you leave. It is the sound of the chamber breathing, and the chamber breathes through ten million mouths.

---

The light is your own. There is no natural light source here — the fissure you entered through is too narrow and too angled to admit anything but the faintest suggestion of brightness, a gray hint at the far end of the crawl that might be the outside world or might be a reflection from the stone. You carry a lamp. The lamp is industrial — a heavy, sealed thing with a glass lens and a metal housing — and it is not the only one of its kind. Near the metal frame on the eastern wall, half-buried in the substrate, there is another lamp, identical in manufacture, its glass cracked, its interior colonized by a species you have not seen elsewhere — a pale, fibrous growth that is not plant and not fungus but something that fills the available space with thin, branching filaments, the closest thing this place has to stillness.

You found the lamp. Someone else found the lamp before you. Someone else found the chamber before them. The marks on the wall, the structures, the cable — these are the traces of previous visits, each one separated from the next by enough time for the chamber to absorb the evidence, to wear it down, to incorporate it into the substrate or to bury it under the endless passage of small, determined bodies. The chamber is patient. The chamber does not resist visitors. It does not welcome them. It simply continues, and the visitors become part of the record, and the record is worn smooth, and the smooth record is walked on.

You are standing in a room the size of a large cellar, beneath a ceiling you can almost touch, surrounded by more living things than you have ever seen in one place, and the noise is immense, and the air is dry, and the stone is old, and the light is industrial, and the world you came from is a long crawl away through a crack in the rock, and the creatures do not care. They have never cared. They will not learn to care. They are the engine of this place, and the place is the engine of them, and together they produce the sound you are hearing, which is the sound of a world so small and so full that it has become, in its own way, complete.

When you leave, the sound diminishes as you crawl — not gradually, but in steps, as the fissure narrows and the walls press in and the air thickens with the mineral smell of the stone. By the time you reach the outside, it is gone. Replaced by whatever the outside sounds like. But the sound of the chamber does not stop when you leave. You know this. The chamber does not require your presence. The chamber has been sounding for longer than you can comfortably imagine, and it will continue sounding for longer still, and the creatures will continue their columns and their digging and their drifting, and the structures will continue their slow dissolution, and the marks on the wall will continue their slow disappearance, and the whole system will continue its blind, deaf, purposeful operation in the dark, in the dry, in the close stone, and it will not stop, and it will not stop, and it will not stop.
