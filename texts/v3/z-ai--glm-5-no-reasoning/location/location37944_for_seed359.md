# The Interior

Something was grown here — or built — and the distinction collapsed so long ago that neither word carries meaning anymore.

You are inside a chamber roughly the size of a cathedral, though the ceiling is uneven, vaulting in some places to thirty meters and in others pressing down to head-height as if the space exhaled and forgot to inhale again. The floor is not floor. It is a dense, interlocked surface of woven fiber and living tissue, warm to the touch, slightly yielding, the color of old rust mixed with cream. Threaded through it are striations of something harder — bone? ceramic? — that crack if you press too hard but reform over hours, knitting back together with a faint sound like lips smacking.

No water. None. Not in the air, not in the ground, not cached in reservoirs or bound in cells. The air tastes of dry mineral and heated sugar. The vegetation — and it is vegetation, unmistakably, aggressively vegetal — has no business being this lush without moisture, and yet it is. Every surface is colonized. Matting, climbing, draping, interweaving. Leaves the size of a child's palm crowd against leaves the size of a thumbnail, layered so densely you cannot see what lies beneath. The colors are wrong. Greens, yes, but also violet-black, and a deep arterial red that appears in patches like bruising, and a white so luminous it seems to glow until you look directly at it and it becomes merely pale. The plants do not wilt. They are turgid, full, pressurized with something that is not water — some viscous analog that seeps when a stem is broken, smelling of ammonia and cinnamon, and evaporates within seconds of exposure to air, leaving a residue like salt crystals on your fingers.

The chamber is not natural. The walls are constructed. You can see the joins, the seams, the places where panels of a fibrous composite material — something between papier-mâché and dried muscle — were fitted together and sealed with a resin that has since hardened to the density of stone. Tool marks are visible. Crude ones. The strokes of hand-held implements, possibly stone or bone, working with patience over months or years. Whoever built this place — whatever built this place — had no metal. The construction is ingenious within that limitation. The walls are double-layered, the gap between layers packed with shredded dry fiber that serves as insulation. The chamber holds its warmth. The temperature inside is significantly higher than what the external environment would produce. Everything hums with low heat.

And the walls are not still.

This is the thing that takes time to notice. The walls flex. Minute, glacial movements — the kind you only register by staring at one point for a full minute and realizing it has shifted by a millimeter. The whole chamber is breathing, or pulsing, or growing. The joints between panels open and close by fractions of a millimeter over hours. The resin seals have not cracked; they stretch. The room is alive at the pace of geology, and you are standing inside a single slow heartbeat.

---

The fauna is where the unease begins.

The chamber is full of animals. Not large ones — the largest is perhaps the length of your forearm, most are much smaller — but they are everywhere. In the vegetation. On the floor. On the walls. Moving across the ceiling in deliberate processions that follow paths you cannot see but they clearly can.

The most common are flat, oval creatures with too many legs — sixteen, twenty, more — that move like fingers walking across a table. They are the color of the floor and are nearly invisible until they move. They do not make sound. They travel in lines, following each other's chemical trails, and when two lines cross, the creatures pause, touch antennae or palps or whatever those anterior structures are, and then continue, sometimes swapping lines. They seem to be communicating something. You cannot guess what.

In the vegetation, there are other things. Bright. A creature like a tube of iridescent blue felt, two centimeters long, that climbs stems with rhythmic contractions and feeds by pressing its mouth — a ring of fine, dry bristles — against the plant tissue and drawing out the not-water. You can hear it if you hold your breath and bring your ear close. A sound like tearing silk, very faint, very slow. The plants do not seem to mind. Or if they mind, they are slow to show it.

There are predators. You see one — a triangular, flattened thing the color of dried blood, clinging to the underside of a broad leaf, utterly still. It has no eyes that you can identify. Its body is covered in fine hairs that each terminate in a small knob, and you realize with a lurch that the knobs are moving independently, tasting the air, tasting the vibrations, building a map of the space around it through means you have no framework for. It does not move for the entire time you watch it. It does not need to. The prey comes to it, following the chemical trails, walking along the leaf's edge, and the triangular thing simply... opens. A mouth that occupies its entire underside. The prey walks in. The mouth closes. Silence.

But here is where the reality begins to come apart.

---

You have been in the chamber for perhaps an hour when you notice that the chamber is larger than it was.

Not dramatically. The far wall is perhaps a meter farther than you remember. You did not move. You are certain of this. But the distance has increased. You pace it off. Yes. Larger. The vegetation has thickened, too — there are more plants, more layers, more of the red and the violet and the impossible white. You did not see them grow. You did not hear the sounds of growth. They are simply *more* than they were.

You sit down on the floor — warm, yielding, that strange woven tissue — and you wait. You watch.

Over the next hour, the chamber changes. Not continuously. Not visibly. But between one glance and the next, a new column of vegetation has appeared where there was none. A wall panel that was flat now has a ridge. A colony of the many-legged oval creatures has established a new line where no line was before. The ceiling has shifted. The room is not growing the way a room grows. It is growing the way a memory grows — by accretion, by invention, by the quiet addition of detail that was always already there if you had only looked.

And the sounds are wrong.

The chamber is nearly silent. You hear the faint dry rustling of creatures in vegetation. The silk-tear of feeding. Occasionally, a low click — something tapping a hard surface. But underneath these sounds, or perhaps behind them, or perhaps *inside* them, there is another sound that you cannot locate. It is not a sound the ear hears. It is a sound the skull hears. A pressure. A tone below tone. It has no source. It comes from everywhere and nowhere. It is the sound the chamber makes by existing, the way a held breath makes a sound that is not a sound but a presence in the chest.

And it changes pitch when you move.

You stand up. The tone drops. You sit. It rises. You walk toward the far wall. It shifts with each step, a sliding scale, as if your position in the room is a variable in some equation that determines the base frequency of reality. You stop. It stops. You take a step backward. It reverses.

---

The vegetation near the far wall is different. It is not the plants you have been seeing. It is — you do not have the word. The structures are plant-like in that they grow upward and branch and have surfaces that could be called leaves. But they are also mechanical in that they have joints, hinges, articulations. And they are also architectural in that they form patterns — arches, corridors, niches — that serve no biological purpose you can identify. And they are also *textual* in that the patterns they form, when viewed from the correct angle, resolve into shapes that could be letters, or could be diagrams, or could be the kind of marks left by a process that does not distinguish between writing and breathing.

You touch one. It is warm. It is not water-filled. It is filled with the same not-water, the ammonia-cinnamon viscous fluid. But when your finger presses the surface, you feel something press back. Not resistance. *Response.* The structure flexes toward you. The adjacent structures flex toward you. A wave of tiny adjustments propagates outward from your touch point, a ripple of attention, and for a moment the entire wall of hybrid structures is oriented toward you, every leaf-hinge-joint angled in your direction, and the tone in your skull spikes to a pitch that is not painful but is *present*, more present than anything you have ever felt, a sound that is also a touch that is also a thought that is also the sensation of being recognized.

Then it recedes. The structures return to their previous positions. The tone drops. The chamber breathes.

You realize, slowly, that the chamber is not a room. It is not a structure. It is not a machine.

It is a conversation.

The vegetation, the fauna, the walls, the floor, the air — all of it is a single continuous process of exchange. The many-legged creatures carry chemical messages between plant communities. The plants respond by growing new structures. The structures alter the air composition. The air composition triggers responses in the wall tissue. The wall tissue flexes, changing the chamber's geometry. The geometry changes the acoustics. The acoustics change the sub-tone. The sub-tone changes the behavior of the fauna. The loop closes. The conversation continues. It has been continuing for — you cannot know how long. The tool marks on the walls suggest hands. The walls suggest builders. But the builders may have been built by the chamber, or the chamber may have been grown by the builders, or the distinction may be a feature only of the language you are using to think about it, and the chamber itself has no use for that distinction.

---

There is one more thing.

In the far corner, where the ceiling presses lowest, there is a feature you missed. A nest, or a cradle, or a seat. Something shaped for a body. Not your body — the proportions are wrong, the limbs too long, the torso too narrow — but a body. Something sat here. Something built this chamber, or was built by it, or grew alongside it, and sat here in this corner and listened to the tone and watched the creatures and tended the vegetation with hand-tools of stone and bone and lived inside this conversation the way you live inside air.

The seat is empty. It has been empty for a long time. The vegetation has begun to colonize it — a vine the color of old bronze is creeping across the armrest, and one of the flat many-legged creatures is walking along the back, following a chemical trail.

But the seat is warm.

Not chamber-warm. Not ambient-warm. Body-warm. The warmth of something that was here recently, or is here still, or was here so long and so intensely that the warmth became part of the material and never left.

You touch the seat. The tone in your skull shifts. The vegetation leans toward you. The creatures pause in their lines. The chamber holds its breath.

And for a moment — just a moment — you understand the conversation. Not the words. There are no words. But the shape of it. The way the parts relate. The way the tone is a question and the growth is an answer and the creatures are the punctuation and you, standing here, touching the seat, are a new word being considered for inclusion in a sentence that has been being written for longer than your species has existed.

The moment passes. The tone drops. The creatures resume. The chamber breathes.

But the seat is warm, and the vegetation near it has grown a new leaf while you were standing there, and the leaf is shaped exactly like your hand.
