The high plateau stretches beyond any horizon you could walk toward. Atmosphere itself thins, and the light takes on quality unique to altitudes where less filtering occurs—colors appear slightly over-saturated, edges too sharp, as if the world is being revealed rather than merely illuminated.

Cold defines the geometry of living things. Frost crystals trace every branch and blade in intricate fractals that rebuild themselves each night. The air is thin and the temperature rarely rises above freezing even at midday, yet the place teems with growing things. Mosses and lichens blanket exposed stone in thick tapestry. Grasses grow in dense tussocks between cracks in the pavement. Stubborn shrubs cling to sheltered hollows. Ferns emerge from warm pockets in the ruins. No trees—nothing with that kind of height ambition—but the low vegetation is impossibly lush, dark greens and deep purples and rust-colored growths that thrive in the perpetual chill.

Moisture arrives unpredictably. Fog rolls across the plateau in slow-motion waves, depositing rime ice on contact. Rain falls as ice needles that dissolve on warmth. Snow forms in sculpted drifts that melt and re-freeze in slow cycles, leaving behind strange terraces and ripple patterns in the accumulation. In the lowest pockets between foundation stones, where residual heat rises from somewhere deep underground, pools of liquid water form, but they're rare and do not last.

Most of the ground is bare rock—the bones of a mountain range so old that the term "mountain" no longer applies. What were once jagged peaks are now gentle swells, rolling undulations across a surface that has been ground down by glaciers, wind, rain, and time into something approaching mathematical flatness. In places, the bedrock is polished mirror-smooth. In others, it's broken into boulder fields, the fragments settling into the lowest points, creating rough terrain that catches the vegetation. Striations score the stone, patterns cut by ice sheets that haven't existed for epochs. The erosion is absolute, complete—the land has arrived at its final shape. Nothing dramatic will happen to this terrain again. It will simply continue to exist, wearing away grain by grain.

The plateau is vast beyond comprehension—continental in scope, a feature on maps of the world itself. Weather systems form and dissolve entirely within its boundaries. Clouds accumulate, merge, discharge, and dissipate without ever encountering a significant change in elevation. From any point on the surface, the world appears to curve away in all directions, flat and endless. The horizon shimmers with cold mirages, distant features appearing to float above the surface.

Across this immensity, the ruins scatter.

They were once a city. The word still applies, though "city" implies a density that no longer exists. What remains is infrastructure—the skeleton of urban organization spread across hundreds of kilometers. Foundations trace the ghosts of streets. Drainage channels follow long-vanished roadbeds. The occasional wall fragment stands against the wind, three or four courses of stone still bonded with mortar that has outlived its makers by millennia.

The construction is primitive but sophisticated in its primitivism. No metal reinforces these walls, but the stones were carefully selected and fitted, their shapes interlocking in ways that distribute load without requiring binding agents. The mortar was clearly organic, plant-based, something that has gradually returned to the earth while the stone remains. Some walls have begun to tilt, their foundations settling as frost heaves the ground beneath them, but the overall network is stable. These ruins have found their equilibrium. They will persist.

Building materials were entirely local—stones pried from the bedrock itself, split along natural fracture lines and shaped with patient hand-tools. No evidence of quarrying in the modern sense. The stone is the same stone as the plateau, just rearranged into patterns that signify human intent. In some areas, the ruins are dense enough to form distinct chambers and passages, low mazes where the wind takes on different tones. In others, single stones lie isolated in the vegetation, the only evidence that anything was ever built.

The city must have housed a substantial population once, though estimating numbers is impossible from the remains. The density was never high. This was a settlement that spread horizontally rather than vertically, buildings separated by gardens, courtyards, public spaces now overgrown with the same vegetation that covers everything. The infrastructure suggests organization—water management, waste channels, the outlines of a planned community.

But there is no animal life. Not now, perhaps not ever. The ecology is botanical only. Pollination happens by wind and by the gradual creeps and reaches of the plants themselves. Seeds travel on the constant high-altitude currents, ice-encased, to germinate wherever they land. The lushness is vegetative ambition unchecked by grazing, by browsing, by predation. The plants grow until they touch, compete for light, establish borders and territories. Without animals, the ecosystem operates on different principles—slow warfare, positional advantage, the strategic colonization of moisture pockets.

The absence creates strange silences. Wind moves through the ruins and generates sounds—tones and whispers as it encounters different surfaces—but no birdsong, no insect hum, no distant calls or footfalls. The vegetation rustles when the air moves, and ice crystals click against each other as they form or break, but these are mechanical sounds, physical phenomena rather than voices. The quiet has texture. It accumulates in the mind.

And then there are the places where the quiet thickens into something else.

The weirdness manifests gradually, revealing itself only when you've spent enough time on the plateau to notice what's wrong. The first sign is usually the horizon—the way it seems closer or further depending on the direction you're looking, distances subtly wrong in ways that don't correspond to topography. The plateau appears flat, but measurements reveal impossible variations. A kilometer walked north might take longer than a kilometer walked south, even at the same pace.

Near certain ruins, the vegetation grows in spiral patterns that have no relationship to wind direction or water flow. The spiral arms rotate clockwise in some locations, counter-clockwise in others, and in a few places, they appear to rotate in both directions simultaneously—a perceptual instability that induces nausea if observed directly.

Some of the foundation walls are straight only when viewed from particular angles. Walk around them, and they appear to bend, the geometry suggesting angles that sum to more than they should. The stone itself is normal, but the space around it has begun to lose coherence. Mortar that has returned to dust hovers in place in some of these zones, particles suspended in configurations that mirror the original bonding pattern.

Time does not pass consistently. Watch a patch of frost form, and you might see it complete in seconds or hours—there's no way to predict which. The sun tracks across the sky at varying speeds, faster at noon, slower near the horizon, as if the plateau occupies a region where celestial mechanics have begun to wear thin. Shadows sometimes detach from their casters and persist for minutes after the object has moved. In a few locations, shadows have accumulated in corners, layer upon layer of darkness compressed into something that has begun to develop mass.

Weather forms spontaneously in small volumes. Clouds the size of rooms condense from clear air, rain falling in precise circles before the system dissipates. These micro-storms follow no pattern, appear without warning, and ignore the larger weather systems moving across the plateau. In some areas, rain falls upward, drawn toward the sky by forces that have nothing to do with temperature gradients. The water freezes in suspension, creating temporary ceilings of ice that crash down when the effect passes.

The drainage channels the builders constructed have begun to transport something other than water. During certain periods—always when the temperature drops below a threshold that varies by location—liquid flows through them that is darker and more viscous than water. It pools in the low points, accumulates, and eventually sinks into the stone itself, leaving stains that cannot be explained by mineral content. Analysis would reveal that the substance has properties of both liquid and solid, and that it is very slowly becoming more of the latter.

The city was built on a reality that has since begun to degrade. The builders may have known this was happening—their architecture suggests attempts to stabilize local space, certain walls positioned to create faraday-like effects against whatever radiation causes the breakdown. If so, they succeeded only partially. The city persists, but the people are gone, and their infrastructure is no longer adequate to prevent further deterioration.

Yet the place remains active. Processes continue. The vegetation grows, adapts, colonizes new territory. The frost forms and reforms in patterns of increasing complexity, as if the ice itself is learning to write. The ruins settle and shift, finding new configurations as the ground beneath them becomes less reliable. Weather moves through, leaving moisture that accumulates in unexpected ways. The erosion continues, infinitely slow, grinding the plateau toward a flatness so absolute it will cease to be a feature at all.

In the areas where reality is most compromised, new phenomena emerge. Geometry behaves according to principles that predate or postdate the familiar. Stone surfaces ripple like water when touched. Light bends around corners before reflecting. Sound arrives before its cause, a faint echo preceding the event itself. In the oldest sections of the ruins, the stone has begun to remember shapes it once held, ghost walls flickering in and out of visibility as the material cycles through configurations it occupied across deep time.

The plateau is approaching a terminal state—not death, but transformation. The erosion will eventually complete itself. The reality breakdown will eventually reach a threshold. The vegetation will eventually encounter conditions it cannot survive. But this process operates on a scale that makes human concerns irrelevant. The plateau exists now, as it has existed, as it will exist.

To stand on the surface is to feel yourself at the edge of something—the end of the world, the end of the world's ability to maintain its own rules. The cold is absolute, the vegetation vibrant, the ruins patient. The quiet contains sounds you cannot quite hear, overtones from adjacent possibilities that have begun to leak through. The light is too bright and the shadows too dark. Distance is unreliable. Time is approximate.

The plateau waits for nothing in particular. It has waited long enough to become waiting itself—ancient beyond memory and present beyond perception.
