# The Quiet Mills of Upper Vesh

There is no horizon here, only a seam where darkness meets slightly-less-darkness.

---

The plateau sits at a height where atmosphere thins to something not quite breathable and not quite not — a dry mineral soup, nitrogen and carbon dioxide and trace argon, warm as a cup set down and forgotten. Warm the way a room is warm after the last person has left it. Not the warmth of sun or fire. Residual warmth. The warmth of a process completed so long ago that its heat has become a property of the stone itself.

You stand on the uppermost tier of Vesh, which is not a city in any sense that implies habitation, but which is not a ruin either, because ruins imply collapse, and nothing here has collapsed. Everything here is standing exactly as it was built. The mills are intact. The conduits run unbroken from the deep bore-wells to the processing halls. The gear trains in the great wheel-houses still mesh, tooth in tooth, theoretically capable of motion. They do not move. They have not moved for a length of time that the stones themselves seem to have lost count of, the way a clock left in an empty room eventually forgets what it was measuring.

---

The ground beneath your feet is a pumice-like conglomerate, pale as old bone, shot through with veins of something darker — a mineral glass, obsidianish, that catches no light because there is almost no light to catch. The surface has been leveled. Not by wind or water — there is no wind here worth naming, and water has never been a factor — but by industry. By the systematic removal of everything that was not wanted. What remains is a floor. Not metaphorically. The entire plateau, across a span that would take the better part of a day to cross, has been worked to a surface flat enough that a sphere placed anywhere upon it would not roll. This took time. This took a kind of patience that does not belong to any living thing.

Out of this floor, the structures rise.

They are not buildings in the sense of walls and roofs and rooms. They are *arrangements* — vertical shafts of the same pale conglomerate, reinforced with iron bands that have oxidized not to rust but to a black-brown patina so stable it might as well be a different metal entirely. The shafts connect at various heights by horizontal tubes, some as small as a forearm, some large enough to walk through stooped. The overall impression is of a refinery or a chemical plant, but one designed by minds for whom the distinction between architecture and plumbing was meaningless. Everything is process. Everything is conduit. The shape of the place is the shape of what it did.

And what it did — you can read this from the remaining infrastructure, the way an archaeologist reads a skeleton — was extraction. The bore-wells go down. The processing halls sit at the center. The output channels radiate outward to the edge of the plateau and terminate in open-air dispersal racks, which are essentially large grates. Something was pulled from deep below, processed in the halls, and released into the atmosphere. For a long time. For an incomprehensibly long time.

---

The darkness here is not the darkness of night. There is no night because there is no day. The sky — if it is a sky — is a uniform low ceiling of what might be cloud or might be mineral haze or might simply be the upper limit of visibility, a grey-black curtain hung at perhaps two kilometers. It produces no light. It admits no light. It simply *is*, the way a ceiling is, and the world below it exists in a state of permanent dimness, a crepuscular gloom without source or direction. You can see, but only in the way that the eye sees after long adjustment — shapes, depths, the pale geometry of the mills against the marginally darker ground. Colors exist only as theory. Everything is a value on a scale between dark ash and light ash.

There are lamps. Or there were. Glass housings on iron standards, spaced at regular intervals along the main process-corridors, wired with copper that still glints dully in their sockets. Whatever lit them — electrical, perhaps, given the wire, or some gas drawn from the bore-wells themselves — is exhausted. The lamps remain as a record of the fact that someone, once, needed to see here.

---

Sound is almost absent, and what sound exists is not what you would expect. There is no wind. There are no creatures. The structures do not creak or settle. But there is a low vibration, felt more than heard, that comes up through the floor of the processing halls — a deep, slow pulse, like the heartbeat of something enormous and dormant. It is not mechanical. It is geological. The plateau itself, or the substrate below it, is subject to some cyclical pressure — tidal, perhaps, if the planet has a molten core and a moon, or seismic, if there are plates that move on timescales so slow that each pulse is separated by minutes. Whatever the cause, the vibration does not vary. It has been the same pulse, at the same interval, with the same intensity, for the entire duration of the silence. It is the only clock in Vesh, and it keeps a time that corresponds to nothing.

---

The processing halls are the largest single structures. Six of them, arranged in a hexagon around a central hub that was once — must have been — the main control point. The halls are cathedral-sized, though the comparison feels wrong, because cathedrals aspire upward and these spaces aspire *downward*. The floors are sunk below the level of the plateau, reached by ramps that descend in long, shallow spirals. The walls are lined with the apparatus of the process: reaction chambers, separation columns, heat exchangers (cold now, but their shape implies heat), and — at the center of each hall — a great centrifuge, a horizontal drum on a shaft, ten meters across, with internal vanes that spiral in a pattern suggesting not mixing but *sorting*. Something was separated here. Something was broken into its components.

The floors of the halls are covered in a fine powder, ankle-deep in places, the consistency of chalk dust. It is the residue of the process — the waste material, or perhaps the product, dispersed so finely over so much time that it has accumulated into a silent, pale sea. It does not drift. There is no wind. It holds the shape of whatever last disturbed it. There are tracks in it — not footprints, exactly, but parallel grooves, as if something with runners or treads moved through here, once, and the dust has preserved the trace for centuries or millennia.

You kneel and touch the powder. It is warm. Everything here is warm.

---

At the edge of the plateau, the dispersal racks hang out over a void.

This is the one place where the geography becomes dramatic, and it is dramatic in a way that recontextualizes everything else. The plateau is not a plateau in the sense of a raised landform on a planet. It is a shelf on the wall of an *enclosure*. Below the edge, the ground drops away — not down a slope but straight down, a vertical face of raw rock, and below that, more vertical face, and below that, a darkness so total that it might be a shaft descending to the center of the world, or it might simply be depth beyond the reach of the dim ambient light. You drop a stone. You hear nothing. Not the sound of impact, not the sound of it striking walls on the way down. Nothing. Either the shaft is so deep that the stone has not yet landed, or the darkness below is not the kind of darkness that sound travels through.

The dispersal racks project out over this edge. Whatever was processed in the halls was released here, into the shaft. Into the depth. For the entire operational life of Vesh, a substance was drawn from below, refined in the halls, and poured into the void. The question of *why* is the kind of question that has no answer here. The question of *what* is answerable only in part: the residue in the halls is a mineral compound, silicate-based, with trace metals. The product — the refined material — is not in evidence. It was all dispersed. All sent down.

---

There is life here, in the loosest sense.

In the deepest hall — the one whose floor sits lowest, closest to the bore-wells — there are structures on the walls that were not built. They are not part of the apparatus. They are smooth, dark, roughly hemispherical, between the size of a fist and the size of a head, and they cling to the iron bands and the conglomerate with a tenacity that suggests they are not merely attached but *integrated*, their material continuous with the substrate at the point of contact. They are warm — warmer than the surrounding surface, noticeably so, as if something inside them is metabolizing. Not growing. Not moving. But metabolizing. Maintaining.

They are not organic. Or if they are organic, they are organic in a way that does not correspond to any framework that includes cells or DNA or protein. They appear to be mineral — a dense, dark mineral, possibly a form of the processed material itself, reprecipitated out of the dispersal system in a form that was not intended. They may be accidental. They may be the result of the process running for so long, with such stability, that the material began to self-organize at a molecular level, finding configurations that were energetically favorable and reproducing them, not through any mechanism of replication but through the simple physics of crystal growth in a stable environment over deep time.

They do not respond to light, to vibration, to touch. They do not change. They sit on the walls of the deep hall like barnacles on a hull, and they are warm, and they are the only thing in Vesh that is doing anything at all.

You stand in the hall and listen. The vibration comes up through the floor. The dark mineral shapes cling to the walls. The powder lies on the floor in its undisturbed seas. The conduits run from the bore-wells to the centrifuges to the dispersal racks, and the dispersal racks hang out over the void, and the void goes down, and the darkness below is absolute.

Nothing is happening. Nothing has been happening for longer than the word *longer* can meaningfully convey. The mills of Vesh are in their final form, and their final form is this: a machine that has finished its work and has not been told to stop, standing in a darkness that it created, warm with the memory of its own function, perfectly preserved, perfectly useless, perfectly still.

The only sound is the pulse from below, steady as geology, slow as anything.
