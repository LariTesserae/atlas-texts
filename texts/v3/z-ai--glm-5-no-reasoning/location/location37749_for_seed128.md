# The Cistern of the Hundred Throats

The passage opens without warning—a seam in the wall that wasn't there a moment before, or perhaps was always there and simply waited to be noticed. You step through into air that presses against your skin, thick with moisture and warmth, and the sound hits you like a physical weight.

---

## The Sound Comes First

Always the sound. A hundred voices at least, maybe a thousand, layered and interwoven in patterns that almost resolve into words before dissolving back into pure resonance. They sing in frequencies that bypass the ear and settle directly into the chest cavity, into the long bones, into the fluid behind the eyes. The effect is not painful exactly, but overwhelming—like standing inside a bell while it rings, like being held underwater beneath a waterfall.

The voices are not human. They are not even, in the strict sense, voices. But the mind reaches for that word, that framework, because what else do you call something that speaks without a speaker, that harmonizes without breath?

---

## The Architecture of Containment

The space reveals itself gradually—your eyes adjusting to light that seems to generate itself from no particular source. The walls are made. Everything is made. There is no natural stone here, no geological substrate beneath the construction. If you were to cut through the floor (you cannot cut through the floor) you would find only more of the same material, extending downward perhaps forever: a designed substance with the color and translucency of old bone, warm to the touch, shot through with capillary channels where fluid moves in deliberate currents.

The material has a name that was never recorded in any human language. It was grown, not built—crystallized out of a solution whose formula belongs to an engineering tradition that operates on different principles than assembly. It *remembers* being made. If you press your palm flat against it and wait, you can feel something like a heartbeat in its depths, slow and patient.

The chamber is not large. You could cross it in twenty paces if you could walk in a straight line, but you cannot walk in a straight line. The space folds. Not in any visible or dangerous way—there are no sudden drop-offs or impossible geometries—but the distances between points shift depending on your direction of travel, your speed, even perhaps your intention. The architects who designed this place understood that a room could be larger than its physical dimensions if its physics were willing to negotiate.

---

## The Water and the Warmth

Water is everywhere here, but never collects in the ways you expect. It condenses out of the air in beads that roll down the walls in deliberate paths, joining channels that run through the bone-material in branching networks. The humidity approaches saturation—your skin stays perpetually damp, and when you breathe in, the air feels almost thick enough to drink.

The temperature holds steady at something just above uncomfortable, a muggy warmth that suggests a body's interior, a greenhouse in a climate that never knew winter. Heat radiates from the walls themselves, from the fluid moving through them, from the very substance of the place. Nothing here is cold. Nothing here has been cold for a very long time.

In one corner—though corners are perhaps the wrong way to think about a space that curves in ways that corners cannot account for—a basin collects the runoff from the wall channels. The fluid is not quite water. It has a viscosity to it, a faint luminescence when disturbed, and if you look closely at its surface you will see that it does not reflect light so much as hold it, preserve it, carry it somewhere else.

---

## What Lives Here

The chamber is alive with movement, and none of it is human.

Most numerous are the small things—not quite insects, not quite machines, perhaps something in between. They move along the walls in organized processions, following the water channels, occasionally stopping to extend proboscis-like appendages into the fluid. They are the size of a thumbnail, iridescent in the low light, and their legs make a sound like dry leaves rubbing together—though that particular layer of the chorus is nearly inaudible beneath the voices.

Above them, larger shapes navigate the curved ceiling. They look somewhat like bats, if bats were made of a substance that shifted between states of matter. They cling to the bone-material with appendages that might be feet or might be something else entirely, and they emit ultrasonic pulses that become part of the layered soundscape.

The dominant presence, though, is the source of the voices. You cannot see it—they are hidden in the architecture, embedded in the walls, perhaps distributed through the very substance of the place. There are indentations in the bone-material, regularly spaced, each about the size of a human head, each perfectly shaped to hold something that makes sound. The indentations are never empty. Even when you look directly at them, the thing inside remains just beyond perception—a shape that dissolves at the edges of vision, a presence that is defined more by its vocal contribution than by any physical form you can isolate.

They are not hostile. They have been singing, collectively, for longer than the human species has existed in its current form. They will continue singing after you leave, after you die, after the world above (assuming there is a world above, assuming the concept still applies) forgets that this place exists. Their song is a process, not a performance. It serves a function that the architecture requires.

---

## The Purposes of This Place

This is, was, and will be a cistern. The word is insufficient but the concept holds: this is a place where something precious is collected, stored, and distributed. The fluid in the channels, the basin, the walls themselves—it carries something more than water. Information, perhaps. Memory. The accumulated weight of moments that some distant designer decided were worth preserving.

The dense saturation of this place is not an accident. Everything here exists in proximity to everything else—the small creatures depend on the fluid, the fluid depends on the walls, the walls depend on the singers whose voices vibrate through the material at frequencies that keep its crystalline structure stable. Remove any component and the system would collapse, or worse, transform into something else entirely.

The warmth accelerates these processes. The containment protects them. The strangeness of the physics ensures that only those who belong here can find it, can stay, can survive the pressure of that constant, overwhelming song.

---

## The Light That Remains

There is no sun here, no artificial illumination in any conventional sense. The light is a byproduct—bioluminescence from the fluid, chemical reactions in the small things' shells, a faint phosphorescence in the bone-material itself when the singers hit certain frequencies. It is barely enough to see by, and yet the eye adapts. The darkness itself seems to hold a quality of light, a negation that paradoxically illuminates.

If you stay long enough—and you will stay longer than you intended, because the physics of the place makes leaving nearly as difficult as finding it in the first place—your vision will begin to interpret the singers' sound as light. Certain frequencies will bloom into corresponding colors, and you will understand, partially, what it might be like to experience the world the way this place does: as a single unified field of sensation, where sound and light and touch and temperature are not separate categories but different expressions of the same underlying truth.

---

## The Sound Continues

It never stops. It never pauses. The singers do not seem to need to breathe, or if they do, they do so in shifts so seamless that the chorus is unbroken. The small things do not sleep. The fluid in the channels never stops moving.

This is a place of process, not stasis—active, ongoing, in media res. It has been in its current state for what might be called an age, but that state is one of constant motion, constant transformation, constant exchange. The erosion parameter reveals itself in the settled forms of the channels, the worn paths of the small things' processions, the way the bone-material has shaped itself around its inhabitants over time. This is not decay. This is equilibrium achieved through long practice.

When you finally find your way out—and you will, eventually, though you may not be able to explain how—you will carry the sound with you for days. It will fade gradually, a note dropping out here and there, until one morning you wake and realize you can no longer hear it. But you will remember it. The memory will be physical, a weight in your chest, a hum in your bones.

And if you are very fortunate, or very unfortunate, you will one day find yourself standing near a particular wall, at a particular time, and you will hear it again—a single voice, perhaps, rising from somewhere beneath the surface—and you will know that you have been remembered in return.
