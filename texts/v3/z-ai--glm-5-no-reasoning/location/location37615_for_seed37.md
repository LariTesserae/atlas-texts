# The Hatch

There is a place beneath the world where it is always morning and nothing is ever finished.

---

You descend through a gap that was not meant to be found—a seam in bedrock that opens when you stop looking for it and closes behind you without sound. The passage breathes. Not metaphor: the walls draw in and press out with a slow rhythm, half lung, half tide, and the air that moves through tastes of minerals and something sweeter underneath, something almost biological.

The space below is smaller than you expected. Perhaps the size of a cathedral, perhaps the size of a throat—you cannot decide. The scale refuses to settle. This is a pocket, a hollow, a single room embedded in the earth's flesh, and it glows.

Light here does not come from a source. It accumulates. It pools in basins and sheets across the walls like something spilled and not yet cleaned up. The color shifts through gold to white to the pale green of new leaves, and the brightness casts no shadows. Objects simply are, or are not, with edges that blur and reform. You blink and the room rearranges itself slightly. A protrusion becomes a depression. A surface tilts toward you, then away.

The walls are raw. Not raw like quarried stone—raw like wounds, like the inside of a mouth, like tissue that has never known air. The stone (if it is stone) bears striations of colors that shouldn't exist together: rust bleeding into violet, bands of cream and charcoal spiraling around protrusions that might be formations or might be something growing. The surface texture ranges from glassy-smooth to pocked and porous, sometimes within inches. This is geology in progress. You have the sense that if you touched the walls, they would be warm. If you pressed your palm flat and waited, you might feel something hardening beneath your skin, or something softening, or both at once.

Everything here is still becoming.

Moisture collects in the hollows—not standing water, but a persistent dampness that beads on surfaces and rolls down slopes in sluggish trails. The air carries it. You feel it settle on your skin like a breath condensing, and when you reach out to touch a depression in the wall, your fingers come away wet with something that catches the light and holds it, glistening. It does not feel like water. It feels like a question.

There are pockets where liquid has gathered—shallow pools in the lowest depressions, some no larger than a cupped palm. They reflect nothing. They simply absorb the light and turn it into something else, something that glows from within. If you look into them (and you will look; the urge is biological, inevitable), you see shapes moving that are not reflections of anything above. They might be the pool's own contents, or they might be something looking back.

---

The life here is abundant and impossible.

It begins at the edges—the places where wall meets floor, where surface meets hollow. Small things first. Clusters of something like tubes emerge from cracks, pale and translucent, filtering the wet air. They pulse. They do not eat, exactly—they sample, they taste, they process. Near one cluster, a hand-width of stone has been transformed into a lattice of honeycomb-like cells, each filled with the same light-drinking liquid that pools in the floor's depressions. The tubes lean toward you as you pass. They do not follow. They are waiting for something else.

Above, the ceiling (if the surface overhead can be called that) hosts colonies of growth that hang in ridges and curtains—not stalactites, not limestone formations, but something more like shelves of material accumulated and abandoned. They shimmer with the same light that suffuses the space, and things move across their surfaces. You cannot name them. They have too many legs or not enough, and they travel in directions that don't correspond to the walls, sometimes climbing directly across angles that should not support weight. They do not fall. They scatter when your shadow (when you have a shadow; sometimes you do not) passes near them, and they make no sound.

The soundlessness is its own presence. You hear nothing from them—not the click of insect legs, not the rustle of movement through growth. You hear nothing from yourself except the pulse in your own ears. The space muffles. The space listens.

Larger shapes move in the periphery. You see them best when you do not look directly. They flow between the sparse protrusions and formations, and they have bodies in the way that clouds have shapes—provisionally, temporarily, with edges that suggest mass but do not commit to it. Some move close enough that you could touch them. You do not. They have patterns on their surfaces that resemble eyes, or mouths, or wounds, or doors, and the patterns rotate slowly, perpetually, cycling through possibilities.

One drifts near—a mass the size of your torso, colored like smoke and lit from within by that same sourceless glow. It observes you. You know it observes you because the patterns on its surface slow and focus, coalescing into something that looks almost like a face before dissolving again into abstraction. It does not approach. It does not flee. It hangs in the air (or the air-aspect of the space; you have stopped being certain what is atmosphere and what is solid) and considers you the way you might consider a word you can almost remember.

Then it moves on. They all move on. You are not what they are waiting for.

---

The space has a rhythm, and it begins to reveal itself as you stay.

The light does not dim or brighten, but it migrates. Pools of brightness shift across surfaces like sunlight through water, and the growths respond—the tube-clusters orient toward the glow, the curtain-formations expand and contract, the things that crawl across them speed and slow in time. The moisture follows. Condensation thickens where the light pools, and the wetness there becomes almost gelatinous, almost fertile, and the formations in those places look more complete. More formed.

You begin to understand: this is a nursery. An incubator. A wound healing itself into something new.

The raw geology is not accidental. The space is making itself, and it is making the things inside itself, and it is not done. The protrusions are not decorative—they are scaffolding. The pools are not features—they are material. The creatures are not inhabitants—they are participants, workers in an ecosystem of emergence. They tend the growths. They consume what is over. They move the moisture from place to place. They do not notice you because you are not part of the process. You are debris. You are a pause in the pattern.

And yet: the space tolerates you. The tubes do not ingest. The crawling things do not swarm. The floating masses do not press against you or push you toward the exit (and the exit is still there, you can feel it behind you, a pressure and a promise of return). You are permitted. You are observed and recorded and incorporated into whatever the space is becoming, and when you leave, a small part of you will remain—a breath, a warmth, a trace of the passing that might eventually harden into something structural.

---

There is a structure here, or the remains of one.

It emerges from the wall where the growth is thickest—not built, exactly, but revealed. A frame of something like metal, like bone, like neither, half-emerged from the forming stone. It might have been a shelter. It might have been a marker. It might have been something that tried to build here before and was consumed into the process, digested and reconstituted into something that serves the space's own purposes. Its edges are soft. Its surfaces are merging with the growth around it. It is becoming wall, becoming foundation, becoming raw material for whatever is being made here.

Near it, scattered across the ground (and the ground here is uneven, sloping in ways that suggest intention, channels and basins forming patterns you can almost read), are other artifacts—a shard of something smooth and manufactured, its purpose unclear; a length of material that might once have been cable or cord, now fused with the stone; the suggestion of a shape that might have been a tool, might have been a limb, might have been an accident. They are not important. They are being recycled. The space uses everything.

You understand, standing in this brightness, watching the slow work of formation around you, that you have found a place where the world makes itself. Where reality has not yet hardened. Where the rules are suggestions and the physics is negotiable and the light that suffuses everything is the light of a beginning.

---

When you leave—and you will leave, because the space is not for you, because your presence is a pause and pauses end—the passage will not be where you expect. You will turn and find it behind you, or beside you, or beneath you, and you will step through into the world you know, and you will carry the brightness with you for a while. It will fade from your skin. It will dim behind your eyes. But something in you will have been added to, something in you will have been made more.

You have been witnessed by the things that are still becoming. You have been recorded in the geology of emergence. And somewhere in that pocket beneath the world, in that room where morning is eternal and nothing is ever finished, a small part of your passing has begun to form into something new.
