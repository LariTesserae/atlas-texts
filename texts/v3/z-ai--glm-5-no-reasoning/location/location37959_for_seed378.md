# The Condensaries of Vash Morrain

## I. Approach from the High Road

You smell the place before you see it. Not rot — nothing here rots in the way you'd recognize — but a mineral sweetness, like wet chalk heated past comfort, like the air above a cooling forge. The high road descends through switchbacks cut into pale granite, and as you drop toward the plateau, the world's palette narrows. Stone. Steam. The faintest tint of iron oxide staining cliff faces in long vertical streaks, as though the rock itself weeps rust.

The plateau of Vash Morrain sits at altitude — high enough that the sky feels close, a pale dome of white heat rather than blue depth. The sun here doesn't seem to shine from a single point. It diffuses through a permanent high haze, a luminous veil that flattens shadows and makes the air itself visible, slightly grainy, like looking through thin silk. On the brightest days — and most days are bright — you can stare toward the sun without pain. It's simply a brighter region of the white, a hot bloom in the overcast.

Everything is bright and wet and hot and nearly silent, and this combination takes getting used to.

The landscape opens as you descend: a broad plateau perhaps four kilometers across, cupped between ridgelines on three sides and open to the south where a long slope falls away toward lower country. The plateau is not flat. It's textured, ridged, terraced in places by old lava flows that cooled in stepped formations, and everywhere — *everywhere* — water moves.

Not dramatically. Not rivers or waterfalls. The water here seeps. It weeps. It appears as a film on stone, as rivulets too small to name, as condensation that forms on surfaces and gathers and runs. The rock of Vash Morrain is a dense, fine-grained basalt shot through with vesicles and fractures, and water sits just below its surface like blood beneath skin. Press your palm to any exposed face and you'll feel dampness within seconds. The stone is warm — always warm, 38 degrees, perhaps 40 — and the water within it is the same temperature, body-temperature, which makes the wetness feel strange. Not cool relief. Just... acknowledgment. The place is wet the way a body is wet. Internally. Persistently.

From the high road, the settlement is visible as geometry against the organic terrain. Straight lines. Right angles. The dark circles of cooling towers. The skeletal frames of gantries and pipe-bridges spanning low points in the ground. Vash Morrain is a town built to harvest condensation, and everything in it serves that purpose.

## II. The Architecture of Waiting

The town — call it a town, though it has none of a town's warmth — holds perhaps six hundred structures of significant size, scattered across the plateau with generous space between them. The density is low. Buildings stand alone or in pairs, separated by thirty, fifty, sometimes a hundred meters of bare stone ground. Paths connect them — not roads, paths, worn into the rock by decades of foot traffic, shallow grooves that collect their own thin films of water.

The buildings are constructed from the basalt itself, quarried on-site, cut into blocks that fit together without mortar. The joints are precise, almost airtight, and the blocks have a matte darkness to them that absorbs light rather than reflecting it. This gives the settlement a strange visual quality: the ground is pale where condensation has deposited mineral films on its surface, but the buildings are dark, negative spaces, shadows built into architecture. From a distance, the town looks like holes in a bright surface. Like something has been removed rather than constructed.

The largest structures are the condensaries — the reason the town exists at all.

A condensary is a tall, narrow building, perhaps twenty meters high and eight meters square at its base, built over a natural seep or fracture in the basalt where warm water rises close to the surface. The building's interior is a vertical chamber, open at the top, lined with a lattice of dark stone plates arranged in overlapping rows. The plates are cold — or rather, cooler than the air, cooler than the water, positioned at the altitude where the perpetual haze begins to condense. Warm vapor rises from the seep below, fills the chamber, meets the plates, and condenses. Water beads on the dark stone, runs down channels cut into the plate surfaces, gathers in collection basins at the bottom, and is piped to storage cisterns or processing facilities.

Each condensary hums. Not loudly. A vibration felt more than heard, a low-frequency thrum at the edge of perception, like a chord played on an instrument too large to see. The sound comes from the engines — steam-driven turbines that power the pumps moving collected water through the distribution system. The engines are simple, robust things, brass and iron and ceramic, designed decades ago and maintained with a craftsman's patience. Each has a rhythm, a breathing pattern of intake and exhaust, and when several condensaries operate near each other, their rhythms interlace into a polyphonic murmur that the residents have lived with so long it registers as silence.

Which is to say: the town is quiet the way a library is quiet. Not empty of sound, but bounded by it. The loudest thing is your own footsteps. Then the wet, mineral hiss of condensation forming on stone. Then the engine-thrum, felt in the soles of your feet. Then — almost nothing. Almost.

## III. What Lives Here

The animals came after the town. This is important. Vash Morrain was built, and then life arrived, drawn by the conditions the town had created — the constant moisture, the warmth, the sheltered pools, the absence of predators. The ecology here is entirely dependent on the settlement. Without the condensaries and their outflow, the plateau would be bare rock. With them, it has become something else.

The most visible inhabitants are the striders.

Standing a meter and a half tall, striders are long-legged, warm-skinned creatures that occupy the ecological niche of wading birds — though they are not birds, not exactly. They lack feathers, their skin smooth and dappled in patterns of grey and warm brown, like wet river stones. Their legs are disproportionately long, jointed in three places rather than two, and they move with a delicacy that seems impossible for their size, picking their way across wet stone surfaces without slipping, without splashing. They gather at the outflow pools where processed water is released from the condensaries, standing in shallow warmth, dipping their narrow heads to feed on what grows in the wet margins.

What grows there is not vegetation. Nothing here is vegetation in any familiar sense. The plateau of Vash Morrain has no plants — no grasses, no mosses, no lichens, no rooted things of any kind. What it has is a mineral film. A living film. A thin, gelatinous layer that forms on wet stone surfaces, pale and slightly iridescent, with the consistency of raw egg white. It coats the ground near outflow pools, spreads along water channels, clings to the lower walls of buildings where runoff trickles. Under bright light, it sheens with color — greens, blues, the occasional copper flash. It is not algae. It is not a bacterium. It is something else, something that the settlement's chemists have studied for decades without satisfying classification, something that metabolizes dissolved minerals from the warm water and sunlight from the bright haze and grows, slowly, in thin, spreading sheets.

The mineral film is the base of everything alive here. The striders eat it. So do the other creatures — the crawlers, the drabblers, the things that have no common names because the residents of Vash Morrain never needed to name them and have no particular fondness for the natural world.

The crawlers are low, broad creatures, perhaps forty centimeters across, flat and dark, with many legs — six, eight, sometimes more, the count varies — that move in coordinated waves across the film-covered stone, grazing. They are slow and methodical, and where they have passed, the stone is clean, scraped to its dark surface, the film consumed. They leave trails. You can map the crawlers' territories by the patterns of bare stone and regrowing film, a patchwork that shifts over days and weeks.

Above the striders, riding thermal updrafts from the warm plateau, are the gliders — and here the strangeness of Vash Morrain's ecology becomes harder to avoid. The gliders are not animals in any comfortable sense. They are membranous things, translucent, perhaps a meter in wingspan, with bodies no larger than a closed fist and vast, veined sheets of tissue stretched between structural ribs. They look like leaves. They look like kites. They look like something a child might make from paper and wishbone sticks, except that they move with purpose, riding the thermals in slow, tilting circles, descending to the outflow pools to drink — they drink, they do not eat — and then rising again to the bright haze above, where they become invisible against the white sky.

The residents tolerate them. All of them. The striders, the crawlers, the gliders, the smaller things that live in the collection basins and must be filtered from the water supply, the things that live in the engines' cooling jackets and must be cleaned out during maintenance. The residents tolerate the life of Vash Morrain the way they tolerate the dampness and the heat and the light — as conditions of the place, neither celebrated nor resented.

This tolerance is, perhaps, the strangest thing about the settlement. There is no agriculture here. No domestication. No partnership between human and animal. The people of Vash Morrain built their town, and life arrived uninvited, and the people looked at it and shrugged and continued working.

## IV. A Day in the Heat

Morning. The haze brightens first, a slow increase from luminous grey to brilliant white, and the shadows — what few there are — sharpen slightly as the diffuse light gains a directional component. Temperature is already high; on the plateau, it does not cool meaningfully at night. The rock holds heat. The water holds heat. The air holds heat. There is no relief, only degrees of intensity.

The condensaries operate on a cycle tied to the light. As the haze brightens, warming the upper plates, condensation efficiency drops. The engines throttle down — you feel the thrum slow, shift pitch, settle into a lower register. Water flow through the distribution pipes decreases. The outflow pools at the base of the plateau shrink, their margins receding, exposing wet stone that steams faintly in the heat.

The striders notice. They always notice. They move between pools in a slow, deliberate migration, following the water, gathering where outflow remains. Their movements are unhurried — nothing here hurries, nothing can afford to waste water in exertion — and they make no sound as they walk. Long legs placing themselves with precision on wet stone. Bodies swaying slightly with each step. They do not call to one another. They do not display. They exist in a kind of collective quiet that matches the settlement's own.

Midday is the brightest. The haze goes incandescent, a white so intense that depth perception suffers, and the plateau seems to flatten into a surface without relief. Buildings cast no shadows; light comes from everywhere. The mineral film on the wet stones glows, iridescent, and the outflow pools become mirrors of light, white surfaces reflecting white sky. The gliders rise to their highest altitudes and vanish. The crawlers retreat to the shade beneath buildings and the interiors of broken stone formations. The striders stand motionless in the remaining pools, legs submerged, heads tucked, conserving.

This is the settlement's working day, paradoxically. The residents of Vash Morrain move through the bright hours performing maintenance, construction, the mechanical tasks that keep the condensaries running. They wear loose, pale garments that reflect the haze and minimize heat absorption. They wrap their hands and feet in mineral-treated cloth that grips wet stone. They do not speak much. Communication is gestural, economical — a pointing finger, a tilted head, a hand placed flat on a surface to indicate *here, this one, this needs attention*. Speech exists but is reserved for the evening, for the shaded interiors of the workshop buildings, for the times when the haze dims enough to make the air feel less like a medium and more like a gas.

The workshops are where the engines are built and repaired. They are the only enclosed spaces in the settlement — the condensaries are open-topped, the storage cisterns are roofless basins, the residences are sheltered platforms rather than rooms — and they are where the settlement's minimal technology is concentrated. Lathes for cutting engine components. Forges for shaping metal pipe and plate. Distillation arrays for refining the minerals collected from the condensate. The workshops are warm, close, lit by the diffuse light that enters through high, narrow vents, and they smell of hot metal and stone dust and the faint, sweet, mineral scent of the water that flows through every crack and seam.

Afternoon. The haze thins slightly, and for an hour or two, the light gains a faint warmth — not yellow, not quite, but less absolute than the midday white. Shadows return, short and dark. The condensaries begin to recover efficiency as their upper plates cool fractionally, and the engine-thrum rises in pitch and intensity. Water flow increases. The outflow pools expand. The striders stir, begin to feed, begin to move. The crawlers emerge from the shade and spread across the regrown film. The gliders descend from the haze, appearing first as faint shapes, then as defined bodies, then as the paper-thin membranes they are, circling lower and lower until they skim the pools' surfaces, drinking.

The settlement's evening is not dark. The haze holds light long after the sun has set, a persistent luminescence that fades slowly through phases of brightness — white to pearl to ivory to a deep, warm grey — and never reaches true black. On the clearest nights, you can see the bright regions of the lower plateau, the outflow pools glowing with the heat they've absorbed during the day, and the dark silhouettes of striders standing in them, still as carved wood.

Night is when the condensaries run at full capacity. The upper plates cool to their lowest temperatures, and the warm vapor rising from the seeps condenses rapidly, running down the plates in continuous sheets. The engines run at full throttle, their thrum deep and steady, and the collection basins fill. The residents sleep on raised platforms in the open air, wrapped in mineral-treated cloth that wicks condensation from their skin, and the settlement is quiet except for the sound of water gathering, water moving, water being captured and stored and distributed to whatever need the next day will bring.

## V. The Feeling of the Place

Vash Morrain is a place that has forgotten why it was built.

Not the mechanical reason — the condensaries work, the water is collected, the engines run, the distribution system functions. The mechanical reason is intact. But the *purpose* — who needs the water, where it goes, why six hundred people live on a hot, wet, bright plateau harvesting condensation from basalt — that purpose has eroded, the way the mineral film erodes the stone it grows on, slowly, imperceptibly, until the surface has changed and no one remembers the original.

The residents are not unhappy. They are not trapped. They are not desperate, not struggling, not surviving against odds. They live well, in the way that people who have mastered their environment live — with competence, with routine, with the small pleasures of craft and habit. They maintain the engines. They repair the condensary plates. They filter the mineral film from the collection basins. They eat — food comes from the lower country, traded for water, which is Vash Morrain's only product — and they sleep and they work and they do not, as far as anyone can tell, wonder.

But there is a quality to the settlement that a visitor feels immediately, a quality that has nothing to do with the heat or the wet or the brightness or the near-silence. It is the quality of a place that has achieved balance with its own strangeness. The animals that live here — the striders, the crawlers, the gliders, the mineral film itself — arrived after the town was built, and they have become part of the town's operation without being part of its design. The residents clean mineral film from pipes and tolerate striders at their outflow pools and watch gliders circle above their condensaries with an expression that is not quite indifference and not quite acceptance but something else, something that has no word because it describes a relationship so specific it exists only here: the relationship between people who have built a machine for harvesting water from stone and the life that has grown around that machine like coral around a sunken ship.

The world has rules here, and they are these: water rises from the stone. The stone is always warm. The air is always wet. The light is always bright. The engines run. The condensaries fill. The animals feed and drink and live. The people work and sleep and do not speak. And the plateau holds it all — the basalt and the water and the mineral film and the striders and the condensaries and the engines and the silence — in a balance that has existed long enough to feel permanent, long enough to feel inevitable, long enough to feel like something that was not built but found, not constructed but discovered, a landscape that was always going to be this and has simply been waiting for the people and the machines and the animals to arrive and complete it.

You stand on the high road looking down at the plateau, and the white haze diffuses the light, and the condensaries stand like dark pillars against the pale ground, and the striders move between the outflow pools with their impossible grace, and the engines hum their subliminal chord, and the water runs and runs and runs, and you realize that what you are looking at is not a town. It is an organism. A mineral, wet, bright, hot, silent organism that has grown around the fracture in the basalt where the warm water rises, and the people and the animals and the engines are its organs, and the film is its skin, and the condensaries are its mouths, and it drinks the sky.

It drinks the sky, and it has been drinking the sky for longer than anyone remembers, and it will continue to drink the sky for longer than anyone will remember, and this is not troubling. This is not strange. This is simply what Vash Morrain is.
