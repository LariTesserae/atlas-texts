# The Singing Hollow

High on the spine of an ancient mountain range, where the air grows thin and the sky presses close, there is a hollow carved from white porous stone—a natural amphitheater perhaps a hundred meters across, rimmed by weathered walls that rise like the bones of a collapsed cathedral. The hollow is old. The hollow is patient. But what fills it is neither.

---

The first thing is the sound. You hear it before you see it, a quarter mile down the ridge. A wall of noise that resolves, as you approach, into distinct threads: clicks and chirrups, grinding rhythmic pulses, high keening whistles that rise and fall like breathing. Insect noise, but wrong. Bird calls, but no bird you know. The sounds layer and compete, frequencies堆stacking into something almost musical, almost mechanical—a chaos of voices that never settles into pattern.

The hollow has no water. No streams, no pools, no dew. The white stone is dry as bone, its surface soft and chalky, rubbing away under fingertips in fine powder. And yet.

---

The vegetation is impossible. That is the second thing.

Mats of deep purple growth cover the floor in scattered patches, each patch isolated from the others by meters of bare stone. They are not plants. They have no leaves, no stems, no roots in any recognizable sense. Instead, each colony spreads in a fractal spiral of flat plates—like succulent leaves crossed with tree fungus crossed with something you have no name for. The plates are thick and fleshy, storing something viscous and amber that you can see through their translucent surfaces. Not water. Something else. Some complex hydrocarbon soup, perhaps, or a sugar-alcohol compound that holds moisture from the air so efficiently that the colony sweats, actually sweats, beads of sweet liquid forming on its surface each dawn when the temperature shifts.

Between the purple colonies, scattered at irregular intervals, stand the growths that made you think, from a distance, of a grove. They reach two, sometimes three meters high—trunk-like structures of bundled fibers, pale green and tough as leather, each one topped with a crown of rigid tendrils that turn slowly, incrementally, tracking something. Not the sun. The sun is dim here, the light filtered through a perpetual haze that makes everything soft-edged and shadowless. No, these track each other. The tendrils are always turning, touching at their tips, exchanging something. Information, maybe. Resources. Arguments.

The air is warm. Warmer than it should be at this altitude. Heat rises from the stone itself, from somewhere below—a geothermal pulse that keeps the hollow in a perpetual growing season, a pocket of summer trapped in the mountain's cold body.

---

The animals are the third thing, and they are everywhere.

You notice the small ones first: creatures like elongated insects, segmented bodies in metallic colors—copper and bronze and pale gold—that move in long looping paths across the stone. They do not fly. They have no wings. Instead they leap, prodigious and precise, clearing meters at a bound, landing on the purple colonies to drink from the sweat, launching again. Their legs are long and jointed wrong, too many segments, and when they land they make the clicking sounds that thread through the larger noise.

Larger creatures move between the fiber-bundle trees. You cannot see them clearly at first—they are fast, always at the edge of vision, blurs of motion that resolve only when they stop. When one does stop, five meters away, to regard you, you see something like a small deer crossed with a reptile crossed with something that should not exist. Its body is sleek and low-slung, covered in fine scales that shift color as you watch. Its face is eyeless, dominated instead by a sensory organ you don't understand—a flattened disk of sensitive tissue that twitches, sampling the air, the vibrations, the constant sound. It has four legs but can stand on two. It has no ears but flinches at the loudest pulses. It watches you, somehow, without eyes. Then it is gone, a blur of motion, and you understand that everything here moves at a speed you cannot match.

The hollow is full of them. You count a dozen species in the first hour, then lose track. There are burrowers in the soft stone, their tunnels visible as pale openings in the walls. There are climbers that spiral the fiber-bundles, harvesting something from their crowns. There are creatures that hang from the rim, suspended on threads, descending to hunt and ascending to rest. And there are the noise-makers—you finally see them, small drab bodies clustered in the highest crevices, opening their mouths to release sounds that hurt the ears, that vibrate in the chest. They never stop. The noise never stops.

---

The fourth thing is the constant change.

You notice it after an hour of watching. A purple colony that was separated from its neighbor by three meters of bare stone is now only two meters away—its edge creeping, almost visibly growing, sending out runners that look like thick veins. The fiber-bundles have turned more, their tendrils reaching new configurations. An orange fungal growth has appeared on the eastern wall where there was none before, and it is spreading, sending out spores that drift in the warm air and catch on things.

This is not a stable place. You realize this with a start. It looks settled—the stone is old, the forms are established—but everything here is in constant competition. Constant motion. Constant transformation.

A fight breaks out between two creatures near the hollow's center. You don't know what they are—something like flightless birds, something like feathered lizards—but they are fast and vicious, their conflict over in seconds, leaving one dead and the other dragging the body away. Before the winner has reached the rim, the spot where blood fell is already being colonized. Small copper-colored insects cluster and feed. A pale fuzz of something like mold begins to spread. The hollow eats what falls. The hollow uses everything.

Within minutes, the dead creature's outline has changed. Within an hour, you cannot tell where it fell.

---

The fifth thing is the marks.

The stone walls, on close inspection, are covered in lines. Shallow grooves, worn smooth by time but clearly intentional. They are not writing—they carry no repeating symbols, no clear grammar. But they are made. Some hand, or claw, or tool, carved them. They follow the natural fissures in the stone, emphasizing some and connecting others, creating patterns that draw the eye up and across the walls in spirals.

Near the rim, on the western edge, the grooves cluster more densely. They form a shape that might be a figure, might be a tree, might be nothing at all. The stone around it is darker, stained by something that was painted or burned long ago. The stain has faded to a faint shadow, but it persists.

You find the structure on the second day.

It is small—a hut, barely, or what remains of one. Built into a crevice at the hollow's edge, constructed from the same pale stone as the walls, its walls are half-melted, worn down by centuries of the same warm wind that carries the sound. The entrance is a low opening, barely large enough to crawl through. Inside, the space is tiny—two meters across at its widest. There are no artifacts. No tools, no vessels, no remains. Only a single shelf carved into the back wall, and on that shelf, a single object.

A bundle of fiber-tendrils, dried and preserved, tied together with something like hair.

You take it down. The bundle is light and fragile. When you unwrap the fibers, something falls out—a small object, stone or ceramic, shaped like one of the creatures outside. It is worn nearly smooth, its features erased by handling. Someone sat here, in this hut, in this hollow, and held this object. Someone listened to the noise. Someone watched the motion. Someone lived in proximity to this chaos and found it normal.

---

On the third day, you begin to understand the rhythm.

The sound changes. It has been changing all along, but you didn't notice—the shifts are gradual, the frequencies sliding up and down like a slow tide. The hollow has a cycle, tied to something you cannot perceive. Not the sun, which moves in its dim arc without variation. Not the temperature, which stays constant, warm and close. Something else. A pressure, perhaps. A vibration in the rock that you feel in your teeth before you feel it in your feet.

When the pressure builds, the sound intensifies. The noise-makers call louder. The leapers jump higher. The fiber-bundles turn faster. And then, at the peak of whatever cycle this is, the hollow erupts.

It lasts only minutes. The purple colonies release their stored moisture all at once—a mist that fills the hollow, warm and thick, carrying the amber fluid in fine droplets. The creatures go still. The sound stops. In the sudden silence, the only noise is the hiss of the mist settling, the soft wet sound of the growths drinking what they released.

Then it ends. The mist clears. The noise returns. The motion resumes.

You realize, watching, that this is the hollow's heartbeat. This is the metabolism of a system that looks like a place but acts like an organism. The vegetation does not grow in soil—it grows in a substrate of constant activity, constant breakdown and rebuilding, constant exchange. The animals do not live in it—they are part of it, their movements and sounds and deaths feeding something that has no name.

The hollow is not a habitat. The hollow is a creature. And you are standing inside it.

---

You leave on the fourth day. You do not descend the same way you came—you find a path worn into the stone, a narrow track that switchbacks down the mountain's face. The noise follows you for a long time, fading only when you have walked for hours, when the air has grown cold and the vegetation has given way to bare rock.

You still have the small stone figure. You are not sure why you took it. You are not sure why you kept it. But when you hold it, you can almost hear the sound of the hollow—quiet, now, a distant memory of that wall of noise, that ceaseless motion, that constant chaotic life.

It is a small thing, this landscape. A room, more than a region. A single feature in a vast and empty mountain range. But it is full. It is loud. It is alive in ways you do not have words for. And it will stay with you, that sound, that motion, that sense of a place that should not be but is—lush and dry, warm and high, settled and chaotic, strange beyond measure and yet occupied, marked, known.

Someone lived there once. Someone listened. And now, for a little while, you have listened too.
