# The Drowned Scaffolding

High above where oceans ought to be, water moves like a living curtain. This is no sea floor—this is a mountaintop plateau caught in the moment of becoming, submerged beneath thirty meters of warm, mineral-rich water that fell not from clouds but rose from cracks in the earth's own bones. The water is geothermal, heated from below, carrying a faint sulfurous sweetness that coats everything in a thin organic glaze.

The plateau sits at impossible elevation, a platform of raw, unfinished stone—no, not stone exactly. The rock here has the texture of hardened foam, pocked with vesicles and ranging in color from bone-white to rust-orange. It is embryonic geology, material still deciding what it wants to become. Striations suggest future fault lines that haven't yet faulted. Ridges rise in smooth, curve-topped formations that dissolve under touch, shedding fine powder into the current. The ground feels temporary underfoot, as though you walk on a thought still forming.

And everywhere, rising from this calcified scaffolding: vegetation.

Not plants as we know them. These are long, reed-like stalks of a fibrous material somewhere between plant and mineral—flexible, warm to the touch, pulsing with slow internal rhythms. They grow in dense thickets, their bases swollen with gas-filled bladders that keep them upright despite the water's depth. Their leaves are broad and translucent, ribbed with veins that carry not sap but something luminescent, a faint amber glow that provides the only real illumination here. At the water's surface far above, sunlight filters down in diminished, blue-green shafts, but most light comes from below, from the vegetation itself, from the slow biological glow of the plateau's growth.

The vegetation is lush not in the manner of a jungle but in the manner of a single organism expanding—homogenous, pervasive, layered. Stalks rise from stalks, leaves overlap leaves, and in the spaces between, smaller growths cling: spongy mats of threadlike material, bulbous protrusions that inflate and deflate on some unseen schedule, coiled shapes that grip the rock and each other in configurations that seem almost architectural. The density creates a feeling of crowdedness, of being surrounded on all sides by presences that aren't quite alive and aren't quite inert.

This is a young place. The low erosion parameter manifests as a lack of finished edges—everything here is rounded, protuberant, in process. No water-worn smoothness, no wind-carved facets. The plateau is rising still, pushing upward through the water at a pace measurable in human lifetimes, and the water rises with it, pulled up by some capillary action in the rock itself. The submersion is ongoing, not a flood but a condition—a medium through which everything moves and breathes.

Sound travels differently here. The water dampens all frequencies, turning distant events into low, felt vibrations rather than audible phenomena. What can be heard is immediate: the creak of stalks bending in currents, the click of gas bubbles releasing from bladder-leaves, the wet scrape of underwater sediment settling. A persistent, low-frequency thrum resonates from deep within the plateau, the sound of geology under construction. It is not loud, but it is ever-present—a bass note that you feel in your sternum more than hear with your ears.

Above the plateau, visible as darker shapes against the distant light above, stretch the remains of human presence.

This was once a settlement. Perhaps still is, in some form. Buildings of a dark, volcanic stone climb the plateau's higher ridges, their walls thick and windowless, built to withstand pressures that never came. The architecture is primitive but competent—dry-stacked blocks fitted without mortar, arched doorways leading to chambers now filled with water, stairs carved into the rock itself descending to depths that seem to serve no purpose. There is no metal here, no glass, no evidence of advanced fabrication. The builders used what they had: stone, plant fiber, bone, and a kind of underwater cement made from the same calcified foam that forms the plateau's crust.

The buildings are layered—older structures at the bottom, newer ones climbing above them, connected by networks of rope bridges and wooden platforms that now float suspended in the water column, drifting with the gentle currents like the skeletons of docks. Ropes made of woven plant-fiber crisscross the settlement, anchoring everything together, thick with accumulated growth and fuzzy with algae-like coatings. Dwellings cluster together densely, walls nearly touching, creating narrow passages between them that are now underwater canyons, shaded and difficult to navigate.

It is impossible to tell if the settlement was built before or after the submersion. Some structures seem designed for dry living, with floors now coated in sediment and ceilings that trap air pockets. Others have the look of deliberate underwater construction—low entrances that prevent floatables from escaping, interior shelves positioned to hold objects in place, channels cut into walls to guide water flow. Perhaps both. Perhaps the inhabitants adapted gradually, building upward as the water rose, learning to live in the half-drowned world they were creating.

Or perhaps they are still here.

Fauna is rare, but not absent. In the shadowed spaces between buildings, in the canopy of amber-lit stalks, things move with unhurried purpose. They are difficult to see clearly—dark shapes, the size of large dogs but with too many limbs, moving with a fluidity that suggests exoskeletons rather than internal bones. They make no sound, or none that travels through water. They watch, perhaps, from crevices and windows, patient as the plateau itself. Occasionally, a singular, ululating cry echoes through the water, a vibration that seems to come from everywhere at once. The source is never visible.

The settlement's original inhabitants left artifacts but no bodies, no obvious signs of violence or departure. Pottery shards drift in the sediment. Tools of bone and stone rest where they were set down. Woven mats unspool slowly in corners. It is as though everyone simply stepped out of time, continuing their lives in a dimension just adjacent to this one. The buildings feel less like ruins than like rooms between uses—dormant, waiting, still holding the warmth of recent occupation.

A particularly dense cluster of buildings rises near the plateau's center, its structures taller and more elaborately connected than the others. This must have been the heart of the community, though its purpose is obscure. Interior walls bear marks—grooves and indentations that might be writing, might be decoration, might be maps. Hard to tell in the dim light, with water distorting everything, with surfaces overgrown by the ever-expanding vegetation. The stalks here have grown through windows and doors, their amber glow brightest in this central place, turning the chambers into organic lanterns.

The vegetation treats the buildings as substrate. It climbs their walls, anchors in their cracks, pulls nutrients from the very stone. In some places, the growth has so thoroughly infiltrated the architecture that it is impossible to tell where building ends and plant begins—the settlement and its garden have become a single entity, a hybrid structure of intelligence and instinct. This merger has a strange beauty, a symbiosis that suggests intent, as though the inhabitants cultivated this interweaving deliberately.

Warm water rises from vents in the plateau floor, keeping the whole place at a temperature just above comfortable—too warm for typical ocean life, too cool for true thermal pools. The heat is constant, emanating from the earth's interior, a slow exhalation that will continue for centuries. It is this warmth that allows the vegetation to grow so lushly at such depth, this warmth that makes the water feel like a living medium rather than a hostile submersion.

The water itself moves in slow, predictable cycles—warm rising from below, cooler descending from the surface, creating gentle currents that swirl through the settlement like breath. The motion is barely perceptible, a drifting of suspended sediment, a swaying of stalks, a gradual mixing of the mineral-rich deep water with the organic-touched shallows. Things float past—debris from above, shed leaves from below, occasional bubbles of gas released from the plateau's unseen depths.

Time moves strangely here. The low dynamic parameter means that change happens, but slowly, on scales that make individual observation difficult. The plateau rises by centimeters per decade. The vegetation expands by millimeters per day. The buildings settle imperceptibly into the soft stone. If you were to sit perfectly still, watching a single point, you might notice nothing moving at all—and yet, over hours, the light would shift, the water would turn over, the shapes of things would rearrange themselves by fractions. Patience is the primary quality of this place, a willingness to exist in a state of perpetual becoming without rushing toward any particular conclusion.

There is no weather here, not really. But at intervals—long, irregular intervals—the water above grows turbulent, and debris rains down from the surface world, far above, whatever world that might be. Shards of wood, fragments of unfamiliar materials, occasional dead things that the scavengers investigate with quiet interest. These events create momentary disruptions, flurries of activity in the otherwise still water, and then the plateau settles back into its patient equilibrium.

The weirdness of this place is not spectacular. It is cumulative. It accumulates in small observations: the way the vegetation's glow intensifies when touched, the way the buildings' interiors feel larger than their exteriors, the way sound sometimes echoes before it occurs, the way time feels elastic, the way the animals watch with eyes that reflect no light, the way the plateau seems to be waiting for something specific—something it has been waiting for since before the settlement was built, since before the vegetation sprouted, since before the rock finished forming. The feeling is of a place that exists in preparation, holding space for an event that has not yet arrived.

At night, if night can be said to exist in this perpetually dim world, the amber glow of the vegetation dims to a faint pulse, a slow heartbeat of light that matches the plateau's subsonic thrum. The animals become more visible then, their dark shapes moving with more purpose, gathering in the central cluster of buildings as though conducting business that requires no language. The water feels heavier, thicker, and the boundaries between things grow uncertain—between solid and liquid, between living and non-living, between the settlement and the world around it, between what is present and what is merely possible.

The Drowned Scaffolding is not hostile. It is not welcoming. It simply is, in a state of perpetual emergence, a place becoming itself at a pace that denies urgency. To be here is to participate in that becoming, to feel the slow motion of geological time, to breathe water that has been breathed a thousand times before, to watch the vegetation grow toward a light source it has never seen, to inhabit rooms that might be awaiting your return or might be slowly absorbing you into their calcified walls.

This is a place that teaches patience by example. It does not explain itself. It offers no narrative, only conditions—warmth, water, light, growth, the slow rise of raw earth toward some surface it may never reach. And in those conditions, something lives. Something builds. Something waits.
