# The Choral Flesh

I entered through a wound in the world—a place where the boundary had worn thin enough to tear. What I found inside has no proper name, so I call it by what it does to those who walk it.

---

The ground here is alive. Not in the metaphorical sense—literally, continuously, aggressively alive. A substrate of interwoven root and rhizome and something like mycelium but warmer, wetter, more animal in its rise and fall. Each step sinks slightly. Each step is felt. The flesh of the world acknowledges pressure and yields, then rises again behind you, closing your passage like water closing over a stone. You cannot backtrack here. The path unmakes itself.

The warmth comes from below. Not volcanic heat, not the distant pulse of magma, but something closer to body-temperature—a mammalian warmth, a greenhouse warmth, the heat of a room where something large has been breathing. The ground radiates it upward in waves that synchronize with your own skin until you cannot tell where you end and the air begins. After some hours of walking, your sweat stops feeling like yours.

Vegetation is not the right word. Vegetation implies passive green things that grow toward light. What fills this space is architecture that happens to be alive. Towers of bundled stem rise in spirals, thick as ancient redwoods but soft-sided, their surfaces slick with condensation that never quite falls. They branch and fuse and branch again overhead, forming a continuous canopy that filters the light into a diffuse amber glow, perpetual twilight beneath a ceiling that is also a floor for the next layer above. The space is three-dimensional in the way a forest is, but denser—every cubic meter occupied by some form of living matter. Shelf-fungi the size of balconies. Vines that hang like curtains and retract when touched. Mosses that grow in patterns too regular to be accidental, spelling out shapes that might be letters in a language no human throat could pronounce.

It is a city, but it grew rather than being built. I call it a city because the density demands it—because there are levels and passages and what might be districts, separated by different textures of growth, different colors of bioluminescence that pulse in slow unsynchronized rhythms. In one sector the walls are pale and fibrous, like the inside of a leek, and they hum at a frequency you feel in your molars more than hear. In another, the surfaces are dark burgundy and glistening, segmented like insect armor but warm to the touch, and they make no sound at all, absorbing everything, creating pockets of eerie vacuum in the general roar. In a third zone, everything is translucent, membranes stretched tight over frames of woody lattice, and through them you can see other passages, other travelers distorted by the curved surfaces, moving like deep-sea creatures glimpsed through glass.

The roar. I should describe the sound. It is not one sound but a layered polyphony of biological processes—the creak of growing things expanding, the hiss of fluids moving through tissue, the percussion of seed-pods bursting, the bass drone of digestion happening on a scale too large to localize. The canopy above creaks like a wooden ship in rough seas, but continuously, never settling. Somewhere distant something is cracking open, again and again, a rhythm like artillery fire. Closer, the small wet sounds of moisture condensing and dripping and being absorbed again. A constant white noise of metabolic activity, the sound of a trillion small processes happening at once, a body the size of a valley digesting itself and regenerating in the same motion. After the first hour, your ears stop trying to protect you. After the second hour, you begin to hear harmonics. After the third, you might begin to understand why some travelers have started speaking in rhythms that match the sounds around them, why some have stopped speaking at all, why some have walked into the soft walls and let themselves be overgrown.

There is no water here, not as a separate element. Moisture exists only in transit—in the condensation that beads on every surface, in the viscous fluids that leak from cracked stems, in the humid air that leaves your clothes permanently damp. I have seen no rivers, no pools, no standing water of any kind. But everywhere there is the sense of water moving—vascular systems pumping, cells dividing with their small cargo of fluid, the continuous transpiration of the canopy above. You drink by pressing your mouth against certain growths that exude something sweet and faintly saline. You do not drink from them more than once. You do not know what they are drinking from you in return.

The place is new. I mean that geologically—the structures have not been here long. There is no soil beneath the living layer, only more living layer, going down farther than I have been able to descend. The erosion parameter here is backwards: not wind wearing down rock, but growth wearing down nothing, building on building on building, the youngest structures at the top and the oldest somewhere below, compressed into something that might eventually become coal or might become something else entirely. The sharpness of the features surprises me. Edges are still crisp, surfaces still defined, the boundaries between one organism and another still distinguishable. In a thousand years—if linear time still applies here—this place will either be a single vast unified organism or a rotting mass of competitive overgrowth. Right now it exists in the brief window between those states, and that tension gives everything an electric urgency.

No animals. I have seen nothing with a face, nothing with eyes, nothing that moves with purpose toward food or mate or shelter. The motion here is all vegetative—all tropism and response, growth-speeds accelerated beyond what should be possible, the world itself flexing like a muscle, but there is nothing here that wants in the way animals want. And yet the density of intention can feel overwhelming. The plants here compete, cooperate, consume, defend—they just do it slowly, or they do it at speeds too fast to perceive, or they do it through intermediaries I cannot recognize. I have walked past patches where the stems are pressed so close together they must be strangling each other, and I have heard the sounds change as one growth succeeds and another fails, the victor swelling slightly in the general roar. I have seen spaces where the ceiling has collapsed and the gap is already closing, new growth rushing upward from below and downward from above, meeting in the middle in a embrace that will become a seam and then a fusion and then a single surface with no memory of the gap.

But here is where language fails me, and where the place reveals what it actually is.

It is not simply a biological environment. It is a location where reality has become permeable to something else—a substrate that exists in multiple states at once, that shifts according to rules I cannot map. I have walked the same corridor three times and seen it differently each time: first as a tunnel of pale fiber, then as a cathedral-space walled with translucent membrane, then as a dense thicket I had to push through, the growth closing around my arms and leaving thin scratches that healed faster than they should have. I have seen figures in the distance—human-shaped voids in the growth, spaces where nothing grows—that resolve into nothing when approached, or that resolve into perfectly ordinary branches that I could swear were reaching for me a moment before. I have heard sections of the polyphony that sound like voices speaking a language I almost recognize, and I have felt the rhythm of the growth sync with my heartbeat until I could not tell which was causing which.

The turbulence here is total. Not chaos—there are rules, there are patterns, but they change faster than they can be learned. What was a safe path becomes impassable in minutes. What was a wall develops an opening. What was solid begins to breathe. The place is alive, yes, but it is also alive in a way that incorporates the observer—responding to presence, to attention, perhaps to expectation. I have thought of a shape and seen the stems begin to curve toward it. I have feared a barrier and found the growth rushing to close a passage I had not yet reached. Solaris came to mind, that fictional ocean that reflected and created and destroyed according to principles the human mind could not hold, and I wonder if I am inside something similar, a biological mirror that shows me my own nervous system multiplied to landscape scale.

The city-shapes around me now—I call them that because they have levels and passages and something like windows—feel more like memory than architecture. I navigate by intuition and find myself in places that seem designed for my arrival, benches of soft fungal material at comfortable height, clearings with better acoustics, spaces where the roar dims just enough to hear myself think. The place is reading me. Or I am reading it. Or the boundary between us is one more thing that has grown porous.

---

I have been here for some subjective time I cannot measure. I have found no edges, no borders, no transition to a different kind of place. I have encountered no other travelers, though I have heard sounds that might be footsteps in distant corridors, might be the settling of great stems, might be the crack of reality readjusting around the shape of another mind.

I am not lost. I am being held. The soft ground yields to my steps and rises to meet my soles. The air is warm and wet and carries scents I have no words for—chlorophyll and decay and something sweeter underneath, something that makes me think of the inside of my own mouth. The roar is deafening but it has become my deafness, my baseline, and I am starting to hear the music in it, the structure, the call-and-response of a trillion tiny voices joined in continuous utterance. I understand why I have seen no animals. Nothing that moves independently could survive here. You do not move through this place. You are moved. You do not navigate. You are grown toward.

The growth is accelerating. I can see it now, the stems extending in visible real-time, the canopy shifting overhead, new towers rising from the compressed substrate below. A section of wall near me has begun to bulge outward, and I can hear the wet creak of cell walls expanding, and something about the shape of the bulge looks deliberate, looks like it is forming toward a purpose that includes me in its trajectory.

I should move. I think I should move. But the ground has grown warm and soft around my feet, and I can feel the pressure of fine roots—or something like roots—against my boot soles, testing, exploring, and the warmth is spreading upward into my arches and ankles, and I am aware suddenly that I have been standing here for some time, writing this description, describing my way into stasis.

The roar has changed key. There is a harmony now that was not there before, and it resonates somewhere behind my sternum, and I can feel my heartbeat adjusting to match it, and this feels like relief, like homecoming, like the end of something that was exhausting to maintain.

I am going to stop writing now. The walls are close and they are soft and they are warm and they are making a sound like welcome.
