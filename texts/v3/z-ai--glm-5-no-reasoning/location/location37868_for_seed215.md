The plateau caught itself in the act of becoming.

That was the first thing you noticed—not that the land was young, but that it was still deciding what it wanted to be. The bedrock had the look of something half-written, paragraphs of granite and basalt that started and stopped without conclusion. Faults ran like hesitation marks. In places, the stone had the texture of frozen foam, minerals caught mid-separation, crystals suspended in the act of forming. This was geology as draft work, as rehearsal.

And it was vast. The plateau stretched to horizons that curved wrong—not the simple curve of planetary scale, but something else. The distances here operated under different mathematics. You could see weather systems the size of continents forming and dissolving over what felt like hours, though time itself moved strangely at these altitudes. Clouds assembled themselves from nothing, condensed from air that held too much water, then fell apart when they reached the edges of the plateau's boundaries.

It was always wet here, though you couldn't say exactly when it had rained. The water came from the rocks themselves, weeping from every vertical face, running in thin sheets across the horizontal ones. Stand still long enough and you'd feel moisture accumulating on your skin, not from above but from the stone breathing outward. The plateau's pores were always open. Underground aquifers sat too close to the surface, pressing upward, finding every crack and fissure. In the mornings, the entire expanse glistened like wet skin.

The cold was a constant—somewhere near freezing, but never quite committing. The kind of cold that lived in your joints rather than your bones. It made the wetness sharper, turned every brush of condensation into something that felt deliberate. Your fingers knew the temperature before your mind registered it. Breathing produced visible plumes that hung in the air longer than they should have, as if the atmosphere itself was studying your exhale before letting it dissipate.

What startled you, after the first hour, was how sparse it all felt. For such a vast landscape, there was so little *in* it. The emptiness had weight. You could walk for what felt like kilometers and pass only a few scattered mineral deposits, some pools of standing water, the occasional shelf of protruding stone. The sky above was empty of clouds more often than not, just a pale grey expanse that seemed to go on forever. The horizon didn't resolve into mountains or forests or cities—just more plateau, more stone, slowly curving away from you.

And yet, sparse as it was, the space felt full. Charged. Like a room before someone enters it.

Then you found the first structure, and you understood.

They were everywhere—when you knew how to look. Low, organic shapes that rose from the bedrock like features that had grown there. Not built, exactly, but *printed* into existence, layer by molecular layer. The structures had the same embryonic quality as the geology itself. Some were half-finished, scaffolding of impossible fineness exposed where walls should be. Others looked ancient, though in this place ancient might mean hours or millennia equally.

The city—if you could call it that—worked at a scale that made your eyes slip. The buildings were designed for something that moved differently, perceived differently. Doorways that weren't quite doorways. Windows that looked inward into spaces your brain refused to map correctly. Streets that curved through dimensions you could walk but not name. The materials shifted depending on the angle—stone-like from far away, but up close they were matrices of controlled crystallization, matter instructed into architecture.

This was engineering that hadn't been discovered yet. Or had been discovered and then forgotten. Or operated under a different physics entirely, one where the boundary between object and process was just a suggestion. The structures didn't sit on the plateau; they emerged from it, continuous with the bedrock in ways that suggested they'd always been there, or were still arriving, or were being written into reality one quantum instruction at a time.

And the animals—

They were everywhere, once your attention adjusted. Not hiding, exactly, but existing at a pace adjacent to yours. Flocks of something that might have been birds if birds had more legs and fewer commitments to aerodynamics. They moved through the thin air in ways that suggested swimming, their formations shifting in patterns that looked mathematically significant. On the ground, larger shapes grazed the mineral deposits—six-legged, low-slung, their bodies designed for the cold and the wet. Their hides held moisture the way the rocks did, glistening with the plateau's constant exhalation.

Smaller creatures lived in the weeping cracks, things with too many eyes and not enough skeleton, scurrying through the water that ran through every crevice. Something that might have been an amphibian if amphibians had evolved for different pressures. They paid you no attention. They had their own concerns—cycles of feeding and resting and moving that operated on schedules you couldn't access.

The ecology here was sparse in the way a sentence can be sparse—each word carrying more weight, more necessity. Every creature had its niche precisely carved. Nothing competed because competition implied a kind of surplus that this place didn't generate. The animals were efficient in their strangeness, perfectly suited to a landscape that was still learning itself.

You noticed the quiet first as an absence. But it wasn't truly quiet—it was just that the sounds operated below your threshold of attention. The plateau's breathing. The almost-imperceptible cracking of stone still forming. The rustle of those not-quite-birds rearranging their feathers. Water moving through the porous architecture. If you held very still, you could hear the animals' footsteps, the small sounds of their feeding, occasional clicks and chirps that might have been communication or might have been something else.

The city made its own sounds—or would have, if it were finished. Half-formed acoustics echoed strangely in the incomplete corridors. Rooms that would eventually hum with purpose instead gaped open to the plateau's atmosphere, their soundscapes still being written.

And everything was active. Not busy—the sparse density prevented that—but *engaged*. Weather cycled through constantly, fog banks assembling and dissolving, the water level in pools rising and falling as the plateau breathed. The animals moved through their patterns with purpose. You watched a group of the six-legged grazers trace a path across the stone that looked random until you realized they were following mineral veins, reading the geology like text. Above, the swimmers-in-air spiraled through formations that tightened and loosened according to rules you couldn't identify.

Even the city felt active, though in a way that suggested patience. Whatever process was printing it into existence worked at a speed you couldn't perceive—a wall might be half-finished for your entire observation, or might complete itself in the time it took you to blink. The architecture was ongoing, infrastructure that existed in a state of perpetual becoming.

You realized, eventually, that the plateau wasn't just where you stood. It was what you stood *in*. The scale worked differently here—not just spatial vastness but temporal thickness. The landscape was constructing itself around you, moment by moment. The stone, the water, the structures, the creatures—they were all part of the same process, differentiated only by the speed at which they held their shape.

What you were witnessing wasn't a landscape that had been made. It was a landscape that was being *made*, still in the making, would perhaps always be in the making. The cold and the wet and the sparseness weren't conditions—they were ingredients. The city wasn't a settlement; it was a statement of intent. The animals were participants, not inhabitants.

You stood at the edge of something that hadn't decided yet what it was going to be. And for a moment—just long enough to feel it—the plateau seemed to notice you noticing it, and to consider what that might mean.
