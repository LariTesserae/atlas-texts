# The Engine Garden

It exists inside a mountain. Or perhaps the mountain was built around it—the geology here is uncertain, still deciding what it wants to be. The stone has the look of something half-dreamed: grainy, particulate, shifting subtly at the edge of vision. Not erosion in reverse but erosion interrupted, geology frozen mid-sneeze. The walls of this pocket-cavern were pressing inward moments ago, or will be, or are pressing inward now in a way that doesn't quite translate to motion.

---

Heat presses down. It rises from below. It radiates from the walls themselves, which hold temperature the way a held breath holds sound. The air tastes mineral—iron and copper and something else, something you might call sweet if sweet had never meant sugar, if sweetness had only ever been encountered in the context of heated metal and organic compounds breaking down.

There should be sweat. There isn't. Moisture exists here only as concept, as memory, as something the body expects and the environment refuses to provide. The dryness has weight to it. It presses in. In this hidden chamber, perhaps thirty meters across at its widest point, water is not absent by accident. It is absent by design, by fundamental exclusion. H₂O molecules simply do not hold together here. They fall apart into hydrogen and oxygen, and those gases drift upward through fissures that lead nowhere, cycling into systems unknown.

---

But life grows.

That's the wrong word. *Vegetation* is what the parameters call it, but these are not plants in any sense that biology would recognize. They are perhaps closer to crystals that learned ambition. They are maybe more like thoughts that a machine had about forests.

The garden—if garden is the word—covers the floor of the pocket in scattered clumps. Spare distribution. Each colony separated from its neighbors by meters of that uncertain, half-formed stone. Between the growths, emptiness. Presence balanced against absence with mathematical intention.

Each cluster operates on its own principles. Some rise in spirals, fibonacci-strict, translucent membranes stretched over frameworks of something like cellulose but purple-black, absorbing light across spectrums that shouldn't exist. Some grow flat, pressed against the warming stone, radial patterns like lichen that decided to become architecture. Some reach upward in tangles that move when you don't look directly at them—not quickly, not dramatically, but the way a clock hand moves, the way shadows lengthen, slow enough that change can only be perceived through duration.

They are alive. They are not animal. They photosynthesize, but light is only part of their diet. They also eat heat. They eat pressure. They eat the low-frequency hum that vibrates through the stone from somewhere below, from somewhere that might be the planet's mantle or might be a basement level of reality that shouldn't be accessible from here.

---

The sound is that hum, and almost nothing else. A subsonic vibration more felt than heard, a pressure in the chest rather than the ears. It comes from the engines.

Not here. The engines are not here. Here is untouched, unbuilt, no constructed structures, no made artifacts. Here is a pocket of raw geology caught in the process of becoming, held in suspension, preserved like a specimen in amber.

But the engines are *underneath*.

Or adjacent. Or parallel, operating in a dimension that touches this one at specific coordinates. The machinery exists in relation to this place without being *in* this place. You can feel it. The whole pocket shudders with industrial rhythm, with the pulse of scaled production, with the relentless cycle of mechanized process. Whatever is being produced, whatever is being electrified and mechanized and assembled, it is happening near enough to vibrate the stone but far enough to remain invisible.

This pocket is embedded within something larger. The mountain, yes, but also an industrial complex. A factory that became a geology. A production line that learned to grow mountains around its more sensitive processes. This garden-chamber is a clean room, perhaps. A containment vessel. A void-space where the machinery's output can be tested against conditions of absolute dryness and pre-formational stone.

---

The light is normal. Jarringly so.

Given everything else—the reality breakdown, the engines that aren't there, the water that won't stay water, the plants that feed on heat and vibration and the unformed stone itself—given all of this, the light should be wrong. Colored wrong. Moving wrong. Casting shadows in directions that don't make sense.

But the light falls from above (from fissures? from luminescent growth on the ceiling? from somewhere) and it illuminates the garden in perfectly ordinary wavelengths. You can see by it. You can judge distance by it. It behaves.

This is perhaps the strangest thing. The normal light. It suggests that whatever is happening to reality here, whatever breakdown is occurring at the edges and depths of this space, it hasn't yet reached the surface level. The visible spectrum remains intact. The eye can still make sense of what it sees.

For now.

---

Time moves strangely. Not slowly—slow implies a consistent rate of change, just reduced. Here, time stutters. It lurches. The garden grows in discrete moments that cannot be connected into smooth progression. You watch a spiral-frond extend, and it is one length, and then you blink, and it is longer, and there was no *becoming* longer, no growth that could be observed, only the before-state and the after-state with nothing between.

The dynamic is slow in aggregate. Over hours or days or what feel like hours or days, the garden shifts, the stone settles, the heat increases by imperceptible degrees. But close up, in the moment, change happens all at once or not at all. Patient processes, yes. Minimal flux on the human scale. But that minimal flux arrives in jumps, in edits, in cuts from one frame to the next with no transition.

Reality is breaking down because reality assumes continuity. It assumes that time moves smoothly, that effect follows cause in an unbroken chain, that matter exists in definite states at definite locations. This pocket is losing that. It is still holding together—the light works, the stone has mass, the plants grow—but the connective tissue between moments is dissolving.

---

No animals live here. No animals could live here.

The absence is active. The pocket rejects animate life, rejects consciousness perhaps, rejects anything that requires water to function or brains to think or blood to carry oxygen from one part of a body to another. Fauna is not merely absent but excluded, pushed out by the dryness and the heat and the stuttering time. Even microbes, even the smallest bacteria, cannot survive in this place. The garden is utterly alone. No pollinators visit its spiraling fronds. No grazers crop its radial mats. No decomposers break down its fallen growth.

What falls stays fallen. The floor between the living clusters is scattered with remnants, with purple-black membranes cracked and curling, with frameworks of not-quite-cellulose bleached pale by the heat. The garden eats its own dead eventually, absorbing what it once was, but slowly. So slowly. The litter persists.

---

Standing here—if standing is the word, if here is a location that can be stood in—you would feel the heat first, pressing up through the soles of your feet. Then the dryness, pulling moisture from your skin before you could notice its absence. Then the vibration, that subsonic engine-hum, rattling your bones.

Your eyes would adjust to the light, and you would see the garden spread across the uncertain stone, clusters of growth at intervals, geometry that almost made sense. You would notice the stillness between the growths. The balance of presence and absence. The way each cluster seemed to operate according to its own private logic, its own version of what plants are supposed to do.

You would feel time stutter. You would not be able to say how long you had been standing there.

And if you stayed long enough—hours, days, however long it takes for reality to notice you and reject you—you would feel your own continuity beginning to fray. You would experience one moment and then another without transition. You would become uncertain whether you had just arrived or had always been here or were just leaving. You would begin to suspect that you, like the water, like the stone, like the light and the time, were in the process of becoming something else, something that could exist in this place where reality has loosened its grip and engines hum in dimensions you cannot touch.

This is a garden. It is growing. It is being grown. It is inside a mountain that might be inside a factory that might be inside a breakdown of the fabric of what-is.
