# The Bellows Room

It began as a pocket. That is the only honest word for it — a pocket in the stone, caught between the rough hands of two converging strata, high in a ridge that has not yet decided whether it wants to be a mountain. The rock here is new. Not new in the way a geologist means, though that too — but new in a way you can almost feel in the texture of it. Soft. Unset. The minerals haven't made up their minds. Walls still carry the grain of whatever process pressed them into being, striated and chalky, like clay left to dry in a mold but not yet fired. There are no shelves, no ledges worn smooth by time. No history of dripping. The surfaces are raw, unedited. You can press your thumb into the wall in some places and leave a print.

The pocket is small. A room, maybe, if you are generous with the word. Four paces long. Three wide. The ceiling is uneven and low enough that a tall person would duck, though the floor drops sharply at the eastern edge into a fissure that might go down forever — you cannot see the bottom, and the one time a stone was kicked over the edge, the sound of its landing never arrived.

---

The water comes from everywhere.

Not a stream. Not a spring. The rock itself seems to be sweating. Moisture beads along every surface, coalesces into trickles that run in branching threads down the walls, converge in the fissure's throat. But that is the slow water — the patient water — and it is not what you notice first.

What you notice first is the sound.

Something far below — far below — is breathing. Or performing an excellent imitation. A vast intake, a pause that presses against your eardrums like a slow ascent in an airplane, and then a long, shuddering exhale that sends a column of water up through the fissure like a geyser without the heat. The water hits the ceiling. The ceiling is close. It has nowhere to go but outward, and so the room fills — suddenly, violently — with a wall of water that goes from ankle-deep to chest-deep in a single beat, cold enough that it locks your muscles, clear enough that you can see the walls distort through it like glass.

Then the water recedes. Drains back down the fissure with a roar that sounds, briefly, like applause. The walls drip. The floor steams — no, not steams, that is just the cold meeting the cold. The room is empty again. Quiet, except for the breathing far below, which has not stopped.

The cycle is irregular. Sometimes the pause between exhales is long enough that you begin to think it is finished. Sometimes the water comes twice in quick succession, the second wave catching the drainage of the first, and the room fills past the ceiling and spills out through a crack in the northern wall that nobody has mapped because nobody has been able to stay in the room long enough to map it. The water is not stagnant. It moves — tugging, swirling, carrying tiny fragments of unset stone that chip and dissolve as they tumble. The room is being carved in real time. Each wave rewrites the geometry slightly. A corner that was sharp yesterday is rounded today. A shelf that held your pack last week is gone.

This is what the low erosion score means: not that erosion is absent, but that the process is so new it has not yet produced a landscape. It is producing one. Right now. You are standing inside the first draft.

---

The light is ordinary, which is strange given everything else. It enters through the crack in the northern wall — daylight, diffuse, the pale gray-blue of high altitude overcast. Enough to see by. Enough that the water, when it comes, catches the light and throws rippling caustics across the ceiling in slow, rotating arcs. The light does not belong here. It feels borrowed from a more reasonable place, a place where rooms are dry and rock is old and things make sense.

There is a secondary light source, faint, biological or mineral — impossible to say which. Along the deepest visible portion of the fissure, where the rock transitions from chalky gray to something darker and denser, there is a low amber luminescence. It pulses. Not in time with the breathing. Not out of time with it either. The relationship is there butunreadable, like two conversations in adjacent rooms.

---

Vegetation is almost absent, but not entirely. There is one thing.

In the southwestern corner, where the wall meets the floor in a seam that stays perpetually damp — not flooded, just wet, always wet — something has taken root. It is not a plant in any familiar sense. It has no leaves. Its structure is pale, almost translucent, like the spine of a fish or the cleaned rib of a ghost. It branches in a way that seems to deliberate to be random, each fork calculating something — angle of incidence, perhaps, or the interval between waves. It does not grow upward. It grows inward, pressing itself against the rock, and where it touches, the stone changes color slightly, going from chalky to something warmer, almost organic.

It has survived every wave. The water flows through it rather than against it. When submerged, its branches vibrate at a frequency you can feel in your teeth, and for a few seconds during each flood, the amber light in the fissure brightens.

Nobody has named it. There has not been time.

---

There are marks. Human marks, or near enough. In the wall above the flood line — which has risen incrementally over the past weeks as the room reshapes itself — someone has driven four pegs of a dark, dense wood into the softer stone. The pegs are rough-cut, hand-shaped, each one slightly different in thickness and length, as though made by someone working with tools that were themselves newly made. From two of the pegs hangs a net, or what was once a net — the fiber is vegetable, coarse, beginning to rot in the perpetual damp. The net's mesh is irregular, hand-knotted, and there are shells caught in it. Small shells, translucent, spiral-formed, from organisms that live in the fissure water and are ejected during each exhale. The shells are warm to the touch. Warmer than the water, warmer than the rock. As though they are still, in some sense, alive.

The other two pegs hold nothing. But below them, gouged into the stone with what might have been a nail or a sharp rock, there are tally marks. They do not count the waves. They count the pauses — the long silences between exhales when the breathing below seems to hold itself in, and the room goes still enough that you can hear your own blood, and the amber light dims to almost nothing, and for a moment the whole pocket feels like it is listening.

The tallies stop at thirty-seven.

---

The fauna are rare, but they exist. In the water — only in the water, only when the room is flooded — there are shapes. Not fish. Not anything with a face. They are dark, thumb-sized, and they move in ways that suggest intention rather than current. They seem to be feeding on the unset stone, grazing on the minerals while the rock is temporarily softened by the water. When the wave recedes, most of them go with it, tumbling back down into the fissure. But sometimes one remains, stranded on the floor, and it pulses — once, twice — and then goes still and begins to dissolve, its body leaving a faint dark stain on the pale rock that becomes, over days, a permanent vein of color in the stone.

A new vein. A new feature. The room, again, being written.

---

And the sound. The sound is the thing that stays with you.

It is not just the breathing. The breathing is the bass note, the foundation, but above it and around it and through it the room produces sounds that seem to come from the interaction of water and stone and air and that strange pale organism in the corner. When the wave comes, the impact of water on the low ceiling produces a sound like a drum being struck underwater — muffled, enormous, felt in the sternum more than heard in the ear. The drainage produces a roar that echoes in the fissure and comes back transformed, layered, harmonized with itself until it sounds like a choir of gravel. And the pale organism, vibrating during each flood, adds a tone — one sustained note, always the same note, a low A or something close to it, that hangs in the air after the water has gone and the room is dripping and empty and the breathing below has paused.

During the pauses, there is a sound so quiet it might be imagined: a ticking, like cooling stone, like settling, like the room deciding what shape to take next.

---

The world outside this pocket is barely relevant. There is a ridge. There is cloud. There is cold air and the smell of wet minerals and the particular loneliness of high places that have not yet been named. But all of that is preamble. The room is the thing.

It is a room being made by a process that has no name, occupied briefly by water and stone and one stubborn un-plant and the ghosts of whoever drove those pegs and made those tallies and then left, or were taken, or became something else. It is a room that will not exist in its current form by the time you finish reading this. The corners are shifting. The ceiling is lowering. The fissure is widening. The pale organism is pressing deeper into the wall.

The next wave is coming. You can hear it — far below, the intake has begun. The air pressure in the room shifts. The amber light brightens. The tallies on the wall seem, for a moment, to glow.

Thirty-eight.
