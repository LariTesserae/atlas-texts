The first thing is the sound. It arrives before understanding—a physical pressure that reshapes how you breathe. You have to consciously decide to inhale because the noise convinces some primitive part of the brain that the air itself has solidified. This is the sound of a billion small bodies in motion, of friction and wings and the clicking of segmented limbs against stone. It is a biological roar, the accumulated voice of an ecosystem in full voice, and it renders conversation meaningless. You could scream directly into someone's ear and they would feel only the warmth of your breath.

You descend into it. The passage down is gradual—a long ramp carved from the same material as everything here, worn so smooth by uncountable centuries of passing feet and dragging abdomens that it has developed a low polish. The angle takes you maybe thirty meters below the surface, though "surface" is a relative term in a place where the boundary between above and below has been obscured by growth. The ramp opens onto what was once, in some distant age, a street.

Now it is a canyon. Not carved by water—the parameters forbid that—but by growth and time. Buildings rise on either side, their upper stories long since merged into a continuous canopy. You walk through a tunnel of architecture and vegetation, and the two have become indistinguishable. What were once walls are now vertical gardens, thick with growth that has no right to exist without water, and yet exists with aggressive abundance. The air is cool, bordering on cold. Moisture beads on surfaces anyway—not from rain or groundwater, but from the slow condensation of biological processes. Everything here breathes. The condensation is the place's exhalation.

The vegetation is the puzzle. In a world this dry, this ancient, this removed from aqueous systems, you expect sparseness, desiccation, the crisp efficiency of desert plants. Instead, you get lushness. The growth is so thick it obscures the buildings beneath—layer upon layer of leaf and stem and tendriled mass, all of it thriving in complete absence of open water. Close examination reveals the secret: these plants do not drink. They have evolved past it. Their cells operate on different chemistry, something viscous and slow-moving that circulates through their tissues like sap but isn't. It pools golden in translucent veins, visible when backlit. The whole canopy glows amber where what little light there is filters through. This is the light of the place—biological, filtered, ambered. The sun above provides only distant suggestions. The real illumination comes from the plants themselves, from their slow golden circulation.

The buildings underneath—because there are buildings, because this was once and in some sense still is a city—are revealed only in fragments. A doorway here, its frame still intact though the door has long since rotted or been eaten. A window aperture,glassless, now serving as an entrance for something that nests in the darkness beyond. The architecture suggests a people who valued smooth curves over sharp angles, who built to accommodate bodies unlike our own. The doorways are tall and narrow. The windows are placed low. Whatever lived here before the plants came moved differently, saw differently, wanted different things from shelter.

And something still does. The fauna are everywhere. They are what make the sound—the billions of them, crawling and flying and skittering through the canopy above and the tunnels below and the spaces in between. Most are small. Some are not. You see them in flashes: things like insects but larger, things like mammals but with too many joints, things that move too fast to categorize. They do not fear you because they have no reason to. You are not food. You are not threat. You are simply present—a walking, breathing disruption in the flow of their normal activity. They navigate around you like water around a stone.

The abundance suggests an ecosystem at its climax, a system that has solved every problem and now simply operates. This is the end-state of biology in a sealed environment: maximum density, maximum interconnection, maximum use of every available niche. Nothing is wasted. When something dies here—and things die constantly, because that is how abundance works—it is processed within hours. The floor you walk on is not soil or stone but compost, layer upon layer of former life broken down and reconstituted into new forms. It gives slightly beneath your feet. It is warm despite the cool air.

The city extends beyond your sight. The scale is regional—you are in a valley of buildings, a whole basin of urbanism that was constructed ages ago and has been growing ever since. The canopy overhead is not a single layer but a stratified complex of growth, each level home to different species. The upper canopy, where the last of the direct light reaches, is dominated by the light-gatherers—plants that have evolved broad, translucent leaves to capture every possible photon. Below them, in the amber glow, live the intermediates—things that feed on the light-gatherers' waste, that thrive in the semi-lit world of filtered glow. And at the bottom, where you walk, are the decomposers and the detritivores and the things that prey upon them.

The sound changes as you move. It is never silent—the parameters forbid that—but it shifts in pitch and texture. Here, a section of canopy dominated by winged insects produces a high, drilling whine that you feel in your teeth. There, a colony of something larger creates a lower vibration, something more like a purr, that resonates in your chest. Elsewhere, the noise becomes rhythmic, almost mechanical, as though vast engines still operate beneath the biological veneer. They do not. The rhythm is the sound of coordinated movement—thousands of bodies moving in patterns that have been repeated so many times they have worn grooves into the stone.

Time moves strangely here. Not slowly, exactly—the parameters suggest slowness, but that is misleading. It is more that time has become irrelevant. This place has existed in something close to its current form for so long that change operates on scales beyond human perception. The plants grow, but slowly—their golden circulation moves like glaciers. The buildings decay, but imperceptibly—each century sees perhaps a millimeter of new growth over old stone. The animals live and die in their quick flickering way, but their populations remain constant, their patterns unchanging. This is a system that has found its equilibrium and will maintain it until something fundamental shifts. Nothing will. The place is too old, too settled, too perfectly balanced.

You pass what might have been a plaza, now a bowl of vegetation so dense you could walk across the surface without touching ground. In its center, the remains of something—a statue, a fountain, a monument of some kind—rise half-consumed by growth. You cannot tell what it once depicted. The erosion has been thorough and supplemented by biological action: roots and vines have worked into every crack, every joint, every weakness, and expanded there, splitting stone with patient hydraulic pressure. Whatever meaning this monument once held has been erased. It is now simply texture—a surface for growth, a shape to be colonized.

The city has no name. The people who built it are gone beyond memory. The plants have no names either, though they might have had names once, might have been catalogued and studied and understood by someone long dead. The animals have names only for each other, in whatever systems of signification they use—chemical signals, vibrational codes, the brush of antenna against antenna. You walk through the accumulated result of countless generations of growth and decay, and you are the first human to do so in perhaps centuries. You are not the first biological entity. You are not special. You are simply another shape moving through a place that has seen shapes before.

The light dims further as you go deeper. The canopy thickens overhead. The amber glow becomes the primary illumination, supplemented by patches of bioluminescence—some insect or fungus or plant that has evolved to produce its own light, perhaps for mating, perhaps for navigation, perhaps simply because it could. The sound remains constant, deafening, total. Your thoughts compress to accommodate it. You find you can only hold one idea at a time, and that idea is usually: keep moving.

But there is something peaceful here. Despite the noise, despite the crowding, despite the strangeness. The city beneath the plants has found its final form. It is no longer a city in any human sense—it is an organism, or a superorganism, a single vast entity made of buildings and plants and animals and the golden circulation that binds them all together. It breathes. It metabolizes. It persists. The erosion parameter suggests that this is the oldest stable state, the end of a process that began when the first builders laid their first stones and will continue until something fundamental changes the conditions.

Nothing will. The place is complete.

You turn a corner—you are still following what might once have been streets, though the concept of street has become approximate—and the canyon opens into a wider space. A forum, perhaps, or a market, or a gathering place of some kind. The canopy rises here, vaulted, supported by the ruins of surrounding buildings. The sound changes, becoming more resonant, bouncing off surfaces that have been polished smooth by centuries of airborne particles. The acoustics are complex. A sound made here would echo for an impossibly long time, dying away over seconds, but no sounds are made except the ongoing roar of biology.

In the center of this space, something grows that is different. A plant, but larger than the others—a tree-equivalent, perhaps, though it does not resemble any tree you know. Its trunk rises in twisted strands, like cable, and its canopy spreads wider than any natural growth should. This is the heart of the system, you realize. This is where the golden circulation is thickest, where the light is brightest, where the sound concentrates into something almost like a voice. The animals avoid this space, leaving a clear radius around the central growth. Not out of fear. Out of something like respect, or perhaps simple recognition.

This is the city, in the end. Not the buildings, not the streets, not the remnants of architecture. This growth, this organism, this accumulation of biological action over time. Everything else is substrate. Everything else serves to support this central mass, this entity that has grown so old and so large and so integrated that it has become the place itself.

You stand at the edge of the clearing. The sound washes over you. The amber light pulses. The cool air moves, finally, circulating in patterns set by the central growth's respiration. You have found the center. You have found the thing that the place has become.

And it does not care that you are there.
