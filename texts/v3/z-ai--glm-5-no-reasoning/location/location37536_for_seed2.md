The water is everywhere, but it is not ocean. It moves through channels designed for it, millions of kilometers of precision-bored passage threading through a structure so large that "building" fails as a word. This is a made ocean, a manufactured sea, and you are deep inside it.

The light comes from everywhere and nowhere. Bioluminescence? No—closer inspection reveals the glow emanates from the walls themselves, or rather from countless kilometres of fibre-optic cabling embedded in the superstructure, carrying illumination from reactor cores somewhere distant. The light is even, clinical. It turns the water to something like glass, something like air. Visibility extends for what might be kilometers.

The structure around you is young. Not young in years—who knows how many centuries this place has floated in the void—but young in formation. The construction is ongoing. You can hear it: the bone-rattling shriek of industrial cutting tools far distant, the rhythmic clang of assembly, the profound bass thrum of pumps moving water through arteries larger than city blocks. The sounds are not muffled by the liquid. They carry, sharp and immediate, conducted by the dense medium.

Vegetation is almost absent, but not quite. Here and there, pale tendrils reach from intake grates—engineered photosynthesizers, perhaps, or invasive growth that found purchase in the nutrient-rich flow. They are sparse enough to seem lonely. Each cluster is an event.

The fauna, by contrast, are abundant. They come in schools and shoals and swarms, metal and flesh intermingled. Some are clearly machines—propeller-driven survey units, snake-bodied repair drones that wind through the corridors of water. Others appear organic: pale fish with too many eyes, crustaceans whose shells have adapted to match the grating and plating of their environment. Most unsettling are those that fall between categories. A swimmer approaches you with a mouth full of spinning blades, checks you with ocular sensors, then moves on. It ignored you. It has a job to do.

The water is cool. Not cold enough to kill, but cold enough to remind you that this is not an environment built for human comfort. The temperature is calibrated for efficiency, for the optimal function of systems far older and more important than you. You are a guest. Or an intruder. Or a contaminant. The structure hasn't decided yet.

Depth is difficult to judge. There is no surface. The station, the megastructure, the artificial world—whatever you call it—exists in layers that descend beyond sonar's ability to map. You are below any nominal "surface level," but there are depths beneath you that would crush a human body in seconds. The pressure is held at bay by active systems, by force fields and material science that require constant energy input. When the power fluctuates—and it does, you hear the generators cycle, hear the systems reroute—you feel the water press in, just for a moment, a reminder of the kilotons waiting to collapse.

The emptiness is profound. For all the activity, all the motion and sound and life, the volume of space is so vast that you could drift for hours without encountering another presence. The corridors of water are cathedral-sized, the assembly chambers big enough to hold weather. And they do—you watch a current form, watched it spin, watched moisture condense into a brief fog that drifted like a cloud through the water before dissipating. A storm, born and died in minutes, small enough to miss if you weren't paying attention.

Everything here was built. Every surface is worked, manufactured, placed. There is no natural substrate—no rock, no pre-existing geology. The structure grew from a seed, perhaps, or was printed in place by machines that built machines that built machines. It is still growing. You can see the fresh sections, the sharp-edged new construction where the water has not yet softened the edges, where the young erosion of liquid flow has not had time to smooth the artificial stone. The boundary between finished and unfinished is marked by scaffolding, by construction drones, by the thunder of creation.

The noise never stops. Even in the quietest moments, there is the pump-hum, the current-surge, the distant percussion of assembly. And beneath it all, a subsonic vibration that you feel more than hear. The heartbeat of the reactor core. The breath of the machine-world.

It is not weird. That is perhaps the strangest thing. It is comprehensible. Industrial, biological, mechanical—you understand the principles even if you have never seen them at this scale. The rules are consistent. The physics is the familiar physics. This is not alien. This is something humanity might have built, given enough time and enough ambition and enough water.

You are inside something that has the scale of a planet but the intentionality of a factory.
