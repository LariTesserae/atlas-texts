# The Hollow of Seven Thousand Mouths

It is not large. You could cross it in two hundred paces, if you could walk a straight line through it, which you cannot.

---

The place sits in a fold of basalt high on the flanks of a ridge that has not yet decided whether it is a mountain. The rock is young — sharp-cornered, dark, ferrous in smell where water has touched it and left orange weeps along vertical fractures. The stone breaks in clean planes. Nothing here has been rounded yet by patient time. The edges of things cut.

Someone built here. Not on the hollow but *into* it — low walls of stacked basalt chunks, unmortared, fitted with the care of hands that understood weight but not geometry. There are perhaps twelve structures, if you can call them that. Some are walls taller than a person, freestanding, enclosing nothing, roofless, purpose obsure. One is a corridor that turns twice and ends at raw stone. Another is a kind of pen, oval, waist-high, open at both ends — but the openings are too narrow for anything the builders might have kept. Lichen has colonized the north-facing surfaces of every wall, a pale green suede, but the south faces are bare, still dark, still new-looking.

The path down into the hollow is worn smooth in places — foot-traffic, old — but the steps cut into the steepest section are crisp-edged, unworn. The place was used, then entered, then left. Or the other way around.

---

There is a seep. Not a spring, not a stream. Water emerges from a horizontal crack in the western wall of the hollow at the rate of a slow exhale — not flowing but *appearing*, a glossy line that swells into drops that gather weight and fall. It has been doing this long enough to carve a shallow runnel down the rock face, maybe a hand's-width deep, dark-stained. The water collects in a basin the builders made — three flat stones leaned together like a cupped palm, mossed over, overflowing into a channel that crosses the floor of the hollow and vanishes into a pile of scree. The air near the seep smells of wet iron and green things. The air ten paces away smells of nothing in particular, or of stone.

Rain comes unpredictably. Not downpours — brief, cold curtains that pass through in minutes, barely enough to darken the rock. After rain, the hollow changes character. Every surface holds a film of water. The walls go black. The basalt reflects a sky it normally refuses to acknowledge. During rain the sound is a whispered static, the impact of drops on leaf and stone so soft it seems generated by the air itself rather than falling through it.

Most of the time it is quiet. Not silent — there is a sound, but you have to listen for it. A low, wet pulse from somewhere beneath the scree. And the insects. The insects are not quiet, but their sound is so constant and so layered that it becomes a texture rather than a noise, indistinguishable from the quality of the air itself.

---

The vegetation is the first thing that contradicts expectations. The elevation is high, the temperature is cool, the water is scarce — and yet the hollow is *full* of green. Not forest. Not meadow. Something between. The floor is carpeted in a moss that is not quite moss — taller, almost grasslike, each strand jointed and faintly translucent, pale green tending toward yellow at the tips. It moves without wind. Individual strands shift, reorient, as though seeking something. When you step on it, it does not crush; it bends aside and returns to position behind you.

On the walls, a different plant dominates — something vine-like, dark-leafed, glossy, with stems no thicker than twine but woody and strong. It forms dense mats over the basalt, sending rootlets into every crack. Where it meets the seep, it fruits: small, hard, blue-black spheres clustered along the stem, each no larger than a peppercorn. The fruit smells, when crushed, of anise and cold soil.

Above, in the cracks where walls meet the natural rock, a fern grows — but only where the seep-water reaches. Leathery fronds, narrow, dark. They uncurl slowly, visibly, over the course of hours. You can watch them move.

There are no trees. The scale of the place would not permit them, and the wind — when it comes — would not either. The vegetation is low, dense, layered, and everywhere there is surface to hold.

---

But the place is not empty. Far from empty. The problem is that you might not immediately understand what fills it.

The walls have mouths. That is the simplest way to say it, and also not simple at all.

In the basalt — natural and built alike — there are openings. Horizontal, lip-like, ranging from the width of a finger to the width of a forearm. They are not carved. They appear to be natural vesicular cavities in the stone that have been *widened*, the edges smoothed by use. From these openings, life emerges.

The first thing you notice is the clicking. A dry, rhythmic sound, like a fingernail on ceramic, coming from everywhere and nowhere. Then you see movement — at the mouth of one of the larger openings, a head emerges. Pale. Segmented. Not insect, not reptile, something that shares properties of both and neither. It is the color of the stone, perhaps because it is the color of the stone. Eyeless. Antennae — or tendrils, or lips — feel the air. The body follows: a handspan long, low, many-legged, moving with a fluidity that suggests no skeleton, or a skeleton that is entirely cartilage. Its back is damp. It glistens.

There are thousands.

Every wall is a colony. Every opening houses dozens. They emerge at intervals — singly, in pairs, in processions of six or eight — to move across the moss, to drink at the seep-basin, to climb the vines and eat the blue-black fruit. They are the primary herbivore of the hollow. Their clicking is their breath, or their speech, or their digestion. It never stops.

And they are not alone.

---

The air above the hollow moves with things you do not see at first. Dim light — the hollow is in shadow most of the day, sun reaching it only briefly in the afternoon, and even then filtered through cloud or the ridge itself — makes the upper air obscure. But when something passes between you and the pale sky, you catch it. Winged. Silent. Larger than your hand. They hunt the clickers. You see one strike — a fast, low glide, a sweep along a wall's surface, and then it is gone upward with something pale and struggling in its grip. No sound. No cry. The clickers in the immediate area go still for three seconds, then resume. The world continues.

There are other things. In the scree at the eastern edge, something moves stones — you hear the scrape, the settling, but see nothing. In the basin, beneath the surface of the seep-water, something small and dark swims, or crawls, its motion leaving v-ripples that vanish before they reach the edge. On the moss, tracks appear — not from the clickers, whose paths are obvious, meandering, tail-dragged — but something heavier, something with fewer feet, moving deliberately from wall to wall to wall, visiting the mouths, never entering.

The ecology is complete. The hollow is a world. It does not need you. It did not need the builders. The builders may have understood this, or may not have. The walls they built have been colonized as thoroughly as the natural rock — the clickers do not distinguish. The structures have become terrain. The corridor that turns twice and dead-ends houses a sub-colony of smaller, paler clickers that never emerge in daylight. The pen with the narrow openings may have been built *for* them — a habitat, a temple, a plea — but there is no way to know. The walls that enclose nothing are climbed by the vines, occupied by the mouths, patrolled by the silent flyers. They have been absorbed.

---

The dimness is important. It is not merely low light. It is a *quality* of light — diffuse, grey-blue, without clear source, as though the hollow exists under a permanent overcast even when the sky above is clear. The ridge blocks direct sun. What reaches the floor is reflected, scattered, attenuated. Colors are muted. The green of the moss goes grey. The blue-black of the fruit goes black. The pale bodies of the clickers go white. Shadows do not have edges. Everything is equally lit and equally dim, which means depth is difficult to judge — the far wall of the hollow seems sometimes close, sometimes distant, depending on nothing you can identify.

The air is cool. Not cold — but the kind of cool that settles into cloth and skin and stays. The kind that makes the damp feel damper. The seep contributes. The elevation contributes. The shade contributes. The breath of a thousand clickers in their stone mouths may contribute. You are aware, standing here, of your own warmth — how unusual it is, how conspicuous. The hollow is not hostile to warmth. It simply does not have any of its own.

---

The silence is the strangest thing. For a place so full of life — and it is *full*, overwhelmingly full, every surface occupied, every niche exploited, the air busy with silent flight, the walls seething with pale bodies — it is remarkably quiet. The clickers click, but the sound is soft, constant, more like the hum of an appliance than the activity of animals. The flyers make no sound at all. The seep makes almost no sound — the drops fall too short a distance to ring against the basin. The moss absorbs footfall. The stone absorbs voice. When you speak, the words do not echo. They fall into the hollow and are received by it and do not return.

The overall impression is of a place that is *happening* — actively, continuously, in every corner — but that does not need to announce itself. The cycles here are tight. The clickers emerge, feed, return. The flyers hunt, carry, return to wherever they roost — somewhere above, in the cracks of the ridge. The seep pulses. The moss grows. The ferns uncurl. The fruit ripens and is eaten and ripens again. The stone weeps orange. The rain comes and goes. All of this proceeds without urgency, without noise, without any acknowledgment that it is, in aggregate, a system — that each process feeds another, that the hollow is a closed loop, a small and complete ecology that has emerged in the brief time since the rock was exposed, since the basalt cooled and cracked and opened its mouths, since someone came and built walls that the mouths could inhabit.

---

The weirdness is not in any single element. It is in the accumulation.

The clickers should not be here — not at this elevation, not in this cold, not in this density. The vegetation should not be this lush for the available water. The light should not be this dim for the altitude. The builders should not have built what they built. The mouths in the stone should not be that regular, that horizontal, that lip-like. The fruit should not smell of anise. The moss should not move. The flyers should not be silent. The thing in the scree should not be moving rocks. The basin-creature should not be swimming in water that has no visible source sufficient to sustain it.

None of these are impossible. Each has an explanation, or could have one, if you stayed long enough to find it. But together, in this small dim hollow on the flank of a young ridge, they compose something that feels less like a habitat and more like a *statement* — a proposition made in the language of stone and moisture and segmented pale bodies and dark vine-fruit and silent wings. The proposition is not addressed to you. You happen to be present for it. The hollow was here before you arrived and will be here after you leave, clicking quietly to itself, feeding itself, opening and closing its mouths in the dark.
