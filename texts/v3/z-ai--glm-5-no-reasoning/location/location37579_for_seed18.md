The chamber is small enough that you could walk its perimeter in three minutes, yet it feels like standing inside the throat of something immense. This is a cathedral of industry, a made-space no larger than a village church, hanging in isolation at an altitude where the air should be too thin for anything to breathe—yet breath comes easy here, warm and wet.

Everything is constructed. There is no stone that was not cut, no surface that was not milled. The walls are riveted iron plates, curved upward into a vaulted ceiling lost behind a permanent fog of condensation. The floor is a grating—thousands of iron slats crossing each other in a lattice, and beneath the grating, nothing. Or rather: the void. You are suspended. The chamber hangs from something above, or is propelled by something below, or simply exists in defiance, and the grating beneath your feet is the only separation between your boots and a drop that has no known bottom.

Through that lattice-floor, dampness rises. Not the comfortable damp of a cellar, but the pressurized exhalation of steam forced through plumbing the scale of subway tunnels. Somewhere far below this iron room, engines labor. The moisture they exhale condenses on every surface—on the rivets, on the pipes that vein the walls like exposed anatomy, on your hands when you press them against the metal. Water beads, gathers weight, and falls through the grating in a constant drip that joins the countless other drips below, a secondary rain that never reaches earth.

And yet life is everywhere. It should not be, but it is.

Ferns erupt from every joint and seam where condensation pools—not the delicate ferns of forest floors but industrial ferns, their fronds thick and dark as machinist's rags, their roots driven into the cracks between plates with the stubbornness of something that has decided to exist and refuses to reconsider. Moss carpets the upper curves where the steam settles thickest, not green but the deep teal of oxidized copper, and it moves—slowly, imperceptibly—reaching toward the sources of moisture in a patient vegetal migration that will take decades to complete.Bracket fungi jut from the pipe-corners in pale semicircles, layer on layer, each one a year of this place's slow calendar. Some are the size of dinner plates. They must have been growing since before you were born. The oldest have begun to fossilize at their bases, the fungal tissue mineralizing into something closer to the iron it feeds upon.

Vines are the engineers of this garden. They are the only thing that moves at a pace you can perceive without waiting hours. Pale, leafless tendrils with the tensile strength of wire rope, they climb the pipes and descend from the ceiling in curtains, always seeking—their tips rotating slowly, almost imperceptibly, tasting the air for the warm vent-points where steam escapes from pressure valves. When they find one, they coil around it, and over months they seal it, and the vine swells with captured moisture until it becomes a living valve, regulating the chamber's breathing through its own growth. The iron infrastructure and the vegetable infrastructure have become a single system. You cannot tell where one ends and the other begins.

The sound is everything. It fills you until you are not certain where your body ends and the noise begins. The engines below produce a fundamental thrum so low it is more vibration than sound—you feel it in your sternum, in your teeth, in the chambers of your heart. Above that, the tenor shriek of steam forcing through ancient pipes. Above that, the constant percussive drip-drip-drip of condensation finding its level. And threaded through it all, the tick of metal expanding and contracting, a slow thermal breath that makes the whole chamber creak and groan like a ship in heavy seas. There is no silence here. There has not been silence here in what must be centuries. The sound has its own weather.

The fauna have learned to live inside the sound rather than against it. Moths with wings the exact grey of the iron walls rest in the folds of the fern-fronds, so still they appear painted, and they communicate—the naturalist in you guesses—through the vibration of the surfaces they stand upon, tapping their abdomens against the metal in patterns too subtle for human perception. Beetles the size of your thumb navigate the grating with a mechanical precision, their carapac es clicking against the slats as they tend to colonies of some amber-colored fungus that grows exclusively on the warmest pipes. They are farmers. You watch one detach a frond of the amber growth and carry it to a new surface, and its intent is unmistakable.

Above, roosting in the vine-curtains where the steam is thickest, are dark shapes you take for bats until one adjusts its grip and you see the outline is wrong—too many limbs, or limbs in the wrong configuration, or perhaps you are seeing the shape wrong through the haze. They make no sound you can hear, but the vines around them vibrate differently when they move, and the ferns nearest them grow in spirals rather than radially, as if their presence reshapes the direction of life.

Light comes from nowhere obvious. It filters through the steam in a diffuse amber glow, sourceless and steady, too warm to be electric but too constant to be fire. It pools in the places where the vegetation is thinnest and abandons the places where the ferns have grown into walls, creating a patchwork of lit and shadowed iron. In the brightest spots, the condensation catches the light and the air fills with slow-moving gold. In the darkest, the moss absorbs everything and the iron goes black.

This place changes, but not for you. It changes on the scale of its own patients: the vine that will seal the final valve in another seven years, the fern that extends a single new frond per season, the rust that deepens its bloom by a shade each decade. The engines will run. The steam will rise. The water will fall through the grating into whatever lies below. The beetles will farm. The moths will drum their silent signals into the iron. The dark shapes will roost and adjust and reshape the growth around them. And all of it—the steam and the green and the thunderous sound—will continue in its rhythm, indifferent to whether anyone stands on the grating to observe it.

You are inside a machine that has become a world, or a world that has always been a machine. The distinction stopped mattering long ago.
