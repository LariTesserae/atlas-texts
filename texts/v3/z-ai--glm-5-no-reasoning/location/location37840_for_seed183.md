# The Weeping Spires

## I

The first thing is the sound. Before the eye settles, before the body orients to the steepness underfoot, there is the sound — a layered, harmonic drone that seems to come from the rock itself. It is not one note but many, cycling through slow intervals, as if the plateau were a struck bell still vibrating after some impact in deep time. On clear days, when the upper air thins and the light comes through in hard pale sheets, you can stand at the edge of the first terrace and feel the sound enter through your sternum before it reaches your ears.

They call it the Weeping. Not because anything here grieves — or if it does, the grief belongs to geology, which is another matter — but because the water moves down through the stone in continuous slow exudation. The whole of the Spire Plateau is wet. Not the wet of standing pools or running streams, but the wet of saturated mineral, of stone that has taken in water until it sweats. The surfaces gleam. The footing is treacherous. And everywhere, on every vertical face and overhang, the water finds its way outward and down in branching films that merge and separate and merge again, and the stone darkens beneath them in patterns that shift over hours.

---

## II

The plateau sits at altitude. Three thousand meters, perhaps more — the air is thin enough that the light carries a scoured quality, a whiteness stripped of softness. Everything casts a hard shadow. The sky is not blue but a pale glassy color that deepens to a saturated cobalt only when you look directly overhead, and even that feels fragile, as though the darkness behind it presses close.

The scale of the place is open, regional. You do not stand in a canyon or a valley; you stand on a vast tilted platform of exposed bedrock that tilts gently to the northeast, broken by the spires themselves — pillars and hoodoos of a dark, ferruginous schist that rise in clusters and rows across the plateau like the stumps of a dead forest. Some are forty meters tall. Some have collapsed into rubble heaps that the water has already begun to reclaim. Between them, the terrain drops in steps and ledges, and the wet stone catches the light in a way that makes the whole surface appear to glow from within.

---

## III

No plants grow here. None have grown here, if the rock record is to be read, for an immense span of time — perhaps ever. The surface is bare mineral. And yet the plateau is not barren.

The stone is occupied.

Across every wet surface — the tops of the spires, the sheltered walls, the flat expanses between the hoodoos — there spreads a film. It is thin, thinner than lichen, thinner than most things you might compare it to. It is colorless in shade and darkens to an iridescent amber where the light strikes it, which is most of the time. It is wet, always. It clings to the stone and draws from it, or feeds back into it, in a process that no one has adequately explained, because no one has been able to study it without the study itself becoming part of the phenomenon.

This is the first sign that the place is wrong.

---

## IV

The film moves.

Not in the way of growth, spreading outward from a center — though it does that too — but in the way of response. When light shifts, the film shifts. When water paths change, the film changes with them, darkening along new routes, lightening where the flow has dried. Over the course of a day, the entire surface of the plateau breathes through this film, which contracts and dilates across thousands of square meters in slow pulses that follow no obvious biological rhythm.

Some of the structures built on the plateau — and there are structures, small ones, crude ones — have film on their interior walls. The people who built them do not seem to mind, or perhaps they rely on it.

---

## V

The structures are scattered across the plateau in clusters of two or three, never more. They are built from the same dark schist, dry-stacked, with no mortar — the stone fits itself, or has been shaped to fit with simple tools. They are low, single-room shelters, some no more than waist-high walls with a lean-to roof of flat slabs. Several have collapsed. One has been partially rebuilt with stones of a slightly different color, lighter, suggesting they were brought from lower down the slope.

The people who use them — if people is what they are — are rarely present. You might find a shelter with a fire ring inside, cold, with ash that has hardened into a glassy mass the color of rust. You might find stacks of flat stones arranged in deliberate patterns on the ground outside — spirals, lines, grids — that serve no evident purpose. You might find a single stone tool: a chopper, a scraper, a flake with an edge still sharp. You will not find refuse, bones, or any sign of sustained habitation. The shelters feel visited rather than lived in. They are waypoints, not homes.

What is striking about them is how they sit within the landscape. They do not impose. They do not clear or level or dominate. They are fitted into gaps between spires, tucked under overhangs, set into natural hollows where the water already gathers. Their builders worked with the plateau's logic, not against it, and this — combined with the roughness of the work, the absence of metal or any but the simplest stone tools — places them in a time that is difficult to guess at. They could be ancient. They could be from last season.

---

## VI

The water is everywhere but it is not deep. Nowhere on the plateau will you find a pool larger than a tabletop, and even those are transient, forming after rain and evaporating within hours. The rain comes frequently, though — it is a wet world, despite the altitude and the thin air. The rain is fine and cold, almost a mist, and it falls almost daily, usually in the late afternoon, when the temperature drops and the condensation begins. The air, which is warm and dry in the morning, grows saturated by midday, and by three or four o'clock the fog rolls in from below — climbing the plateau's eastern escarpment like a living thing — and the rain follows shortly after.

After the rain, the Weeping intensifies. The sound deepens. More water emerges from the stone, as though the rain has awakened something within it, and the harmonic drone drops in pitch and gains overtones. Standing among the spires during this intensification is an overwhelming experience. The sound is not painful, but it is loud — loud enough that you feel it in your jaw, your chest, the backs of your eyes. It is a sound that fills the space entirely and leaves no room for thought. You stop thinking. You stand. You listen.

---

## VII

This is what happens to the light.

In the morning, when the air is dry and the sun is low, the plateau is bright — not warm, not golden, but bright in a way that has no mercy. The wet surfaces reflect everything. The spires throw long shadows westward across the stone, and inside those shadows the film is invisible, the rock pale and ordinary. Outside the shadows, the film glows.

At midday, the light becomes diffuse. The fog has not yet arrived, but the moisture in the air is sufficient to scatter the sun's rays, and the shadows soften and disappear. The plateau becomes a flat field of light. There are no dark places. This is when the film is most active — when the whole surface pulses at once, the amber darkening and lightening across kilometers of stone in a slow wave that moves from the higher ground to the lower, following the water's path.

After the rain, the light changes again. The fog sits heavy on the plateau, but the sun, high enough to burn through it, sends down shafts that hit the wet stone and scatter. The light becomes vertical. It comes from above, and the spires catch it and throw it sideways, and the whole landscape fills with a broken, prismatic light that has no single source. You cannot tell, in this state, whether the light is coming from the sky or from the ground.

---

## VIII

The plateau is wrong in ways that exceed its biology.

The air is thin at this altitude — cold, dry in the mornings — but your breathing does not quicken. You do not tire. You can walk for hours along the wet stone, climbing over rubble, navigating ledges, and your body does not register the effort. It is as if the altitude has been negotiated on your behalf, or as if the rules that govern exhaustion have been suspended here. People who have visited the plateau and returned — and there are some, though they are reluctant to speak of it — report this as the most disorienting aspect. Not the sound, not the film, not the light. The body's refusal to struggle in a place where it should struggle.

Time is also affected. The sun moves across the sky at the correct rate — this can be verified, if you have a watch, and if your watch continues to function, which is not guaranteed — but the days feel long in a way that has nothing to do with the hours. The light seems to hold. The afternoon stretches. The fog, once it arrives, seems to have always been there. You remember the morning, but dimly, as though it happened to someone else or in a previous version of the world.

---

## IX

The stone itself is not ordinary.

The schist of the spires is layered — fine, dark bands alternating with pale quartzite in strips so thin they appear as a single mass from a distance. Up close, you can see the banding, and if you look closer still, you can see that the bands are not straight. They warp. They curve and twist in ways that should not be possible for a metamorphic rock, forming patterns that resemble — nothing. Not writing, not images, not any symbol system you might recognize. But the patterns hold your attention. You look at them and you feel that they are saying something, and the feeling is so strong that it becomes a kind of sound, a silent counterpart to the Weeping, and you find yourself tracing the bands with your finger, following them into the stone, and the stone is wet, and the film is there, and the film is warm — warmer than the air, warmer than the rock — and you pull your hand back and you do not remember why you reached out.

The patterns are not always there. If you return to the same spot, the stone may be blank — ordinary banded schist with no distortion at all. The patterns appear and disappear, or they were never there, or they were always there and you only saw them because the light was right.

---

## X

The place is in balance. That much is clear. Whatever process sustains the film, the water, the sound, the light — it is not new. The plateau has been like this for a long time. The erosion is established. The spires are in their mature forms, neither growing nor collapsing at any visible rate. The water paths are worn into the stone, channels cut by millennia of slow exudation, and the film follows them exactly, as it must have done for as long as the water has flowed.

The structures fit into this balance as well. They do not disturb it. The people who built them — whoever they were, whenever they were — placed them with a precision that suggests they understood the plateau's patterns, or at least respected them. The film grows on their walls. The water seeps through their foundations. They do not rot. They do not age in the usual way. They simply persist, part of the surface now, part of the Weeping.

---

## XI

There is no animal life here. No insects, no birds, no movement of any living thing except the film and the water and the fog. The silence — which is not silence at all, which is a loud and constant harmonic that fills every niche and cavity — is the silence of a world that has dispensed with everything that is not stone and water and light.

If you stand on the plateau long enough — long enough for the fog to come in and the rain to fall and the sound to deepen and the light to break — you begin to feel that the place is not empty. It is full. It is saturated with something that has nothing to do with the life you know. The film on the stone is alive, or it is something that precedes life, or it is something that has replaced life, and the distinction does not matter because the plateau does not care about your categories. It has its own logic. It has been running for a long time. It is not waiting for you. It is not aware of you. But it is doing something — something immense and slow and continuous — and if you are quiet enough, and still enough, and if you let the sound enter you fully, you can feel it.

You can feel it the way you feel a heartbeat that is not your own.

---

## XII

I do not know what the plateau is for.

I do not think it is for anything. I think it simply is — a mineral surface at altitude, saturated with water, resonating with a sound that may be mechanical or may be something else, covered in a film that may be biological or may be something else, lit by a sun that moves across it in the ordinary way and yet produces light that is not ordinary at all. I think it has been here for a long time, and that it will be here for a long time after the structures have fallen and the patterns in the stone have faded and the people who visited have stopped visiting.

I think the Weeping is the sound of something working.

I do not know what it is building.

I do not think it knows either.
