A plain of metal extends in every direction, seam-welded and infinite.

It was manufactured—has been manufactured, is still being manufactured—by something that works at geological scale. The surface underfoot is an alloy, or perhaps a polymer, that holds heat the way stone holds cold. You feel it through your soles: the accumulated warmth of hours, days, seasons perhaps, radiating upward. The temperature differential between sun-baked surface and the cooler air creates a constant shimmer at the horizon.

Where the plain meets the sky—there is no other word for it—the metal surface has been stamped. Not for traction, not for aesthetics, but stamped because stamping is what the machines do. Shallow indentations cover square kilometers at a time before giving way to different textures: brushed patches, areas still sheathed in protective film that peels at the edges, sections that have been ground to unexpected smoothness. The patterns serve no purpose. Or rather, they serve the purpose of the making itself.

This is what total construction looks like when you stand inside it. No substrate beneath, no original ground that was paved over. Just layer upon layer of manufactured material extending downward, manufactured material extending outward, manufactured material extending into the heat haze where distance becomes indistinct. The scale defeats orientation. You walk for hours and the surface features repeat: the same weld-seam pattern, the same cluster of drainage channels, the same discoloration where a different batch of material was used.

Water moves through this place reluctantly. The drainage channels are designed for condensation, for the moisture that accumulates when hot metal meets cooling air. In the hours before dawn, dew forms in the stamped indentations, pools in the brush marks, collects in the low spots where the surface has settled slightly. By mid-morning, it is gone. The water does not soak in—nothing soaks in—so it moves as thin sheets across the graded sections, finding the channels that lead to reservoirs, or perhaps to somewhere else, somewhere lower, somewhere the making continues.

The channels are the closest thing to topography. They're the only features that break the flatness. Some are cut deep, the work of decades of routing water. Others are freshly stamped, still sharp-edged, the metal bright where it was recently sheared. The older channels have developed their own erosion patterns: rounded edges, accumulated sediment that might be dust or might be metal filings, small deposits where the water slows. This erosion is the youngest geology the plain has—deposits measured in years rather than millennia, processes that began after the making reached this section and will end when the making moves on.

And the making does move. You can hear it if you listen: the distant percussion of stamping presses, the sustained groan of welders operating at scales meant for shipyards. The sound travels across the metal surface, clearer than it would across earth, conducting through the material itself. The plain is not finished. Sections ahead remain under construction, their surfaces still being decided, their drainage channels not yet cut. What exists now is a fraction of what will exist—a snapshot of a process that operates on timescales where human presence is barely noticeable.

The quiet between percussion bursts is enormous. No wind in trees because there are no trees. No animal calls because there are no animals. Just the thermal creak of metal expanding, the trickle of condensation finding its level, the occasional groan from sections that are settling under their own weight. When the wind does rise, it moves differently across the surface—a steady, uninterrupted flow that carries only heat and the metallic taste of manufactured air.

Vegetation appears in the older sections, where sediment has accumulated in the drainage channels and moisture collects regularly. Thin films of algae first, then filamentous growths that anchor in the accumulated grit, then the occasional pioneer plant—hardy, chlorophyll-poor, adapted to substrates that leach heat and shed water. These are not ecosystems in any meaningful sense. They are isolated pockets of biology, each drainage channel its own island, separated by kilometers of hot metal where nothing takes root. The plants do not spread. They wait. They survive. They make use of what the making leaves behind.

In the hours before dawn, the plain is almost beautiful. The condensation surfaces catch the first light, turning the stamped patterns into mirrors. The accumulated heat releases slowly, warming the air from below, creating visible layers of temperature differential. The welding flashes on the distant horizon look like summer lightning. For those few hours, before the sun rises high enough to make the surface temperature dangerous, you can exist here comfortably. You can walk the seam-welded sections and imagine that the making is natural, that this is simply the geology of a world where metal grows instead of stone.

Then the sun rises fully, and you seek the shade of the drainage channels, and you wait for evening, and the making continues.
