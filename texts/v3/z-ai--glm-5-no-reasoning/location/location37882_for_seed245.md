# The Underthing

The first thing to understand is that nothing here was grown. Nothing was born. The floor beneath your feet was *laid* — slab by enormous slab — by hands or hand-equivalents that worked with stone and fired clay and bindings made from something that is not resin but behaves like it under certain conditions. The walls were stacked. The ceilings were fitted, though many of the fits have gone wrong. The whole place is a construction, and it goes on and on, and you cannot find its edges, and this is because it does not have edges, not really, not yet. It is still being made.

That is the second thing to understand. It is *still being made.*

---

You enter from somewhere — a hatch, a fissure, a corridor that narrows and then doesn't. The light is low. Not dark, exactly; there is a sourceless amber-brown luminescence that seems to live in the air itself, heavier than air should be, thick as broth, so that objects fifteen meters away already begin to dissolve into murk. You can see your own hand but not your fingertips when you stretch your arm full-length in front of you. The light has a texture to it. It clings.

The ground is a pavement of large tiles, each perhaps two meters across, each cut from a different stone — some chalky and pale, some dark as graphite, some banded with mineral stripes that might be agate or might be something that doesn't have a name yet, because the geology here is not the geology of any place that had a stable childhood. The tiles don't quite meet. The gaps between them are finger-width or hand-width, and from these gaps comes water.

Not dramatically. Not geysers or springs. It weeps. It seeps. It rises slowly, filling the cracks with a dark, warm fluid that is not quite water — it's thinner, it has a faint iridescence, and when it moves it doesn't ripple so much as *fold*, like silk falling in slow air. The fluid fills the cracks to brimming, and then it spills over the tile edges in thin sheets, and the whole floor becomes a shallow warm bath, ankle-deep at most, moving in no particular direction. There is no slope here. The water goes where it goes by capillarity and cohesion and by laws that may have been written differently this morning.

It is warm. The fluid is warm, the air is warm, the stone is warm. Not hot — not burning — but warm the way a body is warm, warm the way breath is warm, a pervasive metabolic heat that suggests this place has a temperature the way an organism has a temperature. You sweat immediately. The air is saturated. Moisture condenses on every surface, including your skin, so you are both sweating and being sweated upon, and you cannot tell which is which. The stone tiles under the fluid are slick. Some have been grooved — carved with simple channels, hand-cut, the tool marks still visible — as if someone tried to manage the water flow, tried to give it direction. The channels are ancient and crude. The fluid ignores them entirely.

---

This place is vast in a way that doesn't resolve into a shape. You walk, and the pavement continues. You walk further, and the ceiling rises — you sense this more than see it, because the amber murk thickens upward — and the space opens into something that might be a hall, might be a plain, might be a room the size of a small sea. There are pillars. No: there are *stacks*. Columns of fitted stone blocks, each block hand-shaped, some with chisel marks still fresh, rising to a height you cannot determine because they vanish into the glowing ceiling-fog. They are spaced widely — twenty meters apart, fifty, a hundred — so that the space between them feels like open country. The floor stretches between the columns unbroken except for the weeping cracks, and the water moves in its slow, folding way, and the silence is enormous.

Not complete silence. There is a sound, but it is a non-sound, the sound of a place that is so quiet its own stillness becomes audible: a low, pressed hum, not mechanical, not vocal, like the sound of a shell held to your ear except the shell is the whole world. And underneath it, if you stop and crouch and put your ear near the water, you can hear something else — a crackling, a ticking, like cooling stone. Like something that was recently hotter and is now, in this particular spot, becoming less hot. But over there, thirty meters away, you hear a different sound: a groan, a shift, a settling of stone on stone, and you see — or sense — one of the far columns *adjusting*, its blocks sliding a centimeter against each other, finding a new arrangement.

The columns are not static. They are being built. They are *always* being built.

---

Now you notice the activity, and once you notice it, it is everywhere. At the limits of vision, in the amber murk, shapes move. Not animals. There are no animals here. There are no plants, no moss, no lichen, no living thread of anything biological. The shapes are — structural. They are like assemblages. Crudely fitted stone and fired-clay forms, vaguely limb-like, vaguely purposeful, moving with a slowness that makes them easy to overlook. They are not machines. They have no joints, no mechanisms. They move the way rocks move in a landslide, except slowly, except upright, except with what appears to be intent. They carry blocks. They carry slabs. They carry fragments of stone that they have shaped by striking them against other stones. Their tools are stones. Their hands are stones. They are made of the same material as the floor and the columns and the ceiling, and they are building more of all of these things, and they have been doing this for a duration that does not translate into years.

You approach one. It does not acknowledge you. It is roughly the height of a kneeling person, a bundling of flat stone pieces held together by the same not-resin that binds the blocks in the columns, a dark, tacky substance that looks like tree sap but is not, that smells like nothing, that is warm to the touch. The stone-thing is fitting a block onto the base of a new column. It lifts the block — slowly, with what would be effort if it had a metabolism — sets it, adjusts it, lifts another. It works by touch. It has no eyes. The amber light does not seem to matter to it. It works in the murk as if the murk were its natural element.

How many are there? You can't tell. The scale is too large, the light too low. In any given direction, at the edge of sight, you can see two or three moving. Given the distances and the spacing, there could be thousands. There could be millions. The space is large enough for weather — and yes, you realize now that the ceiling-fog is not uniform. It moves. It thickens and thins in slow billows. There are currents. There are places where the fog dips lower, forming columns of denser air that almost touch the ground, and places where it rises and the ceiling, if there is a ceiling, becomes almost visible — a fitted stone surface, vast, flat, featureless, extending in every direction. Above and below, the same construction. You are inside a built space that extends in all three dimensions, and it is building itself.

---

The turbulence is not violent. It is relentless. The rules change — not dramatically, not catastrophically, but continuously, locally, the way weather changes. You walk through a region where the fluid in the cracks has become thicker, almost gelatinous, and the stone tiles have begun to curve upward at their edges, forming shallow bowls. You walk further and the tiles have become hexagonal, fitted more tightly, and the fluid is thin and blue and moves quickly in straight lines along channels that appear to have been cut very recently — the tool marks are sharp, fresh. You walk further still and the floor is not tile at all but a single continuous slab of something like fired clay, reddish, cracked in great jagged lines, and from those lines comes not fluid but warm air, a slow exhalation, a breath. And then it goes back to tile. And then something else.

The columns change too. In one region they are thick and crude, blocks the size of your torso. In another they are slender, made of pieces no larger than a hand, stacked to impossible heights, swaying slightly, the not-resin between them flexing. In one place — and you stop here, and stare — a column has been built horizontally. It extends from a wall-like stack of stone outward into open space, unsupported, for a distance of perhaps forty meters, a built bridge of stacked blocks that refuses to fall. The not-resin holds it. The not-resin and whatever local physics permits. You walk under it. It doesn't creak. It doesn't settle. It simply exists, a structural impossibility made mundane by the fact that it is here and it is not falling.

In another place, the floor tiles have been lifted and re-laid at angles, standing on their edges like rows of books, and the fluid runs between them in parallel streams, and the stone-things are moving through these streams like figures in a shallow canal, carrying fragments, building something at the far end that you cannot make out. It might be a wall. It might be a room. It might be the beginning of a floor for a level above this one, a second story, a new layer of construction that will eventually become the ground for someone else, or something else, walking in amber light.

---

The weirdness accumulates slowly. It is not in any single feature. It is in the totality. This place has no outside. It has no before. It is a construction with no constructor — or rather, the constructors are part of the construction, made of the same stone, bound by the same resin, working by the same crude hand-logic of strike and shape and stack. There is no plan. There is no blueprint. The stone-things build where they are, with what they have, and what they have is stone and fired clay and the not-resin and the warm fluid and the amber light and the heat and the vast, vast space. They build because building is what the stone does here, the way water flows downhill elsewhere. It is a mineral process that looks like architecture. It is geology that has learned to stack.

And the rules keep shifting. You walk through a zone where the fluid in the cracks has begun to rise — not ankle-deep but knee-deep, then waist-deep, and the tiles beneath are gone, replaced by a smooth stone basin, and the fluid is warm and body-temperature and silky against your skin, and you are wading through a warm dark sea in a built room the size of a lake, and the columns rise from the fluid like piers, and the fog is low and close and the amber light is dimmer here, almost gone, and the silence is absolute, and you feel the stone-things somewhere beneath the surface, moving, stacking, building in the dark warm water, building the floor that will replace this one. You feel their motion in the displacement of the fluid. Slow. Steady. Patient in a way that has nothing to do with time, because time here is not a thing that passes but a thing that accumulates, like sediment, like the layers of stone.

Then the floor rises again. The fluid shallows. The light returns. The tiles are back. The columns resume their wide spacing. The stone-things move in their slow convoys. The air is warm and wet and still. The place is being made, is always being made, will always be made, and the making is all there is.

---

You sit on a tile. The stone is warm. The fluid seeps around your fingers where you touch the crack. It is warm too. Everything is warm. Everything is made. Everything is being remade. The silence holds you the way water holds a stone. In the distance, something shifts — a column settles, a wall extends, a tile is laid. The sound is small and precise, a tap, a click, a breath of displaced air. Then silence again.

This place does not need you. It does not know you. It is building itself, and it will build over your footprints, and it will build over the tile you sat on, and it will build over the column you touched, and it will build until the amber light is pressed out of existence by the sheer density of stone, and then it will build in the dark, and the warm fluid will fill the new cracks, and the stone-things will carry their crude tools into spaces no one has ever seen, and they will stack, and they will stack, and they will stack.

This is the world. This is all of it.
