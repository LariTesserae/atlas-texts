The plateau goes on forever.

This is the first thing you understand about it—not that it is high, though it is, but that it is vast in a way that makes "horizon" feel like a hopeful concept. The air thins as the land spreads, and somewhere in the distance, too far to walk in a day, the faint blue smear of what might be a mountain range breaks the flat assertion of stone and sky.

But you cannot see much of the sky here. The darkness is not night—there is no sun to mark the passage of time—but a kind of perpetual dusk that lives in the plateau's own physics. The light comes from below and from within: mineral veins that glow with a low, sickly phosphorescence, a green-gold luminescence that pools in the shallow depressions of the rock and illuminates nothing beyond a few meters. The world recedes into shadow quickly. Forms become suggestions. Distance becomes a guess.

**The stone remembers water.**

You see it in the channels carved into the bedrock—smooth, sinuous grooves that wind across the plateau surface like frozen rivers. They are dry now, most of them, but some hold moisture. If you crouch and press your palm into the deeper channels, your hand comes away damp. The stone drinks. Condensation forms in these ancient beds during the plateau's slow cycles, and when the moisture comes—the mist that rolls in without warning, the sudden seep from underground—these channels become temporary streams. They flow for hours or days, then empty again. The water has carved its patience into the rock over millions of years. The erosion is not violent here. It is the erosion of persistence, of a million repetitions of the same gentle process.

Walking the plateau means following these channels. They are the only navigation in a landscape that offers no landmarks, only the gradual shift from one type of stone to another—from the dark, almost black basaltic flats to the lighter, porous rock that sounds hollow underfoot, that cracks if you step wrong. The porous sections are dangerous. They hide deep pockets, sudden wells where moisture collects and colonies of things grow in the dark. You learn to test the ground. You learn to listen for the echo.

**There is industry here, but it is not yours.**

The sounds give it away before you see it. In the mostly-quiet, the mechanical rhythms travel. A deep, resonant thrumming that vibrates in your chest. The high, wheezing complaint of a piston in need of oil. The rhythmic clank-clank-clank of something turning endlessly over, a process that has been running so long it has worn itself into the geology.

Follow the sound and you find the machines.

They rise from the plateau like organs of an exposed body—immense, rust-furred structures of iron and brass, of materials you cannot name, their surfaces crusted with mineral deposits that have accumulated over centuries. They are not built, exactly. They emerged. Or they were placed so long ago that the stone has grown around them, through them, so that now the machines seem to be both manufactured and geological, engines that have become part of the earth's own architecture.

Nobody operates them. The machines run themselves.

Some pump air, pulling the thin atmosphere through vast filtration chambers and exhaling something denser, wetter, the air tasting of metal and ozone. Others drill—slowly, so slowly that you can watch for an hour and see no progress, but the drill-bits are deep, the bore-holes they've made are kilometers long, descending into the plateau's body toward something the machines need or seek. Others do nothing you can identify. They click and whir and shudder, their purpose obscure, their outputs invisible, running processes that no one remembers the purpose of.

They are powered by the glow. The phosphorescent mineral veins that thread through the plateau—they lead to the machines, concentrate beneath them, feed them somehow. A geothermal-sulfur-bacterial-electrical process that the machines have tapped into, or perhaps one the machines created. It is impossible to know. The technology is industrial but alien, from an era of manufacture that predates any recognizable history, built by hands—or appendages, or processes—that left no other trace.

The plateau keeps its secrets.

**Life persists in the margins.**

Where the machines are, there is life. This is the rule. The engines produce something—heat, moisture, chemical byproducts—that draws the fauna, and the fauna are thick here, abundant in a way that the sparse vegetation is not.

The scrub that grows in the rock-channels is hardy and low. Gray-blue succulents that store water in their thick leaves and taste of salt. Clusters of fibrous, thread-like plants that reach toward the machine-heat, that thrive on the runoff, that form dim, scraggly carpets around the base of the oldest engines. Moss—the only true green in the landscape—grows in the damp interiors of the filtration chambers, fed by condensation, tended by things that live inside. In the rare places where water pools for more than a few days, a burst of life appears: tiny white flowers with no scent, insect larvae that mature and die within a week, a bloom of activity that vanishes as quickly as it emerges.

But the animals are the plateau's true inhabitants.

They emerge from the darkness in the phosphorescent glow, drawn to the machines, drawn to movement, drawn to each other. There are many kinds. Herds of small, hoofed quadrupeds that graze on the scrub and startle at any sound, their eyes catching the light and reflecting it back. They have no name—you would have to name them yourself—but they are numerous, their hooves clattering on the stone in numbers that make you realize the plateau is less empty than it seems. They follow the moisture channels. They know where the seeps will form before they form. They have learned the plateau's rhythms over generations.

Larger things move in the deeper dark. Predators. You hear them more than see them—a low, rumbling call that travels across the rock, a sound that might be vocalization or might be the grinding of stone. The herds scatter when they hear it. You learn to pay attention to where the quadrupeds go, because they know. The predators are few and patient. They do not hunt often. They do not need to. They are large enough that a single kill feeds them for weeks, and they rest between meals in the cool interiors of abandoned machine-chambers, their bodies wrapped around the warm metal, their slow breath fogging the air.

There are insects, too. Or insect-adjacent things—hard-shelled, clicking, crawling creatures that swarm around the machine-vents and feed on the mineral residue that builds up there. They are harmless but voracious. If you stand still too long, they will begin to investigate, their thin legs probing your clothing, their mandibles testing your skin. They do not bite, but the sensation is deeply unpleasant. You move to avoid them.

And there are things in the deep wells—the porous rock-pockets that hold water and darkness. You hear them, sometimes. Splashing. A wet, heavy sound of something surfacing and submerging. You do not investigate. The animals avoid those wells, and their instinct is better than yours.

**The atmosphere presses.**

The plateau's air is dense at ground level and thin above. It holds moisture in pockets—damp micro-climates around the machines, bone-dry stretches in the open flats where nothing grows and nothing moves. When the mist comes, it rolls across the land in walls, and the temperature drops sharply. You feel it in your joints. The damp settles into you, and the darkness becomes total, and you navigate by feel and memory until it passes.

The mist brings smells. Iron and sulfur from the machines. The green, living smell of the moss. The musk of animal bodies, distant herds, the sharp tang of waste and the flat smell of old blood. And underneath it all, the mineral scent of the stone itself—ancient and patient, holding the memory of every rain that has fallen here, every living thing that has died and been absorbed, every machine-process that has run and run and run.

The plateau is active in its stillness. Weather cycles through, moving differently here, following patterns unique to this vast and elevated place. The atmosphere turns on itself. Moisture condenses and precipitates and evaporates again in closed loops that can take days or minutes. The darkness varies—sometimes thick and close, sometimes thin enough that you can see the faint suggestion of a ceiling far above, something like a sky but not quite, a dome of rock or something else that contains this world.

Because the plateau, you realize eventually, is contained. It is not open air. It is not a true surface. It is a vast interior space—a cavern that has forgotten it is a cavern, so large that it has developed its own weather, its own ecology, its own lightless day-night cycles. The darkness is above you because the world ends above. The plateau is the floor of an impossible chamber, a hollow planet's inner surface, a place where geology and industry have commingled for so long that they have become indistinguishable.

And the machines—those endless, patient engines—they are part of it too. Not intrusions. Organs. The plateau is a body, and the machines are its systems, processing, filtering, drilling, running processes that keep the whole thing alive or stable or functional according to purposes that no one remembers and nothing questions.

**You are not the first to walk here.**

But you may be the first in a very long time. The plateau has no built structures, no artifacts, no marks of habitation. But it has paths. Not made—worn. The grooves in the stone where countless feet have passed, following the water-channels, moving between machines, traveling routes that made sense for reasons that have been lost. The paths are ancient. They predate the current animal populations. Something else walked here once, systematically, repeatedly, and it has not walked here for a very long time.

The animals use the paths now. They do not know they are paths. They just know that following these grooves leads to water, to food, to safety. The ancient travelers knew that too. Or perhaps they made the paths for that reason. Or perhaps the plateau shaped itself to be walkable, to guide movement in certain directions, to keep its inhabitants away from the dangerous places, the deep wells, the unstable rock.

You walk the paths. You follow the water-channels. You stay near the machines when the mist comes because they are warm, because the animals gather there, because it is better than being alone in the open dark.

The plateau goes on forever. You walk. The machines hum. The darkness holds.

And somewhere, in the distance, another machine begins a new process—a shudder, a grinding, a sound like breath—and the plateau shifts around you, alive with activity you cannot see, carrying on its endless work in the dark.
