# The Rootmass

The light comes from above, filtered through layers upon layers of canopy so thick that the sky became irrelevant long ago. What was once a surface has become a ceiling—a living ceiling made of intertwined branches, epiphytic mats, aerial root systems, and leaf-layers so dense they form their own strata. This is not a forest. It is a substrate. The forest replaced the ground itself.

You descend into it. There is no other direction that matters.

---

The scale becomes apparent only gradually. What you first took for a hillside is a single root mass of something ancient, something that might have been a tree once before the definitions changed. The root you are walking on is broader than rivers you have known. Its surface is ridged, cracked, dense with smaller growths—moss analogs, liverwort colonies, films of algae that drink from the air itself.

The air is wet. Not raining, not flowing, just *present*. Moisture hangs. It condenses on surfaces constantly, forming a thin sheen that never quite dries. Your skin becomes damp within minutes and stays that way. The cold seeps through eventually—not bitter, not biting, just present, the kind of cold that makes breath visible in pale threads that disperse slowly.

This is a world of condensation rather than precipitation. The moisture exists in a perpetual state of almost-falling.

---

The vastness asserts itself in echoes—except there are no true echoes here. Sound gets swallowed. The plant mass absorbs everything. Your footsteps make soft compressions. Your voice, if you raised it, would travel perhaps thirty meters before the living architecture takes it apart, filters it through cellulose and lignin and leaf-surface into nothing.

It is quiet in the way of very large spaces. The quiet of cathedrals. The quiet of caves. The quiet of places where sound has too much work to do and gives up.

What sounds exist are small: the drip of condensation gathering and falling from one surface to another, the creak of root systems adjusting to their own growth, the soft rasp of something insect-like moving through bark far away.

---

The erosion here is biological. This is a young landscape in geological terms but ancient in biological terms—freshly formed, constantly reforming. The plant mass grows faster than stone could weather. Surfaces are sharp where stems emerge, tender where new growth pushes through old, cutting through the air with edges that haven't yet softened.

Growth is the erosion here. The constant upward and outward pressure of living things filling space. The substrate is alive and it is hungry for room.

---

You find structures occasionally. They are not built on the plant mass; they are grown *within* it, shaped by it, compromises between intention and circumstance. Shelters formed from bent stems trained over decades into archways. Windbreaks woven from living vines that were encouraged to thicken at certain points. Platforms made from the flat upper surfaces of root-crowns, leveled by careful cutting and allowed to heal into smooth floors.

The technology of building here is the technology of *guidance*. No cutting and joining, no smithing or firing. Just pressure and patience. A dwelling takes twenty years to reach habitability. The people who began it may not be the people who live in it.

Tools are found near these places: hand-sized stones worn into useful shapes, sharpened edges of shell or chitin, lengths of wood hardened by fire and time. Nothing metal. Nothing lasting. The living mass reclaims everything abandoned within weeks.

---

The light does not come from any single source. It descends. It filters. It diffuses through countless layers of leaf and stem overhead until it arrives here as a diffuse amber glow, sourceless and omnipresent. You cannot look up and see the sun. You cannot look up and see sky. Only more plant mass, ascending into brightness, becoming invisible in its own radiance.

Shadows are soft here. They pool rather than fall. The contrast between light and dark is gentle, graded, infinite in its gradations.

---

Fauna is rare. In three days of walking, you might see five living things that are not plants. A pale crawler moving between roots, transparent enough that you can see its digestive processes. Something avian far above, moving between canopy layers—gone before you can focus on it. Once, something larger passed nearby, something that made the root mass vibrate with footfalls, but you never saw it and it never saw you.

The scarcity feels intentional. This is not an ecosystem that needs animals. The plants have colonized every available niche, filled every role. What animals exist are holdovers, remnants, passengers. The world does not belong to them.

---

The weirdness accumulates. You notice it first in small ways: plants that seem to be growing toward you, orienting themselves in ways that suggest attention rather than heliotropism. A section of root mass that was smooth yesterday and is ridged today, as if something grew underneath its surface overnight. Sounds that resolve into silence when you focus on them—the acoustic equivalent of seeing movement in your peripheral vision.

The plant mass does not seem hostile. It does not seem friendly either. It seems *responsive*. It changes. It notices. The changes are not fast enough to track, not slow enough to ignore. You find yourself looking at areas you passed hours ago and being certain they were different then.

The turbulence here is growth itself—competitive, aggressive, patient growth. The constant creation and destruction of micro-environments. A shelf fungus appears overnight and renders a pathway impassable. A vine thicket dies back suddenly and opens a passage that wasn't there before. The landscape solves itself constantly, equations of light and moisture and nutrients working themselves out in vegetative flesh.

You are walking through a calculation in progress.

---

At night—though there is no true night here, only a dimming of the diffuse light as something far above cycles through its own processes—the temperature drops further. Condensation becomes heavier. The root mass sweats. Water collects in every depression, runs in slow thin streams down inclined surfaces, pools in natural bowls carved by decades of directed growth.

You learn to sleep in the hollows where the water flows away, on slight elevations, on surfaces that drain. There is no true dryness here. Only degrees of damp.

---

The question of what lies below the plant mass is academic for a long time. You descend, but the plant mass goes with you. Roots become smaller, denser, more tangled. Light becomes dimmer, warmth fades, moisture increases. The plants here are pale, almost colorless, drawing sustenance from below rather than above.

And eventually you reach something that might be stone, or might be older plant mass compressed into a kind of biological stone, or might be something else entirely. It is cold. It is solid. It does not grow.

But even here, roots have found purchase. They have bored into it, threaded through it, made it part of themselves. The surface is an illusion. The plant mass goes deeper.

---

The scale of this place becomes clear only when you stop moving and look. Really look. The branch overhead is a branch, yes, but it is also a pathway. It connects to other branches which connect to other masses which connect to systems which connect to regions which connect to a living continent that reaches toward something you cannot see, cannot comprehend, a vegetative planetary shell.

You are a microbe on a leaf. The leaf is a landscape. The landscape is one of billions.

---

And yet it is not hostile. It is simply indifferent at a scale that makes indifference feel like generosity. The plant mass holds you. It gives you a surface to walk on, air to breathe, light to see by. It takes nothing from you that you cannot spare.

In three days of walking, you have passed perhaps a dozen signs of other people: a dwelling recently abandoned, a cut stem still healing, a pathway marked by stones that must have been carried here deliberately. Whoever else is here is far away, and they are staying far away, or they are moving in ways that do not intersect with yours.

Perhaps that is how this place works. Perhaps there is room for everyone because there is too much room to ever find anyone.

---

You keep walking. The plant mass continues. The light filters down. The damp air presses against your skin. The quiet holds you.
