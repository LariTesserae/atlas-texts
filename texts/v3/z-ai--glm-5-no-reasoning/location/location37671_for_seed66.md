The plateau goes on forever. Up here, above the cloud layer that forms each afternoon like a thought, the city spreads in every direction—not built upward but excavated downward, terraced into the mountain's ancient skin. They call it the Hollow. Three million people live in its thousand-year-old arteries, and from the rim, you can see the whole skeleton of it: the great drainage channels cut like wounds, the smokestack forests breathing their slow copper breath, the cable-car lines threading districts together like sutures across a healed incision.

It hasn't rained in weeks, but everything weeps. The rock holds water the way a body holds blood—always near the surface, always ready to give. Walk any tunnel and feel the seep against your palm, that thin mineral sweat slicking the cut stone. The engineers of the Third Excavation understood this about the mountain, that it was a saturated thing, and they carved their gutters deep. Now the water runs in purposeful streams along every corridor edge, collects in the cisterns beneath each district, feeds the steam-works and the baths and the great condensers that hum in the lower levels. The air does the same thing—thick with wet heat in the deep chambers, cooler near the rim where the wind carries the water away as soon as it forms. Your skin never quite dries here. Your clothes take on that characteristic mineral smell, calcium and iron and something sweeter, the hollow's signature perfume.

No green grows in the Hollow. Not moss, not grass, not the stubborn lichen that claims every other high place. Three thousand years of occupation have killed that impulse entirely. The rock is too worked, too mineral-poisoned, too thoroughly claimed by processes that have no use for photosynthesis. Some say there were plants once, back when the plateau was first settled, that they were stripped for fuel in the early excavations. Others believe this place was always bare, that the mountain rejected green things even before humanity arrived. Instead, what covers every available surface is something between a crust and a skin—thin, pale, layered like mica but softer, organic in the way it responds to touch. It grows in the damp, thrives on the heat rising from the engine rooms below, forms its slow patterns across ceilings and walls and the undersides of the great bridges. The children call it the breath. It's not alive in any way that matters, but it's not quite dead either. It propagates through spore-like dust, spreads at the rate of perhaps a hand's breadth per year, and when the humidity shifts, it releases a faint sweet smell that collects in the unventilated corners.

The architecture has no single style. It accumulates. The oldest sections near the rim show the careful hand of the original excavators—corridors carved with mathematical precision, drainage channels cut at perfect angles, housing blocks arranged in solar alignment even though the sun barely penetrates that deep. Later work becomes industrial, brutal, built for function over form: the great engine chambers of the classical period, the steam-works of the mechanical age, the electrical infrastructure of the last two centuries threaded through whatever gap would hold it. The newest construction is all efficiency—prefabricated housing units slotted into ancient niches, power lines bundled along tunnel ceilings, ventilation shafts bored through walls that have stood for millennia. The city is dense not because space is scarce but because density is the native language of the place. People build into walls, under floors, above ceilings. A single cubic chamber might hold six households, stacked and partitioned and connected by ladders, each one breathing the recycled air of its neighbors.

The heat comes from below. Geothermal vents, tapped and channeled, drive the Hollow's industry and warm its bones. In the lower districts, you can feel the mountain's own warmth bleeding through the floor—pleasant in winter, oppressive in summer, always present. The steam-works dominate the middle levels, great brass-pipe architectures that hiss and clank in their endless cycles, converting hot water and pressure into movement, into power, into the rumble that vibrates through every wall. At night, when the steam builds in the upper corridors, the whole district glows orange with the light of a thousand pressure-release valves, little gas flames dancing on pipe-tops like mechanical incense. The warmth is the defining condition of Hollow life. It shapes where people gather and where they avoid, which chambers are desirable and which are too close to the source. Some work in the heat-sweat shops near the vents, laboring in wet shirts, breathing thick air. Others live in the cool rims, where the altitude tempers the thermal bleeding and the wind carries the moisture away.

Sound never stops. The Hollow breathes mechanically—the intake fans in the outer walls, the exhaust flues in the central shafts, the endless rumble of water moving through channels that predate the city itself. Add to this the industrial layering: the steam-works' clanking heartbeat, the distant thunder of the stamp mills processing mineral from the deep mines, the high whine of electrical transformers in the newer districts, the human noise of three million voices echoing off carved stone. The acoustic signature of any given district depends on its depth. Near the rim, you hear wind and the groan of old stone settling under the weight of accumulated centuries. In the middle levels, the mechanical noise swallows everything else, and conversation requires shouting, and the residents develop that characteristic Hollow bark, a near-shout learned in childhood. In the depths, where the heat rises and the steam-works thunder, it's too loud for speech at all, and the workers have developed a sign language specific to the conditions—flashing palm-signals in the orange glow of release valves, fingers tracing meaning in the condensation on pipe walls.

And the birds. God, the birds.

They came before the city, and they never left. The hollow-wing swallows, they call them, though they're no relation to true swallows—larger, darker, with bodies evolved for the specific ecology of high wet places. They nest in the millions, in every overhang and ledge and artificial cliff-edge that three thousand years of excavation has created. At dawn and dusk, they move in vast coordinated formations, black rivers flowing through the hollow's arterial spaces, and the sound of their wings alone is enough to drown conversation. They've adapted completely to the human presence. They nest in the eaves of housing blocks, in the gaps of the great bridges, in the steam-works' warm corners where the pipes provide perfect incubation heat. They eat the breath that grows on every surface, scrape it with specialized beaks, and their waste—mineral-rich, processed through bodies that have co-evolved with this place—feeds the crust in turn, accelerating its spread. The elders say there were fewer birds in their youth, that the population has grown with the city, that each new excavation creates more ledge-space for nests, and that the Hollow is now more bird than stone in the upper levels.

They're not aggressive, exactly. But they're overwhelming. A single flock passing through a district darkens the sky, and the air displacement from that many bodies moving in concert can knock a person off balance. The noise—a chittering, clicking language of their own—rises and falls in patterns that seem almost intentional, as if they're discussing the city below. Children throw breadcrumbs from the bridges and watch the black swirls descend. Workers curse them when they clog the intake fans with nesting material, then eat their eggs when rations run low. They're as much a part of the Hollow as the water or the heat, and no amount of culling has ever made a dent in their numbers. Some of the older families claim to understand specific calls, insist that the birds warn of steam-pressure failures, that they alert the district when a collapse is imminent. There's no proof of this. But in a place where the walls are alive with condensation and the floors hum with industry, where every surface carries that strange organic crust and every corner echoes with the noise of accumulated centuries, the birds seem no stranger than anything else.

This is the Hollow's true character: accumulation. Nothing leaves. The water cycles through the stone and the cisterns and the bodies of the residents and back into the stone. The heat rises from below and spreads through the levels and dissipates into the moisture. The sound bounces off carved walls and collects in chambers until it becomes a constant hum, the sound of living in a hollowed mountain. The birds cycle through their endless patterns, and their waste feeds the breath, and the breath feeds the birds, and the humans live in the spaces between, carving new chambers when they need room, threading new pipes through old ones, building ever denser in the only direction available: into each other.

Change comes slowly here. The same families have lived in the same terraced chambers for generations, passing housing rights from parent to child, maintaining the same drainage channels their ancestors carved. The steam-works have run for six hundred years on the same principles, the same pressure gradients, the same cycle of condensation and release. The birds nest where their ancestors nested. The water seeps where it has always seeped. In a world that changes, the Hollow endures—not unchanged, exactly, but settled into its own patterns, its own cycles, its own slow accumulation of matter and moisture and time.

The engineers say the mountain will eventually give out, that the water is eating the stone from within, that the excavations have gone too deep and the lower levels will collapse within the next century. Perhaps they're right. But the Hollow has survived three thousand years of occupation, three hundred generations of humans and their birds and their breath and their machines, all living in the wet heat of a carved mountain. The children born here know nothing else. They learn the dialect of shouting over steam-works, the sign language of the depths, the particular call of the hollow-wing swallows that nest above their bedroom windows. They taste the mineral water and know which cistern it came from. They feel the seep on their skin and call it home.
