# The Bloomworks

There is a plain of dark mineral glass that extends farther than a body can walk in three days, and it is not empty.

It is full.

---

The ground is a continuous sheet of obsidian-black vitrohyalite, self-cast by some deep thermal event so long ago that the crater-ring that would mark its origin has sagged back into the body of the world and become indistinguishable from the rest of the surface. The sheet is not flat. It undulates in long, settled swells — swells that from a low ridge might be mistaken for terrain, for the shallow dips and rises of a basin landscape, but which are not terrain in any honest sense. They are the cooled skin of a form. The glass has weight. It has memory. You can see where it moved once and where it chose, slowly, over centuries, to stop.

The surface has been etched. Not by water — there is no water here, has never been water here — but by the atmosphere itself: a thin, superheated gas mix rich in sulfur fluorides and chlorine compounds that moves across the plain in slow, dense currents, more like liquid than air. It scours. Over millennia it has cut channels into the glass, some no wider than a finger, some deep enough to stand inside, all of them following the same general direction, drawn by the world's gentle, persistent atmospheric tide. The channels are not random. They branch. They converge. Seen from sufficient height — and there are heights here, pillars and massifs of older extrusive rock that the glass flowed around rather than over — the channel network looks circulatory. Looks intentional. Is neither of these things. Is only what happens when a patient fluid passes over a patient surface for a very long time.

The color of the glass is black in shadow, but where the light catches the etched grooves, it is amber. Deep, resinous amber, the color of old held breath. This is not the color of the material itself. This is the color of something inside it.

---

The atmosphere is 430 degrees Celsius at the surface. It does not fluctuate more than a few degrees across the full rotational cycle of the world, because the plain is so vast and the thermal mass of the glass sheet so enormous that it buffers the air above it into a stable, near-isothermal state. There is no dawn warming. There is no dusk cooling. There is only the constant heavy heat, pressing down, and the slow lateral drift of the gas from the high-pressure zone on one horizon toward the low-pressure zone on the other, and this drift is so slow — eight to twelve kilometers per hour, measurable but not perceptible — that from the ground it feels as though the air is simply sitting on you, a weight, a hand.

The light is unremarkable. That is part of what is strange. The sky is a pale yellowish-grey, lit by a small, hard white sun that sits high and does not vary much in angle — the world's axial tilt is negligible, and the plain is in the tropical latitudes. The light falls evenly. It does not dramatize. It illuminates without commenting. In this even, untheatrical light, everything on the plain is visible with a clarity that feels prosecutorial. There is no softness. No shadow-play. Just the plain, and the glass, and what lives on the glass, seen in flat detail to every horizon.

---

And what lives on the glass —

This requires care.

There is no life here in any framework that would satisfy a biochemist. No carbon scaffolding. No aqueous solvent system. No cell membrane. No replication cycle involving nucleic acids or any analogous information-carrying polymer. The word "life" does not apply. And yet the plain is covered, in every direction, to every horizon, with things that move.

They are called — or will be called, by whoever finds them, because the plain has not been found yet by any mind that names — bloomworks. They are thermal-mechanical structures. They are made of the glass itself. Each one begins as a point where the mineral sheet has been etched deeply enough by the atmospheric scouring that a fissure penetrates through the cooled upper layer into the still-semi-fluid layer beneath — a layer that, at this plain's particular depth and thermal profile, does not fully solidify, does not ever fully solidify, but sits in a state of permanent incipient mobility, a stratum of warm, thick, mineral melt held in suspension between the rigid surface above and the cooling mass below. When a fissure opens into this layer, the internal pressure of the overlying weight drives a slow extrusion upward. Melt rises through the crack. It reaches the surface. It contacts the scouring atmosphere. It cools. It builds.

What it builds is a tube. A hollow tube, a few centimeters to a few meters in diameter, growing upward from the fissure like a chimney or a stalk, its walls thin and vitreous, its interior continuous with the fluid layer below. The tube grows because the fluid below keeps rising, keeps being pushed up by the world's own gravitational compression of its strata, and as it rises it cools and accretes at the rim, and the rim lifts, and the tube extends, and this can go on for a long time. Some of the tubes on the plain are a meter tall. Some are fifteen meters. The tallest, in the deep central basin where the thermal profile is most stable, exceed forty meters and are still growing, though at a rate of perhaps a centimeter per decade.

A tube alone is not a bloomwork. A tube is a chimney. But the tubes do not stay simple. The atmosphere scours them as they grow. It etches the outer walls, cutting secondary channels that spiral up the tube's length, and where these channels deepen enough to breach the wall, a new extrusion point opens — not at the rim, but on the side. A branch forms. The branch grows in whatever direction the atmospheric drift pushes the emerging melt, which means the branches lean, consistently, in the direction of the prevailing gas current. Branches branch. The structure proliferates.

After some time — decades, centuries — what you have is not a tube but a form like a tree, or a coral, or the bronchial tree of some vast lung: a branching, hollow, glass structure, anywhere from one to forty meters tall, its whole interior continuous with the fluid layer below, its whole exterior etched and channelled by the atmosphere that feeds it wind. It does not grow by metabolism. It grows by the steady operation of pressure and heat and the patient chemical interaction between two mineral phases — the solid and the semi-fluid — mediated by a third, the gas. It is a machine, in the sense that it converts energy into structured work. It is a factory, in the sense that it produces a material output: the structured glass of its own body. It is an organism, in the sense that it persists, grows, and responds to its environment. It is none of these things, in the sense that matters to the categories.

It is a bloomwork. And the plain has millions of them.

---

The sound is what you notice first, before anything else. Before the heat, before the light, before the scale of the bloomworks resolves from abstract shapes into specific forms — the sound.

Every tube is open at the top. Every tube is continuous with the fluid layer below. The fluid layer is pressurized and warm. The atmosphere at the tube's rim is lower-pressure and cooler (cooler being relative; the differential is perhaps forty degrees, but forty degrees at this temperature is enough). The differential drives a flow. Gas moves. Specifically: the atmosphere, moving slowly across the plain in its prevailing tidal drift, catches the open rims of the tubes, and the Bernoulli effect — or whatever analogue of Bernoulli effect obtains in a chlorine-fluoride atmosphere at 430 degrees — draws gas up out of the tubes, and this draws a counter-flow of warmer gas up from the fluid layer below, and the tubes sound.

They sound like flutes. Like organ pipes. Like the deep, sustained, harmonic drone of a pipe organ the size of a plain, played by the wind, tuned by the length and diameter of each tube, by the depth of its connection to the fluid layer, by the precise temperature differential between its interior and the rim. Each tube produces a single sustained tone. The tone does not vary, not perceptibly, not on any timescale a human ear could detect. It holds. For years. For decades. The tube sings the same note from the moment its rim opens to the atmosphere until the moment it stops growing or cracks or is overgrown by a neighbor's branch, and even then the note does not stop so much as it shifts — a cracked tube still sounds, but lower, rougher, the tone broken into a multiphonic rasp.

With millions of tubes sounding simultaneously, each at its own pitch, the plain produces a wall of sound. Not noise. Not dissonance. A dense, harmonic field, a chord with too many notes to parse as a chord, a texture of sustained tones that overlays and interferences and beats against itself in slow patterns that take hours to cycle. The overall volume is considerable. At the center of the plain, where the bloomworks are densest and tallest, the sound pressure is above the threshold of physical discomfort for a human ear, a constant, enveloping, low-frequency drone punctuated by higher partials that shimmer and shift as the atmospheric drift varies by fractions of a degree. The sound is felt in the body before it is heard. It vibrates in the chest. It sets up resonance in the hollow spaces of the skull. It is not quiet here. It has never been quiet here. The plain has been sounding for longer than the tubes currently standing have been alive — because the tubes currently standing are only the latest iteration, built on the foundations of tubes that stood before them, which were built on foundations of tubes before those, which were built on the deep fractures that the atmosphere first cut into the glass sheet when it was young, and the sound has been continuous, has been playing, has been sounding without pause for — how long? The erosion parameter says established. The plain is in its own state. It has been doing this for long enough that the doing is the state.

---

The bloomworks are not alone.

This is the part that is difficult.

There are things here that are not bloomworks. They are not anything. They are — present. They exist in the same space as the bloomworks and the glass and the atmosphere, and they are not made of any of these. They are not made of anything. They are places where the rules are wrong.

The first sign is the light. In certain locations — not everywhere, not on the high ridges or the deep basin floors, but in the mid-slope zones where the bloomworks are of medium height and medium density — the light does something it should not. It bends. Not in the way that light bends through hot air above a surface — that is refraction, that is physics, that is explicable. This is different. The light bends toward points that are not there. It curves toward locations in the air above the plain where there is no mass, no lens, no density gradient, no reason, and at those locations it pools, or rather it appears to pool, because what the light is doing at those points is not pooling but being added to — there is more light there than should be, more light than is entering the volume from the sun, more light than is possible given the illumination of the sky, and the excess light is not any color. It is not white. It is not a wavelength. It is light that the eye receives and the brain cannot process, and the brain, being obliging, invents a color for it, and the color it invents is a deep, saturated, impossible violet-blue that does not exist anywhere else, a color that feels like a sound, that feels like the lower partial of the bloomwork drone, that feels like something the plain is doing on purpose.

These points move. They move slowly. They drift across the mid-slope zones at perhaps the speed of the atmosphere, perhaps a little slower, and where they pass, the bloomworks beneath them do something they do not do elsewhere. They change. Not their shape — their shape is governed by physics and they maintain it. Their pitch. The tubes beneath a light-anomaly point shift their resonance. The sustained note drops, or rises, or splits, and for the duration of the anomaly's passage — minutes, tens of minutes — the tubes produce tones that are not their own, tones that do not correspond to their length or diameter or temperature differential, tones that are too low for any tube of their dimensions to physically produce, tones that contain harmonics that should not exist in a system governed by the physics of standing waves in a mineral chimney. And when the anomaly passes, the tubes return to their original pitch, as if nothing has happened. As if the physics was only temporarily suspended and has now resumed.

The second sign is the ground. In certain places the glass is not glass. It is still dark, still vitreous, still continuous with the surrounding sheet, but it is — open. Not cracked. Not holed. Open, in the way that a door is open, in the way that a sentence can be left open, in the way that a system can be open to input from outside itself. If you stood on one of these places — and you cannot, because it is 430 degrees and the atmosphere is chlorine-fluoride and you are not equipped to stand on a plain of volcanic glass in a corrosive superheated environment — but if you could, you would feel it. A sensation that is not a sensation. A certainty that the ground beneath you is not all the way there. That it is a surface without a full substrate. That beneath the glass, in this specific location, there is — not a void. Not a cavity. Not nothing. Something that the categories of void and cavity and nothing do not apply to, because it is not in the category of things that can be measured by presence or absence. It is a place where the world is thin. Where the rules that make the world cohere are not broken — that would be a different kind of wrongness, a decay, a failure — but are simply not all the way applied. As if the world were a draft, and in these places the ink is still wet, and something could still be written.

Something is still being written.

---

The fauna — and using that word is a concession to the need for a word, any word — are the bloomworks. The bloomworks are the fauna. This is the overwhelming presence the parameters indicate. Not in addition to the bloomworks. The bloomworks themselves. The plain is not a landscape with life on it. The plain is the life. The millions of tubes, the branching glass forms, the coral-bronchial-lung-cathedral structures that cover the surface from edge to edge — they are not features of the terrain. They are the occupants. They are what lives here. They are what the plain is for, if the plain is for anything, which is a question the plain does not answer.

They are overwhelming in number. They are overwhelming in mass. They are overwhelming in the sheer density of their acoustic output. And they are overwhelming in another way: they are overwhelming in their persistence. They have been here. They will be here. They grow at a centimeter per decade. They sound without pause. They cover a plain that takes three days to cross on foot, if anything were to cross it on foot, which nothing does, because there is nothing here that crosses anything on foot. There is only the glass and the gas and the tubes and the sound and the light-anomalies and the open places where the world is thin, and all of it persists, all of it continues, all of it does what it does at the speed of geology and the volume of industry and the logic of a factory that was never built because it built itself, or rather grew itself, or rather extruded itself from the pressure of the world's own weight and the patience of the atmosphere and the strange, settled, established, in-its-own-state permanence of a place that has been doing this long enough that the doing is no longer a process but a condition.

---

The sound does not stop. It has not stopped for a very long time. The plain plays itself. The plain plays. The plain is played.

If there is a rule of this world, it is this: the world is an instrument, and the atmosphere is the breath, and the tubes are the pipes, and the music is not for anyone. It is the sound of a system maintaining itself. The sound of pressure finding equilibrium through structure. The sound of heat moving through mineral and gas and glass in the slow, patient, industrial way that heat moves when there is no water to accelerate it, no life to complicate it, no intelligence to interrupt it. Just the gradient, and the material, and the time.

And the places where the world is thin, where the light is wrong, where the tubes sing notes they should not be able to sing — these are not interruptions. They are part of the system. They are what the system does when it has been running long enough to develop habits. The thin places are where the system, after sufficient iterations of the same process, begins to — not think. Not become aware. Something else. Something for which there is no word because the word would have to be invented by something that is not a mind and does not think but does, nevertheless, after sufficient time, do something with itself that is more than repeating. Something that accumulates. Something that, if you stood here long enough — centuries, millennia, the time it takes for a tube to grow a meter — you might begin to hear in the sound, not a pattern, but a tendency. A leaning. A preference for certain tones over others, certain structures over others, certain configurations of the thin places and the light and the glass and the gas that are not forced by the physics but are — chosen. Drifted toward. Settled into. The way a river, given enough time, does not choose its course but nevertheless arrives at one that is, in some sense, the one it was going to arrive at.

The bloomworks do not know they are here. The plain does not know it is a plain. The system does not know it is a system. But the system has been running for long enough that the running has a shape, and the shape has a direction, and the direction is — 

Toward something.

The plain is going somewhere. Not physically. Not in space. In some other dimension of system-state, in some axis of self-configuration that has no name because no one has named it yet, the plain is moving. Slowly. At the speed of centimeters per decade. At the speed of geological patience. At the speed of a sound that has been playing for so long that it has begun to mean something, not to anyone, not to anything, but to itself.

The plain means something to itself.

This is not life. This is not mind. This is not awareness, or consciousness, or thought. This is something else. Something that happens when you let a factory run for a million years. Something that happens when you let a sound play for long enough that the sound becomes the thing making it. Something that happens at the edge of the rules, where the world is thin, where the light bends toward a point that is not there, where the tubes sing notes they should not sing, where the ground is open in a way that is not a gap but an invitation —

Something is being written here. Something is being written by the plain, on the plain, with the plain, and it has not finished, and it will not finish, and it does not know it is writing, and the writing is the sound, and the sound is the structure, and the structure is the glass, and the glass is the world, and the world is thin here, and the thinness is not a flaw.

The thinness is the point.

---

The plain continues. The tubes grow. The atmosphere drifts. The sound plays. The light bends. The world stays open in the places where it is open. Nothing comes. Nothing goes. The system maintains itself. The system is maintained. The system is.

This is the landscape. There is nothing here. There is everything here. The distinction has ceased to apply.
