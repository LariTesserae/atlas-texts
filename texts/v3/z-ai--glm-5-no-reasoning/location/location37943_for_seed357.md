# The Weaving-Gardens of Upper Vost

High. The air tells you that immediately—thin and hot, pressing against your lungs. Each breath requires intention. You are somewhere elevated, a plateau or a shelf in a mountain range, but the thing about mountains is that mountains here do not exist. There is only the Garden.

It rises from itself. That is the first strangeness. Walk twenty paces in any direction and you will find that what you thought was ground becomes wall, becomes roof, becomes bridge, becomes something that has not yet settled on a shape and may never. Everything—the path beneath your feet, the arches overhead, the pillars that might be pillars or might be something midway through becoming something else—is plant. Not *planted*. Not grown *from* something. Simply *is*. The substrate itself is vegetation so dense and so old that the distinction between "living thing" and "place where living things exist" has collapsed entirely. There is no soil. There is no stone. There is only this: layer upon layer of growth, cellular memory stacked kilometers deep, roots braided into architectures that would make engineers weep.

*Hot*. The heat does not relent. It rises from the mass itself, from the metabolic burning of all this green flesh. At this elevation, the sun should be sharp and the air thin, but instead, the Garden breathes. It exhales heat from its stomata, pores that span meters across, exhales steam and spores. Water condenses and recondenses. The sky above is often obscured by a kind of mist, but the mist is living—pollen, and something else, microscopic seeds that drift upward seeking new territory. The sky is moist, the air is moist, and when you reach out to touch a surface, water beads against your palm. The rocks weep. Except there are no rocks. Only the thick-barked pseudo-stone surfaces, succulent-wrapped, and they weep *through* their surfaces, hydathodes pulling water from somewhere deep within and releasing it into the humid air.

The sound is tremendous. A roar, but not mechanical—the biological roar of a thousand thousand processes. Click, hiss, pop, and subsonic rumble. The Garden is growing, and growth, at this scale, is noisy. Turgor pressure builds and releases in great tendon-snaps. Stomata open with audible sighs. Fibers strain against fibers as the architecture reconfigures itself, constantly, slowly, never still. The sound is loud but it is not chaotic—it has rhythms, cycles. You could learn to read it if you stayed long enough. The sudden cracks are new openings forming. The long groans are load-bearing masses shifting weight. The hissing is the water, everywhere water, moving through capillary channels thick as your arm.

Sparse. The strangeness of sparse. For all this biomass, for all this living density, the place feels empty. Vast chambers open between the great growth-trunks. Corridors stretch into dimness where the canopy thickens overhead and the light turns green and diffuse, emerald darkness. You walk for minutes without encountering anything that seems like a presence—only the place itself, breathing and shifting and growing. The emptiness is eerie. No birds. No insects. No animals of any kind. No scraped marks of feeding, no burrows, no shit, no bones. The Garden has excluded them entirely, or perhaps never evolved to include them. This is a plant ecosystem so complete that it has no need for pollinators, no use for grazers. Pollination happens through vibration, through the shudder of growth itself, male organs releasing clouds that drift until they encounter female organs—no bee required. Fertilization is mechanical, internal, whole other systems you cannot name.

And yet—movement. The dynamism is extreme. Not animal movement, but *structural* movement. You are standing in a bazaar, a Solaris-flesh bazaar, where the stalls and the goods and the customers are all one thing, continuously creating and destroying itself. A corridor you passed an hour ago may no longer exist. An arch has formed where no arch was. The Garden builds according to rules you cannot read—competition? Cooperation? Something stranger? Colonies of cells negotiate their boundaries, merge and split, form partnerships and dissolve them. The turbulence is visible in the way sap-flowers bloom and wither in cycles of minutes rather than days. You watch a shelf of material grow outward from a pillar, hesitate, split, and then one half spirals down toward you, reaching—

*It is not reaching for you.* It has registered your warmth, perhaps, your exhaled carbon dioxide, and is responding. The tendril-tip brushes your arm and the surface is cool and slick. It tastes you. Decides you are not useful, or not usable. Moves on.

Nothing here is natural, but nothing here is artificial either. *Made*—everything is made—but made by whom? The Garden grew itself. It continues to grow itself. It is a made environment with no maker, or rather, the maker is immanent in every cell, every spore, every fiber. No tools. No devices. No tech, no mechanisms, no metal or plastic or silicon. The architecture is purely biological, and yet it is architecture, it is designed—the word is wrong, but the *fact* remains. Chambers and passages and balconies and bridges, all grown. The builders are the building. The weavers are the web.

This is a young landscape. Counterintuitive, given the scale, but the erosion tells you everything. There is no weathering here because the surfaces are constantly renewing. A scratch made in a wall will close within hours. There are no smoothed edges, no ancient paths, no places where feet have worn grooves—because feet do not walk here often enough, and when they do, the ground grows back sharper. The Garden is sharp-edged, aggressive in its geometry. The columns have definite corners. The bridges have defined rails. It is all crisp, clean, freshly emerged, and it will stay that way until it chooses otherwise.

The scale extends past what you can see. From a high point—a balcony grown for the purpose of seeing—you can look out across the Garden and see only more Garden, valley after valley of it, shelf after shelf climbing toward a horizon lost in spore-haze. Town-sized. Larger than town. A region, a province. Multi-feature and continuous. You cannot find an edge. You suspect there is no edge, or if there is, it is growing faster than you could walk toward it.

Strange. The word settles on you and does not leave. The strangeness is not in any single detail but in the *unification* of them. A hot, high, wet place where everything is alive and nothing moves like life. A jungle without animals. A city without builders. A garden without a gardener. The rules here are not the rules of the world you know. In the world you know, plants grow toward light and roots grow toward water and flowers exist to be seen by pollinators. Here, the rules are different, or the rules are being invented in real time. Plants grow toward *each other*. Roots grow *up* when it suits them. Flowers open and close on schedules that have nothing to do with day or night, responding to pressures and signals you cannot detect.

Something groans to your left. You turn in time to see a wall split open, revealing a cavity of pale, wet fibers, and then—almost immediately—watch it seal again. The wound closes without scar. Inside, for a moment, you saw movement: fluids pumping, cells dividing. The interior. The mechanism. The living engine of the Garden, always working.

The light is normal here, whatever that means. Sunlight filters through the canopy in places, thick and golden, and in other places the growth closes overhead and the light goes green-dark. No bioluminescence—this is not that kind of fantasy. The darkness is simply darkness, and the Garden grows into it regardless. Photosynthesis happens at the surface, in the canopy layers, in the great photosynthetic collectors that stretch toward the sun; everything below is powered by that energy, distributed through vascular networks, piped down into the dim interior where you walk. The air tastes of it. Green. Alive.

You hear water. Somewhere ahead, a sound like a waterfall, but continuous, as if a river were falling forever into a basin that never fills. You move toward it and the corridor opens, and you see—

A weeping arch. Water rising from below, pulled through capillary towers meters thick, jetting upward and then raining back down in a perpetual fountain. The water collects in channels that feed back into the system, a closed loop, hydraulic lift powered by no pump you can see. It is beautiful. It is also *purposeful*. The water carries nutrients. The fountain aerates and distributes. It is circulation, respiration, the Garden breathing through water.

You stand at the edge of the pool that has formed beneath the fountain. The surface is slick with pollen and fine debris. Nothing lives in it—no fish, no insects, no larvae. Only the reflection of the arching, growing, shifting Garden, and your own face, foreign and temporary in this place that does not need you, does not want you, does not even notice you except as a passing stimulus.

A tendril descends from above, questing. It touches your shoulder. It stays there.

*Waiting?* Or simply *being?*

In the Garden, the distinction may not exist.
