# The Weeping Commons of Mossvale

The first thing is the sound. Before your eyes adjust to the green — and everything is green, a green so total you stop seeing it as color and start experiencing it as medium, as the substance through which all other things travel — the sound finds you. A layered, percussive roar. Not one sound but hundreds, stacked and folded into a wall of vibration you feel in the cartilage of your throat. Wet slapping. Hollow drumming on taut membranes. The clicks and hisses and wet exhalations of too many bodies occupying the same compact air.

You are in a hollow. Not a valley exactly — the land curves down on three sides and opens on the fourth toward a marsh-spread that goes on maybe a quarter mile before it hits a low ridge of dark stone slicked black with constant moisture. The hollow is perhaps two hundred paces across. Maybe less. The ceiling, if you can call it that, is a lattice of interlocking branches so dense it filters the overcast sky into a shifting mosaic of pale grey and deep shadow. You are standing inside a living roof.

## The Architecture of Plant

There are structures here. dwellings, workshops, a meeting hall — but the word "built" almost doesn't apply, because nothing here was assembled so much as grown and then shaped. The walls are living tissue: woody frames of something between banyan and willow, their aerial roots descending in thick curtains that have been woven and pegged and trained into walls, doorframes, load-bearing columns. The wood is still alive. You can see it in the slow pulse of sap beneath bark that glistens with a permanent film of water. Leaves — broad, dark, waxy — emerge from every horizontal surface, oriented to catch what light falls through the canopy above.

The floors are not ground. They are root-mat, a dense interwoven platform of lateral roots and accumulated humus that has been packed and pressed firm enough to walk on. It gives slightly underfoot, a springiness that is both unsettling and comfortable, like walking on a drumhead. Water beads up through the root-mat constantly. You cannot take three steps without your foot finding a shallow puddle. The air smells of peat and green rot and something mineral, like wet chalk.

Everything weeps. The walls drip. The ceiling-foliage condenses moisture from the air and releases it in fat drops that patter constantly on the root-mat floor. Stone surfaces — and there are a few, low walls and path-edgings of a dark porous limestone — run with thin sheets of water as though they have been crying for centuries, which perhaps they have. The water table is so close to the surface here that you can see it in cross-section where the root-mat has been cut for a doorway: a dark, clear layer of water moving slowly beneath your feet like a hidden river.

There are channels cut into the root-mat — drainage ditches, essentially, their sides reinforced with woven root and packed moss — and water moves through them in steady little currents, converging in a central basin that someone has lined with flat stones. The basin is full to the brim. It overflows. It is always overflowing.

## The Density of Bodies

This is what accounts for most of the sound: the people, and the creatures.

There are perhaps four hundred people living in this hollow. The settlement has the feel of a small town compressed into the footprint of a single city block. The dwellings are stacked — not vertically in the modern sense, but layered in the way a forest is layered, with structures growing into and through and over each other. A workshop occupies a space between two dwellings. A food-storage chamber sits beneath a meeting platform that is itself underneath a canopy of trained branches that serves as a watch-post. A child swings from a vine-rope between the wall of one house and the wall of another, a distance of perhaps four feet. The passage between two buildings is the width of your shoulders. The passage between two others is the width of two hands. You turn sideways to pass.

And everywhere, the fauna.

## The Fauna

This is the thing that strikes you most after the sound: the sheer overwhelming presence of animal life. Not as background. Not as occasional encounter. As constant, pressing, inescapable company.

Amphibians first. Frogs and toads in dozens of species, from thumbnail-sized spray frogs that live in the film of water on the walls to fist-sized bullfrogs that sit in the drainage channels and stare at you with gold-rimmed eyes. Their calls overlap into a continuous textured roar that forms the bass layer of the soundscape — a pulsing, circular, polyrhythmic throb that you stop consciously hearing after a few minutes but that continues to vibrate in your sternum.

The spray frogs are everywhere. On walls. On floors. On the edges of bowls. On people's shoulders. They are so small and so numerous that you sometimes cannot tell if a surface is textured or simply covered in frogs. They eat the condensation-lice — tiny pale insects that live in the surface film of water on living plants, in numbers that beggar counting. When you brush past a hanging curtain of foliage, the air explodes with them, a cloud of pale specks that resettles within seconds.

Insects fill the next layer of sound. Cicadas drone from the upper canopy in a sustained electronic whine. Crickets chirp in staccato bursts from the root-mat. A species of large water-beetle, dark and oval, occupies every puddle, paddling in ceaseless small circles. They are considered lucky. People do not step on them, which requires a specific attentive way of walking that the residents have clearly internalized and visitors must learn the hard way.

Then the birds. The hollow is full of birds — not flying overhead but *in* the space, occupying the same air you breathe. A species of small rail, dark-feathered and long-legged, runs along the drainage channels, wading in the shallow water, picking at something. They are semi-tame and do not move when you pass, which means you occasionally nearly step on them, which means they shriek, which means the frogs near you jump, which means the beetles scatter, which means a small cascade of secondary motion follows you wherever you walk.

Higher up, in the lattice-canopy, songbirds call in short descending phrases that cut through the frog-roar and cicada-whine. A species of bright-eyed nectar-feeder — the size of your thumb, the color of wet copper — hovers at the flowers that stud every horizontal branch, its wings a blur that adds a mechanical buzzing to the upper-register sound.

And snakes. You see your first one draped across a doorframe — a thin emerald creature, perhaps two feet long, utterly still, its scales beaded with moisture. It is eating a frog. Slowly. The residents do not remark on this. Later you see another coiled in a drainage channel, and another draped along a root-mat walkway. They are everywhere. They are the reason the residents walk with a particular lifting gait — not exactly high-stepping, but deliberate, each foot placed with a small pause.

The fauna is not decoration. It is infrastructure. The frogs eat the condensation-lice, which would otherwise consume the living walls. The birds eat the frogs, preventing population explosions. The snakes manage both. The beetles process the surface water, consuming organic film that would otherwise clog the drainage channels. The people have not designed this system. They have grown into it. They are one node in a dense metabolic web that fills the hollow to its edges.

## The Cool and the Wet

The temperature is perhaps ten degrees cooler than the surrounding lowlands. The canopy blocks direct sun. The constant evaporation from wet surfaces pulls heat from the air. You can see your breath in the early morning, faintly, before the biological heat of so many bodies — human, amphibian, insect, bird — warms the hollow from within.

There are no fires. The density of wet living material would make combustion impossible, and the residents do not seem to miss it. Heat, when needed, comes from a system of shallow pits lined with flat stones under which slow-burning root-mats decompose in a controlled wet-fermentation that produces a gentle steady warmth. You can sit near one and feel it: not hot, but present, like the warmth of another body. The pits smell sweetly of earth and alcohol.

Clothing is minimal and functional. Woven fiber from the aerial roots, treated with a resin that the bark exudes when scored. The fabric is stiff, water-resistant, and dark. People wear wraps and over-shoulder garments that seem to be as much about carrying things — tools, frogs, food, children — as about coverage. Everything is slightly damp. Everything smells slightly of green.

## The Tools

You see stone tools — a dark chert, probably sourced from the ridge. Adzes, scrapers, a kind of small chisel used for scoring bark to direct the growth of structural branches. You see bone tools as well, slender and fine, used for weaving and for the delicate work of guiding root-train. There is a simple metal — a copper alloy, soft and greenish — worked into small fittings and fastenings, but nothing structural. The structural work is all done by living plant and woven fiber.

The most sophisticated tools are the growing-tools: not implements but *techniques*. You watch a woman score a young branch in a precise spiral pattern and peg the cut edges apart with small bone wedges. The branch, still growing, will thicken at the cut and form a joint — a living hinge — that will harden over the next season into a flexible connection point. She is building a door. The door will be alive. It will grow thicker each year. Eventually it will need to be cut and re-grown. This is understood. Everything here is understood to be temporary in its current form and permanent in its process.

## The Turbulence

For a place so established — and it is established, the root-mat floors are thick and firm, the walls are decades old, the drainage channels are deep and worn smooth — the hollow has a quality of constant, near-chaotic motion.

People move through the narrow spaces with a speed and purpose that seems agitated until you realize it is simply efficient. There is no room for leisurely movement. If you stand still in a passage, you are in someone's way within seconds. The settlement functions like a circulatory system, and its inhabitants are the blood.

Children appear and disappear into gaps between walls that are too small for adults. You hear their voices — sharp, high, competing with the frog-roar — and catch glimpses of small limbs and dark hair before they are gone again into the green dark. They have their own network of passages, grown for them or made by them in the interstitial spaces that no adult can reach.

The fauna adds to the turbulence. At any given moment something is moving: a frog jumping, a bird landing, a beetle paddling, a snake shifting its coil. The water moves in the channels. The walls drip. The leaves overhead shift in a breeze you can feel only faintly at ground level, a stirring of the canopy that produces a ripple of changing light down below. The settlement breathes, and its breathing is not slow.

There is a market — three stalls in a widened space perhaps ten feet across, where people trade food and tools and small bright frogs that are considered a delicacy. The trading is fast, nearly continuous, a low argumentative murmur that is one more layer in the sound. Hands flash. Items change ownership. The space smells of crushed leaf and insect-shell and the faintly sour scent of frog-meat.

## What It Is

You realize, slowly, that this is not a settlement in the usual sense. It is not a place where humans have built structures in a landscape. It is a single organism — or if not an organism, a single system — in which humans are the part that shapes and directs. The plant is the substrate. The water is the medium. The fauna is the maintenance crew. The people are the architects, the wranglers, the market-traders, the ones who score bark and dig channels and decide which branch grows where.

They did not make this place. They grew it. They are still growing it.

And the place, in turn, grew them — into a shape that fits its density, its wetness, its noise, its ceaseless small motion. They are creatures of the hollow: compact, fast-moving, attuned to the position of snakes underfoot, comfortable with the constant presence of water and the constant presence of others. They do not seek solitude. They do not seek silence. They seek exactly this: the green press of living walls, the percussion of a hundred species occupying the same air, the steady weeping of moisture from surfaces that will never be dry.

The frogs call. The beetles paddle. A child swings past on a vine. The water moves in its channels. The walls grow.
