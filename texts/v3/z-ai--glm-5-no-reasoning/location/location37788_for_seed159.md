# The Hatching Caverns

The first thing you notice is the sound. It hits you before your eyes adjust to the filtered amber glow—a wall of noise that isn't one sound but hundreds, thousands maybe, all competing for the same airspace. Chittering. Scratching. The wet slap of soft bodies against stone. A droning undertone like someone blowing across a bottle the size of a cathedral. And beneath it all, a rhythmic pulse, regular as a heartbeat, that you feel in your teeth more than hear with your ears.

You are underground, but not in any mine or tunnel that humans made. The space extends far beyond head-height—vaulted ceilings lost in shadow, galleries branching off in multiple directions, a cathedral-scale architecture that was never built, only *grown*. The walls around you have that organic swirled look of formation rock, but they're wrong, somehow. Too smooth in places. Too regular. As if the limestone itself had intentions.

---

The warmth hits you next. Humid and close, like pressing your face into a cupped handful of your own breath. The air hangs thick and reluctant, saturated with moisture that doesn't quite fall but doesn't quite not-fall either. You feel it settling on your skin, your hair, the fabric of your clothes—a perpetual dampness that seeps rather than pours. This is a place where nothing ever truly dries.

There are pools scattered throughout the cavern system—some no bigger than puddles, others extending into dark lakes whose far shores you cannot see. But the water doesn't move like surface water. It pulses. When that heartbeat-thrum reaches its peak, the pools shiver and ripple. At its trough, they settle into glassy stillness. And around the edges of each pool, where water meets stone, you notice something odd: the rock is soft. Wet-clay soft. Shaping itself. Unshaping. Reshaping.

This is a place still becoming. Still deciding what it wants to be.

---

But the rock and water are merely the stage.

The life here—that's what overwhelms.

They move across the ceilings in living carpets—pale, segmented things the size of your thumb, each with too many legs and a blind determination that makes them scrabble over each other in waves. They don't fight. There's no competition, no predator-prey drama. They simply *move*, thousands of creatures following some impulse you cannot perceive, and their combined mass shifts like a single organism, like the skin of something massive breathing just below the surface of the world.

On the walls, clustered in the warmest pockets of air near the ceiling, larger creatures hang—leathery sacs of membrane and moisture, each one dripping slow condensation onto the stone below. They breathe. You can watch them expand and contract. Some have already split open, revealing glistening interiors that pulse with their own weak bioluminescence, pale green and ghostly blue. Others remain sealed, quivering occasionally as something inside shifts position.

The floor is worse. Or better, depending on your tolerance for proximity to so much living matter. The floor is *moving*. Not the stone itself, but a layer of life above it—beetles, larvae, worms of varieties you have no names for, all writhing past each other in rivers of motion. They flow around your boots unbothered, treating you as just another obstacle in a landscape full of obstacles, another stone, another formation.

They don't bite. They don't swarm toward you or away from you. You are irrelevant to them. That, somehow, is more unsettling than if they saw you as food.

---

The noise they generate is staggering. Each creature contributes its own small sound—the click of chitin on stone, the wet whisper of soft bodies, the high thin whistling of air through spiracles and breathing tubes—and together it becomes a roar that you have to consciously filter, consciously decide to hear as background rather than foreground, or else you cannot think.

When you try to speak, your voice sounds small. Swallowed. The cavern system has no echo because the life absorbs it, eats it, renders your words dead before they travel more than a few feet. This is not a place for human speech.

The light comes from an opening far above—unseen from where you stand, but present, sending a diffuse golden illumination filtering down through the colonies hanging from the ceiling. The light passes through translucent bodies and thin membranes, taking on strange colors, casting shadows that move independently of the light source as living things shift position between you and the sun. The effect is underwater, submarine, as if you're standing at the bottom of an ocean of air rather than water.

---

You find the structures on your third hour of wandering. They're easy to miss at first—low walls of stacked stone, half-dissolved by the same mineral-rich water that built the formations. Pits dug into the soft rock and lined with harder stone, their purposes obscure. A series of marks scratched into a boulder, repeating, deliberate—but worn almost smooth by the passage of damp air and time.

Someone lived here. Or something did.

The technology was primitive. Hand-tools, maybe, or tools made from the bodies of the dead—hollowed carapaces used as vessels, bone splinters worked into picks and scrapers. You find what might be a knife, fashioned from a mandible longer than your forearm, its edge worn dull from use. You find fragments that might be containers, might be art, might be nothing at all—stone bowls with strange residue, chips of rock arranged in patterns that might be deliberate or might be the work of water and time.

The builders are gone. Whatever they were, whatever they made, has been subsumed back into the life around it. The colonies don't acknowledge the ruins. They flow over and around and through them, wearing them down with the slow erosion of ten thousand passing bodies per day, digesting them molecule by molecule back into the limestone from which they came.

---

What disturbs you most—what keeps pulling at the edge of your attention—is the sense that this place is *accelerating*.

At first you think it's imagination, the overwhelming presence of so much life making you perceive time differently. But you watch, and you confirm: the pools are rising. The soft stone at their edges has grown measurably higher since you arrived. New formations appear, wet and raw, where there were none before—nubs of calcite rising from the floor like mushrooms after rain.

And the creatures are changing position. The colonies on the ceiling shift in waves, and when they pass, they leave behind more of those translucent hanging sacs, more eggs or pupae or whatever they are, adhering to the stone. The floor moves faster, the rivers of bodies splitting and rejoining in new configurations. The heartbeat-pulse grows louder, then quieter, then louder again—not regular after all, but *building*, climbing toward some peak you cannot predict.

This place is hatching. Not the creatures—the place itself. The rock, the water, the air, the life. All of it rising toward some event, some transformation, some moment when what is emerging will finally emerge.

You should leave. You should have left hours ago. But the noise is hypnotic, and the warmth is pleasant against your skin, and the motion has a pattern you could almost understand if you just watched a little longer, just stayed to see—

---

The water rises another inch. The ceiling colonies shift in a wave that moves away from you, then toward you, then away again, breathing. Something cracks somewhere in the dark—a stone splitting, a formation reaching its limit, a shell beginning to open.

The sound swells. You cannot tell anymore if it's coming from the creatures or from somewhere deeper, somewhere below the rock, below the water, below the reach of your senses.

The cavern system extends in all directions, cave opening into cave, gallery into gallery, an underground world of warmth and wet and waiting. You are small inside it. You are temporary. You are, in the end, just another element of the landscape, passing through, and the life will flow around you and over you and past you until you are gone, and then it will flow through the space where you were, and the place will continue to become whatever it is becoming, with or without your witness.

The pulse quickens.

The air grows thicker.

Something is about to happen.
