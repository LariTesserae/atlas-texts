# The Weeping Cirque

The sound finds you first—a constant, layered roar that seems to rise from everywhere at once. Water. Not a single waterfall but dozens, perhaps hundreds, sheeting down the inner walls of a natural amphitheater carved from dark granite. The cirque opens northeast, and morning light spills across its far rim, catching the spray in shifting curtains of mist.

**Terrain and Erosion**

You stand on a ledge perhaps forty meters wide—the only ground approaching flatness in this vertical world. The stone beneath your feet is ancient, its edges softened by millennia of freeze-thaw cycles and the relentless work of water. Scratches and grooves trace patterns across the surface, the signature of the glacier that once ground through here and then retreated, leaving this hollow behind. The rock itself is a dark, blue-tinged gray, shot through with quartz veins that catch the light.

The walls rise on three sides, forming a near-circle. They're not smooth—they bulge and recession, with ribs of harder stone standing proud where softer material has worn away. Ledges and shallow caves pocket the surface. The erosion here is in balance; the cirque has found its shape and holds it, changing so slowly that a human lifespan captures only a snapshot.

And everywhere, the water. It seeps from fissures, sheets across smooth faces, drips from overhangs, pools in depressions and overflows. The rock is permanently dark with moisture. In the shadows, you can see the shine of it reflecting light from the open sky.

**Vegetation**

Life has claimed this place. Moss covers every surface not actively being scoured by falling water—a deep, cushiony carpet of greens so dark they border on black, thriving in the perpetual damp. Ferns sprout from cracks in the vertical walls, their fronds dripping, forming hanging gardens that sway slightly in the breeze created by falling water.

In the corners where debris has accumulated, the moss gives way to actual soil—thin, dark, rich stuff—and here, small trees have taken root. Mountain ash, their trunks twisted and stunted by the cold and the altitude, cling to ledges with root systems that run meters along the rock face before finding purchase. Their leaves have turned russet and gold; it's early autumn here. Berries hang heavy and bright.

Ground-level, the ledge supports a dense band of sedges and reeds along its outer edge, where water pools before spilling over into the valley far below. Behind you, where the cliff forms a shallow overhang, the ground is bare rock—the dripping ceiling prevents anything from growing.

**Scale and Density**

The cirque is intimate, despite its height. You could walk its perimeter in fifteen minutes. The ledge you stand on is the only significant flat ground; the rest is walls, steep slopes, and the accumulated jumble of fallen boulders at the base of the cliffs. There's something amphitheatrical about it, a sense of enclosure, a space with boundaries.

And yet—empty. The vastness is above. The sky opens overhead, a dome of pale blue streaked with clouds moving fast at altitude. The air is thin; you're high enough that breathing takes conscious effort, high enough that the clouds sometimes sink below the rim and mist rolls through the cirque like a tide. The space between presences—the distances between trees, between the ledges, between you and the far wall—feels magnified by the clarity of the light and the thinness of the air.

**Temperature and Atmosphere**

Cold. Not freezing, but close—the kind of cold that makes you aware of your skin, that stiffens your fingers and forces you to move. Your breath fogs. The moisture in the air condenses on every surface, including you, beading on your clothes and running down in rivulets. The spray from the falls creates a perpetual mist, and where the sunlight catches it just right, small rainbows shimmer in the air before dissipating.

There's a smell to this place: green and growing, wet stone, something mineral, the faint sweetness of rotting leaves. The air is thick with water vapor, and the temperature fluctuates wildly between sun and shade. Standing in direct light feels almost warm; step into shadow and the cold wraps around you instantly.

**Built Presence**

Someone has been here before.

Against the back wall, beneath the overhang where the water can't reach, a rough shelter has been built. Low walls of stacked stone, fitted together without mortar, form a partial enclosure—more windbreak than building. The work is old and primitive; the stones are weathered, the joints between them thick with moss. Whoever built this used local material, selecting flat rocks from the scree slopes and fitting them by hand.

Inside, a fire ring of blackened stones holds a pile of ash long since washed into mud. A few branches remain—firewood that was never burned. A flat stone near the entrance is worn smooth, possibly a seat or work surface. There are no tools here now, no artifacts—just the bones of presence, the shape of someone's stay.

The structure faces east, toward the rising sun and away from the prevailing wind. It's a thoughtful placement. Someone knew how to read this landscape.

**Fauna**

The noise should scare everything away. Instead, the cirque teems.

You notice the birds first—they're impossible to miss. A flock of small, dark finches works through the moss, pulling up insects, chittering to each other in calls that barely register beneath the roar of the water. They seem unafraid; one lands a meter from your foot and regards you with a bright black eye before resuming its foraging.

Higher up, on the cliffs, you spot movement: a creature climbing the vertical face with impossible ease. A goat, you think—some kind of wild goat or mountain ungulate, its coat the same color as the wet stone. It's stripping bark from a mountain ash growing from a crack, methodical, unconcerned. A second animal, smaller, probably a kid, picks its way along a ledge above.

Insects swarm the wet surfaces—flies, midges, something long-legged and delicate that skates across the surface of pooled water. A spider web spans the gap between two reeds, its threads heavy with droplets, the architect hiding somewhere in the rolled leaf at its edge.

You hear the animals before you see some of them. The thunder of the water masks footfalls but not the calls: a sharp bark from somewhere on the opposite wall (the goat), a whistled alarm call (a marmot, you think, though you can't locate it), the rustle of wings as a hawk circles far overhead, riding thermals above the rim.

And there are predators here. You find evidence: scattered fur and a few bones near the back of the shelter, the remains of something small and furred. Owl pellets, you realize, regurgitated near where the bird must roost. The cirque is a system—prey animals come for the water, the shelter, the vegetation; predators follow.

**Sound**

The water dominates, but it's not a monolith. The falls sing in registers—the high thin spray of the smallest seeps, the bass rumble of the main sheets, the varying tones as water hits different surfaces. When wind shifts, the sound shifts with it. When a gust catches the spray, the noise momentarily swells.

Against this backdrop, other sounds emerge and submerge. Bird calls, muffled by the roar. The clatter of hooves on stone as the goats move. The groan and creak of trees shifting in the wind. Occasionally, a rock fall somewhere in the cirche—a distant clatter of stones bouncing down slopes, swallowed by the greater noise.

**Dynamic Processes**

Nothing is static.

You watch clouds form and dissolve against the rim of the cirque—moist air rising, cooling, condensing into mist, then evaporating again as it encounters sunlight. The falls pulse slightly with some invisible rhythm; perhaps snowmelt feeds them, perhaps underground reservoirs fluctuate with pressure.

A butterfly—you don't expect it, but there it is, orange and black, settling on a fern frond, opening and closing its wings to warm itself in a patch of sun. It won't live long; the cold will claim it soon, or a bird will. But for now, it's here.

The finch flock moves as a unit, flowing across the moss like water themselves, always in communication, always aware of each other's positions. They're feeding on something—seeds, perhaps, or the insects that live in the moss layer.

The goats are working their way up the cliff face, the kid learning the routes, following its mother's path exactly. They'll spend the day moving across terrain that looks impassable, finding purchase on invisible ledges, stripping bark and leaves from the hanging gardens.

And the water never stops. It fell as snow somewhere high, melted, filtered through rock, emerged here as springs and seeps, and will cascade down to the valley, joining streams, rivers, eventually the sea. Then rise again as vapor, fall again as snow. The cirque is a moment in that cycle, a place where water pauses, gathers, and releases.

**The Weirdness**

There's something odd here. You realize it gradually.

The sound should be louder. Standing at the base of a waterfall this size, you should feel it in your chest, your bones. But the cirche has acoustics that disperse the roar, spread it out, soften it. The noise is all-encompassing but not painful. It feels intentional, somehow—the shape of the walls, the angle of the surfaces, the way the overhangs redirect sound upward rather than bouncing it back.

And the light. The mist catches it, and when you look at the spray from certain angles, you see not one rainbow but three: a primary, a secondary, and a faint third arc barely visible, colors reversed. Supernumerary rainbows, rare, formed by the precise size of the water droplets in the spray. The droplets here are unusually uniform.

The goat on the cliff stops eating and looks at you. It holds your gaze for a long moment, and you have the strange certainty that it's not just seeing you but recognizing you—not as an individual but as a category: *person*. You've been categorized.

And the shelter. When you looked closely at the stones, you saw marks—not decoration, not exactly, but the beginning of something. Grooves and notches worked into certain stones, always the ones at eye level. Some kind of tally? A record? Or the absent-minded working of hands with nothing else to do during long cold nights.

**What This Place Asks of You**

The Weeping Cirque doesn't need you. It was here before you arrived and will be here after you leave. The water will fall, the moss will grow, the goats will climb, the birds will feed. Your presence is incidental.

But it offers you something. A place out of the wind. A dry seat. A view of light through water. The company of living things that have no particular investment in your existence but don't mind it either.

The shelter is big enough for one. The fire ring is ready for a spark.

You could stay awhile.
