# The Drowned Lantern-Yard

The cold hits first—a dense, wet cold that settles into the bones rather than biting at the skin. This is a cold that carries water with it, that presses moisture into everything it touches until stone and flesh alike run with slow, steady seepage.

---

The yard sits high, though the scale is intimate—cathedral-sized, yes, but a cathedral compressed, denser than any holy space ought to be. The walls rise close on every side, ancient and deeply weathered, their surfaces carved into channels and gutters by centuries of water moving through them. Not decorative—functional. Every surface here serves the water in some way. The rock itself seems to weep, moisture welling from within, tracing paths down the grooved faces, collecting in carved basins that overflow into carved channels that overflow into other basins below.

The light is enormous. It floods everything—reflected off wet surfaces, off the pale sky that presses down from above, off water that pools in every depression and runs in slow sheets down every vertical surface. The brightness is diffuse, sourceless, coming from everywhere at once until shadows themselves seem thin and spectral. On clear days—and most days here are at least partly clear, the altitude sees to that—the yard becomes almost painful to look at directly, a kaleidoscope of reflected gleam and glittering runoff.

Vegetation is scarce. A few pale lichens cling to the upper walls where the water flows lightest. Here and there, a tough, thread-leaved plant sprouts from cracks, its roots reaching deep into the stone to find the water table that sits always just below the surface. These plants are stunted, colorless things—white and grey and pale yellow, never green. Photosynthesis here runs on different chemistry, or perhaps these are parasites, drawing from the stone itself.

The yard is small enough to hold in a single glance, yet dense with presence. Every surface has been worked. The walls bear carvings—faces, perhaps, or symbols, now so weathered their original forms are suggested more than shown. The floor is a complex arrangement of pools and channels, shallow steps descending from one level to the next, each pool feeding those below in an endless slow cascade. The water moves perpetually but quietly—no roar of falls here, only the softest trickle and splash, the sound of rain that isn't rain, of weeping that never ends.

---

The structures are primitive in technology but sophisticated in understanding. Whoever built this place—and it was built, carved from living rock over generations—knew water the way a clockmaker knows gears. The channels align precisely. The basins overflow at exactly the right point. The whole yard is a hydraulic machine, powered by nothing but gravity and the relentless seep from the stone itself. Hand tools did this work. Patience did this work. Time did this work.

The meaning of the place is harder to read. A temple? Perhaps—certain faces carved into the walls seem to watch over particular pools. A cistern? No—too deliberate, too shaped. A garden? The absence of plants argues against it. Perhaps the yard served all these purposes and none. Perhaps its purpose was simply to be—to channel and honor and contain the water that moves so constantly through this high, cold place.

---

And yet for all this quiet purpose, the yard teems.

Not with people. The builders are long gone, or perhaps they never stayed—only came, did their work, departed. The yard has stood in this weathered equilibrium for longer than memory. But it fills with life nonetheless.

The water creatures came first—small blind things, white and translucent, that swim in the deeper pools and crawl along the channel bottoms on six delicate legs. Then came the insects, larvae swimming in the shallows, adults skating across the surface tension, their legs dimpling the water without breaking through. Then came the creatures that eat the insects—long-legged hunters with needle mouths, walking the channels with careful steps, never wet beyond their feet. Then came the creatures that eat *them*—scaled and watchful, basking on the warm stones, plumping themselves in the brightest light.

The yard is an ecosystem. A complete one, contained and self-sustaining. The water brings nutrients. The creatures cycle them. The stone provides home. And the light—the overwhelming, sourceless light—powers everything through channels no human science quite understands.

The animals have their routines. In the morning, when the light first floods the yard, the crawlers retreat to the deepest channels. The insects swarm instead, laying eggs on the wet stone, feeding on the algae-film that grows wherever water moves slow. The hunters work the edges, plucking insects from the air with needle-mouths, eating on the wing. By midday, the yard is at its busiest—every pool occupied, every channel hosting its procession of tiny lives. By evening, the larger predators emerge, and the yard grows quiet again as smaller creatures hide in the depths.

---

But the yard is not merely busy. It is *turbulent*—in a way that the word doesn't quite capture.

The turbulence is not wind or weather. The air rarely moves here; the altitude is too high for trees that would block the wind, yet the yard sits in a natural bowl that shelters it from most gusts. The turbulence is something else. The water itself shifts—not in volume, the flow is constant, but in *direction*. A channel that ran clear yesterday might run milky today. A pool that reflected perfectly might suddenly darken, fill with sediment, clear again within hours. The creatures that fill the yard change too—not just in activity but in kind. Yesterday's larvae might be today's hunters. Today's prey might be tomorrow's predators. The blind white swimmers in the deep pools—some days there are dozens, some days none at all, and no one who has studied them can say where they go or where they come from.

And the carvings change.

This is the strangest part, the part that visitors struggle to accept. The faces on the walls—the symbols, the patterns—they are not static. Weather alone could not account for the changes. The faces are the same faces, in the same positions, but their expressions shift. Day to day. Hour to hour. A face that seemed serene in morning light might appear sorrowful by afternoon, troubled by evening, blank by night. Some witnesses swear the faces move—turn slightly, eyes tracking something beyond the yard's walls—but these reports are unreliable, contradicted by other witnesses who saw nothing of the kind.

What is certain is this: photographs taken hours apart show differences. Not large ones. Subtle. A line deepening here, a curve smoothing there. A mouth that seemed closed now slightly open. An eye that looked forward now looking down.

The turbulence runs through everything. The water. The life. The stone itself.

---

The silence of the place makes the strangeness more acute. Sound here is muffled, absorbed by water and stone, swallowed by the constant soft movement. A creature diving into a pool makes barely a ripple of sound. A rock falling from the yard's edge lands with a sound like a whisper. Voices carry poorly here—words seem to dissolve before reaching the listener's ear, leaving only tone and gesture.

But the silence is not empty. Listening carefully, attuning the ear, one begins to hear the yard's perpetual voice: the trickling of a thousand tiny streams, the soft splash of a hundred tiny overflows, the ripple of insects on water, the almost-inaudible hum of creatures moving through their endless cycles. The yard is not quiet, exactly. It is *hushed*—as if all its activity occurs beneath a blanket of listening, as if everything here is trying not to disturb something else, something larger, something that might be paying attention.

---

What rules govern this place?

The water always flows. This is the first rule. Even in the coldest cold—when the pools grow ice-skins and the channels run slush-thick—the water never stops. It moves slower, crystallizes partially, but it moves. The day it stops moving will be the day the yard dies.

The light always comes. This is the second rule. Even when clouds mass above and the pale sky goes grey, the yard stays bright—dimmer, yes, but somehow still illuminated, as if the light has pooled in the stone and water and refuses to drain. The creatures here do not tolerate true darkness. The one time a traveler arrived with a covered lantern—seeking to observe the yard at night—the creatures fled to their deepest channels and did not emerge until the lantern was doused and natural light returned.

The turbulence always shifts. This is the third rule. Nothing here stays fixed. The patterns repeat, but never identically. The water finds new paths. The creatures cycle through new forms. The faces on the wall deepen their expressions or smooth them away. The yard is alive in a way that stone should not be alive, and its life follows rhythms no observer has successfully mapped.

Do not touch the water. This is the fourth rule—the only one that seems to apply to visitors, the only one that seems to carry danger. The water is cold—colder than it should be, colder than the air, colder than the stone. Touching it numbs instantly. Leaving hand or foot immersed too long (and "too long" varies—minutes for some, seconds for others) leads to a spreading numbness that does not stop at the skin. Travelers who have lingered here speak of fellow visitors who touched the water and lost feeling in entire limbs, feeling that never fully returned. Some whisper of worse—of visitors who touched the water and seemed to dissolve into it, their forms flowing away, their substance joining the endless cycle.

These stories may be exaggerations. The yard attracts legend like it attracts moisture. But the numbness is real, and caution is warranted.

---

What does it feel like to stand in the yard?

Cold. Wet. Bright. The cold presses in from all sides; the wet rises from below and descends from above; the bright fills every space until closing the eyes makes no difference. The yard feels *watched*—not by the carven faces, exactly, but by something behind them, something that uses them as windows. It feels *full*—every pool, every channel, every surface occupied by some small life going about its business, indifferent to human presence. It feels *old*—the weathering speaks to ages, to patience, to processes that were ancient before the first traveler found this place.

And it feels *suspended*. The turbulence notwithstanding—or perhaps because of it—the yard exists outside normal time. Hours pass here like minutes, or minutes like hours. Visitors lose track of duration. The sun seems to hang in the sky, or to race across it. The creatures cycle through their routines at speeds that vary unpredictably, generations living and dying in the space of an afternoon or a single afternoon stretching across a lifetime.

Some visitors report leaving the yard to find that years have passed elsewhere. Others leave to find that no time has passed at all. Most leave to find that some moderate interval has elapsed—an hour or two, perhaps, matching their subjective sense. But the exceptions are common enough to be concerning, and no one has adequately explained them.

---

The yard has no name in any language that visitors speak. The builders left no records, no inscriptions that can be read, no symbols that map to known scripts. The faces on the walls might once have been gods or ancestors or guardians or simply decoration. Now they are only themselves—watching, shifting, weathering in their slow eternal way.

The yard is a room. A grove. A single feature bounded by ancient walls and open sky. It is a hydraulic machine that runs on gravity and patience. It is an ecosystem complete unto itself. It is a riddle with no answer, or perhaps with an answer that the riddle itself has forgotten.

The water flows. The light pours down. The creatures swim and crawl and hunt and breed and die. The faces watch with expressions that no one quite agrees on. The cold settles into everything. The wet rises and falls and rises again.

The yard endures.
