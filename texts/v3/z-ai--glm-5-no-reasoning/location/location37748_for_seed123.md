# The intercyst

You enter through a maintenance hatch that should not be where it is — set into the footpath of an ordinary suburban street, behind a hedge that people walk past daily without seeing it. The hatch is warm to the touch. It opens with a sigh of damp air that smells of copper and wet chalk. Below: a steel ladder, twelve rungs, each slightly different in gauge and manufacture, as though the ladder were assembled from fragments of other ladders. You descend. The street sounds stop by the fourth rung. By the eighth, the walls close in so tightly your shoulders brush both sides. By the twelfth, you are there.

## The space

The intercyst is a chamber approximately the size of a living room — perhaps four meters across at its widest, six meters long, and never quite high enough to stand fully upright. You are always slightly stooped. This is the first rule of the place: you will not be comfortable here. It was not made for your comfort or for anyone's. It was made, or grew, or was left behind — the distinction is not clear — and it simply *is*, compressed into a gap between things that were never meant to have a gap between them.

The walls are the most immediate feature because they are *everywhere*. This is a place with no distance. Everything is close. The walls are a composite: layers of reddish clay, compacted and still damp, interleaved with sheets of something that resembles corroded iron but is warm and gives very slightly under pressure, like hard rubber. Between these layers, and bulging outward in places like blisters, are deposits of a pale, chalky mineral that sweats moisture. The condensation is constant. You can see it — not dripping, but forming in fine films on every surface, collecting into beads that merge and run in unpredictable paths down the warped stratigraphy. The air is warm and holds that moisture like a held breath. Not hot. Warm, the way a room is warm when something has recently left it or something alive is nearby, producing heat as a byproduct of being alive.

There is no sky. Above, the ceiling is uneven, vaulted slightly, studded with what appear to be roots — pale, thick, non-woody, more like the roots of aquatic plants than trees. They descend from some unseen layer above, penetrate the ceiling material, and hang into the space like organ pipes, some reaching a meter downward, some barely poking through. Their surfaces are slick with condensation and faintly luminescent — a cold greenish-white, not enough to illuminate fully but enough to see by, enough to make the chamber exist in a perpetual medical twilight. Normal light, by the place's own standards. This is what it considers adequate. Your eyes adjust. You can see everything, but nothing looks quite right.

## The machinery

Set into the far wall — embedded, actually, partially extruded from the composite as though the wall grew around it rather than being built to hold it — is a machine. It is not large. It is roughly the size of a washing machine, but shaped like nothing manufactured by a process you can identify. It has the quality of something *found* rather than *made*, and yet it is clearly mechanical. There is a housing of dark, oxidized metal. There are pipes — four of them, copper, leading outward from its core into the wall material in different directions, disappearing into the composite. There is a flywheel, partially exposed, turning slowly. Very slowly. Perhaps one revolution every eight seconds, with a faint, dry clicking at a specific point in its rotation — the only reliable sound in the place. Click. Pause. Pause. Pause. Click.

The machine's function is not obvious. It appears to be a pump, or a compressor, or possibly a slow mechanical heart, because the four copper pipes pulse warm fluid — not water, exactly; thinner, slightly viscous, carrying a faint smell of iron and saponin — outward into the wall material. Where the pipes terminate, the wall is damper. The mineral deposits are thicker. The root systems are denser. The machine feeds something. Or the machine *is* something, and the feeding is autonomic, not purposeful.

There are other artifacts. A second machine, smaller, half-buried in the floor, only its top visible — a brass plate with two gauge faces, both needles trembling at positions that correspond to no marked scale. A section of pipe, separate from the machine, running along one wall for roughly a meter before terminating in a valve that is welded shut. A cluster of ceramic insulators on the ceiling, holding wires that carry a faint current — you can feel it if you bring your hand close, a warmth that is not thermal, a pressure in the fingertips. The current is weak, intermittent, and seems to follow no cycle. It is there, and then it is not, and then it is again. The wires lead into the root mass and do not emerge.

## The vegetation

The roots are the dominant biological presence, but they are not the only one. Growing from the seams between wall layers, in the narrow fissures where the clay has separated from the iron-like material, there are plants — or something plant-like. They are small, pale, and do not photosynthesize. They have no chlorophyll. Their leaves, if they can be called leaves, are translucent and flat, pressing against the wall surfaces like specimens mounted on glass. They draw moisture from the condensation and something else — nutrients, perhaps, dissolved from the mineral deposits by the fluid the machine pumps. There are perhaps forty or fifty of these plants visible in the chamber, each no larger than a human hand, each growing in isolation, separated from the nearest neighbor by a handspan of bare wall. Sparse, in the way that a single fern in a crack is sparse, but here, in this compressed space, they feel crowded. Everything feels crowded. There are too many things in too small a space, even though the number of things is not high. The density is not abundance. It is *compression*. Everything pressed against everything.

There is also a moss, or a moss-like growth, in patches where moisture collects in the depressions of the floor. It is dark green, almost black in the dim light, and it is warm. You notice this when you kneel. The floor is warm. The moss is warmer. It is metabolizing something rapidly enough to produce measurable heat. When you touch it, it has a texture like wet felt and gives off a faint smell — sweet, organic, like overripe fruit. It covers perhaps a third of the floor surface, concentrated near where the copper pipes terminate in the wall.

## The fauna

This is where the place becomes difficult.

The chamber is full of animals. Not large animals. Not animals that take up space in the way you think of animals taking up space. But animals, present, numerous, and engaged in the business of being alive with an intensity that makes the small room feel occupied in a way that a crowd of humans would not.

On the roots, moving slowly along their surfaces, there are insects. They are flat, pale, elongated — something between a silverfish and a centipede, but with too many legs, even for a centipede, and a body that appears segmented in a way that does not follow bilateral symmetry. They are not symmetrical. One side has more legs than the other. They do not seem affected by this. They move with a fluid, rippling gait, feeding on the moisture and the biofilm on the root surfaces. There are dozens of them. In the dim light, their pale bodies catch the faint luminescence and glow faintly, so the roots appear to be covered in slow-moving light.

In the moss, there are worms. Thick, dark, shorter than earthworms and slower, moving through the moss like fingers through clay. They do not surface. You see them only as moving distortions in the dark growth, ridges that form and subside. They are feeding. The moss is feeding them, or they are feeding the moss, or both. The relationship is not clear.

And in the air — in the warm, damp, still air of the chamber — there are moths. Or things like moths. They are small, with wings perhaps two centimeters across, and they are attracted to the luminescent roots. They settle on the roots, walk along them with a strange, deliberate gait, and then fly to another. Their wings make almost no sound. A faint papery rustle, barely audible. In the quiet of the chamber — the deep, heavy quiet that sits on your ears like pressure — the moths are the loudest thing, and they are almost silent. They are numerous. Perhaps a hundred. They do not fly in any pattern. They move from root to root, from wall to ceiling, in trajectories that seem purposeful but unfathomable. Sometimes they settle on you. Their feet are cold. Their wings are warm. They do not stay long.

There is one more creature, and it is the one that changes everything.

In the far corner of the chamber, where the floor meets the wall and the moss is thickest and the mineral deposits bulge outward most prominently, there is a depression — a hollow, roughly bowl-shaped, perhaps thirty centimeters across, filled with a shallow pool of the fluid the machine pumps. It is warm, slightly iridescent, and in it, something is living.

It is not a fish. It is not a larva. It is not a salamander. It is approximately fifteen centimeters long, dark, smooth-skinned, and it moves in the shallow pool with a slow, undulating motion that suggests it is not swimming but *breathing* — that the pool is its lung, and the motion is respiration, and it is consuming the fluid itself as a medium rather than swimming through it. It has no visible eyes. It has a mouth, or a mouth-like opening, on its underside, and it presses this against the floor of the pool periodically, drawing something from the mineral substrate below. It has been doing this for a long time. The hollow is worn smooth where it feeds.

You realize, with a discomfort that takes a moment to identify, that the chamber is not a chamber. It is a *body*. Or a component of a body. The machine is an organ. The roots are a vascular system. The mineral deposits are bone or shell or some structural tissue. The moss is a mucous membrane. The insects are immune cells, or flora, or parasites — the distinction is academic. The fluid is blood. The thing in the pool is — you don't know what it is. A nucleus. A parasite. An embryo. A resident.

The chamber is alive, and you are inside it.

## The rules

The place operates on principles you can observe but not fully parse. The machine cycles: it pumps fluid for perhaps ten minutes, then pauses for three, then resumes. During the pause, the condensation slows. The roots dim slightly. The insects stop moving. The moths settle. The thing in the pool presses itself flat against the hollow's floor and does not move. Then the machine clicks — that single, dry click — and resumes, and everything comes back. The light returns. The moisture returns. The fauna resumes their circuits. This is the heartbeat. This is the rhythm of the place. You learn it within twenty minutes. It becomes the ground against which all other time is measured.

The temperature fluctuates with the cycle. During the active phase, the air is warmer — not hot, but noticeably warmer, approaching body temperature. During the pause, it cools slightly, enough that you feel the dampness more acutely, enough that the condensation on the walls feels cold to the touch. You are, in a very literal sense, standing inside a metabolic cycle.

Sound is almost absent. The click of the flywheel. The faint rustle of moths. Occasionally, a soft, wet sound from the pool — the thing shifting position, pressing its mouth against the floor, the fluid displaced by its body. Once, you hear a sound from outside the chamber — from the walls themselves, from the body of whatever this place is embedded within. A low, slow, groaning sound that lasts perhaps four seconds and then does not repeat. Peristalsis. Structural stress. Something being moved through a system larger than this room, somewhere beyond the walls, in the body that contains this body.

There is no weather here, but there is climate — the micro-climate of an enclosed biological space, governed by the machine's cycle and the warmth of the moss and the moisture that seeps through every surface. The air does not move. It is exchanged, slowly, through whatever boundary exists above the ceiling, through the root channels, through the fabric of the place itself. You can feel it: a very slow exhalation from above, a very slow inhalation from below. The chamber breathes, on a timescale so extended you only notice it by attending to the direction of the moisture on the walls — sometimes it runs downward, sometimes it beads and appears to move upward, against gravity, drawn by some capillary action in the ceiling material.

## The built remains

There are human traces here, and they are the most unsettling thing, because they imply that someone found this place before you and did something with it, or to it.

The ladder is human-made. The hatch is human-made. But these are recent. Older traces exist: the ceramic insulators on the ceiling are mounted with bolts of a type manufactured in the mid-twentieth century. The brass plate with the gauges has a serial number stamped into it, partially legible: 4---7-M, and below that, a word that might be "SECTION" or "SECTIONAL." The valve on the separate pipe has a wheel-handle of a type used in industrial plumbing, and it is welded shut with a weld that is amateur — bubbled, uneven, the work of someone who wanted it sealed permanently and quickly. Someone was here. Someone installed electrical components. Someone monitored something with those gauges. Someone sealed that pipe. And then someone left, or was forced to leave, and the place continued without them, grew around their installations, incorporated their artifacts into its own structure the way a tree grows around a fence post.

The wires that carry the faint current are connected to the ceramic insulators, and they lead into the root mass. Someone connected human electrical infrastructure to the biological system. The current is not coming from a human source now — it comes from the place itself, from the roots, from whatever bioelectric process the living system generates. But the wires are human. Someone tried to measure it. Or feed it. Or draw from it. The purpose is gone. The connection remains.

## What it is like to be here

You crouch in the intercyst — the name comes unbidden, from the shape and the feeling, the sense of being inside a small, fluid-filled sac within something larger — and you are aware of several things simultaneously.

First, the intimacy. This is a room-sized space, but it is not a room. It is an interior. It has the quality of being *inside* something rather than *within* something. The walls are not boundaries you can lean against casually. They are surfaces of a living thing. You are conscious of them as you would be conscious of standing close to a large animal — not afraid, exactly, but aware that the space you occupy is not neutral. It is occupied by you, as an intrusion, and it is occupied by everything else, as inhabitants.

Second, the crowding. Despite the small number of distinct organisms, the space feels saturated. Every surface has something on it. Every cubic centimeter of air has a moth in it, or moisture, or warmth, or a faint electrical charge. The roots take up ceiling space. The moss takes up floor space. The insects cover the roots. The worms fill the moss. The thing in the pool occupies the one standing water in the place. There is no empty space. There is no margin. The density is total.

Third, the wrongness. Not danger — the place does not feel dangerous — but wrongness, in the sense that the categories do not hold. This is biology, but it is also geology, and also industrial infrastructure, and also something else that does not have a name. The machine should not be embedded in a living wall. The electrical wires should not be connected to roots. The thing in the pool should not be there. The plants should not be growing without light. The insects should not be asymmetrical. Each individual element is almost comprehensible. Together, they describe a system that operates on rules that overlap with known rules but do not coincide with them. The place is a proof by contradiction: it exists, therefore your understanding is incomplete.

Fourth, and finally, the quiet. The deep, metabolic quiet of a body at rest. Not silence — there is the click, the rustle, the wet sounds — but a quiet that sits beneath those sounds, a quiet that is the absence of wind and sky and distance, the quiet of a place that has never been open to the air, that has always been enclosed, that exists in the same sonic register as the inside of a chest cavity. If you are quiet enough — and you will be, because the place makes you quiet — you can hear something below the other sounds. A low, continuous thrum, felt more than heard, in the bones of your skull and in the chambers of your own chest. It is the place itself, vibrating at the frequency of its own aliveness. It is not a sound the place makes. It is a sound the place *is*.

You will not stay long. The place is not built for staying. It is built for being, and your being is an interruption, however small. You climb the ladder. You push open the hatch. The street sounds return — cars, a dog, a plane — and the air is cool and dry and smells of nothing, and the hedge is just a hedge, and the path is just a path, and the sky is enormous and empty in a way you had never before considered a mercy.

But the warmth stays on your skin for an hour afterward. And that night, in the dark, you hear a click in your own chest — once, at a point in your breathing cycle — and you are not certain it was always there.
