Somewhere between a mountain range and a body, this place breathes.

The elevation announces itself not through thin air—that comes later, higher up—but through the way sound refuses to travel. You speak and the words fall straight down, absorbed by the living floor before they can scatter into echoes. This is the first sign that the physics here have been rewritten.

The substrate is root. Not roots running through soil—there is no soil, there has never been soil—but root as ground, root as stone, root as the fundamental mineral of this constructed world. Everything that looks like earth is actually the buried bodies of ancient stems, compressed by their own descendants into a dense, fibrous peat that holds water like a squeezed sponge. Water: 1.7 means the moisture is constant but not drowning. It weeps. Stand still on the surface and you'll feel dampness rising through your feet, pressure from below. The root-ground exudes. Not a flood but a persistent upward seep, as if the whole plateau is sweating.

The plateau. The shelf. The vast manufactured terrace that shouldn't exist at this height but does, carved or grown into the side of something taller—a spine of green-gray that continues up into the dim haze until the scale defeats the eye. The built parameter, 2.8, means there is nothing here that wasn't made. But made by what, and when? The makers left no tools. They left only this: a landscape of intentional form, weathered now into something that pretends to be natural. Erosion 2.1. The edges have softened. The construction has settled into its own joints, found its balance, accepted the slow creep of time. But it's still construction. You can see it in the way the root-network follows invisible gridlines, the way the drainage channels curve too precisely, the way certain viewpoints open up like designed rooms in the green architecture.

And it is all green. Vegetation 2.9 means total occupation, and total is the word. There is no gap. What isn't root-floor is stem-column, what isn't stem-column is leaf-canopy, what isn't leaf-canopy is flower-vine-fruit-thread-spore-layer, a vertical density of living mass that makes its own weather and eats its own light. But here's the strangeness: the light is wrong. Light: 0.9, dim. This high, this vast, the sky should blaze. Instead, a permanent diffused twilight. The canopy above—fifty meters up, a hundred, impossible to tell—filters and absorbs and transforms. The light that reaches the floor is green-gray, pre-dawn pale, permanent and unchanging. No sun position shifts the shadows. They are fixed, architectural.

Temperature: 2.4, warm. The warmth rises from below, from the composting root-mass, from the living metabolic engine of the whole constructed ecosystem. The air hangs thick and wet and body-temperature, a fever warmth that never breaks into heat but also never cools. It is the temperature of a mouth, an armpit, a wound.

Scale: 2.8. The plateau extends beyond sight in every direction, and then steps down, and extends again, terrace after terrace of this living architecture, a continental ziggurat of designed biology. Weather forms within it, not above it—local humidity cycles, micro-rains that fall from the lower canopy and evaporate before reaching the ground, wind that moves through the stem-forests in systematic pulses, like respiration. The weather is internal. The sky is irrelevant.

And yet: density, 0.5. Empty. The paradox that defines this place. The biological mass is total, but the volume it occupies is so vast that presence feels like absence. The stem-columns are spaced far apart—you can walk between them without touching, without even brushing the hanging root-threads that descend seeking moisture. The canopy is so high it registers as a different zone entirely, a roof-world with its own ecology. Here at the walking level, you could travel for hours and encounter nothing but root-floor and stem-column and that persistent breathing warmth and the silence.

Silence: 0.5. Because nothing here needs to make sound. The plants don't rustle—they move without friction, their surfaces slick with condensate, sliding past each other in the slow weather-pulses without the dry scrape of branch on branch. There are no birds here, no insects, no small mammals. Sound would serve no purpose in a world of total vegetal presence. But the fauna parameter reads 2.0—abundant. So where is it?

It moves in the canopy. It moves in the deep roots. It moves in the interstices of this constructed world where the physics break down.

The weirdness—reality breakdown—manifests in gaps. Not gaps in the vegetation, but gaps in continuity. Walk far enough in any direction and you'll encounter a place where the root-floor simply stops, not at a cliff edge but at a border with something that isn't space, isn't void, isn't anything describable. A fuzziness. A region where the light refuses to resolve and the warmth has no source and the moisture hangs in the air without falling. The makers built this place at the edges of what can exist, and time has worn those edges into fraying. Near these gaps, the stem-columns twist. They grow into shapes that have no botanical justification. They grow into faces, into alphabets unknown, into diagrams of something else, something from wherever the makers came from or went to.

The fauna lives near these gaps.

They are not animals. They are mobile sections of the whole, autonomous pieces of the constructed ecosystem that have achieved independence and, somehow, intention. You see them best at the edges of your vision—limbed shapes that might be stem-columns on the march, might be root-mass that learned to walk, might be something that was never meant to be separate but became so. The tech parameter, 0.6, means the makers left only hand-tools, simple things, bone and wood and fiber—and indeed you find them sometimes, stuck in the root-floor, useful objects that have been consumed and incorporated. But the fauna uses no tools. It doesn't need them. Its body is its tool. Its body is an extension of the architecture.

They watch. This is what makes the silence heavy—not its absence of sound but its density of attention. The plateau knows you are here. The root-floor feels your weight and moisture. The stem-columns register the displacement of air. The fauna—you can see more of them now, standing still, standing patient, waiting at the edge of the gaps and the edge of the canopy and the edge of the light—observe you in the aggregate. One organism in infinite pieces, or infinite organisms in one piece. The breakdown of reality makes categories impossible.

A weather-pulse begins. The air pressure shifts. The condensation on every surface starts to move, not falling but sliding, running, the whole plateau beginning to weep in unison. This happens every few hours—the plateau excretes, purging the accumulated moisture from its metabolic processes. The root-floor grows slick, then puddled, then briefly stream-covered, water emerging from everywhere at once and flowing nowhere in particular, absorbed back into the system as fast as it emerges. For ten minutes, there is sound: a million tiny trickles, a whisper that fills all the space. The fauna turns its attention to the water, or becomes water, or opens something at the top of its form to receive it.

Then the pulse ends. The water sinks. The silence returns. The light remains unchanged.

You are not supposed to be here. The makers are gone, have been gone for whatever time scale this place operates on. But the plateau still functions. It still receives. It still incorporates.

And it is warm, and empty, and waiting.
