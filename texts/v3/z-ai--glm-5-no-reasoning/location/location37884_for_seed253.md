# The Old Garden

The first thing is the sound. Before anything else — before the dark, before the cold, before the sense of how far down this place goes — there is the sound. A low, continuous roar that is not violent but is everywhere, that lives in the chest more than the ears. Water moving through living architecture. Not falling, not crashing. Pushing. The entire volume of an ocean forced through channels it did not carve but was given, and the channels are singing with it, vibrating with it, and have been vibrating with it for so long that the vibration is no longer separate from the structure. It is the structure. The sound has become the wall.

---

Everything here is made. That is the strangeness and the ordinariness of it. There is no rock. No sediment. No bare mineral anywhere. The substrate is entirely constructed — but constructed the way a coral reef is constructed, the way a forest builds its own floor from generations of its own fallen matter. Everything is made, and nothing was made by hands.

The walls of the place — if they can be called walls — rise in great recurved arches, fluted and ridged, the color of old bone seen through thirty meters of water. They are alive. Or they were alive. They are the lingering architecture of something that grew and grew and then stopped growing not by dying but by finishing. By reaching the shape it was meant to reach. Massive trunk-structures, wider at the base than anything built by people, taper as they rise — and they rise far, rising past the range where light could reach, past the range where the word "height" still means anything, into a darkness above that is so total it functions as a ceiling. As a sky. A black sky that is also a wall, also a plant, also a made thing.

Between these trunks — these pillars — these living-structure-columns — the water moves. It is cold. Not biting, not brutal. The cold of deep places that have been deep for longer than warm things can remember. Eight, nine degrees. The kind of cold that settles into the body slowly and does not leave. The water carries a faint luminescence — not its own, but borrowed, secondhand, the residual glow of biological processes happening in surfaces and films far away, their light diffused through the medium until the water itself seems to hold a gray-blue sort of visibility that is not light and not darkness but something between. A permanent dusk. A dusk that has no evening before it and no morning after.

---

The vegetation is everything, but it is not what you would expect. There are no leaves. The concept of a leaf — a flat surface held out to catch photons from a star — has no meaning here. No light reaches this place from any sun. Instead, the plant-matter has organized itself into forms that make sense for a world of moving water and manufactured energy. Great sheets of translucent membrane, folded and refolded like origami practiced by something that had millions of years to get it right, stretch between the pillars. They are not green. They are the color of the water itself, gray-blue, almost clear, and through them you can see the faint pulse of internal fluid — dark fluid, moving slowly, carrying something that is not sap and not blood but serves the purposes of both. These sheets filter the water. They extract. They are the digestive system of the entire structure, spread out over hectares of vertical surface area.

And the floor. The floor is where the age is most visible. Not because it looks old — it doesn't look decayed, doesn't look ruined — but because it looks *finished*. The floor is a dense, continuous mat of root-like structures, woven so tightly and so completely that there are no gaps. No soil beneath. No bedrock. Just layer after layer of woven living material, each layer older than the one above, compressing slowly under its own weight into something that is halfway between wood and stone. It has been settling for an almost inconceivable amount of time. It has settled into its final form. It will not settle further. It has reached the point where settling is no longer something that happens — where the structure has found its equilibrium and will hold it.

Nothing changes here. That is the rule of the place. The water flows at the same rate it has flowed for — how long? There is no erosion to measure, because the channels were grown, not cut, and they do not widen. The membranes pulse at the same frequency. The cold stays the same. The faint light stays the same. The sound stays the same. If you came here tomorrow, it would be identical. If you came here in ten thousand years, it would be identical. The system is not running down. It is not building up. It has arrived. It is the end state of something that took an enormous amount of time to reach its end state, and now it simply *is*.

---

The fauna move through this like a current through a circuit. They are numerous, and they are loud, and their loudness is part of the place's loudness — clicks and hums and thrumming pulses that join the water-sound and become part of it, become indistinguishable from it.

They are not fish. Not exactly. They are long, ribbon-like things, some of them, pale and translucent, moving with undulations that send ripples of light through their own bodies — bioluminescent patterns that flare and fade in sequences that might be language or might be reflex. There is no way to tell. They do not interact with the architecture in any visible way. They do not eat it. They do not shelter in it. They move *through* it, the way wind moves through a forest — present, numerous, active, but fundamentally separate from the structure they pass through. They have their own business. Their business is not with the garden.

Others are larger. Round-bodied, slow, with surfaces that are covered in some kind of filament or fiber that drifts behind them like a comet's tail. These ones make a sound — a deep, resonant tone, below the range of most hearing, felt more than heard, a sound that seems to come from inside their bodies the way a bell's sound comes from inside the bell. They are not singing. They are not communicating. The sound is mechanical, structural — something their bodies do, the way a heartbeat is something a body does. But it is loud, and there are many of them, and together they create a bass note that underlies everything else, that makes the water feel heavy, that makes the chest feel full.

And smaller things. Clouds of smaller things. They gather in the faint light zones — near the membrane sheets, where the diffuse glow is strongest — and they move in coordinated patterns that look intentional but follow no logic that can be parsed from outside. They turn. They scatter. They reform. They are the closest thing this place has to weather.

---

The scale of it is difficult. The pillars are spaced far apart — fifty meters, a hundred, sometimes more. The space between them is open water, moving water, and the visibility is poor enough that each pillar stands alone in its own faint glow, a solitary column in gray darkness, with the next one a distant smudge of light. You could swim for an hour and not reach a wall. You could swim for a day. The place extends past the range of perception in every direction — laterally, vertically — and there is no indication that it ends. It may not end. It may be that the garden was never given boundaries, because the concept of boundary requires an outside, and there may be no outside. There may only be this: the inside, extending forever, finished, settled, permanent.

The emptiness between the pillars is not vacancy. It is space. It is the space the structure needs to function — the channels for the water, the room for the membranes to filter, the volume the fauna require to move. It is purposeful emptiness, the way a cathedral's central nave is purposeful emptiness. The space is the point. The space is what makes the rest work.

---

And the dark. The dark above, where the pillars converge and the membranes thin and the water pressure becomes something that the structure below was not built to withstand. The dark is not empty either. There are things in the dark — shapes that move differently from the fauna below, slower, or perhaps not moving at all, perhaps simply existing in the high darkness as permanent fixtures. They cannot be seen clearly. They are inferred. A momentary interruption in the faint pattern of light. A sense of mass, somewhere above, where the water is coldest. They do not come down. What lives below does not go up. There is a boundary, invisible, unmarked, but absolute — a layer in the water column where one kind of existence ends and another begins, and neither crosses.

The garden does not know it is a garden. The structure does not know it is a structure. The fauna do not know they are fauna. Everything here simply exists, in the shape it has held for longer than shape has meaning, in the cold and the dark and the roar of water through living channels, and it will exist this way until something fundamental changes, and nothing fundamental will change, because the system has reached its resting state, and its resting state is this: sound, cold, dark, life, architecture, water, silence inside the sound, stillness inside the motion, an ending that does not end.

The garden is complete. The garden has always been complete. The garden will continue to be complete, in the dark, in the cold, in the deep, with its creatures moving through it like breath through a body that has stopped needing to breathe but breathes anyway, because breathing is what it does, because breathing is what it is.
