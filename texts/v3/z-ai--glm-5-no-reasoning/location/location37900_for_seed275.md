# The Rhizome Settlements

## First Impression

You descend through the access shaft—a hollow bored through living tissue—and the settlement opens beneath you like a vast, illuminated lung. The air tastes of pollen and machine oil. No moisture. Your lips crack within hours of arrival. The settlers carry salve in tin pots, reapply constantly, their hands perpetually glossed.

Below the surface, there is no sky—only the pale membrane of the ceiling vault, forty meters up, threaded with fiberoptic cables that harvest light from the surface and bleed it downward in pale golden streams. The light pools. Shadows accumulate in the spaces between.

---

## The Plant-That-Is-Here

They call it the Substrate. Not a plant, exactly—nothing so singular. A continuous fungal-vegetal network that predates any concept of "individual organism." It grows downward from the ceiling in cylindrical masses, in shelves and brackets and spiraling spars, colonizing the underground cavity meter by meter, year by year. The settlers live inside it. They carve rooms from its flesh. Doorways that close slowly over weeks if not reinforced. Walls that breathe, faintly, expanding and contracting on some circadian rhythm that belongs to the organism, not to human convenience.

The texture varies by depth and age. Near the ceiling vault: young growth, pale and rubbery, yielding to the touch like half-set gelatin. Deeper down: older matter, woody and dense, laminated in layers of different colors—rust, cream, charcoal, a deep bruise-purple where the Substrate has been damaged and healed itself. The settlers harvest from the older zones. The younger zones are left to extend.

No water. The Substrate operates on different chemistry. It drinks something from the rock below—a mineral seep, metallic and slow—and exudes a resinous sap that coats everything in a thin, sticky film. The settlers have learned to strip this resin, process it, burn it in their lamps. The smoke adds to the haze that perpetually drifts at head-height, never quite settling.

---

## The Built Environment

The settlement spreads across three primary levels, carved from the Substrate's mass and reinforced with metal bracing scavenged from older, deeper infrastructure. The upper level—where you emerge from the shaft—is administrative: the intake halls, the orientation chambers, the offices of the Settlement Authority. Smooth walls, planed flat, varnished to a dull shine. The Substrate here has been tamed, domesticated, trained into rectangular rooms and straight corridors. It requires constant pruning. You can hear the gardeners at work—small snip-snip sounds carrying through the tissue-walls, the grind of saws against woody fiber.

The middle level is residential. Here the architecture grows more organic. Rooms cluster in irregular patterns, following the Substrate's natural branching rather than imposing grid-structures. Walls curve. Ceilings dome. The settlers have learned to live with the plant's logic: a corridor that narrows suddenly where a branch thickened, a doorway that shifted six centimeters to the left over the course of a decade, a room that slowly subdivides itself as new growth intrudes.

Dwellings are carved hollows, lined with salvaged metal sheeting where the settlers can afford it, bare Substrate where they cannot. The poor live in rooms that bleed sap in humid weather (if "weather" is even the word—humidity rises and falls with the Substrate's metabolic cycles, a kind of vegetal season that has nothing to do with the absent sun). The rich live in chambers sheathed in brass, in ceramic tile imported from the surface, in glass that remains clear while the walls around it slowly reshape themselves.

The lower level is industrial. Here the Substrate grows wilder, less pruned, permitted to extend in massive columns that reach toward the rock floor. Between these columns: the processors. The refineries. The great subterranean engines that harvest the Substrate's resin, distill it, transform it into fuel and lubricant and the thick, bitter paste that the settlers eat when imported rations run low. Smokestacks don't exist here—smoke simply accumulates, drifts, settles into the tissues of everything. The settlers cough. They always cough. Their lungs are stained the same rust-color as the older Substrate.

---

## Movement and Process

The settlement is never still, though it is quiet. The quiet is what strikes you first—a hush that feels pressurized, held in place by the weight of the organism all around. But listen longer, and you hear it: the slow groan of growth, the creak of fibers expanding, the click and whisper of the Substrate's external dermis peeling away in microscopic flakes.

In the industrial district, the sound changes. Engines throb, low and steady. Conveyor belts hum, carrying harvested tissue toward the grinders. Workers shout, occasionally—a distant, muffled bark of instruction or warning—but mostly they communicate in gestures, in a sign-language developed over generations underground. Sound carries strangely here. The Substrate absorbs some frequencies and amplifies others. A whisper close by can be heard across a room; a shout at distance vanishes into the tissue.

Movement: the Substrate grows. It extends downward at a rate of perhaps three centimeters per year—measured, logged, tracked by the Authority's surveyors. The settlement must constantly carve new space to stay ahead of it. Abandoned districts exist, deeper down, where the Substrate was permitted to reclaim rooms and corridors, filling them with dense woody mass. Sometimes the settlers strip these zones for timber. Sometimes they seal them off and let them rot into something closer to soil—a rare commodity here, imported by the sack-load for the surface-farms that supply the settlement's vegetable ration.

---

## The Fauna

The settlers share their subterranean home with creatures that have adapted to the niche—or evolved here, in isolation, for centuries. No one is certain which.

*Silkers* are the most common: pale, many-legged arthropods that spin a fibrous web between the Substrate's branches. The settlers harvest this web, spin it into a coarse thread, weave it into clothing and rope. Silkers are harmless but startling—they drop from ceiling-growth without warning, their bodies the size of a human hand, their legs extending to twice that span. They move slowly in cold air and quickly in warmth. The settlers keep the industrial district heated partially to slow them down.

*Resin-moths* cluster around the processing plants, attracted by the smell of rendered Substrate sap. Their wings are greasy, translucent, veined with something that glows faintly in the dim light. They lay eggs in the processed fuel, and the larvae that hatch are a constant problem—eating machinery seals, clogging filters, breeding in the settlement's infrastructure. The Authority pays bounties for dead moths. Children hunt them with sticky-paddles, earning pocket money by the gram.

Deeper down, in the abandoned districts, larger things move. The settlers call them *growlers*, though they don't growl—they make a low, rhythmic thrumming sound, like a throat clearing, like a vibration passing through wood. No one has seen one clearly. They are shy, and the light hurts them. But the thrumming carries through the Substrate itself, felt in the walls and floor, and when it passes close, the settlers fall silent and wait.

---

## The Light

The fiberoptic cables change color with the surface seasons. In what the settlers call "high light," the cables burn a fierce, electric white, and the settlement is almost bright—bright enough to read by, to work by, to see clearly from one end of a corridor to the other. In "low light," the cables dim to amber, to rust, to a deep red that casts more shadow than illumination. The settlers mark time by these shifts. Their calendars are measured in luminance cycles.

The Substrate responds to light. In high-light periods, it grows faster, extending more aggressively, requiring more frequent pruning. In low-light, it goes dormant, and the settlement grows quieter—the sounds of work replaced by the sounds of waiting. The industrial district slows. The processing plants operate at reduced capacity. The settlers sleep more. They say the Substrate dreams in the dark.

---

## The Strange Rules

The Substrate remembers. This is not metaphor—the settlers believe it literally, and the evidence supports them. Wounds in the tissue heal, but they leave scars that grow thicker and harder over time, and if you cut into the same place twice, the second cut meets resistance, as if the Substrate has armored itself against repetition.

More unsettling: the Substrate responds to patterns. Corridors that are used frequently develop smoother walls, easier passage. Rooms that are abandoned fill in faster than expected. The settlers have learned to maintain even unused spaces—walking through them occasionally, touching the walls, leaving traces of presence—or risk losing them entirely.

There are stories of settlers who became lost in abandoned districts and were absorbed, their bodies incorporated into the tissue, their shapes visible in the grain of the wood for years afterward. The Authority dismisses these as folklore. The settlers are not so sure.

---

## The Ecology of Absence

Water does not exist here in any usable form. The rock below contains frozen brine, inaccessible and toxic. The Substrate processes it somehow, strips out what it needs, leaves the rest in crystalline deposits that the settlers call "salt-flowers"—beautiful, delicate formations that shimmer in the industrial light and dissolve instantly if touched with bare skin.

The settlers import water from the surface, paying premium prices for sealed canisters that arrive in the supply lifts. Each settler receives a daily ration. It is never enough. They supplement with moisture pressed from the Substrate's younger growth—a bitter, viscous liquid that tastes of mineral and resin and leaves a residue on the teeth. Long-term settlers develop a specific pallor, a tightness to their skin, a perpetual thirst that no amount of drinking can ease.

---

## The Town Below the Town

The settlement has a name, but it is a surface name, an administrative designation—*Processing Settlement 7-Delta*—and the settlers do not use it. They call their home *the Below*, or simply *Below*, and they divide it into districts that have nothing to do with the Authority's official maps.

*Rootward* is the direction of deeper growth, toward the abandoned zones, toward the growlers and the darkness. *Skyward* is the direction of the ceiling vault, the access shafts, the surface. The terms are spatial but also moral: rootward is dangerous, old, associated with death and loss; skyward is safe, regulated, connected to authority and supply.

Between these poles, the settlement sprawls in a loose spiral, following the Substrate's natural branching. At the spiral's heart is *the Hub*—not an official designation but the place where the main vertical shafts converge, where goods arrive from the surface, where the Authority maintains its primary offices. The Hub is the brightest zone, the most heavily pruned, the most frequently trafficked. It is also the most expensive to live near. Property values decrease the further one moves from the Hub, which means the poorest settlers live deepest, closest to the abandoned zones, closest to the things that move in the dark.

---

## What Emerges

The longer you stay, the more the settlement feels like a single organism—not the Substrate alone, but the whole system of plant and human and machine, locked in mutual dependence. The settlers prune the Substrate; the Substrate provides fuel, structure, air. The machines process the Substrate; the processed fuel powers the machines. The growlers thin the weakest settlers; the settlers harvest the growlers' silk. Nothing is wasted. Nothing leaves.

The air pressure changes subtly as you move through the levels—higher near the ceiling vault, lower in the depths. The settlers say this is the Substrate breathing, and the comparison feels apt. You find yourself timing your own breaths to the pressure shifts, inhaling when the air grows dense, exhaling when it thins. The settlers notice. They nod, approvingly. You are learning to live *with*, they seem to say. Not just *in*.

And perhaps that is the strangest thing: the Substrate does not feel hostile. It does not resist the settlement's presence. It grows around the rooms and corridors, incorporating them, accepting them as part of its structure. The settlers speak of it with something like affection, something like respect. When a child is born, the parents press its hand against a newly exposed growth, leaving a print in the damp tissue. The print fades within hours. But the Substrate remembers. They believe this. They say the child is known, now. The Substrate will recognize it, will grow around it more gently, will make space.

Whether this is true or not, the children who have been presented to the Substrate do seem to navigate the settlement more easily—finding passages that others miss, sensing which walls will yield and which will resist, moving through the Below as if they belong to it.

Perhaps they do.

---

## Leaving

The access shaft lifts you skyward, and the Below falls away—a vast, quiet cavity of living tissue and human industry, suspended beneath the surface like a thought held in the throat. The light changes as you rise. The air grows warmer, drier in a different way—the clean dryness of surface desert, not the resinous thick dryness of the Substrate.

Your skin is already cracking. Your lungs have begun to clear; you cough, bringing up rust-colored phlegm, leaving part of yourself behind. You wonder how long it would take to adjust fully. You wonder whether full adjustment is possible, or whether the Substrate simply accepts you in degrees, never entirely, always holding some part of you in reserve.

The shaft breaches the surface. Light—real light, not the filtered glow of fiberoptic cables—strikes your face. For a moment, you are blind.

When your vision clears, you see the desert stretching away in all directions, featureless and vast. And behind you, the shaft entrance: a dark circle in the rock, sealed with a metal grate, breathing slowly in and out.
