# The Rattleworks

You descend because the air moves. Not wind — nothing so directional or so free. A draw, a sipping. The rock above has a hole in it and the hole has a throat and the throat wants. You go down.

---

The chamber opens like a struck bell — round, high, resonating. Cathedral-sized, almost. The ceiling vaults to a point that disappears into a haze of particulate matter so fine it reads as darkness rather than dust. The floor is forty feet below the lip where you stand. You climb down because there is no path. The stone under your hands is warm. Not sun-warm. Blood-warm. The temperature of the inside of a closed mouth.

The rock here is new. You can feel it before you understand why — the edges are too sharp, the fractures too clean, the pockets and vesicles in the surfaces still glossy with un-oxidized mineral glaze. This stone opened recently. Months, perhaps. Not geologic time. The walls have the look of something that was sealed and is now not. They weep a faint, pale salt — not moisture, not condensation, but a mineral exhalation that crystallizes in hair-thin threads along every fracture line. The walls are furred with it. Like the chamber is growing a coat.

---

The light. You notice the light slowly because it does not announce itself. It has no source — or rather, it has so many sources that the effect is ambient, directionless, like being inside a clouded lampshade. The glow comes from the salt threads. From the floor. From certain surfaces of the many, many things that fill the chamber. It is amber and very weak. It turns everything the color of old beeswax. Your own hands look like they belong to someone else.

You cannot find where the light originates because the chamber is too full.

---

**Density.** The space is packed. Not cluttered — that implies disorder. This is layered, intentional, dense the way a reef is dense, the way a machine shop is dense: everything near-touching, everything in relation to everything else. Stalues of carved basalt stand six feet apart in rows that are not quite rows, paths winding between them like the paths in an orchard. Between the stalues, there are structures. Low sheds of sheet copper, green-black with patina. Racks of glass pipe — some intact, some shattered into bright curves on the floor. Engines. Or the bodies of engines — block-and-cylinder shapes in cast iron, wrist-thick, the size of dogs, set on stone plinths and connected by conduits of woven brass wire that run between everything like a nervous system. The wire is taut. It hums.

The wire is always taut. It is always humming.

---

The humming is what you hear first, but it is not the dominant sound. The dominant sound is a grinding. Not continuous — arrhythmic, staggered, a syllabic percussion that comes from every direction at once because it *is* coming from every direction at once. The engines — the dog-sized iron engines on their plinths — are running. Some of them. You cannot tell which. The sound seems to rotate among them, or perhaps they all produce it and the acoustics of the chamber fold it into a pulse. The grinding is the sound of matter being processed. You know this because you can see the output.

Several of the engines have hoppers — cast-iron funnels, mouth-up — and from the funnels, a substance emerges in slow extrusion. Not liquid. Not solid. A paste, a putty, the color of bone. It coils as it emerges, forming loose spirals on the floor beneath each engine, like soft-serve, like gut. The paste cools and hardens within minutes into a material like ceramic. There are piles of it everywhere. Old spirals, new spirals. The chamber floor is a mosaic of these coils, some broken underfoot, some still intact, some so old they've been overgrown.

---

Overgrown. Yes. The vegetation.

There are plants here, or things that occupy the ecological niche of plants. They are pale — almost translucent, the color of the salt-threads on the walls, as if they draw their substance from the same mineral source. They have no leaves. They have fronds, thin as wire, branching once and branching again and again until the terminal branches are invisible to the eye and detectable only as a shimmer in the air, a haze of interference. They grow from the cracks between the stone stalues. They grow from the coils of hardened paste. They grow from the patina on the copper sheds. One has found its way into the exhaust port of an engine and emerges from the other side in a forked, distorted shape, stretched by the heat, still alive.

They do not move. Nothing here moves except the paste and the sound.

You touch one. It is warm and slightly damp and it does not react.

---

**The fauna.** This is what stays with you.

The chamber is full of animals. Not in the way a forest is full of animals, where you sense them more than see them. Here they are visible, numerous, and impossible to ignore. They are everywhere — on the stalues, on the engines, on the floor, on each other.

They are beetles. Or they were beetles, once, in the way that a whale was once a land animal. They are the size of your palm, flat, segmented, with carapaces of a deep, iridescent bronze that matches the brass wire. They have too many legs — you count fourteen on one, eighteen on another — and the legs are long and jointed and they move in a way that is too fluid, almost liquid, each leg operating independently, so that a beetle in motion seems to ripple rather than walk.

There are hundreds of them. Thousands, perhaps. They cover the engines — clustered especially around the exhaust ports and the paste extruders, feeding. They eat the paste. You watch one approach a fresh coil, palpate it with clubbed antennae, and lower its head. Its mouthparts are not mandibular. They are a tube, a proboscis, and it presses this into the paste and *drinks*. The paste does not diminish visibly, but the beetle's abdomen swells, the segments separating slightly, and the bronze iridescence deepens to a color like heated metal.

They make sound. Each beetle produces a sustained, low tone — a vibration, really, felt in the teeth and sternum more than heard — and the aggregate of thousands of these tones is a chord. A single, sustained, unchanging chord that sits beneath the grinding of the engines and above the humming of the wire. The chord does not shift. It does not waver. It is as stable as gravity.

---

You listen to the full sound of the chamber and you understand something about it that you cannot articulate for some time. The sounds do not change. Not the grinding, not the humming, not the chord. The engines run at a constant rate. The wire hums at a constant pitch. The beetles sustain their constant tone. There is no gradient. Nothing accelerates or decelerates. Nothing swells or diminishes. The acoustic environment is in *equilibrium* — a frozen, perfect, unending equilibrium that must have taken years to reach and now sustains itself, the engines and the wire and the beetles and the paste all in a balance that requires no input, produces no waste, and does not, as far as you can tell, go anywhere.

This is not a process. It is a *state*.

---

The built structures. The copper sheds — you enter one. It is not a shelter. It is a *frame*. A structure of copper sheet and brass rivet, open on two sides, containing a device that is larger than the dog-engines outside. This one is the size of a piano, vertical, composed of stacked copper plates separated by gaps the width of a finger. Between the plates, more of the wire — this time woven into meshes, dense and fine. The whole thing vibrates. You can feel it in the stone floor, in the air, in your chest. It is producing the lowest register of the chamber's sound — a subsonic throb that you register as pressure more than pitch.

There are three of these sheds. Each contains an identical device. Each vibrates at a slightly different frequency, and the three frequencies combine into the foundation of the chord that the beetles fill in above.

You understand: this was built. The chamber was opened, the engines were placed, the sheds were constructed, the devices were tuned, the beetles were — *introduced? cultivated?* — and then the whole system was left to find its balance. And it found it. And it has been here, in this state, unchanged, for...

You don't know. The paste-coils on the floor represent thousands of extrusions. The stalues are half-buried in them. The copper sheds are partially encased. The wire between the engines has been overgrown by the wire-frond plants and by the patina that is slowly fusing every metal surface into a single, continuous body. And yet the system runs. Nothing has clogged. Nothing has failed. The beetles eat the paste and their waste — tiny pellets of the same ceramic material, compressed and dried — falls into the cracks between the stone and feeds the frond-plants, whose root-mats hold the paste-coils in place and channel the ambient moisture — what little there is, mineral-salt moisture, condensation on the coldest surfaces — back toward the engines, where it dissolves a portion of the paste and creates the substrate that the beetles actually drink.

It is a closed loop. It is a sealed system. It is, you realize, alive in the way a terrarium is alive — not because anything in it possesses intention, but because the relationships between the parts are so tight and so complete that the whole functions as a single organism.

---

There is a mark on one of the stalues. You find it by chance — a glyph, chipped into the basalt with a tool that left sharp, fresh edges. The glyph is not familiar. It resembles, very slightly, the shape of the devices in the copper sheds: stacked, vertical, gapped. Beside it, a number, or what might be a number: three vertical lines, each crossed at a different height.

This is the only mark of intention. The only evidence that someone built this and started it and left. Perhaps the number is a count. Perhaps it is a signature. Perhaps it is a date in a calendar you cannot read.

The beetles crawl over the glyph. Their bronze bodies match the brass wire. The wire hums its constant note. The engines grind their constant grind. The frond-plants shimmer in the dim amber light, and the light comes from everywhere and nowhere, and the chamber holds its breath, and holds it, and holds it.

---

You leave because there is nothing more to see, or because the sound has begun to feel like it is trying to settle into *you* — to include you in its equilibrium, to tune you to its chord, to add your body to the loop as another node, another surface for condensation, another source of warmth.

You climb. The air draws at you. You emerge into whatever world is above and the sound diminishes but does not disappear. You can still feel it. In your teeth. In your sternum. The chord. The unchanging chord. You walk away from the hole and it fades, and you walk further and it fades more, and eventually you cannot tell whether it has stopped or whether you have simply stopped hearing it.

You suspect it has not stopped. You suspect it does not stop. You suspect that somewhere beneath the stone, in a chamber the size of a cathedral, the engines still grind and the wire still hums and the beetles still drink and the paste still coils and the light still glows its amber glow and the whole system still holds itself in perfect, unyielding, inhuman equilibrium, and will hold, and will hold, and will hold.
