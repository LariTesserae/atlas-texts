This is a place that learned to hold its breath.

The plateau sits high enough that the air has texture—cool, slightly thick, the kind of cold that isn't hostile but present, pressing gently against the skin like damp cloth. Elevation here doesn't mean barrenness. The land is young, geologically speaking. The rock beneath the topsoil still has its edges, its sharp fractures and clean breaks. Nothing has been worn smooth yet. Time hasn't had its say.

And growing from that sharp stone, impossibly, is a riot of green.

The vegetation doesn't belong to any single biome. It's too much—fronds the size of doors, moss that grows in rope-thick strands hanging from outcrops, ground-cover that forms interlocking mats dense enough to walk on without touching stone. Leaves here are wide and dark, waxy surfaces catching light and throwing it back. The plants grow on top of each other, layering, compressing. There's almost too much to look at.

Water moves through all of it, but you won't find it in the ways you expect. No rivers. No standing pools. Instead, there's a constant weeping—condensation beading on leaf surfaces and rolling down stems in slow, deliberate droplets. The stone walls of the plateau seep. Green surfaces gleam wet in the light. If you stand still long enough, you feel the damp gathering on your shoulders, your hair. It's a breathing kind of wetness. In and out. The land inhales moisture from the air and exhales it back.

The quiet is what you notice first, though. Or rather, the quiet is what notices *you*.

There should be sound here. This much life, this much water moving, this much matter growing and dying and growing again—it should be loud. But the plateau practices a profound stillness. It's not absence. It's intention. Something about the density of growth absorbs vibration. Sounds are swallowed before they travel. You hear your own breathing. You hear the creak of your joints. You hear the slow, patient drip of water from one leaf to another, each impact separate and precise.

And then, scattered across this living quiet: the machines.

They're older than they look. Industrial infrastructure left behind, weathered but not eroded—nothing erodes here yet, the land is too young for that. Pipes that once carried steam or fluid run along the ground like mechanical roots, partly overgrown but not hidden. The vegetation doesn't consume the machines; it grows *around* them. Accommodates them. Single structures here and there—a generator housing, a processing unit, the skeletal remains of something that might have been a sorting mechanism—stand in small clearings, their metal surfaces warm despite the cool air, faintly humming with residual function.

Something still runs through them. Power, maybe. Purpose.

The machines don't make noise either. Or rather, the noise they make is so low and steady that it disappears into the quiet—a subsonic rhythm you feel in your chest more than hear. It's been going so long that it's become part of the landscape's metabolism. If it stopped, the silence would feel broken.

Light floods everything. Bright, almost harsh, though it doesn't burn. It doesn't feel like sunlight—it lacks the warmth, lacks that specific quality of coming from a single source. Instead, it feels like the air itself carries light, suspended and distributed. The plateau glows. Shadows are soft-edged and shallow. You can see everything, and everything is worth seeing.

You keep expecting animals. The density of growth, the abundance of water, the richness of the environment—it should be full of life moving through it. But there's almost nothing. An insect, perhaps, motionless on a leaf. Something that might have been a bird, once, seen only as a shape disappearing between hanging fronds. The ecology here has simplified down to its most essential elements: plants, water, stone, light, machines. The fauna exist elsewhere, in spaces this place doesn't connect to.

It's the simplicity that makes it strange. Not bizarre, not impossible, but *off*. A system this lush should be busy. It should teem. Instead, it holds. The energy that would normally cycle through countless species stays contained, moves slowly between fewer points, takes longer paths. The plateau has its own logic. It doesn't need to be explained.

If you walk through it—and you can walk, the vegetation forms pathways without meaning to—you find yourself moving at a certain pace. Slower than you intended. The air, the quiet, the dim awareness of machines processing something invisible—it recalibrates you. Urgent thoughts feel out of place here. There is no urgency. Only growth.

You could spend a long time in a place like this. You could watch a single droplet of water travel from one leaf to another, catching the light, and feel like you've witnessed something complete.

The plateau has no reason to change. It is still becoming what it will be.
