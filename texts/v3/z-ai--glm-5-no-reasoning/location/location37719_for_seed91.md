# The Rootworks

The noise arrives before anything else—a grinding, compression-layered compression that you feel in your back teeth before you consciously register it as sound. Industrial thrum. The recursive roar of machines feeding machines.

---

You are underground, though "underground" implies a surface above that matters. Here, the surface is a memory. This is a hollow carved from living matter, a cathedral-space where the ceiling and the walls and the floor are the same continuous thing: root. Pale root, woven root, root in braided thicknesses that range from finger-slender to tunnel-wide, pale as sprouted potatoes, as grubs, as things that never needed chlorophyll because light was never coming.

The damp is everywhere and nowhere. Condensation beads on the root-walls in irregular constellations. You can taste it in the air—cold, mineral, faintly sweet with that distinctive root-smell of carbohydrates waiting to become something else. Every hundred meters or so, the weave above you catches enough moisture to form a slow drip that falls with an audible *plink* into the sawdust and compacted matter beneath your boots. These drips have built small scalloped basins in the floor-material over years of patient accumulation.

The floor is not root. The floor is older—compost becoming soil, decades of dropped root-sheaths and shed bark and the slow accumulation of organic matter that compacts underfoot with a muffled crunch. In some places the floor has worn into channels and paths. Someone has walked here before.

---

The pipes are what surprise you. When your eyes adjust to the dimness—the low, amber-glow of sodium lamps strung in utilitarian lines along cables that bite into root-bark with metal staples—you see the infrastructure. Black iron pipes, wrist-thick, running parallel through the space, clamped to root-columns with brackets that have rusted into the surface. The pipes sweat. The pipes vibrate. The pipes are warm, or some of them are, carrying something from somewhere to somewhere else, generating a heat that the root-mass drinks greedily.

This is where the sound comes from. The pipes sing with pressure. Somewhere far off, pumps are working. Somewhere far off, the pipes connect to reservoirs and holding tanks and systems that require maintenance. Here, in this open space where root has pushed outward into emptiness and infrastructure has threaded through the gaps, you are in the middle distance of something functional. A transit point. A hollow that exists because the root-system needed room to breathe and the machines needed room to run.

The noise is not one thing. It is layers: the deep bass-throb of distant pumps, the midrange hiss of pressure through valves, the irregular percussion of condensation dripping onto iron, the undertone of root-fiber settling under its own impossible weight. The sound fills your skull. After a few minutes, you stop trying to separate the components. It becomes weather. It becomes an environment all its own.

---

The structures are sparse. A work-shed made of corrugated metal leaning against a root-column, its roof indented where a thinner root has pushed through overhead. A rack of tools, rusted solid, hanging from hooks that have been there long enough for root-bark to begin growing around the mountings. A single chair, wooden, its legs sunk inches into the softening floor-matter. No one sits in it. The seat is covered in a fine layer of pale dust—sawdust, perhaps, or spore-cast, or something the roots release in cycles you cannot perceive.

There are lights, but they are old. Industrial fixtures from decades past, wired with cable that has no business being this far from any official electrical grid. The bulbs hum at frequencies you can feel more than hear—except you can hear everything, so you hear that too, a whining harmonic beneath the greater roar.

You walk. The space extends past the limits of your vision. Sodium lamps create pools of orange-yellow every thirty meters or so, and between them, darkness. Not true darkness—there is always another light ahead, another pool of industrial illumination—but the kind of dimness that makes distance difficult to judge. The root-ceiling rises and falls in irregular swells. Sometimes it is close enough to touch, brushing the top of your head with a texture like cold, dry leather. Sometimes it rises into shadows that the lamps cannot reach, cathedral-high, and you hear the drip-drip of condensation falling from heights you cannot see.

---

There are no animals. The space feels wrong for them—the noise, the absence of true day-night cycles, the way everything leans toward a vegetable patience that operates on timescales incompatible with warm blood and fast heartbeats. But you find traces. A single feather caught in a junction where root meets pipe, too old to identify. Tiny droppings, desiccated, in a corner where the root-floor has folded over on itself. Something lived here once, or passed through. Something might still, in the deeper reaches where the lights stop and the pipes continue into darkness.

The plants here are not plants in the way you understand them. There are no leaves. There is no photosynthesis. This is root-system, rhizome-mass, a subterranean organism that has become its own architecture. It did not grow *in* this space; it *made* the space, pushing outward through soil and stone until the pressure created a hollow. And then it kept growing, reinforcing the hollow with thicker root-walls, reaching upward and downward simultaneously, weaving itself into a living framework that has no surface-access and needs none.

This is not a forest. This is the underside of a forest, the hidden weight beneath the canopy. Or it was, once. Now it exists for itself. The pumps and pipes and lights are intrusions. The roots have accepted them. The roots will eventually digest them.

---

You notice the growth-patterns after an hour of walking. The roots are not random. Near the pipes, they are thinner, more frequent, a fibrous mat that drinks condensation from metal surfaces. Near the lamps, they are pale and reaching, growing toward light with vegetable desperation even though there is no spectrum here that could sustain them. In the darker stretches between illumination, they are thicker, slower, older. You pass root-columns that are three meters wide, their surfaces scarred with old clamp-marks from equipment that was removed decades ago. The scars have healed over. The root remembers.

The cold is consistent. Not freezing, but the cold of refrigeration, of earth cut off from sun-warmth, of moisture evaporating slowly from surfaces and carrying heat with it. You can see your breath in the lamp-light. Your fingers ache after a while. The damp gets into your clothes.

---

The scale of the place reveals itself gradually. You walk for what feels like an hour and the character of the space does not change—same root-walls, same pipes, same sawdust floor, same amber lights at regular intervals—but the small details shift. Here, a pipe-junction with a valve-wheel you could turn if you had the strength and a reason. Here, a section of root-ceiling that has been cut away, the wound still pale and fresh-looking, sap drying at the edges. Here, a work-light on a stand, unplugged, its cable coiled neatly beside it, waiting for someone to return.

This is a workspace. Or was. The industrial elements are not ruins; they are simply paused. The pumps still pump. The lights still light. But no one is here to work. No one has been here for a while.

The sound tells you that. For all its roaring presence, the noise has a quality of emptiness. There are no voices layered beneath it. No footsteps. No distant clang of tools on metal. Just the machines, doing what machines do when they are left alone: work.

---

You reach a place where the root-ceiling opens up entirely. A true chamber, twenty meters high, circular, formed by roots radiating outward from a central point like the inside of a wooden chrysanthemum. The pipes converge here—they come from four directions and exit through a single massive conduit that rises through the root-floor and into darkness below. This is the node. This is the pump-station, or whatever this place actually *is*. The heart of it, if plants have hearts.

The noise is unbearable here. You have to press your palms against your ears to think. The central conduit shakes with the force of whatever moves through it. Condensation falls like rain from the root-ceiling, and the floor here is slick with moisture, pale threads of root-fiber drinking from shallow standing water.

There is a control panel, rusted, its glass face clouded with age and moisture. You cannot read the gauges. A single switch, large, industrial-red, sits in the ON position. The switch is warm to the touch. Vibration, or current, or both.

You stand in the center of the chamber, and for a moment, you feel the place as it actually is: a living system that has absorbed industrial infrastructure into its metabolism. The pumps are not pumping *through* the roots. The pumps are pumping *the roots*—or something the roots produce, something that requires extraction and transport and processing far away from here. The root-system and the machine-system have become one thing. A cyborg landscape, half-vegetable, half-industrial, neither fully in control.

---

You leave the way you came. The walk back is longer, or feels longer—the noise has become a kind of exhaustion, a pressure-drain on your attention. You notice things you missed before: small drain-holes in the root-floor where moisture collects and vanishes. Small tubes, clear plastic, running alongside the iron pipes, carrying something viscous and amber. A series of numbers stamped into the root-wall at head-height, so weathered they are nearly invisible, a code from whoever built this place.

You do not know what this place is. You know only what it contains: root and pipe and damp and dark and the endless grinding song of machines maintaining a system too large to perceive from inside. You are in the belly of something. You are passing through.

The last light flickers as you approach the edge. Ahead, darkness. Behind, the roar of the rootworks, continuing its patient industrial metabolizing, feeding and fed, extracting and extracted, a process that will outlast your presence by decades, perhaps centuries.

The roots will eventually digest the pipes. But not soon. Not soon at all.
