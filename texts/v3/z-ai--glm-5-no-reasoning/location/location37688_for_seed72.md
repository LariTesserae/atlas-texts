# The Cistern Yards

## I.

You descend. There is no other verb for it. The ramp — poured concrete, century-old, cracked in tooth patterns where roots once tried and failed — tilts inward at four degrees and carries you below the datum of the surrounding land. By the time the ramp flattens you have lost roughly eight meters of sky. The air changes first. It thickens. Not humid in the usual green, vegetal way — humid like breath, like the interior of a closed mouth. You are entering a place that respires.

The Cistern Yards occupy a depression that may once have been a natural sinkhole before it was shaped, walled, floored, and put to work. From the bottom, the rim of the world stands roughly three meters above your head on all sides — a retaining wall of blackened limestone block, mortared with something that has wept rust-colored stains down its face for longer than anyone has been counting. The yard is perhaps sixty meters across. Roughly oval. You can see the far wall clearly because it is close. Everything here is close. The horizon has been removed.

---

## II.

The floor is wet. Not flooded — there is no standing water deeper than a centimeter anywhere — but every surface carries a film. The concrete underfoot darkens in patches where the water pools along shallow grades. The ground was poured in panels, each about two meters square, separated by expansion joints that have since filled with a black biological matter — not moss, not algae, something lower, something that has negotiated a treaty with dampness and won. Walking is a matter of choosing dry panels, and there are few enough of those. Your boots will be wet within the first twenty steps. This is not a failure of drainage. The drainage works. You can hear it — a slow, continuous咕 gargling somewhere beneath the floor, like a throat clearing over and over. The water comes in faster than it leaves. Not by much. By enough. The yard is gaining water at perhaps the rate a glass fills from a dripping tap. Over hours this is nothing. Over decades it is a design philosophy.

The walls weep. This is not metaphor. Half a dozen points along the retaining wall produce continuous thin rivulets that track down the stone face in branching paths, like the veins of some small animal laid over the rock. Where the rivulets reach the floor they spread into fan-shaped stains, mineral-dark, and the stone there is smooth in a way the dry stone is not — polished by water that has been doing exactly this, at exactly this rate, for an unknowable span of time. The water is warm. If you touch the wall where it weeps, the stone is warm too. Not hot. Warm the way a body is warm. The temperature below the surface here is elevated — geothermally, perhaps, or perhaps from the industrial processes that still grumble in the adjoining chambers. The water rises from below, hits the wall's inner face, and emerges. It has nowhere to go but down. It has nowhere to go but here.

---

## III.

The built structures are the first thing you notice and the last thing you understand. Along the western curve of the wall stands a row of structures that are half-building, half-machine — sheds of corrugated steel and concrete block, each about the size of a garage, each connected to its neighbor by overhead pipes that sag between rusty brackets. Some of the sheds have doors. Some have doors that have rusted open and can no longer close. Inside, you can see the remnants of machinery: pump housings, valve assemblies, the round bodies of pressure vessels bolted to concrete pads. Most of it is dead. The paint has flaked to primer and bare metal. The pipes have fractured at their joints and drip, or have been capped with temporary fixes that have become permanent.

But not all of it is dead.

Two of the sheds — the third and the seventh — still function. You know this because you can hear them. The third shed produces a low, cyclical groan every forty seconds, a sound that begins in the subsonic and climbs into the audible range before falling back. It is a pump. It is pumping something — water, probably, from the sumps beneath the floor back into some upper circuit — and it has been doing this for so long that the rhythm has become part of the yard's metabolism. When the pump groans, the puddles on the floor tremble. When the pump rests, the silence is not silence but the absence of groaning, which is its own sound. The seventh shed is quieter. It emits a continuous electrical hum, sixty cycles, the sound of a transformer doing its patient work. A single bulb, bare, caged in wire, hangs inside the shed's open door and produces a light that is less illumination than suggestion. It is the brightest thing in the yard.

This is not saying much.

---

## IV.

The light. Yes. The yard exists in a state of permanent dimness that is not night and not shade but something more architectural. The retaining wall blocks direct sun for most of the day. During the hours when the sun is high enough to clear the rim, its light enters at a steep angle and reaches only a narrow corridor across the yard's center — a shifting band of actual brightness, maybe two meters wide, that slides across the floor like a slow beam and then vanishes. For the rest of the day, the yard is lit by reflection: sunlight bouncing off the upper wall and scattering, diffuse and reduced, into the space below. The color of this light is gray-yellow, like old paper. It is enough to see by. It is not enough to see well.

The sheds contribute their own light. The bulb in the seventh shed. A few indicator lamps — red, amber, green — on the control panels of machinery that may or may not still be attended. These lamps are tiny, pinpricks of color in the gray, and they give the yard the look of a place seen from very far away, or through water, or in the moments before waking.

At night — and the yard does have night, a deepening of the dim into actual darkness — the indicator lamps become the only light, and the yard becomes a field of scattered colored points, like a constellation viewed from underground. The pump still groans. The water still weeps. The darkness changes nothing. The yard does not sleep.

---

## V.

The vegetation is almost absent, and what exists is not thriving so much as enduring. Along the base of the eastern wall, where the weeping water has created a continuous strip of damp, a single species of plant has colonized — a tough, narrow-leaved thing, knee-high at most, pale green verging on yellow, that grows in scattered clumps rather than continuous cover. It has no flowers. It reproduces by rhizome, apparently, sending slow runners through the wet gaps in the stone. Each clump is connected to the others underground. The entire eastern population may be a single organism. It does not grow toward the light. It grows toward the water. This is a plant that has chosen its priority and will live with the consequences.

Nothing else grows. The concrete is too alkaline. The shade too deep. The foot traffic — such as it is — too frequent in the wrong way. A few stems of something dried and brown can be found in the corners, the remains of plants that tried and failed in some previous season. They snap underfoot like old string.

---

## VI.

And then there are the creatures.

This is the yard's secret. This is what you do not expect.

The insects have found this place and have made it a city.

They come in waves — not metaphorically, not as a figure of speech, but in actual waves, pulses of movement that pass across the wet floor like wind across grass. The primary species is a small, dark-winged fly, perhaps three millimeters long, that breeds in the standing water and the damp biological matter in the expansion joints. Their numbers are not countable. They are estimable only in the way that rainfall is estimable — by intensity, by duration, by the sense of volume they produce. On a warm afternoon, the air above the wettest parts of the floor shimmers with them. Not a swarm. A medium. The flies occupy the air the way the water occupies the floor: thinly, continuously, pervasively.

They are loud. Not individually — a single fly is inaudible — but collectively, the yard hums. A high, thin, persistent whine that sits just below the threshold of real annoyance, like a pitch you can almost ignore. The pump groans, and the flies whine, and the water gurgles beneath the floor, and these three sounds layered together produce the yard's characteristic acoustic: a warm, dense, organic drone that fills the space the way the light fails to. Sound is what this place has instead of illumination. You hear it more than you see it.

The flies are not alone. They are the foundation. Upon them, other creatures have built their own economies.

Spiders — a species of long-legged cellar spider, pale and translucent — have strung webs across the upper corners of the sheds, between the overhead pipes, in the angles where wall meets ceiling. Their webs are not the ornamental orb webs of gardens but the disordered, layered sheets of creatures that have given up on geometry in favor of volume. Each web is enormous relative to its maker, and each is thickly furred with the dust of captured flies. Dozens of webs overlap in the darker corners. The spiders sit in them and wait and are never disappointed. When you walk beneath a web, you feel the faint tick of silk strands against your hair, and you hear, very close, the dry scrambling of a spider adjusting its position.

On the floor, among the puddles, beetles work. They are dark, oval, a centimeter long, and they move with a deliberate, aimless persistence that suggests they are searching for something they will never find and have accepted this. They patrol the edges of the wet panels. They investigate the expansion joints. Occasionally one encounters a dead fly and pauses, and there is a small silent drama of consumption, and then the beetle moves on. There are hundreds of them. You see them mostly in your peripheral vision — small dark shapes resolving and unresolving against the wet concrete.

And above all of this, in the dim upper air, moths. Not many. A dozen, perhaps, at any given time. They are large — thumb-width — and gray-brown, and they fly in slow, erratic circles around the indicator lamps and the bare bulb in the seventh shed. They are the yard's largest visible inhabitants. Their shadows pass across the walls in the lamplight, enormous and wavering, and for a moment each shadow looks like something else — a hand, a bird, a reaching arm — before the moth turns and the shadow collapses back into insect form.

The yard is, in terms of biomass, almost entirely non-human. The structures were built by people. The machines were installed by people. But the people are gone, or come rarely, and the yard has been inherited.

---

## VII.

The inheritance has rules.

The creatures do not avoid the sheds. The sheds are, in fact, the preferred habitat — warm, sheltered, lit by the dim glow of indicator lamps, rich with the organic matter that accumulates in undisturbed corners. The machinery and the ecosystem have reached an accommodation. The flies breed in the water that pools around the pump housings. The spiders build webs in the cooling vents of the pressure vessels. The beetles live in the gaps between the concrete pad and the base of the transformers. The moths navigate by the light of the control panels. The yard's industrial infrastructure has become, without anyone intending it, the skeleton of a cave.

This is a place that was made into one thing and has become another.

The process is slow. The dynamic of the yard is geological in pace. The water is winning — each year, a little more of the concrete is wet a little more of the time. The biological matter in the joints is deepening. The plant clumps on the eastern wall are extending by centimeters per season. The rust on the pipes is progressing at its own rate, which is the rate of oxidation in warm, damp air, which is not fast but is not ever absent. The machinery that still functions is functioning less. The pump's forty-second cycle will, at some point, become a forty-five-second cycle, and then a fifty, and then a silence. But not yet. Not now. The yard is in a long, slow middle — past its purpose, before its end, in the extended patient interval where things that were made to do work continue to do work while the world rearranges itself around them.

---

## VIII.

Standing in the center of the yard at the hour when the sun's band crosses the floor — that narrow moving stripe of actual light — you would see the following: the stripe approaching your feet across wet concrete, and as it reaches you, the flies in the air above it suddenly visible, their wings catching the light in a thousand brief flashes, like sparks above a fire. The stripe reaches your boots. The concrete dries instantly in a patch no wider than your shadow. The warmth is immediate and real. Behind you, the stripe has already moved on, and the concrete is wet again. Ahead, the flies are flashing. The pump groans. A beetle walks across your boot and does not pause. The retaining wall weeps. The spider webs in the upper corners are invisible except where, for a moment, the sun's band illuminates a single strand, and it glows like a wire, and then it is gone.

The yard is this moment, repeated, for a very long time.
