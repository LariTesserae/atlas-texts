# The Gall-Heart of Wickwright

## I.

There is a town inside a single wound in a tree.

Not a great tree — not a world-tree, not a cathedral oak. A black willow, maybe forty feet tall, growing where a low hillside weeps groundwater into a fen. The wound is a burl: a gall the size of a small room, formed where something — infection, insect, lightning, no one remembers — opened the trunk decades ago and the wood closed around the opening like a hand closing around a stone. Inside the gall, the heartwood has hollowed. Not rotten — *re-routed*. The grain grew inward, folding into shelves and alcoves and partial walls, as if the wood were trying to build something and only half-remembered the plans.

People live here. Eight, perhaps ten. A settlement.

They call it Wickwright.

---

## II.

The gall opens on the hillward side, where the willow's trunk has thickened into a gnarl the shape of a cupped palm. The entrance is a fissure — a vertical crack in the living wood, three feet wide at its widest, narrowing to a slit at top and bottom. You enter sideways. The wood is damp under your hand. Not wet the way a puddle is wet — wet the way a living thing is wet, interior-wet, the moisture held in the grain itself, released slowly through minute fractures in the bark. The surface is cold. Not stone-cold. Body-cold. The temperature of something alive and not quite warm enough.

Inside, the space is perhaps ten feet by twelve. Not a room — a *cavity*. The walls are living wood, sapwood and heartwood exposed in concentric layers where the gall has split and healed and split again. The layers are not flat. They fold and ridge, forming alcoves, shelves, partial partitions that separate the space into zones without fully dividing it. It is architecture, but it is not built. It is *grown*. The wood has made these spaces the way a body makes an organ — by becoming the shape of its own function.

The floor is root-mat. Not boards, not packed earth. The willow's roots, entering from below, have woven into a dense mesh, slightly springy underfoot, always damp. Water moves through the roots — you can feel it if you stand still. A faint pulse. Not a heartbeat. A seep. The groundwater that feeds the willow moves through the floor the way blood moves through veins, and the floor is faintly warm where the flow is closest to the surface.

The ceiling is the underside of the gall, a dome of inward-growing wood, smooth and pale, stippled with small pores that weep a clear, faintly sweet liquid. It drips. Constantly. Not rain — condensation. The willow pulls water from the fen and pushes it upward, and the gall, sealed except for the entrance fissure, holds a private atmosphere: humid, close, saturated. The air is fog. Not a fog that rolls in — a fog that *lives* here, that exists because the temperature inside the gall is slightly colder than the water moving through the wood, so the air can never quite hold all the moisture the walls release. The fog is thin at the floor, thicker at the ceiling, and in between it moves in slow drifts, stirred only by breath, by body heat, by the faint draft from the entrance.

You cannot see the far wall when you enter. You see shapes. The fog thickens and thins in cycles that have nothing to do with weather and everything to do with the willow's transpiration rate — faster in the false-day of the entrance-crack's faint light, slower in the deep hours. The fog has a smell. Green. Wet. The smell of the inside of a living thing — sap and cellulose and the faint iron tang of water that has passed through root and stone.

---

## III.

Light does not live here.

The entrance fissure admits a gray, filtered leak — the light of the fen outside, already dimmed by the willow's curtain of hanging branches, further dimmed by passing through the crack's narrow aperture. It reaches perhaps three feet inside and dies. Beyond that, nothing. Not darkness the way a cave is dark — darkness the way a body is dark. Interior darkness. The darkness of an enclosed living space that was never meant to be seen.

The residents of Wickwright do not use fire. Fire kills the tree. They do not use lamps — they have no fuel, no oil, nothing to burn that the wood would not also feel. They use something else.

The walls glow.

Not the wood itself. The *film* on the walls — a biofilm, a living crust of micro-organisms that has colonized the inner surface of the gall the way moss colonizes a stone. It is pale, faintly blue-green, and it glows. Not brightly. Not the way a lantern glows — the way a bruise glows, the way certain deep-sea creatures glow, a cold luminescence that is less light than the *suggestion* of light, enough to see shapes by, not enough to see color. The biofilm is patchy. It grows thickest along the grain lines, where the sap flows closest to the surface and feeds it. It grows in the alcoves where the air is still and the moisture is constant. It does not grow near the entrance, where the draft dries the walls.

The residents tend it. They scrape it where it grows too thick — thick patches go dark, suffocating themselves — and they spread the scrapings to areas where the walls are bare. They know which zones of the gall glow best at which hours. They navigate by the glow the way a sailor navigates by stars: not by looking at it directly, but by feeling where it is strongest and moving toward or away.

The light is blue. Not daylight-blue. Blood-blue. The blue of veins under skin. In this light, the wood walls look alive — which they are — and the people look dead — which they are not, but the comparison is unavoidable. Everyone in Wickwright is pale. Everyone is damp. Everyone moves in the blue glow with a careful, deliberate slowness, and from across the gall, it is sometimes hard to tell the people from the wood.

---

## IV.

Eight people. Perhaps ten. It varies.

They are not a family, exactly. They are a *colony* — the way the biofilm on the walls is a colony. Individuals who came to the gall separately, at different times, for different reasons, and who have organized themselves around the gall's capacity to shelter them. They have no shared history before the gall. They have no shared language, fully — they speak in a pidgin made from fragments of three or four outside tongues, held together by gesture and by the shared vocabulary of the place itself: the words for the alcoves, for the root-floors, for the drip-patterns, for the biofilm, for the willow.

They have names, but the names are more like *addresses*. The Gall-Keeper lives in the deepest alcove, where the wood is thickest and the glow is faintest. The Floor-Tender lives near the root-mat's main junction, where the water flows strongest. The Light-Gardener lives along the eastern wall, where the biofilm grows best. The others — the Forager, the Weaver, the two children who have no titles yet — live in the alcoves between, in spaces the wood has made that are just large enough for a body to curl into.

The architecture is *them*, and they are *architecture*. They have shaped the wood — not by cutting, which would wound the tree, but by *pressure*. By living against the wood for years, pressing their bodies into the alcoves, the residents have caused the grain to grow around them. The alcoves are body-shaped. The shelves are arm-height. The partitions curve where shoulders have leaned against them for a decade. The gall has *accommodated* its inhabitants the way a shoe accommodates a foot — not by being made for them, but by being reshaped by their presence.

They sleep in the wood. Not on it — *in* it. The alcoves are deep enough that a person can lie inside them, curled, with the wood closing above them like a lid. Not flush — there is air space, there is the glow, there is the drip — but close. The wood sleeps with them. The gall's temperature drops at night (though "night" is a fiction here — the darkness is constant, the only cycle is the fog's thickening and thinning) and the residents curl into their alcoves and the wood seems to close slightly, the grain swelling with the night-cold, narrowing the gaps.

In the morning — in the *thinning*, when the fog lightens and the glow brightens — they emerge. It is like watching something being born. Each person peels out of their alcove with a thin film of condensation on their skin, and for a moment, in the blue light, they look like they are made of the same material as the walls.

---

## V.

The animals are the overwhelming fact of Wickwright.

Not the people. The people are a footnote. The gall is not theirs. The gall belongs to the creatures that were here first, that are here still, that outnumber the people by factors the people do not have numbers for, and that tolerate the human presence with an indifference that is more unsettling than hostility would be.

The wood is *crawling*.

Not with vermin — with *citizens*. The gall is a city, and the eight humans are not its rulers or even its largest inhabitants. They are merely the largest *visible* inhabitants. The rest — the vast, teeming, overwhelming rest — are smaller.

The wood-borer beetles came first. They are the gall's architects. The fissures, the channels, the alcoves themselves — these were begun by beetles, their mandibles carving galleries into the heartwood, their larvae thickening the walls with frass and resin. The beetles are still here. They are everywhere. In the wood, in the walls, in the floor, in the ceiling — a constant, sub-audible clicking as they work, mandible on grain, carving, extending, maintaining. The residents of Wickwright do not fight them. They cannot. There are too many. Instead, they have learned the beetles' patterns: which galleries are active, which are abandoned, which can be used, which will be reclaimed. The humans live in the beetles' *finished* work, the way hermit crabs live in shells made by others. When a beetle gallery is abandoned — when the larvae have matured and departed — the humans move in. When the beetles return to reclaim a gallery, the humans move out. There is a rhythm to it. The humans have learned to read the wood the way a sailor reads the sea — not to control it, but to move with it.

The mites came second. Or perhaps they were always there. The mites are the *medium* — the connective tissue of the gall's ecosystem. They are everywhere, on every surface, in every crevice, so dense that the walls seem to *shimmer* in the blue glow, and only when you look closely — very closely, your face an inch from the wood — do you realize the shimmer is not the biofilm but movement, millions of legs, millions of bodies, a living film that is not one organism but a *nation* of organisms, each the size of a grain of dust, each feeding on the biofilm or the frass or the mold or each other, each producing waste that feeds the biofilm, which feeds the mites, which feed the beetles, which shape the wood, which shelters the humans. The cycle is closed. The cycle is complete. The humans are inside it, but they are not necessary to it. This is the thing the Gall-Keeper understands and the others are learning: *they are not the point*.

The centipedes are the reason the humans sleep in alcoves.

The centipedes are not large — four inches, perhaps five — but they are *many*, and they are *fast*, and they hunt the mites and the beetles and anything else that moves on the wood's surface, including, occasionally, a finger or an ear or a child's hand. They are not venomous enough to kill. They are venomous enough to *hurt*, and the children of Wickwright have the marks to prove it — small, paired punctures on their hands and wrists, the record of every time they reached into a crevice without checking first. The centipedes live in the deep galleries, the ones the beetles have abandoned, the ones the humans cannot follow. They emerge at the thickening — the fog-dark hours — and range across the walls in hunting parties, their legs catching the blue glow, their bodies moving with a fluid speed that is beautiful and that the humans have learned to watch for. When the centipedes are out, the humans are in. In their alcoves. Wood closed above them. Breathing shallow. Waiting.

The spiders build in the corners. Not webs — *channels*. The spiders of the gall are weavers of a different kind: they spin silk *lines* along the wood's grain, connecting shelf to partition, alcove to ceiling, creating a network of trip-lines that catch the mites and the small flying insects that enter through the entrance fissure. The silk lines are invisible in the blue glow until the fog condenses on them, and then — for a few minutes, before the condensation drips away — the gall is strung with silver, a geometry of threads that maps the space in a way the humans' eyes cannot, a map of connections and distances that the spiders know and the humans can only admire.

The moths are the light.

Not the biofilm — the moths. In the deeper hours, when the fog is thickest and the biofilm dims, the moths come. They are small — the size of a thumbnail — and they are pale, and they *glow*. Not the way the biofilm glows, steadily, from a surface. The moths glow *internally* — a light that moves through their bodies, pulsing with their heartbeat, brightening when they fly, dimming when they land. They enter through the fissure and they spread through the gall like sparks through a dark room, and for the hours they are here, the gall is *luminous* — not the faint, cold blue of the biofilm but a warm, golden, living light that moves and shifts and makes the wood look like something lit from within, and the humans stop whatever they are doing and watch. It is the closest thing to daylight that exists in Wickwright. It is the closest thing to joy.

The moths do not stay. They come, they spread, they glow, they mate, they lay their eggs in the wood's pores, and they leave. The eggs hatch into larvae that burrow into the heartwood and become — no one knows. No one has followed. The larvae go *in*. They do not come *out*. The willow absorbs them, or they become something else inside the wood, or they simply cease. The humans have theories. The Gall-Keeper believes the larvae become the biofilm — that the glow on the walls is the accumulated light of a thousand generations of moths, their children dissolved into the wood, their light remaining. No one can prove this. No one can disprove it. In the blue glow of the walls, it feels true.

## VI.

The fen outside is where the foraging happens.

The entrance fissure opens onto the willow's trunk, and the willow stands at the edge of a fen — a low, wet, cold place where groundwater seeps to the surface and forms shallow pools among the roots of other willows, alders, sedges, mosses. The fen is the source of everything: the willow's water, the gall's humidity, the insects that enter through the fissure, the organic material that washes in during the seasonal floods. The fen is also where the humans forage, because the gall, for all its life, does not produce food. It produces shelter. It produces light. It produces *air* — the fog is breathable, the biofilm's metabolism freshens it. But food must be found outside.

The Forager goes out. Once a day, in the thinning — the hours when the fog inside is lightest, which correspond, roughly, to the hours of dim daylight outside, though "daylight" in this fen is a generous term. The sky here is permanently overcast — not by clouds, exactly, but by the canopy. The willow is one tree in a stand of trees, and the stand is one stand in a forest, and the forest is one forest in a landscape of forests, all growing in the same low, wet, cold ground, all so dense that the canopy is a continuous roof. Light at the fen's surface is the light that filters through two or three layers of leaves — green, dim, directionless. It is possible to stand in the fen at noon and not know which way is south.

The Forager moves through this dimness with the patience of someone who has done this route a thousand times. She follows the root-ridges — the willow's roots, surfacing and submerging in the fen's soft ground, forming a path of sorts, a network of raised lines that she walks like a tightrope, her bare feet finding the roots by touch, the cold water of the fen lapping at her ankles. She gathers: mosses from the alder trunks, which are edible when boiled in a bark-bowl set on a heated stone. Insect larvae from the fen's shallow pools, which are eaten raw, or wrapped in sedge leaves and set in the alcoves to ferment. Fungi from the rotting wood at the fen's edge — bracket fungi, pale and rubbery, which grow in the cold, wet, dim conditions like nothing else grows. These are the staple. These are the food that sustains Wickwright when everything else fails.

The Forager also gathers the things the gall *needs*: fragments of bark from other trees, which the humans place in the entrance fissure to modify the draft. Stones from the fen's stream-bed, which are heated and used for boiling. Fibrous sedge-stalks, which are woven into the mat that covers the entrance, the only *made* thing in Wickwright, a curtain of woven plant-fiber that reduces the draft and traps the fog while still allowing the moths to enter. And, occasionally, a new piece of biofilm — scraped from a log in the fen, brought inside, spread on a bare patch of the gall's wall, where it takes hold or doesn't, where it grows or dies, where it adds its faint light to the light already there.

The Forager is outside for two hours. In that time, she is *exposed*. The fen has its own ecology — its own insects, its own predators, its own pace — and she is a visitor in it, a warm body in a cold, wet, dim world that does not know her and does not accommodate her. She returns to the gall with her gatherings, slips through the entrance fissure, replaces the sedge-mat, and sits in the first alcove — the one closest to the entrance, the one the Gall-Keeper does not use because the draft dries the biofilm — and she breathes the humid, blue-lit, mite-thick air of the interior, and it is *home*. She has said this to the others. The gall is home. The fen is not. The fen is the place where food is. The gall is the place where *life* is.

## VII.

Sound in Wickwright is almost nothing, and the almost-nothing is everything.

The gall is quiet the way a body is quiet. Not silent — *interior*. The sounds are the sounds of living wood: the creak of grain shifting as the trunk sways in wind it cannot see. The tick of beetles carving. The almost-subliminal hiss of water moving through roots. The drip — the constant, variable, never-ceasing drip of condensation falling from the ceiling pores to the root-mat floor. Each drip has a pitch. Each drip has a location. The residents know them all. They know the drip that falls into the Floor-Tender's alcove, the high one, the one that sounds like a finger tapping glass. They know the drip that falls into the empty alcove, the one no one uses, the one that sounds like a stone dropping into a well, low and hollow and *patient*. They know the drip that falls onto the root-mat itself, the one that is not a drip at all but a *series*, a string of drops that fall so close together they sound like breathing.

The human sounds are small. Bare feet on root-mat. The scrape of a stone on wood. The wet sound of the biofilm being spread. The Forager's return — the sedge-mat pulled aside, the draft, the footsteps, the sedge-mat replaced. The children, occasionally, making sounds that are not words — the sounds children make when they are learning to be alive, which are the same sounds the beetles make, which are the same sounds the wood makes, which are the sounds of *growth*.

The centipedes make no sound. This is the thing that the humans fear most. The beetles click. The mites are sub-audible, a faint static at the edge of hearing. The spiders are silent but *visible*, their silk lines catching the fog. The moths are audible — a faint, papery flutter, the sound of light moving through air. But the centipedes — the hunters, the ones that range across the walls in the thickening hours — make *nothing*. No sound of legs on wood. No sound of mandibles. No sound of breath. The only sign of a centipede is the movement itself, the long, fluid, leg-driven glide across the wall, visible only if you are looking, and in the blue glow, looking is not always enough.

The humans have developed a system. It is not a warning system — there is no one to warn, no one outside to call to. It is a *checking* system. Before reaching into a crevice, before placing a hand on a shelf, before leaning against a partition, the residents *tap*. A single knock, knuckle on wood. The vibration travels through the grain. If there is a centipede on the other side, it moves. The movement is audible — not as a sound, but as a *change in the wood's response*, a shift in the way the vibration returns, a hollowness that was not there before. The residents have learned to feel this. It is not hearing. It is not touch. It is something between — a sense that the wood itself provides, a sense that exists only because the wood is alive and the residents are pressed against it and the vibration moves through both.

The Gall-Keeper says the willow is teaching them. No one argues. No one is sure. The wood does not feel like a teacher. It feels like a *medium* — the thing through which information moves, not the thing that sends it. But the Gall-Keeper has lived in the gall longest, and her alcove is the deepest, and her skin is the most like the wood, and when she speaks, which is rarely, the others listen.

## VIII.

The seasons are not seasons.

The fen does not have summer. The fen does not have winter. The fen has *wet* and *wetter*, and the gall has *drip* and *flood*.

In the wetter season — the season that corresponds, by the Forager's count, to the colder months — the groundwater rises. The fen's pools merge. The willow's roots, which in the drier season are partly exposed, are submerged, and the willow drinks more, and the gall's humidity rises until the fog is not a fog but a *rain* — a fine, constant, indoor precipitation that falls from every surface, that makes the walls stream, that fills the alcoves with water that must be scooped out and poured onto the root-mat floor, where it is absorbed and sent back through the tree and returned as more rain. The gall is a water cycle in miniature. The gall is a weather system contained in a wound.

In this season, the biofilm dims. The moisture is too much for it — the organisms drown in their own substrate, and the glow fades to a minimum, a bare suggestion of light that makes the gall feel larger than it is, because the walls disappear into darkness and the space seems to extend in every direction, infinite, unbounded, a blackness that is warm and wet and breathing.

The centipedes retreat in this season. The deep galleries flood, and they move upward, into the walls, into the shelves, into the alcoves. The humans share their sleeping-spaces with centipedes. Not by choice. By *necessity*. The centipedes are in the wood, and the humans are in the wood, and the wood is one thing, and so they are in the same thing, and the tapping system fails because the centipedes are *everywhere* and the vibrations overlap and the wood's response is a chaos of small movements that cannot be distinguished. In the flood season, the humans sleep with their hands inside their clothing. The children sleep in the deepest part of their alcoves. The Gall-Keeper does not sleep at all, but sits in her deep alcove with her hand on the wall, feeling the centipedes move, and in the morning she tells the others where the worst concentrations are, and they avoid those areas until the water recedes.

The flood season lasts — the Forager counts it — approximately sixty drips. Sixty cycles of the fog's thickening and thinning. It is not a long time. It is long enough. When the water recedes, when the fen's pools separate again, when the willow's drinking slows and the gall's rain returns to fog and the biofilm brightens, the humans emerge from their alcoves and the gall is *different*. The wood has grown. The galleries have shifted. The beetles have carved new channels. The spiders have spun new lines. The alcoves are not quite the shape they were. The residents must *re-learn* the space. They must press their bodies against the wood again, let the grain accommodate them again, let the gall become *theirs* again.

This is the rule of Wickwright: the place is not fixed. The place is *growing*. And the people who live in it must grow with it, or be grown around.

## IX.

The Gall-Keeper is the oldest, but she was not the first.

The first was a man whose name is no longer spoken — not because it is forbidden, but because no one remembers it. He came to the willow during a flood season, fleeing something — the Forager's mother knew the story, but she is dead now, and the Forager knows only the outline. He found the fissure. He entered the gall. He stayed. He was alone for — the count is uncertain — perhaps ten drips. Perhaps more. When the second person came, he was already part of the wood. His skin had taken on the quality of the grain. His eyes had adjusted to the blue glow. He had learned the beetles' patterns and the centipedes' hours and the biofilm's needs. He was the first Gall-Keeper.

He is gone now. Not dead — *gone*. He went deeper. The Gall-Keeper says this is what happens to those who stay too long. The gall grows around them. The wood absorbs them. Not violently, not painfully — slowly, the way the gall has absorbed everything: by accommodating, by growing to fit, by becoming the shape of what is inside it. The first Gall-Keeper went deeper into the wood, into the heartwood, into the part of the gall that no one can reach, and the wood closed behind him, and he is there still, or he is not, or he is *part of the tree*, which is a different thing from being alive and a different thing from being dead.

The current Gall-Keeper has been here for — she counts in drips, not years — four hundred drips. Perhaps five hundred. She is not sure. She knows she was young when she came, and she is not young now, and the wood has grown around her in ways she can feel but not see. Her alcove is her shape. Her skin has the grain. Her eyes see only blue. She knows the centipedes by their vibrations. She knows the beetles by their clicks. She knows the willow the way you know your own body — not by looking, but by *being*.

She will go deeper. She knows this. She does not fear it. The gall has been good to her. The gall has been *her*. When she goes, someone else will become the Gall-Keeper, and they will sit in the deep alcove, and they will put their hand on the wall, and they will feel the wood, and they will feel the Gall-Keeper in the wood, and they will know that she did not leave. She *became*.

This is the religion of Wickwright, if it has one. Not worship. Not reverence. *Participation*. The gall is not a god. The gall is a *process*. The people who live in it are part of the process, the way the mites are part of the process, the way the beetles are part of the process, the way the water and the fog and the biofilm and the moths are part of the process. The process is: living things shape the wood, and the wood shapes the living things, and the boundary between them erodes — not metaphorically, but *physically*, the wood growing into the shape of the body, the body taking on the quality of the wood, until the distinction is academic and then irrelevant and then *gone*.

The Gall-Keeper says: *We are not living in the tree. The tree is living in us.*

No one argues. In the blue glow, in the fog, in the drip and the click and the silence of the centipedes, it is not a statement of belief. It is a statement of *fact*.

## X.

The two children were born in the gall.

They have never been outside. They do not know the fen. They do not know the sky — the dim, overcast, canopy-filtered sky of the Forager's world. They know the fissure, which is a rectangle of gray light that appears during the thinning and disappears during the thickening. They know the sedge-mat. They know the Forager's smell when she returns — the cold, wet, green smell of the fen, which is different from the warm, wet, green smell of the gall. They know the moths, which are their daylight. They know the centipedes, which are their darkness.

They are being raised by the gall.

Not by the people — the people are busy. The people are foraging, tending, checking, sleeping. The children are with the people, but they are also with the wood, and the wood is a teacher in a way the people are not. The wood teaches by *pressure*: it grows around them, shapes their sleeping-spaces to their bodies, creates alcoves that fit them perfectly and then, as they grow, *stop* fitting, forcing them to find new alcoves, to re-learn the space, to adapt. The wood teaches by *temperature*: the cold of the walls, the warmth of the roots, the way the difference between them maps the interior of the gall in a way the eyes cannot. The wood teaches by *sound*: the drip-pattern, the click of beetles, the silence of the centipedes — a language of presence and absence that the children learn before they learn the pidgin speech of the adults.

The children play. Their play is the play of the gall: they climb the partitions, they crawl into the galleries the beetles have abandoned, they follow the spiders' silk lines, they catch the moths — gently, cupping them in their hands, feeling the warmth of the light, releasing them. They eat the biofilm. It is not food — it does not sustain them — but they eat it anyway, scraping it from the walls with their fingernails, and it tastes like the gall smells: green, wet, alive. They have the marks of the centipedes on their hands. They have the pallor of the biofilm on their skin. They have the silence of the wood in their movements.

They will be the next Gall-Keeper. One of them. Or both. Or neither — perhaps they will go outside, into the fen, into the world beyond the fen, into whatever exists beyond the forest and the canopy and the dim. The Forager hopes so. The Gall-Keeper does not. The Gall-Keeper says: *They are already wood. They just don't know it yet.*

The children do not hear this. They are in their alcoves, curled against the grain, the wood closing above them, the blue glow on their skin, the fog in their lungs, the drip in their ears. They are sleeping. They are growing. The gall is growing around them.

It is the thickening. The fog is dense. The biofilm is dim. The centipedes are out, ranging across the walls in their silent, fluid, leg-driven glide. The beetles are clicking. The spiders are still. The moths are gone. The Forager is in her alcove. The Weaver is in hers. The Light-Gardener is pressing a new scrap of biofilm to a bare patch of wall, and the scrap is taking hold, and the glow is spreading, and in the deep alcove, the Gall-Keeper has her hand on the wall, and she is feeling the wood, and the wood is feeling her, and the distinction between the two is — as it has always been, as it will always be — a matter of opinion.

The gall is a room. The gall is a town. The gall is a wound that has healed into a world.

It is very small. It is very full. It is very dark, and very wet, and very cold, and very alive.

It is enough.
