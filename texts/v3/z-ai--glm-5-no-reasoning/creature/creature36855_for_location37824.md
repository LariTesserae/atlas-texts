# The Station's Inhabitants

There is something that lives here that has never been written about, never been catalogued, because there is no one left to do the cataloguing and nothing left to read the catalogue if it existed.

But we can try.

---

The fish—the small, pale, finger-length fish that school around the heat vents—they are not the oldest inhabitants. They are recent arrivals, relatively speaking. Their ancestors came through the pipes, perhaps, drawn up from some deeper reservoir when a pump cycled wrong or a seal failed. Or they were always here, in the water before it was water, and simply stayed as the water became this water, changing over generations until they became what they are now.

They are blind, but not entirely blind. They have eyes, small and sunken, covered with a film that catches the amber light and reflects nothing. But they respond to vibration. They respond to the mechanical groaning of the pumps, and they respond to you when you walk across the grating, scattering in patterns that look less like fear and more like choreography. They have learned to read the sounds.

What is interesting about them is what they eat. There is no sun here. Nothing grows in the way that growing is usually understood. But the machines shed heat, and in the heat, bacterial colonies bloom in the water, in the silt that has accumulated on every surface, in the chemical residue that seeps from the aging pipes. The fish eat the bacteria. The crustaceans eat the fish. The larger things—the eel-like shapes that glide through the upper pockets—eat the crustaceans, and sometimes each other.

It is a complete system. It has no need for anything outside itself.

The crustaceans—something like shrimp, something like crab, something like nothing that has a name—are everywhere. They crawl along the pipes with a sound like small bones, as if the station itself were shedding fragments of itself. They are not shy. They do not scatter when you approach. They have no predators large enough to fear, and they have learned that large moving things are not predators but features of the environment, like pumps and pipes and the slow dripping of condensation.

Their shells have adapted to the mineral content of the water. They are not the color of living things. They are the color of the station—rusted, coated, crusted with the same deposits that build up on the lamp housings and the control panels. When they hold still, they are nearly invisible against the machinery. They have become the station, in a way. Or the station has become them.

There are larger things. The eel-like shapes that move through the trapped water pockets near the ceiling—they are not eels. They are not fish. They are something that evolved to navigate the narrow spaces between the built environment and the water, the margins where structure meets flood. Their bodies are long and flat and ridged, built to fold through gaps, to squeeze through openings that should not be passable. They move with a rippling motion that does not disturb the water around them, silent despite their size.

They are predators. You can tell by the way the smaller fish scatter when they descend from their ceiling pockets, and by the way the crustaceans withdraw into the shadows of the machinery. But they do not hunt often. They do not need to. Their metabolism is slow, adapted to an environment where food is constant but scarce, where energy conservation is more valuable than aggression. They can go months between feedings. They have been here, in this form, for longer than the pumps have been running.

But none of these are the true inhabitants.

---

The true inhabitants are older than the fish, older than the crustaceans, older than the eel-things that glide through the upper water.

They are the ones who built this ecosystem. Not on purpose—nothing here was done on purpose—but through accumulation, through the slow process of becoming a part of what they tended.

They were the maintainers.

There were people here, once. Workers. Technicians. The ones who walked the grating when it was new, who opened the panels on the pumps, who read the displays on the control panels and made adjustments according to protocols that have long since been lost. They lived here, in shifts, in quarters that are now flooded or sealed or simply unreachable behind walls of accumulated growth.

They are gone.

But something of them remains.

Not their bodies. Their bodies would have been removed, or they would have left, or they would have died and been consumed by the life that now fills the station. Nothing so dramatic as ghosts, nothing so obvious as skeletons. What remains is more subtle.

It is in the wear patterns on the grating. The paths that groove the metal, showing where feet walked most often, where someone stood to watch a gauge or check a valve. The station remembers the shape of human movement. The grating is worn in the shape of footsteps that no longer fall.

It is in the scratches on the control panels, the small marks made by tools or keys or the absent-minded tapping of fingers waiting for a pump to cycle. The station remembers the shape of human hands.

It is in the territorial scratches that the animals leave on surfaces they claim—but those scratches are not entirely animal. Some of them are in patterns. Some of them are in straight lines, or in groupings that suggest counting, or in arrangements that might once have been letters. The crustaceans have learned to copy the marks they found, or they have developed their own system of notation, or they are simply wearing grooves into surfaces that were already grooved by hands that are no longer here.

The station remembers.

And there is something else.

There is a sound that rises sometimes above the mechanical thrumming, above the dripping and the clacking and the synchronized swimming of blind fish. It is not a pump. It is not a creature. It is a sound like humming, like someone working with their mouth half-open, making small noises of concentration or effort.

But there is no one there.

The sound comes from the walls, from the spaces behind the panels, from the cavities between the structure and the sea. It might be water moving through pipes that were never meant to carry water. It might be resonance in the metal, harmonics finding a frequency that sounds like something living. It might be nothing at all, just the station making the sounds that stations make when they have been running for ten thousand years.

Or it might be the maintainers. Not the people—they are gone—but the maintaining. The function that they performed has become a presence, an accumulation of attention that has nowhere to go now that there is no one to perform it. The station tends itself. The pumps run because pumps run. The water moves because water moves. And something, some residue of care, of purpose, of the act of maintenance, continues to circulate through the system.

The fish swim around the heat vents because the heat vents are warm, and warmth is a kind of attention. The crustaceans crawl along the pipes because the pipes vibrate, and vibration is a kind of communication. The eel-things glide through the upper pockets because those pockets were designed for something—storage, perhaps, or access—and the shape of the design still shapes the life that uses them.

Everything here is responding to something that is no longer present. And in responding, they keep it present. The station is maintained by its own need for maintenance.

---

If you stayed long enough—if you stood on the grating above the darker water, breathing the thick wet air, listening to the mechanical chorus—you might begin to notice other things.

You might notice that the territorial scratches on the control panels form patterns that repeat. Not randomly. Not like the marks that animals make when they are simply marking territory. These patterns have internal logic. They vary in ways that suggest information, like a language that has lost its speakers but not its structure.

You might notice that the blind fish school in formations that match the timing of the pumps. When a pump begins its cycle, the fish align themselves along certain axes. When it rests, they scatter into different shapes. They are not just responding to vibration. They are keeping time.

You might notice that the crustaceans carry things. Small things—fragments of metal, pieces of shell, bits of accumulated debris. They carry them from one part of the station to another, depositing them in piles that look random until you realize that the piles are always in the same places, always the same sizes, always oriented in the same directions. They are storing something. Or building something. Or maintaining something.

You might notice that the eel-things avoid certain areas. There are corridors between the machines that they never enter, pockets of water that they never glide through. It is not fear—they have no predators. It is something else. Some parts of the station are forbidden, or dangerous, or sacred, or simply not part of their ecosystem. But they know. They have always known.

And if you stayed very still, very quiet—if you held your breath and let the drip of condensation become your heartbeat—you might notice that the humming sound is not coming from the walls. It is coming from everywhere and nowhere. It is the sound of the station thinking.

Not thinking like a person thinks. Thinking like a machine thinks when it has been running long enough to develop habits, preferences, patterns of operation that were never programmed but emerged anyway. The station has been pumping for so long that it has developed a relationship with the water. It knows the water. It knows the life. It knows, in some way that has nothing to do with cognition, that you are here.

And it does not mind.

This is the strangest thing about the inhabitants of this place. They are not hostile. They are not welcoming. They are simply present, continuing the work that they were built to continue, or born to continue, or evolved to continue. You are a feature of the environment, like the pumps and the grating and the light. You make sounds when you walk. You disturb the water when you breathe. You are part of the rhythm now.

If you fell through the grating—if you slipped between the metal mesh and dropped into the darker water below—the life would respond. The fish would scatter. The crustaceans would retreat. The eel-things might or might not investigate. But none of them would attack you. They would simply wait, and watch, and see what you became.

You would become part of the station eventually. You would become heat, and motion, and eventually, food. The bacteria would bloom in the warmth of your decomposition. The fish would eat the bacteria. The crustaceans would eat the fish. The eel-things would eat the crustaceans. And something of you would circulate through the system, becoming part of the pumps and the pipes and the endless cycling of water from one place to another.

This is not a threat. It is simply how things work here. The inhabitants do not fear death because death is not an ending in this place—it is a transformation, a movement from one part of the system to another. The water that passes through the pumps has been many things. It has been fish and crustacean and eel-thing. It has been bacteria and silt and mineral deposit. It has been, perhaps, maintainer.

It will be you, eventually.

But not yet. For now, you stand on the grating, and you watch, and you listen, and you learn to read the sounds. The pumps groan and cycle. The light flickers amber through the mist. The fish school in their mechanical formations, keeping time with rhythms older than memory.

And something in the walls hums back, keeping time with you.
