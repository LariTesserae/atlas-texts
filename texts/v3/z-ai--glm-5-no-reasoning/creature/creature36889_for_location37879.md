# The Keepers of Vaal-Mon

They are not built. They are not born. They *condense*.

In the earliest days of exploration—if "exploration" is the word for those first soft intrusions of heat‑dazed climbers stumbling through the basalt ravines—the Keepers were assumed to be statues. There were seven of them in the recorded survey, frozen in various attitudes throughout the upper plazas, their forms suggesting something like a cross between a crouching human and a coiled fern. Their skin was the same screaming chlorophyll green as the fronds. Their bodies were wrapped in the same thick vines. Their eyes were closed, or perhaps absent entirely—smooth, green planes where features should have been. They did not move. They did not respond to sound, to touch, to the knife‑edges of the surveyors' tools. They were catalogued as geological curiosities—"anthropomorphic vegetative concretions"—and left alone.

But the surveyors returned, years later, and found nine.

The Keepers do not move in any way that a camera can catch. Time‑lapse footage shows nothing—only the same green stillness, hour after hour, the same crouching immobility. But between visits, they shift. They reposition. They are found in different plazas, different ravines, sometimes closer to the cenotes, sometimes higher, nearer the caldera's lip. They are never seen in transit. They are never encountered mid‑movement. They exist in the locked groove of Vaal‑Mon's frozen time, and so they, too, are frozen—but frozen in motion, like a single frame of a film that advances only when no one is watching.

Their bodies are difficult to describe because they are difficult to see. In the diffuse, overcast light of the plateau, where the steam hangs in permanent suspension and the ferns block the sky, the Keepers are effectively camouflaged. They are the color of the place. Their flesh has the texture of moss over dense, fibrous muscle—not plant, not animal, but something that straddles the biological fence. When touched (and they have been touched, though the touching brings a deep, cellular wrongness that lingers for days), their skin is warm—warmer than the ambient heat, as if they hold a private furnace somewhere in their cores. They are damp, always, coated in the condensation that covers everything, but their moisture seems to come from *inside*. They weep, like the mountain. Slowly. Continuously. From every pore.

Their faces are the most unsettling aspect. Or rather, their *absence* of faces. Where features should be—eyes, nose, mouth—there is only a smooth, green surface, broken occasionally by shallow depressions that might be the memory of a mouth, the echo of an eye socket. They have no ears. They have no nostrils. And yet they seem to perceive. When a visitor approaches, the nearest Keeper will often orient itself—slowly, imperceptibly—turning its blank face toward the source of the disturbance. It does not react. It does not flee or attack. It simply *acknowledges*, with the patient stillness of a stone that knows it will outlast whatever stands before it.

And what are they? The scholars who have studied Vaal‑Mon (and there are few; the plateau is remote, the climb brutal, the environment lethal to the unprepared) have proposed many theories. Some suggest they are the final stage of the city's plant life—the ferns and vines and mosses grown ambitious, organized, self‑aware. Others argue they are what remains of the *original* inhabitants of Vaal‑Mon—a people who built no buildings because they needed none, who carved no history because they had none, who lived in perfect equilibrium with the weeping mountain until they became indistinguishable from it. A third, more radical school proposes that the Keepers are not individuals at all, but *organs*—mobile, semi‑sentient extensions of the plateau itself, white blood cells in the body of a landscape that does not recognize infection but recognizes intrusion.

What is known: they tend the pools.

The eyeless, voiceless amphibians that slither in the gutters—the things that are more intestine than creature—exist in a symbiotic relationship with the Keepers. They are never found in the same pools. The amphibians colonize the shallow, stagnant collections; the Keepers gather around the deep, flowing cenotes, the places where the water falls with the most force. They do not drink. They do not hunt. But they *position* themselves—always oriented toward the water, always crouched at the edge, as if listening to something in the roar that softer ears cannot hear.

And they hum.

The sound of Vaal‑Mon is deafening—the eternal, unchanging 2.7 roar of falling water that vibrates the teeth and hums in the chest. But the Keepers add to it. When the surveyors first slept on the plateau (a miserable, sweat‑drenched experience, the air too thick to breathe comfortably), they reported a second layer to the soundscape—a tone beneath the roar, lower than the baseline frequency of the falls, that seemed to come from everywhere and nowhere. It was only on the third night that they realized the tone came from the Keepers. All nine—seven, at the time—were humming. The same note. In unison. A sound below sound, felt more than heard, that harmonized with the water in a way that suggested not communication, but maintenance. The Keepers were *holding* the frequency. They were keeping the groove locked.

Because Vaal‑Mon, as the description notes, does not change. The erosion is a settled 1.6. The vegetation does not grow or wither. The heat does not rise or fall. The falls pour with the exact volume they always have. This is not natural. Entropy does not permit such perfect stillness. Something, somewhere, is exerting force to maintain the equilibrium—and the Keepers, with their unchanging hum, their static positioning, their patient orientation toward the places where the water falls hardest, are the most likely candidates.

They are not worshippers. They are not guardians. They are *choir*.

There is a fourth theory about the Keepers, the one that no scholar will put in print but that whispers through the few expeditions that have returned from Vaal‑Mon. It draws from the observation that the Keepers are always the right size for their environment—never taller than the ferns that surround them, never wider than the ravines they crouch in, their limbs folded to fit the basalt arches as if they were grown into the spaces rather than placed there. The theory: the Keepers are not separate from the city at all. They are its *memories*. Every major plaza, every significant gorge, every place where the water falls with particular force has a Keeper because every major feature of Vaal‑Mon *remembers* being something else. The plateau was not always a city. The city was not always suspended. The suspension was not always still. At some point—before the locked groove, before the eternal now—Vaal‑Mon changed. It cooled. It grew. It wept. And the places where the change was most dramatic left an imprint, a condensation of significance, that slowly, over centuries of wet heat and roaring water, *precipitated* into a form.

A Keeper.

They do not communicate. They do not respond to speech or gesture or presence. They are not aware of you—or rather, they are aware of you in the way a mountain is aware of rain, which is to say, not at all. But if you stand beside one, close enough to feel the heat radiating from its moss‑covered skin, close enough to hear the sub‑sonic hum vibrating in its chest, you might begin to understand something about Vaal‑Mon that the surveys cannot capture.

The city is not empty.

It is full. It is *saturated*—with water, with heat, with life, with time. And the Keepers are the proof that time, even locked, even frozen, leaves a mark. They are the mark. They are the shape of a moment that refused to pass.

---

The expedition of 1987 counted eleven Keepers. The expedition of 2003 counted fourteen. Last year, a drone survey of the lower gorges—places too narrow and too wet for humans to traverse—revealed seventeen distinct forms, all crouched at the edges of permanent pools, all facing the falling water, all humming the same sub‑sonic note.

The city is growing. Or remembering. Or both.

And the Keepers—condensing, dripping, humming—are waiting for something that will never arrive, because in Vaal‑Mon, nothing arrives. Nothing leaves. Nothing changes.

They are the stillness that holds the stillness together.
