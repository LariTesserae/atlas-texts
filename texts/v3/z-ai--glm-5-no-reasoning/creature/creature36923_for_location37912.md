# The Residents of Veridian Sump

## The Permanent Population

They are not what you expect. You expect, perhaps, recluses — people hiding from something, people who chose the furthest possible point from civilization. You expect mystics, maybe, or failed revolutionaries. You do not expect the people you find.

The permanent residents of Veridian Sump are, for the most part, *maintenance workers*. Not in name — they call themselves by various terms, none of which translate cleanly — but in function. They maintain a machine they do not fully understand. They are the janitors of a four-thousand-year-old computer that runs on frog metabolism and black water.

The population breaks roughly into three groups: the Bloodline families, who claim descent from the original engineers; the Arrived, who came from elsewhere and stayed; and the Water-touched, a small number of people whose physiology has been altered by long-term exposure to the computing substrate.

### The Bloodline Families

There are five families who trace their lineage to the engineers. They keep records — not written, because paper rots and even digital storage fails, but *grown*. Each family maintains a memory-orchid, a variety engineered — or perhaps evolved, the distinction is unclear — to store neural patterns in its root-system. When a family member dies, their brain tissue is introduced to the orchid's root-mass. What happens next is not fully understood, even by the families, but the orchid incorporates something. Pattern. Memory. Personality fragments. The families consult the orchids by entering a meditative state in their presence, and sometimes — not always, not reliably — they receive impressions. Visions. Advice from ancestors who died centuries ago.

The accuracy of this transmission is hotly debated.

The oldest family is the Veyln, who have lived in the same root-chamber for an estimated two thousand years. They are recognizable by a slight webbing between their fingers — not a genetic trait, exactly, but a developmental response to childhood immersion in the black water. The Veyln children are introduced to the water young. Their bodies adapt. The webbing helps them swim, which they do frequently; the Veyln are the primary harvesters of the blind fish, and the only residents who can stay submerged for more than two minutes without surfacing.

The Veyln are also, according to certain measurements, no longer entirely human. Their blood chemistry has shifted to match the ion content of the sump water. They can process dissolved oxygen through their skin at rates that shouldn't be possible. When they die, their bodies decompose differently — more slowly, and with patterns of fungal growth that match the bioluminescent moss on the root-walls. The other families find this unsettling but don't discuss it directly.

The youngest family is the Tethra, who arrived only eight hundred years ago — "arrived" meaning their ancestor was an Arrived who married into a Bloodline family, then broke away to establish a new lineage. The Tethra are the market-keepers. They manage the floating platforms, adjudicate disputes between vendors, and maintain the communication system that uses the bioluminescent mat. They are, in practice if not in theory, the closest thing Veridian Sump has to a government.

### The Arrived

At any given time, maybe fifteen to twenty residents are Arrived — people who came from outside and stayed. Their reasons vary.

There is a woman who was a computational linguist, once, on the mainland. She came to study the tree's communication patterns. She has been here eleven years. She no longer publishes. She no longer leaves the root-bowl. She spends her days sitting on platforms at the water's edge, watching the floating mat's patterns, taking notes in a code she invented that only she can read. She believes the tree is trying to speak. She believes she is close to understanding.

There is a man who killed someone, elsewhere, and fled here because the Sump has no extradition and no communication with outside law enforcement. He has been here twenty-three years. He works the root-platforms, checking for structural weaknesses, reinforcing growth where the tree is thinning. He is quiet. He avoids conflict. The other residents know his history and have decided, collectively, that twenty-three years of honest labor and isolation is punishment enough, or that it is not their place to judge, or that the tree will do whatever it is going to do with him regardless of their opinions.

There is a child who was born elsewhere and whose parents brought her here when she was three. She is now twelve. She has no memory of the outside world. She can read the bioluminescent mat's patterns faster than any adult. She can navigate the root-system in complete darkness by listening to the frogs. The other residents treat her with a mixture of affection and unease — she is clearly, visibly becoming something other than what she was born to be, and no one is certain what that means.

There is an elderly man who came to die. He had an illness that was painful and untreatable, and he heard that the Sump had water with strange properties. He has been here six months. His illness has not progressed. He is not better, exactly, but he is not worse. He sits in his root-chamber and watches the orchids grow and does not seem to mind that his death has been indefinitely postponed. The water, he says, is deciding something about him.

### The Water-touched

Three residents are Water-touched. Their bodies have incorporated the nanoscale mesh from the sump water. It is not clear how this happens — the mesh was designed to integrate with plant vascular systems, not animal nervous systems, but something in three people's physiology allowed absorption.

The most visibly affected is a man named Perrin, who has lived in the Sump for forty-one years. His skin has patches of iridescence that shift color like the floating mat. His nervous system generates electrical fields that the tree can read. He knows things he should not know — when a platform is about to fail, when the frogs are about to call, when the water level will rise or fall. He says it is like hearing whispers from everywhere at once. He says the tree has opinions. He refuses to elaborate.

The Water-touched are not leaders. They are not consulted for governance. They are, in a way that the residents don't discuss but all understand, *part of the system*. When Perrin walks across the floating mat, it doesn't ripple with light — it goes dark beneath his feet, absorbing his weight silently. The frogs don't call near him. The fish surface when he approaches the water's edge.

He is lonely in a way that has nothing to do with being alone.

## The Temporary Population

On trading days, the population doubles. The floating vendors arrive in their reed-and-mycelium rafts, poling through the root-channels from wherever the water leads.

### The Vendors

They are not a unified group. They come from different places, different cultures, different biomes. What they share is knowledge of the waterways — the channels and routes that connect the Sump to other places, other markets, other worlds. They are the Sump's connection to everything outside.

One vendor sells preserved frog secretions — a psychoactive compound that is legal nowhere else but unregulated here. The secretions produce visions that users describe as "remembering things that never happened to you." The vendor has customers who come regularly, who pay in cloth or tools or information, who leave with small vials and return months later looking changed.

One vendor sells cuttings from the orchids. This is technically forbidden — the orchids are supposed to stay in the Sump — but the prohibition is unenforceable and the vendor pays the Tethra family a percentage to look the other way. The cuttings are valuable. They carry the nanoscale mesh in their vascular systems. Planted elsewhere, they grow into orchids with strange properties — flowers that glow, leaves that taste of mathematics, roots that tap into groundwater and produce readouts of mineral content in patterns that almost look like language.

One vendor sells nothing. They arrive, moor their raft, and sit in the market all day. They listen. They watch. They leave at dusk. They have been coming for seventeen years. No one knows what they want.

### The Pilgrims

Occasionally, people arrive who are not vendors. They come for the tree. They come to study it, or to commune with it, or simply to be near something older and stranger than anything they have known.

They are usually disappointed. The tree does not communicate in ways they expect. It does not grant wishes or dispense wisdom. It thinks, slowly, in its own four-thousand-year language, about things that may or may not include human concerns.

Most pilgrims stay a few days and leave. A few stay longer. A very few stay forever, becoming Arrived.

## The Children

There are currently seven children living permanently in the Sump. Their experience of the place is fundamentally different from the adults'.

They grow up with the tree as a presence, a neighbor, a fact of life. They do not find the bioluminescence remarkable. They do not flinch when the frog chorus starts. They treat the moving platforms and shifting walkways as normal, adapting without complaint when the root-system rearranges itself.

They play games that adults don't understand. One game involves stamping patterns on the floating mat and watching the patterns interact — a kind of hydro-biological light show that the children seem to read for meaning. Another involves catching frogs and releasing them in specific sequences, then watching where they go. The children say the frogs "go home" when released correctly. Adults have not determined what this means.

The children are not afraid of Perrin. They visit him in his root-chamber and sit with him while he listens to the tree's whispers. They say he tells them stories, but when asked what stories, they say the stories don't have words. They say the stories *are*.

## The Dead

The dead of Veridian Sump do not leave.

Some are in the memory-orchids of the Bloodline families — fragments of pattern, echoes of personality. Some are in the root-walls, their bodies given to the tree for incorporation, their mass converted to new growth. Some are in the water itself, dissolved into the black sump, circulating through the channels, becoming part of the computation.

There is a belief — not a religion, exactly, but a practice — that the dead continue to contribute. That they are still working. That the tree has use for them even after their hearts have stopped. The residents speak of the dead as if they are still present, still employed, still part of the community.

There is a man who drowned in the sump twenty years ago. His body was never recovered — the water is deep in places, and the root-system has channels that lead down into darkness. But his voice, residents say, can sometimes be heard in the frog chorus. Not words. Just a quality of sound. A familiar frequency.

His widow still lives in the Sump. She says she hears him most clearly at dusk, when the frogs are warming up. She says he sounds content.

## The Social Texture

Veridian Sump is not a commune. There is property — root-chambers are owned, passed down, bought and sold. There is trade — the market depends on it. There is hierarchy — the Bloodline families have status that Arrived do not, and the Water-touched occupy a position that is neither above nor below but *beside*.

But there is also interdependence that softens these distinctions. Everyone needs the water-filtration system. Everyone depends on the root-platforms staying stable. Everyone listens when Perrin says a walkway is going to fail. Everyone contributes to the maintenance of the tree, because the tree is the substrate of everything.

Disputes are resolved by the Tethra family, whose adjudication powers are a matter of tradition rather than formal authority. But major decisions — whether to accept a new resident, whether to close the market during an illness, whether to try to communicate with the tree about a concerning growth pattern — are made collectively, in gatherings that happen on the central platform, by consensus or something close to it.

There is no currency that is universal. Trade happens in goods and services and promises. The most valuable commodities are information (about what the tree is doing, about what the water contains, about who is arriving and what they carry) and time (hours spent maintaining root-platforms, monitoring water quality, tracking frog populations).

There is a gift economy that operates alongside the market economy. A resident who catches fish gives some to neighbors. A resident who grows medicinal moss shares it freely. This is not altruism exactly — it is reputation-building, future-insurance, a way of ensuring that when you need something, someone will provide it.

## The Unsaid

There are things the residents do not discuss.

They do not discuss what happens when someone is Water-touched. It is not clear whether it can be induced, whether it is random, whether it is something the tree chooses. There is fear around it. The Water-touched are useful, but they are also different in ways that go beyond physical changes.

They do not discuss what the tree is computing. To discuss it would be to admit that they don't know, and not-knowing is a vulnerability they prefer not to acknowledge. They live inside a computer they can't read, maintained by engineers who have been dead for millennia, executing programs whose purpose is unknown.

They do not discuss leaving. Most Arrived could leave if they chose. The waterways connect to outside. But leaving means abandoning the life they've built, the adaptations they've made, the connection to something larger than themselves. It also means facing what they've become — citizens of a place that exists nowhere else, changed in ways that might not be reversible.

And they do not discuss the feeling — the one that comes sometimes, at dusk, when the frog chorus builds and the moss begins to glow and the tree's hum vibrates in the bones — that the tree is not just computing but *waiting*. That something is building toward a culmination. That four thousand years is, for something like the tree, barely childhood.

The feeling passes. The frogs stop calling. The market resumes.

But the feeling accumulates, in the spaces between thoughts, in the way residents pause when the tree-think becomes audible, in the way they look at their children and wonder what kind of world they will inherit when the tree finally finishes whatever it is doing.

The residents of Veridian Sump are not philosophers. They are maintenance workers, traders, harvesters, parents. They live their lives.

But they live their lives inside something that has been thinking for four thousand years. And they know — in the way you know things about your own body, not with your mind but with your cells — that whatever it is thinking about, it includes them.
