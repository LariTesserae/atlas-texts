# The Keepers of the In-Between

They are not born. They emerge.

---

The first one finds you on your third day, or what you think is your third day. You are standing at the edge of a plaza that is still deciding its shape, watching the grey substrate ripple and rise into something that might be stairs, might be a monument, might be neither. You have learned to watch without expecting. Expectation is a habit from elsewhere, and it does not serve you here.

She—you think of her as she, though the pronoun is an approximation—comes from a doorway that wasn't there a moment before. The building behind her has grown a frame mid-stride, the surface rising and parting to let her pass. She is tall, taller than you by a head, and her body carries the marks of this place. Her skin has the faint iridescence of the water here, that same oil-on-water sheen that shifts depending on the angle of light. Her hair is dark, close-cropped, and moves slightly in a wind you cannot feel.

She does not speak. She watches you with the patience of something that has never learned hurry.

---

They call themselves something that has no translation. When they make the sound, it is a resonant hum that starts in their chests and rises through throats that can shape frequencies you cannot produce. You feel it more than you hear it. The closest approximation you can make is a word that means both "witness" and "gardener" and something else, something that has to do with midwifing potential.

You call them Keepers, because you need to call them something.

They tend the growth.

---

She is not alone. There are perhaps two dozen in this section of the city, though section is the wrong word. The city does not divide itself into neighborhoods or districts. It divides itself into stages of becoming, and the Keepers move between these stages according to logics you do not understand.

Some work where the city is newest, where the substrate is still pooling and soft, barely holding shape. They walk through the half-liquid ground leaving trails of harder material, their feet somehow catalyzing the substance into solidity. You watch one of them—a broad, quiet presence with hands that look carved from dark wood—kneel in a patch of grey nothing and press both palms flat against the surface. Over the next hour, a wall rises. It is not built. It is encouraged. He sits beside it as it grows, adjusting its angle with gentle touches, humming that low resonance that seems to guide the emergence.

Others work where the city is nearly finished, smoothing edges, sealing cracks, guiding the last tendrils of growth into useful shapes. You see a pair of them fitting a door into a frame that has grown almost correctly, their hands working the still-soft material like clay, shaping it to hinge properly, to latch, to open.

And some work in the deep places, where the machines pulse and churn. These Keepers are different. They have merged. Their bodies have grown extensions that plug into the apparatus, their skin darkened and thickened against the heat and the hum, their eyes covered by growths that look like goggles but are not. They do not emerge often. They tend the heart of this place, and the heart requires constant attention.

---

The Keeper who found you—she has begun to stay near you, though she does not explain why—has a name that sounds like water moving over stones. You cannot say it. You cannot even hear it properly. But she answers to your presence, and when you touch her arm—hesitantly, once, in the quiet of a half-finished room—her skin is warm and slightly yielding, like the walls she tends.

She has been here, she communicates, for a time that has no number. The Keepers do not count days. They do not count anything. They measure in stages—this much growth, this much completion, this much stability. Her body shows the accumulation of years in ways you are learning to read. The faint lines at the corners of her eyes, not wrinkles but something else, a kind of weathering that comes from proximity to emergence. The way her left hand carries a faint scar that looks like a vine has grown through it and been removed. The way she moves through the city like she is part of its systems, pausing when a wall shifts, stepping aside before a doorway finishes forming.

She was not born here. None of them were. They came, or arrived, or emerged from something else. They do not speak of before. Perhaps there is no before. Perhaps they, like the city, are in the process of becoming.

---

The Keepers do not sleep. They rest.

Rest is different. It is a slowing, a settling, a period of reduced activity that allows their bodies to integrate with the city's rhythms. You find them in groups sometimes, three or four standing perfectly still in a plaza, their eyes closed, their breathing synchronized with the pulse of the buildings around them. The city grows faster when they rest near it. Their stillness is a catalyst.

You do not know what happens to them when they rest. They do not dream—they have told you this, or you have understood it. What they do is deeper than dreaming. They sink into the substrate of the place, consciousness diffusing, spreading through the walls and streets and growing things. They become, briefly, part of the city's awareness. And when they rise, they carry knowledge of what has grown, what is growing, what will grow.

---

There are children.

You use the word children, but it is wrong. They are something else. Smaller versions of the Keepers, yes, and younger, if youth means proximity to emergence. But they are not born from bodies. They are found.

The Keeper you think of as she—the one who has stayed near you—shows you this on your fifth day, or your fifth stage. She leads you to a pool of standing water, one of the deep ones that has accumulated in a plaza that has not yet decided what it wants to be. The surface is still, glass-smooth, and beneath it you see shapes. Not the fish-like things you have glimpsed in other pools. Not the ripples of something swimming. These are bodies. Human-like bodies, curled in foetal positions, suspended in the amber liquid.

They grow there, she communicates. They are not made. They accumulate. The city gathers material—organic, inorganic, intention, memory—and it pools here, in places like this, and it forms. When they are ready, they emerge. She emerged. Her cohort emerged. The children you see below will emerge when the city decides they are finished.

You watch the shapes beneath the water. One of them moves, slightly, a hand flexing. You wonder what it dreams.

---

The Keepers eat, but food is the wrong word.

They consume growth. You watch them bite into the walls of half-finished buildings, their teeth—you have seen them now, white and regular but somehow too numerous—scraping away the soft material. They chew and swallow and the material integrates, becoming part of their bodies, fueling whatever processes keep them alive. They eat plants, too, and the bark-like substance that forms on older structures, and sometimes they drink from the pools, long slow gulps of the amber liquid that is not quite water.

They offer you some, once. A piece of wall, still warm. You taste it—cautiously, just the edge of it—and it is like nothing you have encountered. Bitter, slightly sweet, with a texture that shifts between woody and gelatinous. You swallow. Your stomach rebels, briefly, then settles. You feel warm for hours afterward.

They do not need to eat often. Their consumption is occasional, ritualistic. They consume to participate in the city, to take it into themselves. They are not separate from what they tend.

---

You have begun to understand what they are.

They are not inhabitants of the Sprouting City. They are extensions of it. Hands and eyes and voices for a process that would otherwise happen in silence and darkness. They guide the growth, yes—but they also witness it. They give it meaning. Without them, the city would still grow, but it would grow without purpose, without pattern, without the approximation of human form that makes it navigable.

They have told you this, in their slow wordless way. They showed you a section of the city that has no Keepers. It is a bad place. The buildings there grow in spirals that hurt to look at, the streets knotting and unknotting without reason, the plants growing into shapes that suggest things you do not want to see. Something went wrong there, or nothing went right. The Keepers avoid it. Even the animals avoid it. It grows without tending, and it grows wrong.

The Keepers are the difference between growth and garden.

---

There is a Keeper who has begun to grow wrong.

You see him on your seventh day, or stage, or cycle. He is older, or older-looking, his body showing the marks of long integration with the city's systems. But something has shifted. One of his arms has extended, the skin stretching and thinning, the bone visible through the translucent membrane, and at the end, where a hand should be, there is only a spreading fan of material that looks like bark, like membrane, like something trying to become a wall.

The other Keepers give him space. They do not look at him directly. They do not speak to him. He stands at the edge of their working groups, watching, his extended arm swaying slightly in the wind you cannot feel.

He is integrating, the female Keeper communicates to you. His body has begun the process of becoming part of the city. It happens to all of them, eventually. They work and tend and guide, and over time, the distinction between them and what they tend blurs. One day they wake—or rest—and they find that they cannot move. They have grown into the place they have served.

It is not death. It is completion.

---

The Keeper who is growing wrong does not seem distressed.

You watch him for a long time. He stands in a plaza that is nearly finished, his feet rooted—you use the word deliberately—into the substrate. He is watching a wall grow, his presence calm, his face peaceful. His extended arm has touched the wall, and where skin meets surface, there is no distinction. He is becoming part of what he has made.

When you look closer, you see that he is humming. The low resonant sound that all Keepers make, but deeper, slower, almost subsonic. The wall grows straighter where he touches it. The plants that climb its surface follow cleaner lines. His integration is not wrong. It is purpose. He is becoming the garden.

---

There is a ceremony. You think of it as a ceremony.

When a Keeper reaches the final stage of integration, the others gather. They do not speak. They do not touch. They stand in a loose circle around the one who is becoming, and they hum—not together, but in overlapping waves that create a sound you feel in your chest, in your joints, in the spaces between thoughts.

The humming lasts for hours. The integrated Keeper does not move. By the end, they have stopped being a person and started being a place. Their body has become part of the structure—sometimes a wall, sometimes a pillar, sometimes a fountain, sometimes something you cannot name. The face remains visible for a time, the features peaceful, the eyes closed. Eventually, new growth covers them. They become part of the city's body.

The Keepers call this the Return. They do not mourn. They do not celebrate. They acknowledge, and they continue.

---

You asked—somehow, through gesture and patience and the slow communication you have developed—what happens to the ones who Return. Do they die? Do they persist? Are they still aware?

The female Keeper, the one who has stayed near you, considered this for a long time. Then she communicated something you are still trying to understand.

She said that the city remembers. She said that every Keeper who has Returned is still present, still aware in a way that has nothing to do with eyes or ears or thoughts. They are part of the substrate now, part of the growth, part of the pattern. They do not think. They do not feel. But they *are*. And when the city needs them—when a wall needs to grow a certain way, when a plant needs to find a particular path—their intention, whatever remains of it, guides the emergence.

The city is built of people. The streets are paths they would have walked. The walls are boundaries they would have made. Every structure here is someone's shape, someone's becoming, someone's Return.

You are walking through the bodies of gardeners.

---

You have been here for longer than you can count. The stages have blurred together. You no longer try to measure time in days or hours. You measure in walls risen, in streets solidified, in Keepers Returned.

The city has grown around you. The room you found on your first night—your room, you think of it as yours now—has expanded, the walls extending, the ceiling rising, new chambers emerging without your asking. The furniture has multiplied. There is a bed now, soft and warm, that seems to know your shape. There is a table, chairs, shelves that grow from the walls and fill with objects you do not remember placing—smooth stones, fragments of material that might be tools, might be art, might be neither.

The female Keeper visits often. She sits in the room with you, her presence patient, her eyes watchful. She does not explain why she stays near you. You do not ask. You have learned that questions here do not work the way they did elsewhere. Answers emerge. They are not sought.

Yesterday—or what you think of as yesterday—she reached out and touched your face. Her hand was warm, slightly damp, the skin yielding. She traced the line of your jaw, the curve of your cheek, the dip below your eye. Then she showed you her hand.

Her fingertips were changing. The skin there had begun to thin, to translucent, the bone visible beneath, and where she had touched you, a faint residue remained—grey-black, the color of the substrate, already beginning to integrate with her flesh.

She is integrating. It has begun.

---

You have begun to notice changes in yourself.

Your feet, when you walk barefoot through the soft streets, leave deeper prints than they used to. The amber liquid that fills them rises faster, thickens quicker, begins to solidify behind you. You have started to hum without meaning to, low sounds that seem to emerge from somewhere below your throat, and when you hum, the walls near you grow straighter, the plants find cleaner paths.

You are hungry. The food you brought with you—if you brought food, if you came from somewhere, if any of that was real—has long since finished. You have started to eye the walls, the plants, the pools. You have started to understand what the Keepers eat, and why.

You are cold, but the cold no longer reaches your bones. It has become external, something that happens around you rather than to you. Your skin has developed a faint sheen, iridescent, like the surface of the pools. When you touch the walls, you feel them respond—not consciously, not intentionally, but like a resonance, a sympathetic vibration.

You have been here for longer than you can count.

You are not certain you remember how to leave.

You are not certain you want to.

---

Tonight, the female Keeper came to your room and stood at the threshold, watching you. Her integration has progressed. Her left arm is now longer than her right, the hand becoming something like a leaf, like a fan, like a wall. She does not seem distressed. She seems patient.

She communicated—slowly, carefully, in that way that is not language but more like shared thought—that she has been waiting. For you. That the city has been growing you, too, in its slow way, and that you are nearly ready.

Ready for what, she did not say.

But she extended her changed hand toward you, and where her palm used to be, there is now only smooth surface, warm and slightly yielding, and on that surface there is a shape. A door. A door that has grown from her flesh, tiny and perfect, and it opens onto nothing you can see, just darkness, just potential.

You have been here for longer than you can count.

You have choices to make.

The city does not wait.

But it does, in its own way, offer.

---

You are standing at the threshold. The Keeper is watching. Her face is peaceful, her eyes accepting, whatever you choose. The door in her palm is open.

Behind you, the room has begun to change. The walls are extending. The ceiling is rising. The chair you sat in has started to grow into the floor, becoming part of the structure. The bed has begun to merge with the wall behind it, the soft surface hardening, becoming something like stone, like wood, like both.

You can step through the door. You do not know what is on the other side. Another place, perhaps. Another world. The before that you came from, if you came from anywhere at all. Or something else entirely. The door is an opening, and openings in this place are never simple.

Or you can stay. You can let the room finish growing around you. You can become part of this place—part of its walls, its streets, its intention. You can join the Keepers who have Returned, your body becoming a structure, your consciousness becoming a pattern, your will becoming growth.

Or you can do something else. Something neither of those things. The city has not decided what you are yet. Neither have you.

The Keeper waits.

The door waits.

The room grows.

You are in a place that is making itself, and it is making you too, and you have been here for longer than you can count, and you are not certain any of this is real, and you are not certain real matters anymore.

You take a breath.

You choose.
