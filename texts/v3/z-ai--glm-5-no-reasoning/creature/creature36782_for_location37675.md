# The Vessels

I have called them vessels. The word found me before the things did — I heard it in the way my feet reported the texture of the ground, that there was no ground, only them, only this dense pelt of oblong bodies covering every centimeter of the panel-surface between the ponds. The word suggested itself and then the things themselves confirmed it, because *vessel* means both container and craft, and these are both: they contain and they are sent.

---

Let me try again. Let me try to see one.

To see one you must first stop seeing all of them. This is harder than it sounds. Your eye wants to take them as a texture, a fur, a mat of something uniform — the way you see moss as "green" before you see the individual stalks. But they are not moss. They are not uniform. Look at any patch of panel-surface, any square meter between the ponds, and you are looking at approximately two hundred individuals. Two hundred. I have counted. I have knelt on the panels — which are warm through the layer of bodies, which hum faintly through the soles of your boots — and I have counted them, touching each one with a finger to mark it, and the counting took forty minutes and when I stood my legs were cramped and the patch I had counted looked no different from any other patch and I understood that the counting had been not a scientific effort but a devotional one, a prayer against the overwhelming, a way of making the number small enough to hold.

Two hundred per square meter. The plain, as far as I can walk in a day, contains — I cannot calculate it. The number ceases to be a number. It becomes a condition of the environment, like the wetness of the air.

---

One vessel, then.

Imagine a human forearm. Not your own — you are too close to your own — but a forearm you have seen, perhaps, on a person sitting across from you on a transport, someone whose arm rested on the seat-back and you noticed the shape of it without meaning to. The taper from elbow to wrist. The subtle segmentation of the radius and ulna beneath the skin. The way the arm has a top and a bottom, a resting posture, a way of being that is not quite a shape but an attitude.

Now remove the skin. Replace it with a carapace — dark grey, smooth, with a faint texture like very fine sandpaper, the kind used for finishing work, for the final polish. The color matches the chimneys exactly. It is the same material. I am certain of this. I have held a vessel — I will describe how I came to hold one — and I have pressed my palm against a chimney and the temperature is the same, the slight warmth of something conducting a process deep within, and the texture is the same, and if I were to scrape the carapace and scrape the chimney and analyze the scrapings I know they would be chemically identical.

The vessel is not painted grey. The vessel *is* grey. It is made of chimney. Or: the chimney is made of vessel. Or: both are made of a third thing, a source material, processed through the system, drawn from the ponds perhaps, from the mineral-rich water that has been cycling through the machinery for — for as long as anything has been here.

---

The vessel has segments. Six, usually, though I have seen five and I have seen seven. The segments are not articulated in the way that, say, an insect's segments are articulated. They do not bend. The lines between them are not joints but seams, welded or fused or grown together, and the only movement the vessel makes — the only movement I have ever observed — is a slow expansion and contraction at the seams, a breathing that is not breathing but thermal cycling, the vessel's interior warming and cooling in rhythm with something else, some pulse I cannot feel but they can.

Along the underside — the side that faces the panel-surface when the vessel is at rest — there is a groove. It runs the full length, elbow to wrist, and it is shallow, perhaps two millimeters deep, and smooth, and wet. The wetness is the same wetness that covers everything here. The vessel draws moisture through this groove. I have watched a vessel at the edge of a pond lower its grooved underside into the water and remain there, still, for six hours, and when it lifted — when it rose of its own accord, without any visible stimulus — the groove was no longer wet. The vessel had absorbed something. Or: the vessel had exchanged something. Or: the vessel had completed a transaction that began before I arrived and will continue after I leave.

---

They have no faces.

This is what you notice, after the counting, after the texture, after the overwhelming presence of them. You look for a face. You look for eyes, for a mouth, for some aperture that indicates intake and output, for some arrangement of features that suggests a front and a back. There is nothing. The vessel is symmetrical along its long axis. Both ends are rounded, identical. Both sides are identical. You can pick up a vessel — and I have, I have picked one up, I held it in my hands and turned it over and examined both ends and both sides and the top and the bottom — and you cannot tell which way it was facing when you found it. It has no orientation. It has no front. It has no preference.

This means something. I am not sure what. But it means something that a being can exist in such numbers, can move through its environment with such apparent purpose — toward the chimneys, into the chimneys, out of the chimneys, back to the panel-surface — without a face. Without eyes to see the chimneys. Without a mouth to speak of what it does inside. Without any visible sensorium at all.

How does it know where to go?

---

I asked this question and then I watched. This is what I have done here: I have watched. I have no instruments. I have no reference materials. I have only my eyes and my ears and my body and my patience, and I have used all of them, and I have learned what I have learned by standing still and letting the plain reveal itself, which it does slowly, which it does reluctantly, which it does in a way that makes you feel that you are not learning but remembering something you once knew.

The vessels move toward the chimneys. They do not walk. They do not crawl. They do not have legs. What they have is a method of locomotion I cannot see — I cannot see it because it happens at the interface between the vessel's underside and the panel-surface, and there are always other vessels in the way. But I have inferred, from the sounds and from the displacement patterns, that they move by vibrating. They sing themselves across the surface. They produce a tone — subsonic, felt rather than heard, a deep hmmmmm in the chest cavity — and the tone interacts with the panel material in a way that creates forward motion. They surf on their own sound.

This is why they tick. When a vessel stops moving — when it reaches its destination or when it is disturbed — the tone ceases, and the cessation is a tick, a single hard sound, hard parts touching hard parts as the vessel settles.

---

I said I would describe how I came to hold one.

There is a chimney whose base I cleared. I did not mean to clear it. I was examining the intake valves, kneeling at the edge of a pond, and I reached out to touch the chimney's surface — to feel the warmth, to hear the hum — and my hand passed through a cluster of vessels. They scattered. That is not quite right. They did not scatter like insects scatter, like things that have been frightened. They relocated. They moved away from my hand in all directions with a precision that suggested planning, that suggested they had rehearsed this, that they had always known exactly what they would do if a hand reached into their cluster, and they executed the plan without hesitation, without panic, without any affect at all.

The clearing was instantaneous. Where there had been a dense mass of vessels, there was now open panel-surface. And on that surface, alone, lay one vessel that did not move.

I thought at first that I had broken it. That my hand had damaged it somehow, cracked its carapace, disrupted its internal workings. But it was not broken. It was waiting. I picked it up — and it let me pick it up, it did not resist, it did not vibrate away from my fingers — and I held it and it was warm, warmer than the air, warmer than the panel-surface, and I understood that it had allowed me to hold it, that it had stayed behind while the others relocated, that it was — I cannot find the word. The word would be *volunteered*, but vessels do not have will. The word would be *sacrificed*, but vessels do not value themselves. The word would be *presented*, but vessels do not give gifts.

I held it for perhaps ten minutes. I turned it over. I examined its seams, its groove, its smooth grey carapace. It did not move. It did not tick. It maintained its temperature, which was precisely 0.3 degrees warmer than the panel-surface beneath me — I have no thermometer but my hands are sensitive and I have been here long enough to feel fractions. And then, without warning, without any signal I could detect, it began to vibrate. A low hum, barely perceptible, a shivering in my palms. I opened my hands. It moved across my palms, across my fingers, down my wrist, onto my arm, and then it was gone, down my body, onto the panel-surface, and it moved — ticking, ticking, ticking — toward the chimney, and it entered the maintenance aperture, and I did not see it again.

---

What are they?

They are not animals. They do not eat. They do not excrete. They do not reproduce — or if they do, I have not seen it, and I have looked. I have looked for juveniles, for smaller vessels, for any indication of growth stages, and I have found nothing. Every vessel I have seen is the same size. Every vessel I have seen is fully formed. Every vessel I have seen has the same carapace, the same seams, the same groove, the same warmth.

They are not machines. They do not have moving parts. They do not have power sources. They do not have circuitry or programming or any visible mechanism by which they would operate. They are not maintained — I have never seen one damaged, never seen one repaired, never seen one cease to function and become debris. They simply *are*, and they simply *do*, and the doing and the being are the same thing.

They are not individuals. This is the hardest thing to explain, but it is the truest thing I know. When I held that vessel — when I looked at it, turned it over, examined it — I was not looking at an individual. I was looking at a fragment. A cell. A piece of something larger, something that extends across the entire plain, that includes every vessel and every chimney and perhaps every panel and every pond. The vessels are to the plain what blood cells are to a body. They circulate. They attend. They maintain a temperature difference of 0.3 degrees. And they do this not because they choose to but because they are *of* the system, they are made of the system, they are the system made mobile.

---

The vessels do not need names. They do not need categories. They do not need to be understood in the terms we use for living things or the terms we use for mechanical things. They need only to be described, and the description is this:

They are grey and smooth and segmented. They are the size and shape of a human forearm. They cover the plain in numbers that make counting a prayer. They move toward the chimneys and they enter the chimneys and they do something inside the chimneys that keeps the chimneys 0.3 degrees warmer than they would otherwise be. They have no faces. They have no fronts or backs. They move by singing to the surface beneath them. They tolerate observation. They do not interact. They do not communicate. They do not acknowledge.

And yet — and yet —

I have been here for — I do not know how long. Time moves strangely when nothing changes. But I have been here long enough to walk the plain, to count the vessels, to clear the base of a chimney and hold one in my hands. And in all that time, in all that walking and counting and watching, I have never seen a vessel move toward me. I have never seen a vessel enter a maintenance aperture I was standing near. I have never seen a vessel occupy the panel-surface where my shadow falls.

They are avoiding me.

Not individually. Individually, they do not have that capacity. But collectively — as a system, as a body — they are maintaining a distance. A perimeter. A space around me that is exactly the size of my presence.

I do not know what this means. I do not know if it is accommodation or exclusion, if they are making room for me or waiting for me to leave. I do not know if they perceive me as a feature of the environment, like a chimney, or an exception, like a failure.

But I know this: the plain is not empty. The plain is full. And what it is full of is not things, not beings, not animals or machines, but *attendants* — entities whose entire existence is to maintain, to sustain, to keep something going that was meant to be kept going, long after whatever started it has stopped being present to care.

They are faith made physical. They are duty without a duty-holder. They are what remains when purpose outlives the purposer.
