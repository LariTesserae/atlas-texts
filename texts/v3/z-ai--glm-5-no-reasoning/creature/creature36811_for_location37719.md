# The Keeper

He is not a man who was born here. He came from somewhere with sky, though the memory of sky has gone soft in him, become a thing he can recall the way you recall a dream: brightness, and distance, and the feeling of wind on the thin skin of his eyelids. That memory has been his companion for thirty years, maybe longer. He keeps it like a stone in his pocket, turning it over in his mind when the dark presses close.

He lives in the Rootworks.

He does not call it that. He calls it the line, or the works, or sometimes, when he speaks to himself in the dim hours of his cycle, he calls it *down here*. He has a vocabulary for the place that is specific in the way only a lone practitioner's vocabulary can be. The main arterial pipe is Old Singing. The junction where three smaller pipes converge is the Knot. The chamber with the central conduit—the one you found, the chrysanthemum-heart of the whole system—he calls the Body, and he goes there only when he must.

---

He is lean. Not starved—the roots provide, in their way, a starchy pulp that he has learned to harvest from the younger growth, and there are supply drops somewhere, scheduled arrivals he fetches from a point half a day's walk east, though these have grown infrequent and the boxes have begun to carry dust on their lids. He is lean the way someone is lean who walks fifteen kilometers a day underground, who climbs root-columns hand over hand, who carries tools and pipe-fittings and the weight of upkeep on his shoulders.

His hands are the most remarkable thing about him. They are thick-knuckled, scarred across every finger, the skin cracked deep at the joints but the calluses precise—a working man's hands, specific in their damage. The left thumb carries an old break that healed crooked. The right palm has a burn-scar from when he gripped a pipe that had grown too hot, a thing he won't do again. He can feel the vibration of the pumps through his hands the way a doctor feels a pulse. He reads the system through his palms. A change in frequency, a new rattle, a heat that wasn't there yesterday—his hands know before his mind does.

He carries a light. Not the sodium lamps—those are the system's, permanent, wired, humming their fixed amber. His light is a headlamp strapped to a hardhat so old the plastic has yellowed to match the sodium glow. The hardhat has a dent where he caught a falling root-segment eight years ago. He was unconscious for what he estimated to be four hours, based on his cycle-clock. He walked back to the Body with blood in his eyes and did the work that needed doing before he slept. There was no one to tell. There was no one to help.

---

He is not the first Keeper.

You saw the chair with dust on its seat. You saw the tools rusted solid. You saw the scarring on the root-columns where equipment was removed. There was someone before him, and perhaps someone before that. He has found their marks—a stencil-painted number sequence on the wall near the western junction, a journal page, paper-thin with rot, caught between root and pipe, with a handwriting that was not his own. The journal page said: *Valve 7 sticking again. Tap with the heel of the wrench, don't hit the housing.* He has never found Valve 7. He does not know if Valve 7 still exists.

He has his own rituals of marking. He carves a notch into root-bark at the start of each cycle. The notches are his calendar. He has lost count of how many times the count has started over. The root-bark has healed over some of his older notches, swallowing them, which means the system remembers him too, in its way.

There were others, and they are gone, and he has never learned where they went. This is the loneliness that lives in him most deeply—not the absence of company, but the absence of explanation. He does not know if the previous Keeper died down here or left. He does not know if he will die down here or leave. The question is irrelevant, mostly. The work does not pause for questions.

---

The work.

The pipes carry something. He has never been told what. The amber fluid in the clear plastic tubes—he has seen it, tapped it once, watched it pool viscous and gold in his cupped palm. It smelled like the root-smell amplified a thousandfold: sweet, vegetal, dense with something that made his hindbrain think of growth, of proliferation, of *more*. He did not taste it. He is not certain it would hurt him. He is not certain it wouldn't.

His work is this: the pipes vibrate, and sometimes they vibrate wrong. A fitting loosens. A bracket rusts through and the pipe sags, pressing into root-fiber that grows toward its warmth. The roots will grow around the pipes if he lets them. He has seen it: a pipe fully consumed by root-mass, the metal dimpling inward, crushed by slow vegetable pressure, the flow inside stopping, backing up, building heat and pressure until— He has seen what happens when a pipe bursts. Once. The sound was different from the ordinary roar. A sound like screaming, if metal could scream, and the amber fluid flooding the floor, pooling, and the roots drinking it *greedily*, growing visibly in the hours before he could contain the leak. He patched the pipe with a repair-clamp and watched the root-fiber retreat, sulking, from the spill. The system restored itself. The system always restores itself.

He is, he has come to understand, not maintaining the pipes *for* the system. He is maintaining the pipes *against* the system. Or rather, maintaining the balance between them. The roots want the pipes. The pipes want to run. Without him, the roots would digest the infrastructure in a decade, maybe less, and the flow would stop, and whatever the flow feeds—somewhere up there, somewhere out there, somewhere he cannot reach and has stopped trying to imagine—would go quiet.

He is the mediator of a marriage between living and industrial, and neither partner is fully in control.

---

His shelter is where you saw it: the corrugated metal work-shed leaning against the root-column. You saw it from outside. Inside, it is smaller than it looks, the roof pressed down by the root that pushed through, the walls bowed inward by years of slow pressure. He sleeps on a pallet of compressed root-sheath, which is not comfortable but is dry, mostly. He has a second lamp, battery-powered, charged by a line he spliced into the sodium-lamp cable. He has a pot. He has a knife. He has a set of tools maintained with an obsessive care—oiled, wrapped, stored in a cloth roll that he keeps elevated on a bracket to prevent the damp from finding them. The rusted tools you saw on the rack were the previous Keeper's. He keeps them out of something he would not call respect but which is, in its way, exactly that.

He has a routine. He wakes when his body tells him to wake, which is not morning or night but a rhythm he has learned to trust, a cycle that may or may not correspond to the surface. He eats. He walks. He checks the pipes in the near section—the stretch he can reach in four hours of walking. He listens. He touches. He tightens what is loose. He clears root-fiber from pipe-surfaces with the knife, slicing away the pale fibrous mat that creeps toward warmth. He checks the valves. He walks to the supply point when the schedule tells him to, which is less and less often, and the supplies that come are fewer. He returns. He eats. He sleeps. The noise never stops.

He talks to himself. Not much. A narration of the work: *Bracket's loose. Tighten that. This one's running hot. Feel that. Getting warm. Watch it.* Sometimes, in the deep of his cycle, when the loneliness becomes a pressure equal to the pump-roar, he talks to the roots. He says things like *not yet, not yet* when he slices their fibers from pipe-surfaces. He says *I know* when he passes a place where a root has grown around a pipe so completely that removal would kill both the pipe and the root. He does not know if the roots hear him. He suspects they do not care. He suspects they might.

---

His mind has changed down here. Not broken—he would be offended by the word, if anyone were here to use it—but *adapted*. His hearing has sharpened until he can distinguish individual pumps by their harmonic signatures, can hear a failing bearing three hundred meters away, can feel in his jaw when a valve sticks. His vision has adapted to the low-amber until he moves through the dark places between sodium lamps without slowing, reading the root-topography by feel and memory. His sense of smell, he thinks, has gone strange. He can smell the amber fluid through the pipes. He can smell when the root-system is growing— a green-tinged sweetness in the air that means active metabolism. He can smell himself, too, and has stopped caring about it.

He thinks in cycles now, not days. He thinks in pipe-sections, not landmarks. The space between his ears has become a map of the Rootworks, precise and detailed, a living atlas of vibration and pressure and root-growth that he consults by closing his eyes. When he tries to think of the surface—and he tries, sometimes, deliberately, the way you would exercise an atrophying muscle—the images come blurred and unconvincing. A field. A building. A face? Whose face? He turns the stone in his pocket. Brightness, and distance, and wind.

He is not unhappy. He is not happy. He is *present* in a way that neither word can contain. He is a component. He has accepted this. The system needed a maintenance presence, and he is the maintenance presence, and the work is the work, and it continues, and he continues, and the roots grow and the pipes sing and the dark is warm with industry and the damp is everywhere and the noise is the weather of his life.

---

Sometimes, in the deep reaches, he finds new things. A pipe he hasn't seen before, branching off from a junction he was certain he knew. A place where the root-ceiling has opened into a new chamber, small, wet, empty, the root-walls smooth and pale as bone. A tool he doesn't recognize, half-consumed by root-bark, the handle protruding like a bone from healed skin. He wonders, in these moments, whether the system is growing, or whether he is simply discovering its true scale. He suspects both.

Once, he found a boot. His size. In good condition, if you ignored the root-fiber threading through the eyelets. He took it. He felt no guilt. The previous Keeper would have done the same.

Once, he found a door. Metal, industrial, set into a root-wall so thick the bark had grown over the frame entirely. He spent a cycle trying to open it. The mechanism was frozen. He could hear nothing behind it. Eventually he moved on. He does not know if this is wisdom or cowardice. He has stopped asking the question.

---

He is aging. He knows this because his knees have started to protest the longer walks, and his breathing has grown louder in his own ears, and the hair on his arms has grayed, which he noticed one cycle when the amber light caught it at the right angle. He has no mirror. He does not need one. The root-walls, when wet, reflect a smear of a figure that is enough.

He has thought about what happens when he can no longer do the work. He does not have an answer. The system, he suspects, will find a way. It found him. It will find someone else, or it won't, and the roots will take the pipes, and the pumps will stop, and the amber fluid will stop flowing, and whatever it feeds will go quiet, and the Rootworks will be a different kind of place—a patient place, a place of slow vegetable metabolizing, a place without roar, without song, without the marriage of machine and root that gives the space its purpose and its strange, unbearable beauty.

He does not want this. He is surprised by how much he does not want this. The thought of the pipes going quiet—of the silence that would fill the space where the roar lives now—settles in his chest like grief. He is a component, and the system is his system, and the noise is his noise, and he will hold it together for as long as his hands can grip and his ears can hear and his legs can carry him through the dark, amber-stitched distances of the Rootworks.

He is the Keeper. He does not call himself this. But it is what he is.

He is the only living thing in the Rootworks, and he is holding back the roots.
