# What Lives in the Rearing Forest

The creatures here do not drink. This is the thing you must understand first, before you see them—really see them. They have no conception of water, no ancestral memory of streams or rain. Their cells are built around a different solvent entirely, a viscous copper-bearing compound that moves through them like slow oil, that tastes of pennies and oxidation. If you were to cut one open—and this has happened, accidentally, in the early days of survey work—what pours out is not red but blue-green, thick as honey, and it smells like the inside of a battery.

They have evolved to drink the forest itself. Not the fluids within it, but the forest as process, as event.

---

Let us start with what you might see first, what you almost cannot miss: the threshers.

They are the size of small dogs but built wrong, too many joints in each leg, bodies that seem to have been designed for a creature twice their scale and then compressed. Their fur—if it can be called fur—is not the soft pelage of mammals you know. Each hair is a hollow tube, a capillary system that draws particulate matter from the air and channels it inward to be processed. When the light catches them right, they seem to shimmer, the constant flow of air through their coats creating a haze around their bodies like a halo of static.

They move through the forest in packs of six to twelve, though "pack" implies a cohesion that isn't quite right. They tolerate each other. They coordinate. But there is no affection in it, no bonding—only the mathematics of survival, the recognition that the forest's yields are more predictable when multiple threshers work a single area. They are not predators. They are not prey. They are something else entirely: bioaccumulators, living sieves that strain the forest's exhaust and convert it to mass.

Their young are born in the high canopy, in pockets of slightly clearer air where the fumeroles thin and the ozone concentration drops enough to permit the development of softer tissues. You have seen them, if you climb high enough—pale, eyeless things that cling to the undersides of platforms and buttresses, their mouths already open and working, filtering the forest's breath before they are fully formed. They do not nurse. They have no mothers in the mammalian sense, only depositors who lay them in suitable substrate and move on. They grow by eating what the forest exhales, and when they are large enough to descend, they drop—literally drop, fifty meters or more, their bodies designed to absorb the impact—and begin their lives in the lower levels.

The threshers communicate through vibration. You feel it before you understand it: a low thrumming in the lattice beneath your feet, a pulse that travels through the substrate and into your bootsoles. They have no vocal cords, no ears. They sense each other through the forest floor, through the stems and stalks and pillars, reading position and intention and state of being through tremors too subtle for human perception. When one thresher dies—and they die frequently, their bodies burning out after only three or four years, their chemistry too volatile for longevity—the others know within seconds. They do not mourn. They simply redistribute.

---

Then there are the sail-bearers.

You will not see them at first. They live in the thermal layers between the shaded depths and the bright clearings, in the turbulence where cold air and warm air meet and churn. They are enormous—fifteen meters across, sometimes more—but almost weightless, their bodies not flesh so much as membrane, vast sheets of photosensitive tissue that capture both light and chemical energy from the air.

They look like nothing you have words for. Kites, perhaps. Manta rays swimming through atmosphere. But these comparisons fail, because the sail-bearers are not swimming and they are not flying; they are simply *being*, suspended in the updrafts, their bodies extended to catch every possible photon and particulate. They have no heads, no limbs, no organs you can identify. They are pure surface area, evolution pushed to an extreme where the organism becomes nothing but interface with its environment.

They reproduce by fragmentation. When a sail-bearer reaches a certain size—or when it sustains damage beyond repair—it tears. A piece breaks off, carried by the winds, and if that piece is large enough to contain the necessary cellular structures, it begins to grow. It is not birth. It is not even cloning. It is continuation, the same individual consciousness (if they have consciousness) distributed across multiple bodies, the sail-bearer that was now the sail-bearers that are.

You have watched them pulse in the thermal currents. Their surfaces ripple with color—green and blue and silver and sometimes, when the light is right, a deep purple that seems to absorb illumination rather than reflect it. They are feeding constantly, their membranes permeable to the forest's chemical exhalations, their cells converting what they absorb into the buoyant gases that keep them aloft. They are filters. They are lungs. They are the forest's way of recycling its own output, closing loops that would otherwise bleed atmosphere into the upper reaches.

The sail-bearers have predators, though "predator" is perhaps the wrong word. The harpoon-crawlers do not eat them for meat. They eat them for the gas.

---

The harpoon-crawlers are the closest thing this forest has to wolves.

They are solitary hunters, working the middle levels where the sail-bearers drift and the threshers forage. Their bodies are long and low and segmented, built for threading through the dense architecture of the forest, each segment articulated and independently mobile. They have twelve legs—four pairs for walking, two pairs modified into something else entirely. The forelimbs are clamping mechanisms, strong enough to grip a sail-bearer's membrane and hold it against the thrashing that follows. The hindlimbs are anchors, hooked and barbed, designed to lock the harpoon-crawler into the vegetation while it works.

But their namesake feature is the projection that extends from their cranial segment: a hollow tube, reinforced with metallic compounds leeched from the forest's substrate, that can be extended and retracted with devastating speed. When a harpoon-crawler sights a sail-bearer—by vibration, by chemical signature, by the subtle disturbance in air currents that the membranes create—it positions itself, calculates trajectory, and fires. The harpoon punches through the sail-bearer's tissue and injects a compound that causes rapid cellular collapse. The gas cells rupture. The membrane loses buoyancy. And the sail-bearer, helpless, begins to sink.

The harpoon-crawler descends with it, still attached, still feeding, draining the concentrated chemical energy that the sail-bearer has accumulated over its lifetime. It is a slow process. The crawler eats as it falls, and by the time the sail-bearer's remnants reach the forest floor, the crawler has stored enough energy to survive for months without feeding again. It detaches, finds a crevice in the lattice, and enters a state of torpor that can last for a full seasonal cycle.

You have found their husks after waking—the shed exoskeletons, picked clean by the forest's smaller scavengers, the metallic harpoons still attached, gleaming in the green-shifted light. They are beautiful in their way, these instruments of death. The forest produces nothing that is not.

---

And what lives in the forest floor, in the meters-deep lattice of root-matter and collapsed growth? The decomposers, the recyclers, the ones who make the whole system possible?

They are the hardest to see and the hardest to describe.

Let us call them the grubs, though they are not larvae of anything, not a juvenile stage awaiting metamorphosis. They are the final form, the bottom of the food web, the organisms that take the fallen and the dead and render them back into substrate. They have no eyes. They have no limbs. They are tubes, essentially—segmented digestive systems that tunnel through the forest's foundations, eating what was once living and excreting what will become the base for new growth.

Their scale is difficult to comprehend. Some are the size of your thumb, pale and soft and wet. Others are the size of your arm, dark and armored, capable of burrowing through even the densest growth. And a few—the queens, though they are not queens in any reproductive sense—are enormous, meters long, their bodies thick as tree trunks, moving through the forest floor like earthworms through soil. You have seen the trails they leave: collapsed tunnels, freshly turned substrate, the smell of decomposition so strong it burns your sinuses.

They do not hunt. They do not flee. They simply process, their bodies breaking down complex organic matter into simpler compounds that the forest can absorb. They are the forest's gut flora, essential and uncelebrated, their existence discovered only because you fell through a weakened section of lattice and found yourself in a tunnel, face to face with a grub the size of a leg of lamb, its blind head pressing against your chest, tasting you.

You were not food. It moved on.

---

But there are others. You have seen them only in glimpses, in the corner of your vision, in the space between knowing and not-knowing.

Something large moves through the upper canopy. You have heard it—the vibration of its passage, the groaning of the architecture as it shifts weight, the sudden silence of the threshers in its wake. You have never seen it clearly. A shape, immense and dark, moving with purpose through the living towers. A shadow that passes across the bright clearings and then is gone.

Something else lives in the deep fissures, the catastrophe-zones where the forest has collapsed and not yet regrown. You have found evidence of habitation: scratches on the exposed surfaces, patterns that might be territorial markers, the remains of meals you cannot identify. Whatever lives there is large enough to take a thresher, and it does not announce itself.

And something—something—lives in the forest's deepest reaches, in the meters-deep substrate where the grubs work and the light has never reached. You have felt the vibration of its movements, slow and rhythmic, like the breathing of something vast. You have wondered, in the darker hours of your survey work, whether the forest itself might be alive in a way you have not yet understood, whether the substrate might be a body and the towers its limbs and the fumeroles its voice.

You have wondered whether you are walking through an organism rather than an ecosystem.

---

The bird-thing that perches near your shoulder is not a bird. You understand this now. It is a mobile extension of the forest's sensing systems, a probe that moves through the architecture collecting data that the stationary towers cannot reach. Its eyes are not eyes but chemical analyzers, tasting the air, reading the signals that the forest sweats, building a picture of its own vast body in real time.

It has no fear of you because it has no concept of you. You are not food. You are not threat. You are a data point, a anomaly, a source of chemical signatures it has never encountered before. It will remember you. The forest will remember you. When you leave—if you leave—the memory of your passage will persist in the forest's chemistry, a trace that might last for centuries before it is finally subsumed.

You are not the first visitor. You have found the evidence: the corroded remains of survey equipment, the collapsed tunnels of previous expeditions, the skeletons of creatures that were not forest-born. The forest ate them, eventually. It eats everything, not from hunger but from process, from the slow endless churning of its own metabolism. It does not hate. It does not welcome. It simply incorporates.

And what lives here—the threshers and sail-bearers, the harpoon-crawlers and grubs, the vast unseen things in the canopy and the depths—they are not separate from this process. They are its instruments, its extensions, its way of knowing itself. They live because the forest lives, and when they die, they become the forest again, their bodies feeding the grubs, their cells joining the architecture, their matter cycling through the endless machinery of photosynthesis and respiration and decay.

This is what lives in the Rearing Forest: not creatures, exactly, but components. Not inhabitants but organs. Not a community but a corpus, vast and interlinked and singing the same endless song.

The bird-thing opens its soft mouth and drinks the ozone, and the forest shudders, and the light falls green and giddy through the canopy, and you are very small, and very temporary, and very much not the point.

---

This is what survives when water is not the answer.

This is what survives when the question is different.
