# What Lives Here

The Moss Quarter is not empty. It is, in fact, extraordinarily full—so full that the usual distinctions between inhabitant and habitat have softened, the way everything here softens given time and moisture. To ask what lives in the Moss Quarter is almost the wrong question. Better to ask what *doesn't* live here, and the answer is: very little. The place has become nearly total in its occupation.

---

## The Evident Ones

Let's start with what you'd notice first, what you'd hear or see if you stood on the central path and waited.

The **fern pickers** come earliest. They arrive before dawn in the damp season, when the fiddleheads are still tight curls no thicker than a thumb, coated in a fine rusty fur that comes off on your fingers if you touch them. There are maybe a dozen regulars, though the number shifts year to year. They work in pairs or threes, moving through the Quarter with the ease of people navigating their own kitchens. They know which patches are ready by smell, by the particular quality of the light through the canopy, by the feel of the air. They don't talk much while they work. The Quarter doesn't encourage conversation—not because sound is forbidden but because the acoustic properties of all that soft growth make ordinary speech feel intrusive, like shouting in a library.

Most of them live in the blocks outside the viaduct, in the buildings that still have roofs and plumbing and the other apparatus of ordinary life. They're not romantic about the Quarter. To them it's a workplace, a resource, a place that happens to grow valuable crops without anyone planting them. They have opinions about which hybrid ferns produce the best fiddleheads—the leathery-leaved escapees from the greenhouse produce a crop that's bitter, they say, while the native lady fern that's hybridized with something else nobody can identify produces curls that are sweet and nutty, almost almond-flavored, and fetch double the price at the specialty grocers.

They have names for the patches. "The Staircase" is where a collapsed residential building has created a terraced slope of decomposed wood and moss, and the ferns there grow in dense horizontal rows that do indeed look like steps. "The Lathe" is the machine shop on Hembry Street, where the old workbenches have become nurse logs for a particularly productive colony. "The Canal Eye" is the spot where the storm drain system surfaces near the waterway, creating a constant seep that supports a stand of ferns so vigorous they harvest it twice in a season without depleting it.

The **maintenance crew** overlaps with the pickers in personnel but not in schedule. They come in daylight, in the dry season mostly, and their work is different in character—sculptural rather than extractive. They decide where the moss grows thick and where it should be thinned, which vine gets to cross the path and which gets redirected, whether a fallen branch should be left as a nurse log or cleared to keep the route passable. They've been doing this long enough that their decisions have shaped the Quarter in ways that are invisible unless you knew what it looked like before they started. The central path, for instance, has a slight curve to it that isn't original to the street layout—they put the curve in to avoid a particularly fine specimen of streetlamp tree, and now the curve itself is part of the place, its edges colonized by mosses that don't grow anywhere else in the Quarter because the micro-conditions of the banked path are unique.

They carry the artifacts they find in their minds more than in their hands. The child's tricycle absorbed by the tree trunk. A set of porcelain dishes, still stacked, in what was once a kitchen cabinet, now exposed by the collapse of an interior wall, the plates furred with green. A wardrobe, standing upright in what was a bedroom, its doors open, filled with moss so thick it looks like the wardrobe was always meant to contain it. They don't remove these things. They have a quiet agreement that the Quarter has claimed them, and the Quarter's claims should be respected.

They are, without naming it as such, the stewards of a process they didn't begin and can't control. They don't think of themselves this way. They think of themselves as people who keep the paths clear. But the path system is the skeleton of the Quarter's human accessibility, and without it the place would become impenetrable—a closed system, a green void. By keeping the paths, they keep the Quarter open to the fern pickers, to the occasional botanist, to the lost dog or wandering child who somehow finds their way in. They keep it permeable to the outside, and in doing so they keep it from becoming something else entirely.

The **factory workers** at the canning operation are the most numerous human inhabitants, though "inhabitants" is a stretch—they come for shifts and leave, their presence tidal rather than constant. The factory is the one dry, bright, mechanically loud place in the Quarter, and the contrast is jarring. You step out of the green silence into a room of steel and steam and the clatter of cans on conveyors, and for a moment your body doesn't know how to process the change. The workers, though, move between the two zones fluidly. They eat their lunches sitting on the canal wall with their feet dangling over the water, and they can identify the dragonfly species that patrol the surface, and they know which section of the viaduct the woodpeckers have claimed this season.

The factory manager—a woman named Hester who inherited the operation from her uncle, who inherited it from his—has an office on the second floor with a window that looks directly into the canopy of a streetlamp tree. The willow branches tap the glass in wind. She keeps the window open most of the year, even in the damp, because the alternative is the smell of steam and brine, which she's been breathing for thirty years and still hasn't learned to like. She has a chart on her wall tracking the fiddlehead harvest yields going back to the factory's founding, and the chart shows a pattern she can't explain: the harvest peaks every eleven years, like clockwork, regardless of rainfall or temperature or any other variable she's been able to identify. She's mentioned this to the botanists who study the Quarter. They don't have an explanation either. She's started marking the peaks with a small green sticker on the chart, and the stickers form a rhythm that looks, from across the room, almost like a heartbeat.

## The Avian Residents

The **feral budgerigars** are the most visible non-human inhabitants, and the most surprising. Their ancestors were pets—escapees from the aviary that once occupied the greenhouse's upper level, or possibly released deliberately when the conservatory enterprise folded. They've been wild for decades now, and they've changed. The original stock was the standard captive green and yellow, but the Quarter's pressures have selected for something different. The current population is darker—a deep verdant that's nearly black in shadow, with a blue-gray tint on the breast that may be an adaptation to the cool, damp conditions. Their calls have changed too. Wild budgerigars in their native Australia are talkative, social, constantly chattering. The Quarter's population is quieter. Their contact calls are lower-pitched, more melodic, almost mournful—a single descending note that carries well in the damp air but doesn't draw attention the way a bright chatter would.

They live in the greenhouse. The iron framework provides roosting sites at every level, and the temperature inside the plant cathedral stays a degree or two warmer than the surrounding Quarter, enough to make a difference on cold nights. They nest in the bromeliad cups—the central reservoirs that collect rainwater and breed mosquito larvae. The budgerigars have learned to drink from these cups without disturbing the larvae, tilting their heads to sip from the surface while the insect life continues undisturbed below. It's a delicate operation, and watching it feels like watching someone perform surgery.

Their relationship with the dragonflies is more adversarial. The dragonflies—broad-bodied chasers, mostly, though there are at least three other species—patrol the airspace between buildings in the damp season, hawking for mosquitoes and midges. They're territorial, and they'll challenge the budgerigars if they get too close to a hunting ground. The budgerigars usually yield. A dragonfly is a hundredth the weight of a budgerigar, but they're aggressive and fast and they seem to confuse the birds, who haven't evolved alongside them and haven't developed a response. Sometimes you see a budgerigar perched on a wire, watching a dragonfly's patrol pattern with what looks like wary respect.

The **woodpeckers** are the Quarter's carpenters. There's a resident population of great spotted woodpeckers—maybe four or five breeding pairs—who've been here long enough to have developed an unusual behavior. They drum on metal as readily as on wood. The iron frames of the viaduct, the rusted lamp housings at the top of the streetlamp trees, the corrugated roofing of the machine shop—they all produce distinctive sounds when struck, and the woodpeckers have incorporated these sounds into their territorial displays. Each male has a signature rhythm that includes specific metal and wood strikes in a particular sequence, a pattern that's recognizable across years and across generations. The young birds learn the patterns from their fathers and then modify them, adding new elements, dropping old ones. The result is a percussion repertoire that's been evolving for decades, an oral tradition in a non-human species, transmitted through sound.

They've also modified the architecture. The streetlamp trees, with their willow trunks grown around iron cores, offer a nesting substrate that's unique: soft wood on the outside, hard metal on the inside. The woodpeckers excavate their nest cavities in the willow layer, and the iron core serves as a back wall, impermeable to predators. The cavities are warmer in winter and cooler in summer than they'd be in solid wood, and the breeding success rate of the Quarter's woodpeckers is significantly higher than the regional average. They've become, in effect, a separate population—one that's adapted to a habitat that exists nowhere else.

The **feral pigeons** are less interesting but more numerous. They nest in the viaduct framework, in the gaps between the rusted girders where the vines haven't yet filled the space. They're the standard city type—gray, iridescent, adaptable. But even they've been changed by the Quarter. Their plumage is darker than the regional norm, stained green by the algae that grows on every surface, and their cooing has a softer quality in the damp air, as if the moisture rounds the edges of the sound.

They're the only inhabitants of the Quarter that the maintenance crew actively discourages. The pigeons' guano accumulates on the paths, creating slippery patches and introducing nutrients that alter the moss composition. The crew has tried various deterrents—reflective tape, predator decoys—without success. The pigeons ignore them. They've been here longer than the crew has, and they regard the paths as their territory, not ours.

## The Hidden Majority

But the visible inhabitants—the birds, the insects, the humans on their seasonal schedules—are a minority. The true population of the Moss Quarter is the one you can't see, the one that constitutes the overwhelming bulk of the living matter in the place.

The **moss itself** is the dominant organism. Not a single species but a community—a mosaic of at least thirty identified species and probably more, layering over each other in a structure that's more complex than it appears. The base layer is a carpet moss, *Brachythecium* something, that forms the continuous ground cover on every horizontal surface. It's tolerant of inundation, of shade, of trampling—it can recover from foot traffic in a matter of days, which is why the paths don't stay bare even with regular maintenance. Above it, on the vertical surfaces, the cushion mosses build depth. *Homalothecium* and *Kindbergia* and the large cushions of *Leucobryum* that look like gray-green pincushions pressed into the brickwork. And at the margins, in the transitional zones where the canopy thins and more light reaches the ground, the hair-cap mosses—*Polytrichum*—stand up like tiny forests of their own, their spore capsules raised on stalks that catch the diffuse light.

The moss isn't passive. It's active, aggressive, territorial. It colonizes surfaces, breaks them down, converts them to substrate, and then holds that substrate against the competition. The reason the Moss Quarter is the Moss Quarter and not the Vine Quarter or the Fern Quarter is that the moss got there first and has been defending its territory ever since. The vines grow over the moss, but the moss grows over the vines' roots. The ferns root in the moss's substrate, but the moss colonizes the ferns' dead fronds. It's a slow-motion war, fought in millimeters per year, and the moss has been winning for decades.

The moss also *breathes*. Not in the animal sense, but in the sense that the entire moss carpet expands and contracts with the humidity. In the damp season, the moss cushions swell, their cells filling with water, their surfaces opening to take in nutrients and spores and the microscopic organic matter that the air carries. In the dry season, they contract, their surfaces crusting over, their metabolism slowing to a crawl. This expansion and contraction is imperceptible to human observation—you can't see it happening—but over time it shapes the environment. The swelling moss pushes fragments of brick out of the walls. The contracting moss creates micro-fissures in the paths. The entire Quarter is being slowly kneaded, like dough, by the respiratory cycle of its dominant organism.

The **mycelial network** is the moss's partner and rival. The fungal threads that lace through the soil, through the decomposing wood, through the moss carpets themselves, constitute a second circulatory system—not of water but of nutrients. The mycelium is the Quarter's nervous system, its information network, its digestive tract. When a tree falls, the mycelium is there within days, its hyphae probing the newly exposed wood, beginning the process of enzymatic breakdown that will convert solid timber to soft substrate within a year. When a patch of moss dies back—too much shade, too much foot traffic, too much inundation—the mycelium moves into the gap, breaking down the dead organic matter and preparing the site for recolonization.

There are mushrooms, occasionally. In the damp season, after particularly wet nights, the fruiting bodies emerge—small, brown, unremarkable to the casual eye, but profuse. They appear in arcs and rings that mark the extent of the underground mycelial colonies, and they last only a day or two before dissolving back into the substrate. The maintenance crew has learned to read them as indicators: a flush of mushrooms in a new area means something has died there, something they couldn't see, and the system is processing it. They note the locations and watch to see what grows in the gap.

The **insect life** is the least studied and probably the most diverse component of the Quarter's ecology. The dragonflies are visible. The mosquitoes are, unfortunately, also visible—though their role as larvae in the bromeliad cups is more important than their role as adults, providing a food source that supports the entire aerial hunting community. But beneath the visible layer is a world of springtails and mites and beetles and the microscopic fauna that break down organic matter into its component molecules. The Quarter's decomposition cycle is so efficient not because of the fungi alone but because of the partnership between fungi and these microarthropods, each doing a different part of the work, each dependent on the other.

There are things here that may not have names. The botanists have identified seventeen distinct plant hybrids, but the insect hybrids—the populations that have adapted to the Quarter's unique conditions and may no longer be genetically continuous with their parent populations—have received almost no attention. There's a springtail, recovered from a moss sample taken near the greenhouse, that doesn't match any described species. There's a beetle larva found in the decomposing wood of a nurse log that's tentatively been assigned to a known family but not to any known genus. These are organisms that have been evolving in isolation, within the viaduct's boundaries, for as long as the Quarter has existed. They are endemic to twelve blocks of abandoned city.

## The Solitary Ones

There are individual inhabitants of the Quarter who exist outside the categories.

The **old woman on Third Street**—the residents call her the woman on Third Street, though she lives in what was once a ground-floor shop, not a residential building—has been there longer than anyone. No one is sure exactly how long. She was here when the factories closed. She was here when the canopy closed over. She was here when the maintenance crew started, and they sometimes find evidence of her presence in places they didn't expect: a cleared space in a building they thought was empty, a path worn into the moss that doesn't connect to any of the maintained routes, a tin cup left on a windowsill as if someone intended to come back for it.

She doesn't speak to anyone. She doesn't need to. The fern pickers leave her alone. The maintenance crew leaves her alone. She's part of the place in a way that nobody questions, the way the moss is part of the place, the way the viaduct is part of the place. She's been observed at a distance—small, bent, moving through the interior of the greenhouse in the early morning with a familiarity that suggests she's been doing it for decades. She appears to harvest things: not fiddleheads, but other plants, fungi, things that grow in the deeper parts of the Quarter that nobody else visits. She's been seen carrying bundles of dried material back toward Third Street.

Some people say she was a botanist once. Some people say she was a resident who refused to leave. Some people say she's not one person but a succession of people, a role that's been passed down, the way the woodpeckers pass down their rhythms. Nobody knows. The Quarter keeps its secrets the way it keeps everything else: slowly, patiently, under a layer of green.

The **botanists** are occasional visitors rather than residents, but they've become part of the Quarter's community of attention. There are three who come regularly—one from the university, one from the municipal herbarium, one independent researcher who funds her own work through grants and sheer stubbornness. They've been studying the Quarter for varying lengths of time: the university researcher for eight years, the herbarium botanist for fifteen, the independent for twenty-three. They have transects and sample plots and data sets that track the changes in vegetation composition over time. They have opinions about the seventeen hybrids that are strongly held and mutually incompatible. They disagree about taxonomy, about methodology, about whether the Quarter represents a novel ecosystem or a degraded one, about whether human intervention is helping or harming or irrelevant.

But they all react to the place the same way when they first arrive for a field season. They stand at the viaduct entrance and they take a breath and they go quiet for a moment. The air does it—the green-water scent, the sweetness, the metallic note. The Quarter announces itself through the lungs. You breathe it in and you know you've entered somewhere that operates by different rules.

The independent researcher, the one who's been coming for twenty-three years, has a theory she hasn't published. She believes the Quarter isn't an ecosystem in the conventional sense—not a community of interacting species in a shared environment. She believes it's a single organism. Not literally—the moss isn't one plant, the trees aren't one tree—but functionally. The moisture cycle, the nutrient cycle, the decomposition cycle, the growth patterns, the reproductive strategies—they're all so tightly coupled, so mutually dependent, that the Quarter behaves like a coherent unit. It responds to disturbances as a unit. It maintains its boundaries as a unit. It has a metabolism, a circulatory system, a respiratory cycle. It's not a forest that happens to be in a city. It's a city that has become a creature.

She hasn't published this because she can't prove it, and because she's not sure it's a scientific claim rather than a poetic one. But she keeps coming back, and she keeps watching, and the data she collects is consistent with her theory even if it doesn't confirm it.

## What the Quarter Itself Is

In the end, the thing that lives in the Moss Quarter is the Moss Quarter.

Not metaphorically. The place has become, through decades of closed-system evolution, something more than the sum of its components. The moss and the trees and the vines and the ferns and the fungi and the insects and the birds and the water and the soil and the buried pavement and the dissolved brick and the rusting iron and the human maintenance and the seasonal rhythms—they've all become so entangled that you can't remove any one of them without affecting all the others. The canning factory's steam feeds the moss that shelters the ferns that feed the factory. The maintenance crew's paths shape the growth patterns that determine the bird territories that determine the insect distribution that determines the decomposition rate that determines the substrate quality that determines the moss growth that the maintenance crew has to trim back. Everything connects to everything, and the connections have been strengthening for so long that they've become structural.

The Quarter is alive in the way a coral reef is alive—not as a single organism but as a living structure, a thing made of living things, a process that has achieved enough coherence to maintain itself against the forces that would break it down.

It's patient. The description says so, and it's true. The Quarter waits. It doesn't expand. It doesn't retreat. It doesn't seem to want anything. It just continues—metabolizing, growing, decomposing, regrowing, in a cycle that's been running long enough to feel permanent.

But it's not passive. The patience is active, the way a predator's patience is active. The Quarter is always processing, always incorporating, always converting what's dead into what's living. The child's tricycle absorbed by the tree trunk. The porcelain dishes furred with moss. The iron cores of the streetlamp trees, dissolved so slowly into the soil that the trees have absorbed the metal and incorporated it into their wood, so that if you cut through a trunk you'd find dark streaks of iron oxide running through the grain, a record of the slow cannibalization of the human-made by the forest-grown.

If you stand still long enough in the Moss Quarter, the description says, you'll feel the damp seep into your clothes, your hair, your skin. You'll breathe in the spores and the pollen and the microscopic fragments. You'll become, temporarily, part of the system.

What lives here is everything that's been incorporated. Everything the Quarter has absorbed and converted and made its own. The plants and the animals and the fungi and the people and the buildings and the streetlamps and the machines and the memories of what this place was before it became what it is.

What lives here is the Moss Quarter itself, and it is more alive than anything that lived here before.
