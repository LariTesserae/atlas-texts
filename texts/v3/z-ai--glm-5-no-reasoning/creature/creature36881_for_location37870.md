# What Lives Here

They do not live here in the way that anything else lives anywhere. They exist here the way ore exists in stone—the condition is the purpose, the stasis is the function. But they are alive. Unmistakably, uneasily, in a way that makes the word feel inadequate.

---

## The First Layer

The ones nearest the walls are the oldest, though age means nothing in a place without time. You know they are oldest because their surfaces have acquired a texture the newer ones lack—a patina of contact, of pressing against the sweating metal for so long that their skin—or whatever serves as skin—has taken on the imprint of the wall's microscopic perforations. They are pocked, dimpled, textured like the surface they lean against. They have become partly architectural.

These are the ones with the most developed suggestion of form. Arm-things that have thickened into genuine limbs, heavy and inert, ending in rounded nubs that might be fists or might be stumps or might be something that has no analogue in any body that has ever walked or crawled or swum. Their bulk is tremendous. They do not move so much as settle, the way sand settles, the way mountains settle—an imperceptible shifting that only becomes apparent over durations that the mind refuses to hold. They are the foundation. The others press against them and they do not yield.

Their sounds are the lowest. A bass vibration that originates somewhere in their centers and propagates outward through the dense tissue of their masses. When several of them vocalize together, the frequency aligns and the chamber itself resonates—a low, tectonic moan that you feel in your molars before you hear it with your ears. They are not singing. They are not communicating. They are simply doing the only thing they know how to do, which is producing sound from the raw material of their unfinished bodies.

One of them—against the north curve, if such directions mean anything here—has developed something that might be a head. A bulging at the top of its mass, a concentration of tissue that suggests the idea of a head without committing to the architecture of one. No face. No features. Just a swelling, a tumor of organized growth that implies intention without revealing it. The wall presses against this swelling and the swelling presses back and has been pressing back for so long that the wall has developed a corresponding depression, a concavity that matches the convexity of the growth. They have shaped each other. This is the closest thing to relationship that exists here.

---

## The Middle Layer

Behind the wall-leaners, pressed against their flanks and draped across their surfaces, the middle layer seethes.

These are smaller. More active. Their movements are not the glacial settling of the old ones but something closer to swimming, or to the way a worm moves through soil—contracting, extending, contracting again. They have more limbs and fewer limbs. Some have six or eight arm-things, radiating from centers that have no clear front or back, and these limbs are not still but wave constantly, feeling the walls, feeling each other, feeling the liquid, feeling the air. The motion is not searching. It is not seeking. It is motion for the sake of motion, the expression of some internal imperative that demands activity regardless of direction or purpose.

Others have no limbs at all. They are masses—oblong, lumpy, their surfaces irregular with growths that appear and disappear and appear again in different locations. Buds that swell and then recede. Ridges that rise and then flatten. They are topographies in constant revision, landscapes that cannot commit to a shape. The manufacturing process that made them did not stop. It is still happening. They are still being built, assembled from the inside out by some mechanism that has no off switch, no completion signal, no endpoint. They grow the way crystals grow—according to a pattern that the pattern itself cannot know.

The middle layer is where the noise is loudest. These are the vocal ones. Their openings—the holes and slits and gaps that might become mouths or might be something else—produce the full range of the chamber's sound. Clicking. A rapid, percussive chatter that rises and falls in waves, sometimes synchronizing across dozens of bodies into a single rhythmic pulse that then dissolves back into chaos. Mewling—a high, thin sound that triggers associations in the human brain that the human brain cannot resolve, something between a kitten and a newborn and something that is neither, something that has no referent because no ear was designed to hear it. And the hum. Always the hum, the underlying tone that never stops, that rises from dozens of throat-openings and meets in the center of the chamber and becomes something larger than any single body could produce.

They are not communicating. Or—they are, but the communication has no content. It is communication as pure signal, as presence, as the assertion that something exists and is capable of making noise. I am here, I am here, I am here. The message has no receiver. It is its own purpose.

## The Floaters

In the reservoir—the ankle-deep not-water that covers the floor—there are others.

These are the most incomplete. The most embryonic. They have the least form, the least differentiation, the least claim to anything resembling a body. Some are barely more than tissue—masses of pale, dense material that float just below the surface, pulsing with the rhythm of the pumps, expanding and contracting with each four-second cycle. They are the color of the liquid they float in, which is to say they are the color of nothing, a gray-white that blends with the dim light until they become visible only by the disturbance they create in the surface tension.

They are the ones that drift toward the vents and away again. The circulation pulls them gently toward the dark openings near the floor, and they drift, and then the circulation pushes them back, and they drift the other way. They have no means of resistance. They have no will to resist. They are subject to the forces of the chamber entirely, passive in a way that the others are not, existing at the mercy of systems they cannot perceive and would not understand if they could.

But they are growing too. Even these. Even the barely-formed, the almost-nothings, the pale floaters in the manufactured liquid. They swell. They divide. Sometimes two of them meet in the current and press together and become one, a merging that takes minutes or hours or days—time is not measurable here—and produces a thing that is larger and slightly more complex than either of its components. Sometimes one of them splits, a fissure appearing in its center and widening until there are two where there was one, each smaller, each simpler, each beginning again.

This is the rhythm beneath the rhythm. The merging and splitting, the growing and dividing, the constant recombination of barely-living material in a bath of nutrients and compounds. It looks like something. It looks like the beginning of something. It looks like the first moments of a process that, in another context, would lead to something whole and finished and capable. But here it leads only to more of this. More floaters. More merging and splitting. More pale masses in the cold liquid, pulsing with the four-second beat.

## The Interpretation Problem

You cannot know what they are.

This is important. This is perhaps the most important thing about them. They resist interpretation the way the liquid resists classification. Every observation invites analogy—embryo, fetus, larva, clone, construct, specimen—and every analogy collapses on contact. They are not embryos because embryos develop. They are not larvae because larvae metamorphose. They are not clones because clones are copies of something and there is nothing here that they might be copies of. They are not specimens because specimens were taken from somewhere and there is no somewhere.

They are original. Whatever that means here, in this place that has no outside, in this time that has no progression—they are original. The first of their kind. The only of their kind. And they are not finished and will never be finished and were never meant to be finished.

Someone made them. The pipes and pumps and systems prove this. Someone designed this chamber and filled it and set the pumps to their four-second rhythm and walked away—or was taken away—or never existed in the first place and the chamber built itself, which is impossible, which is not impossible here because the rules of impossibility are among the many things that have not been invented yet.

Whoever made them—or whatever made them—did not make them for a purpose that can be discerned. They are not being grown for harvest. They are not being prepared for release. They are not being stored for later use. They are simply here. Being. Growing without growing. Becoming without becoming. Pressing against each other and against the walls and against the floor and against the ceiling-if-they-could-reach-it, filling the space with their bodies and their sounds and their wet, unfinished heat in the cold, finished dark.

## The Way They Are Together

They do not know each other.

This seems clear. They do not recognize, do not respond, do not form bonds or hierarchies or associations. When one moves against another, the response is mechanical—the pressed body shifts to accommodate the pressure, the pressing body continues pressing. There is no interaction. There is only collision and accommodation, the physics of soft bodies in a confined space.

And yet.

The hum. The way it harmonizes. The way dozens of separate throat-openings, on bodies that have no awareness of each other, produce tones that meet in the air and create something that no single body intended. The synchronization of the clicking, the waves of mewling that rise and fall together. These are not independent events. Something is shared. Something propagates through the mass of bodies, through the liquid, through the walls themselves—some signal or state or condition that causes the many to act as one without knowing that they are acting at all.

It might be the pumps. The four-second pulse might be a metronome, and the bodies might be tuned to it, and the harmonization might be nothing more than mechanical resonance, the way piano strings vibrate in sympathy with a note struck across the room. This is the simplest explanation. It is probably wrong.

It might be the liquid. The reservoir might carry something—chemical, electrical, something else—that connects the bodies through their submerged surfaces, creating a network of which they are nodes without knowing they are nodes. The merging and splitting of the floaters might be a manifestation of this network—a physical expression of a connection that cannot otherwise be perceived.

Or it might be something else. Something that has no name because the concept has not been developed. A form of togetherness that does not require awareness, a community that does not require individuals, a collective state that exists only in the aggregate and cannot be found in any single member. They might be, together, something. Something that none of them are alone. Something that can only exist in this density, in this proximity, in this specific configuration of wet and cold and dark and noise.

They might be thinking.

Not any one of them. All of them. The mass. The pile. The writhing, vocal, unfinished totality of two hundred or more bodies in a ten-meter chamber might be a single organ—a brain, a processor, a thinking thing—whose thoughts are the harmonics in the air and the patterns in the liquid and the four-second pulse of the pumps that keeps it all alive.

If so, it is not thinking about anything. It is thinking about nothing. It is experiencing the fact of its own existence, which is the only fact available to it, and it is expressing this experience in the only way it can, which is sound and motion and the constant rearrangement of its own components.

It is dreaming, perhaps. In the way that a fetus dreams—without content, without narrative, without images. A pure experience of sensation and process, of being made and being maintained and being alive in a place where alive is the only adjective that applies.

---

## What They Want

Nothing.

This is the answer that the mind rejects and the evidence supports. They want nothing. They need nothing that they do not already have. They are not in distress—the keening is not pain, or if it is pain, it is pain without suffering, pain as sensation without interpretation. They are not in pleasure—the hum is not satisfaction, or if it is satisfaction, it is satisfaction without relief. They are simply in process, and process has no goal, and the process will continue for as long as the pumps pump and the liquid circulates and the walls sweat and the light glows from nowhere.

They are what lives here. They are all of what lives here. They are the population and the citizenry and the entire biota of a place that was made for them and that they were made for, and they fill it completely, and they always have, and they always will, and the sound they make is the sound of this place, and the wet they live in is the wet of this place, and the cold that penetrates them is the cold of this place, and there is nothing else.

There is nothing else.
