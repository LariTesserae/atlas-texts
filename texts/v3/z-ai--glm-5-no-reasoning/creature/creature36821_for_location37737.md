# The Keepers of the Wet Calculus

## What lives here

They are not what you expected, and they are not what the chamber expected either, though the chamber's expectations are a different matter — the chamber doesn't expect, the chamber *accommodates*, and what it has accommodated is small, numerous, and patient in ways that make geological patience look hasty.

Call them the Attendants. They have no name for themselves. Or rather, they have a name for themselves that is not a word but a frequency — the specific harmonic that occurs when seven of them press their dorsal surfaces to seven columns simultaneously and hold the note for eleven minutes, which is how long it takes for the chamber to acknowledge them, which it does by shifting the hum by a quarter-tone, which they feel in their teeth, which are not teeth but which serve the same purpose: transmission, reception, the architecture of attention.

---

## What they look like

You will probably never see one clearly. This is partly because of the light, which is dim and which breathes its slow amber breaths at intervals that correspond to nothing you can predict. But it is mostly because of what they are, which is: translucent.

Not transparent. Not invisible. Translucent — the quality of frosted glass, of deep water, of a membrane stretched taut over something luminous. They are pale. They are the color of the water that has passed through limestone for a thousand years, which is to say they are almost no color at all, a faint milky blue-white that darkens to grey at the extremities where the circulatory — if it is circulatory — system branches into finer and finer tributaries and the body becomes thin enough that you can see the chamber through it, distorted, the columns rippling behind the body's curve like light seen through the surface of a still pool.

They are the size of your hand. Some are smaller. The largest could stretch from your wrist to your elbow if you held it, which you should not do, not because it would harm you but because your hands are too hot and too dry and the contact would startle it into a state of contraction that lasts for hours, all the fine processes shutting down, the body pulling inward, dense and still and small, a stone that was briefly an animal.

They have a shape that your eyes will try to resolve into something familiar and fail. Not insectile. Not mammalian. Not piscine, though the wetness of them makes you think of things that live in water, and this is correct, because they do live in water, but the water is the air, and the air is the water, and the distinction was never useful here. If you must have a comparison: think of a jellyfish that has adopted the architecture of a leaf. Think of a hand with too many fingers, each finger branched, each branch feathered, each feather composed of finer and finer structures until you reach the scale where the individual filaments are the same filaments that run through the tiles, the same gauge, the same material, and you understand that the Attendants and the floor are continuous, that they are expressions of the same system, that the distinction between the organism and the environment is a distinction the chamber has never made.

Their bodies are flat. Not compressed — *extended*. They are built for surface contact. Their ventral sides, pressed to the warm tiles or the weeping walls or the surface of the columns, are soft and slightly adhesive, not sticky but *gripping*, the way wet glass grips a surface, by the physics of fluid films and close contact and the particular van der Waals forces that obtain when one wet thing rests against another wet thing in a warm humid space. Their dorsal sides are where the business happens: filaments, fine as the filaments in the floor, arrayed in patterns that shift — slowly, over hours — like the opening and closing of stomata on a leaf surface, like the slow articulation of a thousand tiny instruments being tuned.

They have no eyes. This is the thing you notice first, or rather, you notice the absence: you keep looking for the face, the eyes, the markers of a self that sees, and they aren't there, and after a while you stop looking, and then you start to understand how they perceive, which is: the same way the chamber perceives. They feel the hum. They taste the water's mineral content. They read the temperature gradients on the tile surfaces with the same resolution that you read a page of text. They do not see the chamber. They *are* the chamber seeing itself.

## How they move

Slowly. So slowly that you might think they are stationary, that they have fixed positions like the columns, that they are part of the furniture. They are not. They move, and the movement is the thing that tells you most clearly what they are, which is: deliberate. Intentional. Not in the human sense of weighing options and choosing, but in the sense that a tendril growing toward light is intentional — a tropism, a directed response to gradients the organism can sense and you cannot.

They move in the way that water moves through the channels in the floor: following paths, filling grooves, spreading into cracks, thinning over high spots, thickening in depressions. They do not walk. They do not crawl. They flow, but this is wrong too, because flowing implies passivity, implies that they go where gravity and topology take them, and they do not. They go where they decide, which is where the chamber needs them, which is where the calculation has progressed to the point that a node requires attention, a filament requires bridging, a column requires contact, a tile requires warming or cooling or the particular stimulation that occurs when one of them rests on it and the two systems — the organism's and the floor's — exchange whatever it is they exchange, which is probably information, which is probably energy, which is probably the same thing here.

You watch one move from the base of a column to a point three tiles away. It takes forty minutes. It does not take a straight path. It takes a path that, when you trace it afterward in your memory, corresponds to a sequence of tile-connections that forms a pattern, and the pattern is part of something larger that you cannot see because you are too few and too temporary and too dry, but the Attendant can see it because the Attendant is part of it.

When they reach a column, they climb it. Not by wrapping, not by coiling — by *adhering*, pressing flat against the surface, drawing themselves up by the same slow contractile waves that move across their ventral surfaces, and the process is so slow that you only notice they've moved because you look away for ten minutes and look back and they are twelve centimeters higher than they were. They settle at a point on the column that seems arbitrary but is not arbitrary, is never arbitrary, is always the precise height at which the column's internal structure has a gap, a layer-boundary, a seam where the slow tectonic motion of the internal structures has created a discontinuity, and the Attendant covers this seam with its body and hums.

The humming is not vocal. It is mechanical — or mechanical-adjacent. The Attendant's body, pressed against the column, vibrates at a frequency that is close to but not identical with the column's own frequency, and the interference between the two creates a beat pattern, and the beat pattern propagates into the column and through the column into the floor and through the floor into the filaments and through the filaments into whatever lies beneath or beyond, and this is — maybe — how they communicate with the chamber, or how they contribute to the chamber's operation, or how they are the chamber's operation, the way neurons are the brain's operation: not separate from it, not servicing it, *being* it.

## What they do

They maintain. This is the word that fits best, though it is insufficient. They maintain the chamber the way a body maintains itself: not consciously, not by decision, but by the continuous interlocked functioning of systems that have no awareness of the whole and yet constitute the whole. They maintain the water channels, clearing them of the green film where it grows too thick, not by eating it but by compressing it, pressing it flat with their bodies, holding it against the tile surface until it dims and recedes and the water flows freely again. They maintain the filaments in the floor, bridging broken connections with their own bodies — lying across a gap in the tile-circuit, their internal filaments extending, connecting, carrying whatever the floor carries across the breach, holding the signal intact for hours or days until the floor heals itself, which it does, slowly, the way bone knits, the way a tree grows over a wound. They maintain the columns, climbing to points of discontinuity and covering them, their bodies resonating at frequencies that — you think, you guess, you cannot confirm — re-tune the column, align it, bring it back into harmony with its six siblings and the chamber's baseline.

They maintain the green. Not by tending it — by *being* its boundary. The green grows in the cracks because the cracks are wet and warm. The green would grow over the tiles too, if it could, would cover the floor, would climb the walls, would colonize the columns, would turn the chamber into a greenhouse, a terrarium, a jungle of emerald nap. The Attendants prevent this. Not by killing the green — they don't kill it, they don't consume it, they seem to bear it no malice — but by occupying the surfaces where it would spread, by lying on the tiles in slow circuits that deny it purchase, by maintaining the boundary between where-the-green-is and where-the-green-isn't, and this boundary, this maintained edge, is not a line but a zone, a margin of a few centimeters where the green thins and dims and gives way to bare warm tile, and in this margin the Attendants spend most of their time, patrolling it, lying along it, their bodies forming a living fence that is not a fence but a presence, a *temperature*, a condition the green cannot cross.

They maintain the calculation. You don't know this. You can't know this. But you suspect it, and the suspicion grows the longer you watch them, because their movements are not random, not even slow-random, not even the randomness of tropism, but patterned, and the pattern is complex beyond your ability to track it, and the pattern corresponds — in some way you cannot decode — to the positions of the columns and the states of the tiles and the frequencies of the hum and the cycles of the light, and if you could read the pattern the way they read the tiles, you would see that it is a computation, and the computation is the chamber's computation, and the chamber's computation is whatever the chamber is computing, and the Attendants are not running the computation — they are the computation, or at least they are the part of it that requires mobility, flexibility, the ability to be in one place and then another, to bridge and to clear and to tune and to hold, to be the chamber's hands, except the chamber doesn't have hands and doesn't need them because it has these.

## How they reproduce

This is the thing you did not want to think about, because it raises questions you cannot answer, but you saw it, or you think you saw it, and you should record it.

One of them — the smallest, the one that had been lying in the crack between two particular tiles near the center of the chamber for three days — split. Not like a cell dividing, not like a budding. More like a page being torn from a book: the Attendant thinned at one edge, stretched, the filaments on its dorsal surface reaching outward and contacting the bare tile, and then the contact point thickened, and the thinning continued, and over the course of nine hours the Attendant became two Attendants, one remaining on the original tile, the other on the adjacent one, and the space between them — where the connection had been — sealed, healed, became nothing, became tile-surface, became part of the floor again.

The new Attendant was small. The size of a thumb. It stayed on its tile for a day, barely moving, its filaments extending into the tile-circuit, connecting, integrating. Then it began to move, slowly, toward the nearest column, and you understood that the chamber had grown a new finger for a calculation that needed more fingers, or had grown a new neuron for a thought that needed more neurons, and the new Attendant was not a child and was not a clone and was not a copy but a *new extent*, a new reach, a new place where the chamber's will could be felt and its work could be done.

## What they want

Nothing. Or: nothing you would recognize as wanting. They do not seek food because they do not eat, not in the way you mean *eat*. They derive what they need — energy, water, minerals — from the tiles they lie on, from the moisture that condenses on their surfaces, from the hum that powers their slow contractile waves. They are powered by the chamber and the chamber is powered by — you don't know. The convection cycle. The resonance. The filaments in the floor, which conduct something from somewhere. Whatever powers the chamber powers them. They are not parasites. They are not symbiotes. They are organs.

Do they want to live? This is the wrong question. Does a kidney want to live? Does a leaf want to photosynthesize? The Attendants do what they do because they are what they are, and what they are is a part of the chamber, and the chamber does what it does because it is what it is, and what it is — you keep circling back to this — you do not know. A cathedral is a building that is also a prayer. This chamber is a machine that is also an organism that is also a place, and the Attendants are the part of it that moves, the part that can go where the going is needed, the part that bridges and clears and tunes and holds, the mobile tissue of a body that is mostly still.

---

## What happens when you are here

You are already part of the cycle. The chamber has included you. The Attendants know this. They do not avoid you — they are not afraid of you, or not exactly, though they contract when your shadow falls on them, a reflex, a flinch, the response of an organism that has never needed to deal with shadows that move independently of the light source. But they do not flee. They come near you. One of them — one of the larger ones, the color of deep limestone water — climbs onto your boot while you are sitting on the floor, and it lies there for twenty minutes, and you feel its warmth, which is the tile-warmth, and its weight, which is almost nothing, and its hum, which is the column-hum translated through a body the size of your hand, and it is doing something to your boot, or through your boot, or with your boot — reading the leather, perhaps, or the rubber, or the salt from your skin that has dissolved into the surface, or the heat signature of your foot, which is unlike any heat signature in this chamber, which is new data, which is interesting, which is — for a value of *interesting* that means *something the calculation had not previously encountered* — being incorporated.

And then it leaves. It slides off your boot, onto the tile, and moves away, slowly, toward a column, and it carries with it whatever it learned from your boot, and the calculation advances by some infinitesimal fraction, and the chamber hums its hum, and the water weeps, and the green glows in the cracks, and the light breathes its amber breath, and you are here, and then you are not, and the Attendants do not notice the difference, because you were a brief input in a long calculation, a single data point in a series that began before you arrived and will continue after you leave, and the series is the point, and the point is the series, and the Attendants are the fingers that hold the thread, and the thread is the hum, and the hum does not stop.
