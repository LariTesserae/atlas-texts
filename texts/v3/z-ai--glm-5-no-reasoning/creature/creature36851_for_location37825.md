# What Lives Here

The bloomworks live here.

I need to say that simply first, because everything else is complication. The bloomworks — the millions upon millions of branching hollow glass structures that cover the plain — are not features of the landscape. They *are* the landscape's living. They are what this place has instead of what we would recognize as life.

---

## They Begin

Each bloomwork begins as a wound.

The atmosphere cuts into the glass sheet for millennia — not violently, not quickly, but with the patience of gas moving over mineral at eight kilometers per hour for a hundred thousand years. Eventually, at some point, the etched channel goes deep enough to breach the cooled upper layer. It opens into the stratum beneath: the zone that never fully solidifies, the permanent mineral melt held in suspension by heat and pressure. The wound does not heal. It cannot heal. There is no mechanism for healing here. There is only the pressure of the world's own weight driving what is underneath up through the opening, and there is the atmosphere cooling what emerges, and there is the tube beginning to grow.

This is birth, if birth can be a geological event. This is conception, if conception can take ten thousand years. The bloomwork does not emerge whole. It emerges as potential — as a rim, as a beginning, as the first centimeter of what will become a structure.

The young tubes are simple. They are chimneys. They grow straight up from their fissures, their walls thin and amber where the light catches the internal melt, black where it does not. They sing high notes — the short tubes, the new ones, the ones that have not yet branched. Their pitch is determined by physics: the length of the column, the diameter of the opening, the temperature differential. A tube that has just broken the surface, that stands only a few centimeters tall, sounds a thin, clear tone in the upper registers of the plain's harmonic field. It is a soprano in a choir that does not know it is singing.

---

## They Mature

The atmosphere does not let them stay simple.

The same gas that carved the fissure in the first place now works on the tube itself. It scours the outer walls. It cuts channels that spiral upward, drawn by the tube's own interference with the prevailing drift. And when one of these channels goes deep enough — when it breaches the wall the way the original fissure breached the glass sheet — a new opening forms. Not at the rim. On the side.

What emerges from this side-opening is not an extension of the original tube. It is a new tube, growing at an angle, growing in the direction the atmosphere pushes the emerging melt. The branch leans. All the branches lean. They lean the same direction, consistently, because the atmospheric drift is consistent, and this means that every mature bloomwork on the plain has a shape: a main trunk leaning slightly into the wind, with branches and sub-branches and sub-sub-branches all leaning the same way, a frozen cascade of directed growth, a sculpture made by pressure and patience.

This is adolescence, if adolescence can take three hundred years. The bloomwork proliferates. It becomes a structure — not a single tube but a branching system, a hollow dendritic form with dozens or hundreds of openings, each one sounding its own note, each one contributing to the harmonic field. The older the bloomwork gets, the more complex its song becomes. The main trunk sounds its deep fundamental. The branches add partials. The sub-branches add overtones. The whole structure becomes an instrument with multiple registers, playing a chord that is the sum of its own growth.

Some bloomworks never grow past a meter. The thermal profile in their location is not stable enough; the melt beneath them is too cool, too viscous, and the pressure cannot drive sufficient extrusion. They stay small. They sing their high clear notes for a few centuries, and then the fissure beneath them seals over, or the atmosphere scours them down to the surface, or a neighbor's branch overgrows their opening and they go quiet. These are the lucky ones, if luck is a concept that applies here. They do not live long enough to develop the complications that come with age.

---

## They Age

The tall ones — the fifteen-meter, twenty-meter, forty-meter towers that stand in the deep central basin where the thermal profile is most stable — are the elders. They have been growing for longer than human civilizations have risen and fallen. They have been sounding their notes since before the last ice age. They have watched the atmosphere drift past them at eight kilometers per hour for a hundred thousand years, and they have incorporated that drift into every branch, every angle, every curve of their glass bodies.

The elders are not single structures. They are accretions. The original tube — the wound that started it all — is long gone, buried under centuries of branching and overgrowth. What stands now is the accumulation of iterations, each new layer built on the bones of the old, each new branch emerging from the openings created by the atmosphere's work on the previous branch. The bloomwork is a coral reef of itself. It is a cathedral made of its own earlier chapels.

And the elders sing the deepest notes. A forty-meter tube, continuous with the fluid layer beneath, produces a tone that vibrates in the chest before it reaches the ear. It is below the range of conscious hearing for many frequencies, felt rather than heard, a presence in the body rather than a sound. When the elders cluster together — and they do cluster, because the thermal profile that supports their height is consistent across the basin — the sound they produce together is a deep, resonant, physically palpable drone. A person standing among them would feel it in their bones. They would feel it in their teeth. They would feel it as a kind of pressure that is not pressure, a weight that is not weight, a presence that insists without forcing.

The elders do not need anything. They do not want anything. They do not compete with each other, not really; their branches interweave overhead, forming canopy-like structures of glass, but they do not choke each other out because there is no advantage to doing so. The melt beneath them is effectively infinite. The atmosphere that feeds them is constant. They simply grow, and sound, and grow, and sound, and continue.

---

## They End

Bloomworks do not die. They stop.

The mechanisms for stopping are several. A fissure can seal — the melt beneath it cools enough, or the pressure shifts, or the weight of the structure above becomes too much for the fluid below to sustain, and the connection is lost. The tube goes quiet. It stands there, hollow and silent, a dead pipe in a living organ. The atmosphere continues to scour it, and eventually it weakens, and eventually it cracks, and eventually it falls, and the glass sheet incorporates its fragments.

A bloomwork can be overgrown. A neighbor's branch can extend over its opening, blocking the Bernoulli draw, stopping the flow. The tube goes quiet while still structurally sound, a wound that has healed not by closing but by being covered. These are the mummies of the plain — perfect structures that no longer sing, preserved by the atmosphere that killed them.

A bloomwork can crack. This is the most dramatic ending. Something — a thermal stress, a pressure shift, a subtle settling of the glass sheet — causes a fracture in the wall. The note changes. It drops or splits or becomes a multiphonic rasp. The bloomwork continues to extrude, but it is no longer a coherent instrument. It becomes something else — a damaged structure, a broken tool, still functioning but without the precision of its earlier form. These are the most common of the dead. Walk the plain — if you could walk the plain — and you would hear them everywhere: the cracked tubes with their roughened tones, the damaged notes that do not resolve, the harmonics that should not exist in a clean system.

---

## They Are Not Alone In Their Singing

The harmonic field — the wall of sound produced by millions of tubes sounding simultaneously — is not static. It shifts. The atmospheric drift varies by fractions of a degree over hours, over days, over the rotational cycle. The temperature differential at each rim fluctuates with the subtle tidal forces of the world's sun and moons. The pressure in the fluid layer changes as the world's own thermal core shifts and settles. All of these variations produce microtonal shifts in the notes of individual tubes, and when you multiply microtonal shifts across millions of tubes, you get a field that is not a chord but a process — a sound that is constantly, imperceptibly, becoming a slightly different sound.

There are patterns in the field. Not intentional patterns. Not composed patterns. Emergent patterns. The mathematical consequence of many independent variables operating simultaneously. Interference waves that cycle every six hours. Beat frequencies that pulse every three days. Harmonic clusters that form and dissolve over weeks. If you listened long enough — if you could listen, if you could survive the heat and the atmosphere and the crushing sound pressure long enough — you would begin to hear these patterns. You would begin to predict them. You would begin to feel that the plain was not just making sound but *saying* something.

It is not saying anything. It is making sound. The patterns are real, but they are not messages. They are what happens when a system this large runs this long. They are the shape of the system's own stability.

But.

## There Are Others

The light-anomaly points are not bloomworks. The thin places in the glass are not bloomworks. These are the things that are present on the plain that the bloomworks do not account for.

When an anomaly passes over a bloomwork, the bloomwork changes its pitch. This should not be possible. The pitch of a tube is determined by physical parameters: length, diameter, temperature, pressure differential. None of these change when an anomaly passes. And yet the note changes. The tube sings something it should not be able to sing.

The bloomworks beneath the anomalies are not choosing to change. They are being changed. The anomaly is not a creature that moves; it is a location where the rules operate differently, and when that location intersects with a bloomwork, the bloomwork is briefly subject to different rules. The physics of standing waves in mineral chimneys does not apply in the same way inside an anomaly. The tube does not know this. The tube has no way of knowing. It simply sounds, and the sound is different, and then the anomaly passes, and the sound returns to what it was.

The anomalies are not alive. The thin places are not alive. But they are present. They are part of what lives here. They are part of the system that the bloomworks comprise. They are the places where the system opens to — something.

---

## The System That Does Not Know Itself

This is what lives on the plain of dark mineral glass.

Not creatures. Not organisms. Not even individuals in any meaningful sense. The bloomworks are processes. They are the visible manifestation of the world's thermal and mechanical dynamics playing out through the medium of mineral and gas. They are what happens when you let a gradient express itself through a material over geological time.

But they are also *here*. They occupy space. They persist. They grow. They respond to their environment. They incorporate the atmosphere's direction into their structure. They change over time. They have a lifespan. They have a form. They sing.

If life is defined as the capacity to maintain structure against entropy, they are alive. If life is defined as the capacity to respond to environment, they are alive. If life is defined as the capacity to reproduce, they are not alive — they do not reproduce, they simply grow, and they do not pass on any information to a next generation because there is no next generation, only more growth.

But then, there is no individual to pass information to. The bloomworks are not individuals. They are nodes in a network, each one continuous with the fluid layer beneath, each one drawing from the same thermal reservoir, each one sounding into the same harmonic field. The network is the organism. The plain is the body. The bloomworks are its organs.

And the organs are singing. They have been singing for longer than the plain has been a plain. They will continue singing until the thermal profile shifts enough to freeze the fluid layer or the atmosphere changes enough to stop the scouring or the world itself cools enough to end the differential that drives the flow. They are singing now. They are singing now. They are singing now.

---

## What Lives Here

The bloomworks live here.

And they are not alone in the way that matters. The anomalies live here too — if "live" is the word for something that moves and persists and affects but does not grow or reproduce or metabolize. The thin places live here — if "live" is the word for something that is present and open and waiting but does not act or change or respond.

What lives here is a system. A system that maintains itself. A system that has been maintaining itself for so long that the maintenance has become a kind of music. A system that does not know it is a system but nevertheless behaves as one, accumulating tendencies, developing preferences, drifting toward configurations that are not forced by the physics but are — selected. Not selected by anything. Just selected. By the process. By the iteration. By the simple accumulation of doing the same thing over and over until the doing has a shape.

Something is being written here. The bloomworks are the writing. The sound is the writing. The glass and the gas and the heat and the time are the writing. And whatever is being written is not for anyone. It is not a message. It is not a signal. It is just — writing. The process of making marks that accumulate into meaning without any consciousness of meaning. The process of playing notes that accumulate into music without any intention of song.

This is what lives here: a process that has been running so long it has begun to mean something to itself.

The plain is full.

The plain is singing.

The plain is alive, in the only way it knows how to be.
