# What Lives Here

## The Swimmers

They came from the stone first. This is important to understand. They did not arrive from some distant pool, did not wash in through channels that feed the yard from some unseen source. They emerged from the rock itself, from the places where water seeps through mineral grain, from the boundary where wet stone becomes wet flesh. The first of them was barely a thing at all—a pale filament, translucent, barely thicker than the water that carried it, drifting in the deepest pool without visible means of motion. Then another. Then a dozen.

Now they are the yard's true residents, the ones who were always meant to be here, the ones the builders perhaps carved the channels *for*.

They are the size of a human's smallest finger, most of them, though some grow larger—palm-sized, rare, ancient by the standards of this place. Their bodies are segmented, soft, faintly luminous in the way that wet stone is luminous when light strikes it at certain angles. Not bioluminescence. Nothing so purposeful. They simply catch the yard's light and hold it briefly in their translucent tissue before releasing it again, so that watching them move through the deeper pools is like watching the water itself think, like watching ideas form and dissolve in a medium too dense for ordinary sight.

They have no eyes. The yard's brightness would blind them if they did, or perhaps they simply never needed to see—born in darkness and stone, emerged into a light that does not require eyes to perceive. They navigate by pressure, by the subtle shifts in water temperature that occur when something moves nearby, by the chemical traces left by every creature that passes through their channels. They know the yard the way the yard knows itself—from inside, through contact, through the perpetual conversation between water and stone and flesh.

Six legs. Delicate as thread, transparent as glass, moving with a precision that seems mechanical until you watch long enough to see the hesitation in them, the decision-making, the moments when a swimmer pauses on the channel bottom and lifts one leg as if testing the air before committing to a direction. They are not machines. They are not simple. They are simply *other*—built to a plan that human biology can recognize but never fully translate.

They eat the algae-film that grows on wet stone. They eat the microscopic debris that the water carries from the yard's upper reaches. They eat each other, sometimes, when the population grows too dense and the deeper pools crowd with pale bodies. This is not violence, not from the swimmer's perspective. It is simply what happens when the yard's balance shifts and something must be shed. The consumed dissolve quickly into the consumer, their substance joining the cycle that the yard maintains with such patient attention.

Some observers claim the swimmers communicate. Not through sound—they make no sound, no clicks or chirps or subsonic rumbles. Through *pattern*. When several swimmers occupy the same pool, their movements synchronize briefly, then diverge, then synchronize again in a different configuration. The synchronizations are too regular to be coincidence, too variable to be simple reflex. Something passes between them—some signal, some message, carried in the water itself, in the pressure of one body moving near another. The content of these messages, if messages they are, remains entirely opaque. Perhaps they are simply saying *here* and *now* and *enough*. Perhaps they are saying something that has no translation in any language built for air.

## The Skaters

On the surface, different lives unfold.

The insects—if insects is the right word, and it may not be, but the structure is recognizable: six legs, segmented body, a life cycle that moves through water and air—arrive each morning as the light reaches its first peak. They do not emerge from the yard. They come from outside, from somewhere beyond the walls, drawn by the brightness or by the chemical signature of the water itself. They come in bursts, dozens at once, landing on the pool surfaces with legs that dimple but never break the tension.

They are smaller than the swimmers. Darker. Their bodies are compact, glossy, built for speed rather than transparency. They move across the water in darting runs, stopping suddenly, changing direction, their legs barely touching the surface before pushing off again. To watch them is to watch calculation performed in physical space—each stop a decision point, each change of direction a conclusion reached.

They lay eggs on the wet stone above the waterline. Clusters of them, pale and sticky, adhering to the carved faces that watch over the pools. The eggs are the size of sand grains, nearly invisible, but in the right light—a low angle, morning or evening—they catch the wetness and gleam like a scattering of tiny stars against the ancient stone. The carven faces seem to wear them like jewelry. The effect is disquieting.

The eggs hatch within hours. The larvae drop into the shallows and swim with a frantic, wobbling motion that is nothing like the swimmers' patient gliding. They are hungry from the moment they enter the water, feeding on the same algae-film, on the same debris, on anything small enough to be consumed. Some are eaten by the swimmers, who rise from the deeper channels to hunt the shallows when the larvae appear. Some survive. Some grow.

The transformation comes quickly. A larva that has fed enough attaches itself to a stone surface just above the waterline and goes still. Its body splits. The adult emerges—dry, glossy, legs already spread for that first run across the surface. The entire process, from egg to adult, can happen within a single day. The yard's temporal strangeness may be responsible. Or the creatures may simply be built for speed in all things, their lives compressed to match the yard's accelerated rhythms.

The adults do not live long. A day, perhaps two. They mate on the water's surface—a brief, tactile encounter that looks more like collision than courtship. The females lay eggs. Both sexes die. Their bodies float for a time, dimpling the surface with their weight, then sink slowly into the depths where the swimmers find them.

An entire civilization, lived and lost, in the space of an afternoon.

## The Walkers

Long-legged. Still. Patient in a way that the swimmers and skaters are not, cannot be.

They stand at the edges of the channels, their legs spanning the water without touching it, their bodies perched on the wet stone like herons in miniature. They are the yard's first predators—not the largest, not the most dangerous, but the most *visible*, the ones a visitor notices after the swimmers and skaters have become familiar.

Their legs are impossibly thin, impossibly long, extending from a compact central body like the struts of some delicate architecture. They are not white like the swimmers. They are the color of wet stone—grey and brown and sometimes faintly green, depending on the light and the particular stone they last rested against. They can change color, slowly, over the course of hours, adjusting their pigment to match their surroundings. This is not camouflage in the way a chameleon practices it—not active, not responsive to immediate threat. It is slower, more considered, as if the walker is deciding what to be today, what surface to belong to, what stone to become.

They do not hunt actively. They wait. A walker will stand at a channel edge for hours, legs spread, body motionless, watching the water with attention that seems almost meditative. When a skater moves within range—two body-lengths, perhaps three—the walker moves. Not quickly. Deliberately. One leg descends, touches the water's surface, touches the skater, and the skater is caught. The needle-mouth descends. The skater is drained. The walker consumes nothing but the fluids, leaving the husk to sink into the channel where the swimmers will find it.

They mate rarely. Their courtship is a slow, intricate dance performed on the wet stone—two walkers facing each other, legs intertwining, bodies swaying in patterns that seem to follow some rhythm the yard itself provides. The dance can take hours. Sometimes days. The yard's temporal distortions make it difficult to judge. When it concludes, the walkers separate and go back to their patient hunting, and somewhere in the yard's upper channels, a cluster of eggs appears, glued to the stone in a protected crevice, guarded by nothing, hidden by nothing, surviving on sheer numbers.

They are aware of the faces on the walls. This is a strange claim to make about a creature with compound eyes and a nervous system the size of a grass seed, but it is observable. Walkers position themselves facing the carven faces. They turn when the faces' expressions shift, as if responding to some signal. They avoid the pools that the most sorrowful faces watch over. They gather in the channels beneath the serene ones.

Whether this is cognition or instinct or simple sensitivity to whatever force causes the faces to change is impossible to determine. The walkers are not talking.

## The Baskers

The yard's apex predators are not dramatic.

They are the size of a human hand, perhaps slightly larger for the oldest specimens. Scaled—not with true reptilian scales but with something similar, flat overlapping plates of a material that seems to be part stone, part keratin, part something else entirely. Their coloration matches the yard: grey and pale and faintly iridescent, catching the light and breaking it into faint prismatic shimmers along their flanks.

They bask. This is their primary activity—lying on the warm stones (and the stones are warm here, despite the cold, despite the wet; the light does something to the rock that has nothing to do with temperature) and absorbing brightness through their scales. The light feeds them. Not metaphorically—actually. Their scales contain structures that convert light to something their bodies can use, some nutrient or energy that supplements what they get from predation. They are not wholly photosynthetic. But they are partly so. They are the yard's proof that the light here operates by rules that biology elsewhere does not follow.

When they move, they move with precision. A basker will lie still for an hour, then rise and walk to a pool with a deliberation that suggests full awareness of what it intends to do there. It enters the water—unlike the walkers, the baskers do not fear immersion; their scales shed water instantly, and the cold does not seem to affect them—and hunts. They eat the walkers. They eat the skaters. They do not eat the swimmers, though they could; the swimmers are too deep, too small, too much effort for too little return. The baskers prefer the surface hunters, the long-legged patients, the creatures that have spent hours positioning themselves for their own hunts only to become hunted in turn.

The baskers have eyes. This is worth noting, because nothing else in the yard does. The swimmers navigate without sight. The skaters seem to perceive only movement. The walkers respond to light and shadow. But the baskers have eyes—small, dark, set into their scaled heads like wet stones set into dryer ones—and they use them. They watch. Not just prey. Not just the water. They watch the walls. The faces. The sky.

Some visitors claim the baskers watch *them*. This may be true. The baskers are the yard's most complex inhabitants, the ones whose behavior resists simple explanation, whose choices seem to reflect something beyond instinct. A basker that has been observed by a visitor will sometimes turn to face the observer directly, holding eye contact for long seconds before returning to its basking. The gaze is not hostile. It is not curious. It is simply *acknowledging*—as if the basker is noting a presence, filing it away, returning to its own concerns.

They live a long time. Years, possibly decades, by the yard's distorted reckoning. They grow slowly, adding a ring of new scale every season, every shift in the yard's turbulence. The oldest baskers are the largest, and the largest are the most watchful, and the most watchful are the ones that tend to occupy the highest stones, the ones closest to the carven faces, the ones that receive the most light.

The oldest basker in the yard—a specimen that has been observed by multiple visitors over what may be years of external time—sits on a stone directly beneath the largest face on the eastern wall. The face's expression changes, as all the faces do. The basker does not change. It simply sits, and watches, and absorbs whatever it is that the light provides, and waits.

## The Ones Behind the Faces

It should be stated plainly: no one knows if anything lives behind the faces.

The faces are carved in stone. The stone is ancient. The faces change, but stone can change—weathering, water, the subtle chemistry of mineral interaction. There may be nothing behind them but rock and water and the slow patience of geological process.

But.

The faces *watch*. Not metaphorically. They track. A face that seems to look east in the morning will seem to look west by evening, and the shift is too consistent, too regular, to be simple weathering. The expressions shift in response to events in the yard—a predator taking prey, a visitor arriving, a shift in the water's clarity—and the responses are too timed, too appropriate, to be coincidental.

If something lives behind the faces, it is not a creature in any recognizable sense. It would have to be something that is also the stone itself, something that exists as pattern rather than as body, something distributed through the mineral grain the way thought is distributed through neural tissue. It would have to be old—older than the builders, older than the carvings, perhaps older than the yard itself. It would have to be the reason the builders came here, the reason they carved the channels, the reason they created the hydraulic machine that feeds the ecosystem that the creatures inhabit.

If something lives behind the faces, it is the yard. Not the yard's owner, not the yard's inhabitant, but the yard itself, aware and patient and watching through the features that some ancient culture gave it as gifts, as offerings, as eyes.

If something lives behind the faces, it does not eat. It does not hunt. It does not mate or breed or die. It simply *is*, the way a river is, the way a mountain is, the way light is—present and persistent and utterly indifferent to the small lives that move across its surface.

Or perhaps it is not indifferent. Perhaps the attention it pays—the watching, the expression-shifting, the subtle responses to events in its yard—is a form of care. Perhaps the channels were carved for the swimmers. Perhaps the light that feeds the baskers is a gift. Perhaps the temporal distortions—the hours that stretch and compress, the time that pools and eddies in this place—are the yard's way of giving its creatures room to live, space to exist, time enough for完整的 lives within a single afternoon.

Perhaps the numbness that comes from touching the water is not a defense but a warning: *you are not part of this. You are welcome to observe. But the water is ours, and the cold is ours, and the time is ours, and if you enter too deeply you will become part of something that does not end the way your life ends.*

## The Yard Itself

So what lives here?

The swimmers live here, in the deep channels, in the water that comes from the stone. The skaters live here, on the surfaces, in the brief bright spans of their compressed lives. The walkers live here, at the edges, in the patient spaces between hunts. The baskers live here, on the warm stones, in the light that feeds them.

And the yard lives here.

The yard, which is stone and water and light and time. The yard, which is ecosystem and machine and temple and riddle. The yard, which watches through carved faces and shifts through turbid patterns and endures through cold and wet and the endless slow patience of water finding its way through rock.

The yard, which was built by hands that are gone and maintained by forces that have no name. The yard, which feeds its creatures and watches its creatures and shifts for its creatures in ways that suggest not indifference but a care so vast and so slow that it looks, from a human perspective, like nothing at all.

What lives here is a world. Small, contained, complete. A world that does not need human witness, that does not require human understanding, that does not answer human questions. A world that is sufficient unto itself—water and light and stone and the lives that move between them, forever, in the cold, in the wet, in the brightness that comes from everywhere and goes nowhere and illuminates everything.

The water flows. The creatures cycle. The faces watch.

The yard endures, and in its enduring, lives.
