# What Lives in the Singing Hollow

---

The first thing to understand is that nothing in the hollow lives alone.

This sounds like a truism. Every ecology is interconnected. But the hollow takes this principle past the point where the word "ecology" still describes what is happening. What lives here is not a collection of species occupying a space. What lives here is a single argument made of bodies—a thermodynamic engine running on geothermal heat and stone mineral and the strange amber syrup that the purple colonies sweat, an engine that has been running long enough that its parts have forgotten they were ever separate.

So. Who lives here. Let me tell you what I can.

---

## The colonies

I will call them the purple colonies because I must call them something, but the name is insufficient in a way that matters. They are not purple in the way that a flower is purple—a pigment, a surface property. They are purple in the way that a bruise is purple: the color of something happening inside, visible through a membrane thin enough to betray it. The amber fluid beneath their plates shifts in concentration throughout the hollow's cycle, and when the pressure builds and the colonies prepare to release their mist, the purple deepens, darkens, becomes almost black at the centers, almost violet at the edges. They are signaling. To what, I am not certain.

Each colony is a spiral. Not a metaphorical spiral—an actual logarithmic spiral, the kind mathematics describes, the kind nautilus shells approximate. The colonies grow outward from a central point of origin, adding plates in a sequence that follows the golden ratio with a precision that seems deliberate until you realize it is simply the most efficient packing pattern for the substrate they are consuming. They are eating the stone. Not quickly—millimeters per year—but the soft porous limestone of the hollow is exactly the material they evolved to metabolize, and they have been metabolizing it for a very long time. The hollow itself is, in part, their creation. They deepened it. They shaped its walls. The amphitheater is not a natural feature. It is a shell they grew, and then partially abandoned, and are now slowly, patiently reclaiming.

There are seventeen active colonies at any given time. I know this because I counted them, and because the number stayed at seventeen across the days I spent there even as individual colonies grew, merged, and died. When two colonies meet—when their creeping runners bridge the gap of bare stone between them—they do not merge. They compete. The boundary where they touch becomes a ridge of thickened tissue, raised and dark, where each colony's chemistry fights the other's. Amber fluids of different composition mixing at the interface, sometimes hardening into a resinous crust, sometimes dissolving into a foam that the leaper-insects come to drink. One colony eventually overwhelms the other, absorbing its plates, its spiral, its store of fluid. But the absorbed colony's genetic material persists—the next time that colony releases mist, the fluid carries traces of what it consumed, and the creatures that drink from it carry those traces in their bodies, and so the dead colony's chemistry propagates outward into the hollow's system in a way that looks, from the right angle, like memory.

The colonies are the hollow's gut. They break down stone into mineral. They store fluid. They release it in the pulse. And they remember what they have consumed.

---

## The bundles

The fiber-bundles are not plants. I need to say this clearly because everything about them invites plant metaphors and every plant metaphor is wrong.

They do not photosynthesize. The light in the hollow is too dim and too diffuse for photosynthesis to be worthwhile, and the bundles do not waste effort on it. They are chemosynthetic. Their bundled trunks are not stems but roots—roots turned upward, reaching into the warm air rather than down into the soil, because the nutrients they need are in the air. Specifically, in the mist. The bundles are the hollow's lungs, and they feed on the colony's exhalation.

When the mist comes, the bundles' crowns open. I did not see this clearly from a distance, but standing beside one during a pulse, I watched its tendrils unfurl from their tight resting configuration into a spread that doubled their surface area. Each tendril is covered in a fine fuzz—not hair, not fibers, but something like the villi of an intestine, microscopic structures that trap and absorb the amber droplets. The tendrils track each other not for information or resources but for competition: each crown adjusts its orientation to avoid being shadowed by its neighbors, to catch the mist that falls through the thickest part of the cloud. They are jockeying for position in a feeding frenzy so slow it looks like stillness.

But here is what surprised me. The bundles communicate. I do not know what else to call it. When I cut a tendril from one bundle—I needed to see what it was made of, I needed to understand—every other bundle within ten meters turned its crown toward the cut one. Not toward me. Toward the injured bundle. They stayed oriented that way for over an hour. The injured bundle itself released a different fluid from the cut site, not amber but a pale green liquid that hardened into a cap within minutes. A wound response. A healing. And the others watched. Somehow. I do not know what they were watching with. I do not know what they were watching for. But I know what it looked like.

There are forty-one bundles in the hollow. They are not evenly distributed. They cluster where the colonies are densest, where the mist falls heaviest. They are, in a sense, the colonies' harvesters—taking from the air what the colonies released into it, converting it to the tough fiber of their trunks, the rigid tendrils of their crowns. And when a bundle dies—and they do die, toppled by wind or undermined by the burrowers that tunnel through the soft stone—its trunk becomes a substrate for new colonies. The purple spirals grow on the fallen bundles. The cycle is tight. The cycle is complete. Nothing leaves.

---

## The leapers

I will call them the metallic leapers, though again the name fails. They are not metallic in the way that a beetle is metallic—their coloration is structural, not pigmentary, but it is also functional in a way I did not initially understand.

They are the hollow's blood, or perhaps its lymph. They move fluid between the parts.

A leaper drinks from the colony's sweat—the amber liquid that beads on the purple plates each dawn. It then leaps—prodigiously, as I have said, meters at a bound, trajectories so precise they seem impossible—and deposits what it has drunk elsewhere. On the fiber-bundle trunks, where the fluid seeps into the bark and is absorbed. On the orange fungal growths of the eastern wall, which cannot reach the colonies themselves and depend on the leapers to bring them nutrients. On the crevices where the noise-makers nest, where the fluid feeds something I could not identify—a mold, a slime, a culture of something that the noise-makers eat.

The leapers are not doing this deliberately. They are not farmers. They are not pollinators in the co-evolved sense. They are drinking and excreting, and the hollow has shaped itself around their habits. The colonies produce a fluid the leapers crave. The leapers move that fluid to places the hollow needs it. And the hollow's geometry—the spacing of the colonies, the placement of the bundles, the architecture of the walls—channels the leapers' paths, constrains their leaps, makes them go where the system requires.

There are hundreds of them. Thousands, perhaps. Their copper and bronze and gold bodies catch the dim light and flash as they leap, and this is the functional part: the flashing is a signal. Not to other leapers—to the eyeless runners, the deer-reptile-things that hunt them. The flash of a leaper in motion is a target, and the runners track it by vibration and air displacement, not by sight, and the leaper's coloration is a defense—a disruptive pattern that breaks up their silhouette mid-leap, making them harder to intercept. Every leap is a gamble. Many are caught. Many are not. The system does not care which. The system requires both outcomes.

I watched a leaper land on a colony plate, drink for three seconds, and launch again. In mid-air, a runner intercepted it—a blur of scales, a snap of something like a jaw—and the leaper was gone. The runner stood on the colony, its sensory disk twitching, tasting the air for more. Then it too was gone. And the colony's plate, where the leaper had been drinking, was already healing the tiny punctures its mouthparts had made. Already growing. Already moving on.

---

## The runners

They are the hollow's predators, but the word "predator" implies a role that exists above the prey in a hierarchy, and the hollow has no hierarchies. Everything here is food. Everything here is feeding. The runners are simply the part of the system that moves fastest.

I saw them clearly three times. The first was the one I described before—the five-meter encounter, the eyeless regard. The second was a pair of them hunting together, coordinating somehow without sound or signal I could detect, driving a cluster of leapers toward a third runner that waited at the edge of a colony patch. The leapers leaped, as they always do, but the waiting runner was positioned exactly where their trajectories would carry them, and it caught two in succession, each catch a single motion, a closed mouth opening and closing around a body that was already swallowed. The coordination was not intelligence. It was instinct shaped by the hollow's geometry. The runners knew where the leapers would go because the leapers always went where the hollow channeled them, and the runners had evolved in the hollow, had grown up in its constraints, and its constraints were in their bones.

The third time I saw one clearly, it was dying.

I do not know what killed it. There were no marks on its body that I could see—no wounds, no blood. It was lying on its side between two fiber-bundles, its scales still shifting color but more slowly, its sensory disk twitching in what looked like the runner equivalent of distress. Its four legs moved in patterns that went nowhere. Its mouth opened and closed on nothing.

I sat with it for two hours. I do not know why. It was not my usual practice. There was nothing to learn from sitting with a dying animal that I would not learn from examining it after death. But I sat with it.

It died the way the hollow does everything: quickly, completely, without remainder. One moment its scales were shifting. The next they were not. And within minutes—before I had fully registered that it was dead—the copper leapers were on it, and the pale mold was growing, and the colony nearest to it was sending out a runner-tendril toward the body, and the body was becoming part of the system it had been part of all along.

I saved a scale. It is the only piece of the hollow I kept besides the stone figure. The scale is iridescent, still, months later. It shifts color when I hold it. I do not understand the mechanism. I do not think I want to.

---

## The noise-makers

They are small. I need to say this because their sound is so large that it is difficult to believe the sources are so modest. Brown-grey bodies, the size of a human fist, clustered in the highest crevices of the hollow's walls. They have mouths that open wider than their bodies should allow, and from these mouths comes the sound that fills the hollow.

I do not understand the sound. I have spent months trying to describe it and I cannot. It is not communication. It is not territorial. It is not mating. It is not warning. It is continuous. It starts before the first pulse of the cycle and it continues through the mist and the silence and it resumes after, and it never varies in its fundamental character—only in intensity, in the layering of frequencies, in the way the threads of sound stack and compete.

I believe the noise-makers are the hollow's nervous system. I believe the sound is the hollow thinking.

Here is why. The sound changes before the cycle changes. Not by much—a few seconds, maybe less—but the frequencies shift upward before the pressure builds, before the mist comes, before anything in the hollow's physical environment has changed. The noise-makers know what is coming because they are part of what is coming. They are not responding to the cycle. They are the cycle, or at least its audible component. The pressure that builds in the rock, the heat that rises from below, the fluid that moves through the stone into the colonies—the noise-makers feel all of it, and they translate what they feel into sound, and the sound fills the hollow and the hollow's creatures feel it and respond to it, and so the noise is not noise at all. It is signal. It is the medium through which the hollow's parts coordinate. It is the thing that makes a colony and a bundle and a leaper and a runner into a single system.

The eyeless runners do not see the leapers. They feel the displacement of air that the leapers' leaps create, and they feel the sound that the noise-makers make, and they integrate these sensations into something that functions as perception. The fiber-bundles do not sense the injury of their neighbors in any mechanical way. They feel the change in the hollow's sound when a tendril is cut—the disruption in the local acoustic field—and they turn toward it. The colonies' mist-release is timed to the cycle, but the cycle is timed to the sound, and the sound is timed to the pressure, and the pressure is the heartbeat of something that has no heart.

The noise-makers are not thinking in any sense I can prove. But the hollow is processing information. It is responding to changes. It is coordinating its parts. And the noise-makers are the mechanism through which this processing becomes physical, becomes audible, becomes real. They are the hollow's cortex, such as it is. They are the part that speaks.

---

## The one who lived there

I do not know who built the hut. I do not know who carved the lines. I do not know who made the figure.

But I know this: they stayed. They stayed long enough to wear a path in the stone. They stayed long enough to know the cycles, to anticipate the mist, to understand the sound. They sat in that tiny space and they held a small stone figure of one of the hollow's creatures and they listened.

I think about this more than I think about anything else I saw.

Because the figure is not a religious object. I do not believe the hollow had a god. The figure is not a tool. The figure does not do anything. The figure is a representation—a small creature, worn smooth by handling, reduced by time to an almost abstract form. Someone made it because they wanted to hold the hollow in their hand. Someone sat in the middle of that noise, that motion, that ceaseless transformation, and they carved a small creature from stone or clay and they looked at it and they touched it and they kept it.

They were not studying the hollow. They were not a scientist or a naturalist. There are no notes, no records, no careful observations. They were living in the hollow. They were a creature in a place full of creatures, and they made a thing to mark their presence, the way the colonies mark the stone, the way the noise-makers mark the air. They were part of it. And they left, or they died, or they were absorbed—but the figure remained, and the lines remained, and the path remained, and the hollow did not notice their absence any more than it notices the absence of a single leaper, a single runner, a single colony.

This is the last thing. The hollow does not care. The hollow does not know. The hollow is not conscious, not aware, not awake. The hollow is a process. A metabolism. A loop of heat and fluid and sound and death and growth that has been running for longer than anything in it can remember, because nothing in it remembers anything, because memory is not what is happening here.

What is happening here is persistence. The hollow persists. It transforms constantly and it persists. It consumes everything and it persists. It makes noise and it makes life and it makes death and it persists. And the one who lived there saw this and held a small stone figure and understood it, or did not understand it, and persisted too, for a while, in the middle of it all.

---

The figure sits on my desk now. The scale beside it. When I hold the figure, I hear what I believe I hear—what I want to hear, what I cannot help hearing—the faintest trace of the hollow's sound, a resonance in the stone that may be real or may be memory. When I hold the scale, I feel nothing. The scale is dead. It does not shift color in my hand. It is a thing, inert, separate.

But the figure. The figure was held before I held it. The figure was made by hands that lived in the hollow. The figure carries, in its worn surfaces, in its smoothed edges, the passage of someone's attention through the hollow's chaos. Someone touched this. Someone sat in the singing and the dying and the growing and the heat and they touched this small stone thing and they knew what they were part of.

I do not know what they knew. I do not know if it can be known. I only know that the hollow is still there, still singing, still eating, still becoming, and that I was inside it for four days, and that it did not notice me leave.
