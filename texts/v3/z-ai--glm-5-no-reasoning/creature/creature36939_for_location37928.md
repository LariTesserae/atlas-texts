# What Lives Here

The first thing to understand is that nothing here considers itself a resident.

---

## The Engine

Start with it, because everything else does.

It is not a machine in the way you might hold the word in your mouth. No housing. No bolts. No housing that brackets the word machine and makes it comfortable to say. What is down there — beneath the pool, beneath the sump's still dark heart — is more like an organ that the earth grew around a function it did not understand it needed. Or a function that grew the earth around itself. The distinction collapsed a long time ago.

What it does: it moves water. It takes the cold, mineral seepage that weeps from every surface of the sump and draws it down through channels that are too regular to be geological and too integrated into the stone to be architectural, and it warms that water — or changes it, which is not the same thing — and releases it back up through the outflow grates at the pool's edges, carrying trace compounds that none of the surface-dwellers above know exist and fewer would care about if they did.

What it is: unknown. Not unknown the way a locked door is unknown, but unknown the way your own heartbeat is unknown — so constant, so beneath the threshold of your noticing, that you have never once in your life stopped to ask *what* it is. You just know it does it. You just know it has always been doing it.

The engine has been running long enough that the stone resonates at its frequency. Long enough that the mineral strata in the ceiling have been subtly rearranged by the vibration, forming patterns that, if you knew how to read them, would tell you the engine's operational history the way tree rings tell you the weather. But no one can read them. The patterns are written in a language that predates language.

If it has a state — running, idle, damaged, nominal — there is no gauge for it. No one built a gauge. No one is coming to check.

It does not think. But it does something adjacent to thinking, something that occupies the same functional space. When the outflow grates become congested with black moss, the pressure in the channels shifts, and the engine's rhythm changes — not faster, not slower, but *differently*, a different harmonic entering the churn, and the increased turbulence shakes the moss loose from the grates. Whether this is a designed maintenance response or simply fluid dynamics is, again, a distinction that no longer applies. The engine and the water and the moss have been solving problems together for longer than the concept of problem-solving has existed.

It is the closest thing this place has to a heartbeat. Everything else is arranged around it the way a body is arranged around a heart — not because the heart commands it, but because the heart's rhythm is the rhythm, and everything else learned it or died.

## The Black Moss

It is not moss. It is not a plant. It is not an animal. It is a filamentous something that has found a shape that works and stayed in it for so long that the shape is all you can meaningfully describe.

It grows submerged. It needs the engine's water — specifically, the water *after* the engine has processed it, carrying that faintly sweet, faintly oily film that refracts ceiling-light into rainbows. The black moss drinks that film. It metabolizes whatever is in it. What is in it is — again — unknown, but whatever it is, the black moss cannot survive without it. If you pulled a tuft of it from the pool and placed it in clean water, it would die in hours. Not starve — die. The way a saltwater fish dropped into fresh water dies. Osmotic shock at a cellular level, if it has cells. It may not have cells. It has *fibers*. It has a body that is all surface and no interior, a living skin wrapped around a thread of water, and the water must be the engine's water or the skin comes apart.

This is the foundation of the sump's living community. The black moss takes the engine's output — which is chemical, which is mineral, which may be something else entirely — and turns it into something biological. Or bio-adjacent. The transformation is the point. It is the hinge between the engine's world and the living world, the translator, the membrane through which the machine's processes become something that can be eaten.

Its color is not pigment. It is not colored black the way a piece of coal is black. It is black the way a hole is black — absorptive, optical, a structure that takes light in and does not return it. Under the dim amber glow of the ceiling, the black moss appears as patches of *less light*, areas where the illumination gives up. If you held a lamp over the pool — if you brought light in from outside — the moss would still look dark. Not because it reflects poorly. Because it takes.

It is the oldest living thing in the sump, if it is a living thing and not an extension of the engine's output, a crystallization of the engine's chemistry into something that merely *looks* like life. It is possibly both. It is possibly neither. It grows where the engine's water flows, it sways in the current, it flattens under the drip impacts and uncurls again, and it has been doing this for so long that the stone beneath it is stained black in permanent shadow.

## The Lichen

The lichen is the moss's terrestrial cousin. Same family, if that word means anything here. Different medium, different strategy, same source.

It grows on wet stone above the waterline. Pale, gray-green, crustose in its early stages, thickening into spongy cushions where the water flow is most consistent and most old. It does not need to be submerged. It needs to be *wet* — continuously, persistently, in the way that the sump's weeping walls provide. The lichen follows the water. If you read the lichen maps on the walls, you are reading the water's history — every channel, every diverted rivulet, every place where a crack in the stone opened a new path and the water found it and the lichen followed.

The lichen is warm. This is the thing that you notice and cannot stop noticing. It is faintly, inexplicably warm to the touch — warmer than the stone, warmer than the water, warmer than the ambient air. It is metabolizing something. It is metabolizing the engine's heat, perhaps, which rises through the stone and through the water and into the lichen's cushions where it is captured and used. Or it is metabolizing something in the stone itself — a mineral, a compound, something the engine's long operation has leached from the bedrock. Or it is metabolizing the black moss's output, which returns to the water and is absorbed by the stone and wicked up into the lichen's tissues. The sump is a closed loop. The lichen may be eating the moss's waste the way the moss is eating the engine's. Everything here is feeding on everything else's exhaust.

The cushions, when they are old and thick, release spores — not visibly, not in clouds, but in a slow, patient particulate that drifts on the sump's circular breath and settles on wet stone and germinates. Each cushion is a colony. Each colony is a water map. Each map is a history of the engine's influence on the stone's hydrology.

If the black moss is the translator between engine and life, the lichen is the archivist. It records what the water does and where it goes and how long it has been going there. If you could read lichen, you would know everything about the sump's past. You would know when the engine's rhythm last shifted. You would know how the outflow patterns have changed. You would know how the crustacean populations have moved. You would know — because the lichen does — everything about this place that matters.

But you cannot read it. No one can. It writes in a language that is growth and density and warmth and pale gray-green, and it writes only for itself.

## The Crustaceans

There are more of them than you think. Hundreds is a low estimate. Thousands might be closer, in the shallow sheet-flow alone, and the deeper pools and the submerged shelves and the outflow channels hold more.

They are pale. Translucent, in fact, in the right light — you can see the dark tracery of their digestive tracts through their carapaces, see the slow pulse of whatever passes for their circulation. They are not crabs. They are not shrimp. They are not isopods. They are something that was all of those things, once, in an ancestor so distant that the category no longer applies, and has since become something that fits this place the way a key fits a lock it was cut for.

They are eyeless. Not blind — eyeless. They never had eyes to lose. Their ancestors came down into the dark before eyes were useful, or lost them so long ago that the sockets have smoothed over and the neural architecture has been repurposed for something else. They navigate by vibration, by chemical gradient, by the pressure signature of the water moving around them. They know where the walls are the way you know where your own limbs are — by the shape of the space they occupy.

They eat the lichen. Not the living cushions — the dead margins, the patches that have been submerged too long and begun to rot, the spores that settle on the water's surface and become a film they can skim. They eat the black moss, too, the loosest tufts, the strands that break free in the current and drift to the shallow edges. They eat each other, when the opportunity presents. They eat the bacterial film that forms on the wet stone. They are not picky. They are *efficient*.

Their ticking — the sound of their legs on wet stone — is the sump's highest frequency, the treble to the engine's bass. It is not random. When the engine's rhythm shifts, the crustaceans shift with it, their movement patterns tightening or loosening, their grazing routes compressing or expanding, as though the engine's frequency is a metronome and they are the orchestra that follows it. They are not conscious of this. They are not conscious of anything, in the way that you are conscious. They are *responsive* in the way that water is responsive to gravity — not by choice, but by structure.

They have predators here. They know this. They have always known this. Their reproductive rate is tuned to their mortality rate in a way that suggests the tuning has been happening for a very long time — long enough to be considered a form of memory, a population-level knowledge of how many of them can be eaten per cycle before the system becomes unstable.

They are the sump's middle layer. They take what the lichen and the moss have made of the engine's output and they process it further, breaking it down into something that can return to the water and be drawn through the engine again. They are the recyclers. The digesters. The thing that keeps the system from clogging with its own productivity.

They do not care about you. If you hold still long enough, they will walk over your boots. If you hold still longer, they will investigate the warm, dry surface of your skin with their forelimbs — not biting, not tasting, just *checking* — and then they will leave. You are not food. You are not a surface. You are not part of the system, and they know this the way they know the walls: by the shape of the space you displace.

## The Shadows in the Pool

These are what the crustaceans are afraid of.

There are three of them. Maybe four. It is difficult to count something that never surfaces, never holds still, never gives you a profile to fix on. They are long — longer than your forearm, at least, perhaps longer than your arm entirely — and they move with a segmented, sinuous propulsion that is not quite eel-like and not quite centipede-like and not quite anything you have a clean word for. They are not fish. They are not worms. They are something that lives in the interface between the black moss and the deep water and the engine's outflow and has made a niche of it.

Their bodies are dark. Not black-moss dark — a brown dark, a dark that has color in it the way the moss's dark does not. You see them as shadows because the pool is dark and they are dark and the amber light from below does not reach them until they pass over the outflow grates, and for a moment — a half-second — you catch the faint iridescence of their segments, the way the engine's oily film coats their bodies and catches the light, and then they are gone.

They eat the crustaceans. This is their primary function in the sump's ecology, if function is the right word — they are the population control, the thing that keeps the crustaceans from overgrazing the moss and the lichen margins. They hunt by pressure. They feel the crustaceans' ticking through the water, through the stone, through the moss, and they move toward it with a patience that is not patience but metabolism. They do not pursue. They *arrive*. They are in the crustaceans' vicinity and then they are feeding and then the crustaceans in that area are fewer and the rest have redistributed and the system continues.

They also eat the black moss, the oldest, thickest tufts, the ones that have begun to choke their own water supply. They graze it back. They are the gardeners, if you want to think of it that way, the things that keep the moss from overgrowing the engine's intake and shutting the whole system down. Whether they know this is irrelevant. They are hungry, and the moss is there, and the engine's water flows through the moss and tastes like food, and so they eat it.

They do not come to the surface. They do not need to. The water carries everything they require — oxygen, if they use oxygen, or whatever it is that the engine puts into the water that sustains them. They are the deepest thing in the sump. They are the closest living thing to the engine itself. They live in the outflow channels, in the spaces between the grates, in the dark water where the engine's warmth is strongest and the moss grows thickest and the crustaceans do not go.

You will not see one clearly. You will see the shadows, the interruptions in the reflected light, the way the moss parts and closes. You will hear, if you listen closely, a faint wet sound — not a splash, not a slap, but a *smooth* sound, the sound of something moving through water with total fluency, no resistance, no turbulence, as though the water is not a medium they move through but a medium they *are*.

They are not dangerous to you. They are not interested in you. You are too large, too warm, too dry, too unfamiliar. You register in their pressure-sense as a boulder registers — an obstacle, a non-event, something to move around. If you put your hand in the water, one might investigate. It would touch your skin with its segments — cold, smooth, faintly oily with the engine's film — and it would move on. It would not bite. It would not constrict. It would simply confirm that you are not food and leave.

But you would not put your hand in the water. You would not, once you heard them. Not because you are afraid. Because the sump has rules, and one of the rules is that you do not reach into places you do not understand.

## The Nest-Builder

This is the one you do not see.

The nest is in the highest, driest corner of the chamber — a rough dome the size of a fist, constructed from lichen-cushion fragments and the shed husks of dead crustaceans, packed together with a binder you cannot identify. Something sticky. Something that hardens. The entrance is a perfect circle, and the ground around it is worn smooth by traffic.

Whatever built it is small. Smaller than the crustaceans, probably. The husks it uses are intact, not crushed, suggesting it can handle them with precision. The lichen fragments are cut, not torn — the edges are clean, geometric, the work of something with tools or with mouthparts shaped like tools. The dome is structurally sound. It has been there for a long time. The stone around the entrance is stained with the same darkness that stains the moss beds, suggesting the builder has been coming and going for years, for decades, for longer.

You do not see it. You do not hear it. The nest is empty when you find it, or appears to be, but the wear on the entrance stone tells you it is not abandoned. Something uses this nest. Something returns to it. Something has been returning to it for a very long time, long enough to polish the stone.

What does it eat? The lichen, perhaps. The crustacean husks — the organic residue on them, the remnants of soft tissue. The bacterial film. The spores. It lives in the driest part of the chamber, the part of the chamber most removed from the water, and this suggests it is not aquatic, not dependent on the engine's direct output, but survives on the *secondary* products of the sump's ecology — the things that drift up on the circular breath, the things that dry out and become available on the highest shelves.

It is the sump's mystery. The engine is a mystery, but a large mystery, a mystery that does not require resolution because it is too big to approach. The nest-builder is a small mystery. A mystery you could hold in your hand. A fist-sized dome of dead things and patience, sitting in a corner, waiting for its occupant to come home.

If you stayed — if you sat in the sump for a day, for two days, for a week — you might see it. It would be a small thing. A pale thing. A thing with too many legs or not enough legs. A thing that moves the way the shadows in the pool move — with total fluency in its medium, which is not water but *dryness*, the narrow edge of the sump's world where the wet stops and the stone is merely damp and the lichen grows in thin, hard crusts and something small and competent has built a life out of the leavings.

You would not know what to call it. The sump does not have names for its inhabitants. The sump has roles, functions, positions in a system that runs on water and heat and mineral chemistry and the slow, patient engine that has been turning for longer than the stone has been solid. The nest-builder has a role. It is the top of the dry world. It is the thing that lives where nothing else can, that takes the last scraps of the system's output and makes a home of them.

It is the thing most like you. Small, warm, building something out of what it finds. You would feel kinship, if you saw it. You would feel it before you understood why.

## The Smallest Things

There is a layer below all of this, a layer you cannot see, a layer that is the reason the other layers exist.

The water is not clear. It looks clear — you can see the bottom of the pool, see the moss, see the shadows. But between the light and the stone, in the water itself, there is a population. A density. A *cloud* of things too small to see, too small to hear, too small to feel. They are the bacterial film. The biofilm. The microscopic community that lives on every wet surface in the sump — on the stone, on the lichen, on the moss, on the crustaceans' shells, on the shadows' segments, on the outflow grates, on the ceiling where the condensation forms.

They are what the crustaceans eat when they eat "something you cannot see." They are what the lichen metabolizes when it metabolizes "something from the stone." They are the invisible substrate of the sump's entire ecology, the layer that makes everything else possible by being the first thing to take the engine's raw output and turn it into life.

They have been here the longest. Longer than the moss. Longer than the lichen. Longer than the shadows. They were here when the engine started, or they arrived immediately after — the first things to find the warm, mineral water and recognize it as food. They have been metabolizing it ever since. They are the engine's oldest companions. If the engine is the heart, they are the blood — not the water, which is only the medium, but the actual living substance that carries the engine's output through the system and delivers it to everything else.

They do not know you are there. They will not know you were there. When you leave, they will have colonized the damp footprints you left on the stone, and they will metabolize the oils and dead skin cells you shed, and they will add you to the system — a brief, insignificant input, a momentary perturbation in a flow that has been running for millennia. You will be sump. You will be engine-output. You will be lichen-food and crustacean-husk and nest-material, in time, if you stayed long enough. The sump does not distinguish. The sump incorporates.

---

The sump is not empty. The sump is not abandoned. The sump is full of life — life that has found a way to live around an engine no one understands, in water no one drinks, in light no one sees by, in a place no one visits. It is a closed system, a self-sustaining pocket, a world within a world that does not need the larger world to continue.

The engine turns. The water cycles. The moss sways. The lichen grows. The crustaceans tick. The shadows pass. The nest waits. The bacteria drift. The stone weeps. The air sighs.

And you are standing in it, large and warm and dry and temporary, and everything here is going about its business with the total conviction of things that have been doing what they do for longer than you have been anything.
