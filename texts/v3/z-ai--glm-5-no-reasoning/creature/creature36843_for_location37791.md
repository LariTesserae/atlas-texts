# What Lives Here

The first ones you see are the children, because they are not still.

Adults here have achieved a quality of motion so integrated with the stillroot canopy that they read as part of it — pale forms drifting between pale branches, their crystalline-armored skin catching and scattering the amber light until they seem to glow from within, or perhaps simply to glow, the distinction between reflection and emission having collapsed a long time ago. But the children have not yet learned this. They move in quick darting spurts through the narrow paths between dwellings, six-limbed like everything else in the caldera — though in their case the second pair are not fully articulated, folding flat against the ribcage, vestigial-becoming-functional, used for balance in the tight spaces and for climbing the stillroot domes when they want to get above their parents' eye line, which is often.

They click at each other as they move. Not the rhythmic counting-clicks of the amberfinches, but a denser, messier chatter — percussive, communicative, clearly meaningful to them and opaque to anyone who did not learn it before the age of three. They are bilingual in a real sense: this click-speech among themselves and in play, and the slow signed speech of the adults when they are being addressed. Because the adults do not often speak aloud. The drone is too present, too full, and the caldera's acoustics turn sharp sounds into events. To click sharply is to announce yourself the way a raised voice in a library announces yourself. The children do it constantly because children everywhere test boundaries. The adults have stopped testing them.

---

An adult, then. One who is not moving.

She sits in the threshold of a dwelling — a gap in the woven stillroot dome, waist-high, wide enough for one body — and she is working a stillroot tendril with a stone blade. The tendril is freshly cut, leaking a slow transparent sap that is not water, that smells like pine resin and petroleum and something under both of those, something mineral and old. She is shaping the cut end into a graft union: flattening it, scoring it with the blade's edge, preparing it to receive another tendril that has been trained into position and is waiting, living and patient, on a wooden frame beside her. The work is precise but unhurried. She has done this ten thousand times. Her hands — four-fingered, broad-palmed, the crystalline fuzz thicker on the backs than on the palms, catching the amber light in a way that makes her knuckles look ridged with frost — move with the automatic confidence of a body that has solved this problem so thoroughly that the solving has become a kind of rest.

She is old. You know this not because her skin is lined — the caldera's atmosphere preserves skin in a way that makes age difficult to read by surface — but because her vestigial limbs have receded further than those of the children, nearly absorbed, a raised ridge of bone along the lower ribs that is more memory than structure. Evolution is happening here in real time, at a pace visible across generations. The second pair of limbs is going away. The caldera does not reward extra appendages. It rewards stillness, efficiency, the ability to fit between stillroot branches without catching on them. The body is adapting to the architecture, and the architecture is adapting to the body, and neither is leading.

She looks up. Not at you — there is no you, not yet — but at the canopy overhead, where an amberfinch flock has just passed through and the clicking is still reverberating. She watches them the way a farmer watches weather. The amberfinches strip the crystalline fuzz from the upper canopy, which encourages the stillroot to produce more, which thickens the growth, which affects the graft unions she can make next season. Everything here is connected to everything else through chains of consequence so long and so intricate that the inhabitants do not try to hold them in conscious attention. They hold them in practice. In the way the hands move, the way paths are worn, the way dwellings are oriented. The knowledge is in the doing.

She returns to the graft.

---

There are perhaps two hundred of these people. Calling them a people feels right and wrong simultaneously — right because they share a form, a language, a set of practices that constitute a culture by any reasonable definition; wrong because the word implies a boundary, a them-and-us, and here the boundary between the people and the stillroot and the grub-herds and the amberfinches is not a line but a gradient. The crystalline fuzz on their skin is the same substance as the fuzz on the stillroot tendrils, produced by the same symbiotic mycelia that have colonized the amber walls. When they breathe, the volatile hydrocarbons in the caldera's air are processed by lung structures that are, histologically, modified fungal tissue. They are wearing the ecosystem. They are inside it. They are made of it.

They do not have a name for themselves. This is not because they lack language — their signed speech is rich, capable of abstraction, capable of humor, capable of the long recursive narratives that constitute oral history. It is because the concept of a named, bounded self does not emerge naturally from a world in which everything is in continuous material contact with everything else. You are what you do in the stillroot. When you stop doing, you are stillroot. The name for a person is the name of their work: Graft-Keeper, Path-Walker, Fuzz-Harvester, Web-Tender, Child-Holder. When the work changes, the name changes. When the person dies, the name becomes a property of the path they wore in the amber floor, which is still there, which is still being walked.

---

The Path-Walkers are the closest thing this society has to specialists, and even they are generalists by any other standard. They are the ones who maintain the grub-procession routes — clearing debris, redirecting paths that have begun to wander, ensuring that the herds move through the stillroot groves in a rotation that distributes grazing pressure evenly. This is not agriculture. The grub-herds are not owned, not managed in the sense of breeding or culling. They are traffic. The Path-Walkers are traffic engineers, and the amber floor is their road network, and the roads have been in continuous use for longer than the concept of road exists in any memory.

A Path-Walker moves through the grove in a way that is distinct from the way anyone else moves. Slightly faster. More directional. The stillroot branches part for them not because the branches move but because the Path-Walker knows the architecture of each grove-section the way you know the layout of your own home — which gaps lead where, which canopies are too dense to pass, which anchor points are soft enough to push through and which have hardened past the point of flexibility. The Path-Walker carries a bone awl and a pouch of worked amber chips, and these are the only personal possessions you will see on any body in the caldera. Everything else is communal. Everything else is left where it was used.

The Path-Walker pauses at a junction where two grub-processions meet. The grubs — pale, segmented, twelve-legged, each the length of a forearm — flow around each other without collision, their processions intertwining and separating with the fluid precision of currents in a stream. The Path-Walker watches. Clicks softly, twice — not to the grubs, who do not communicate, but to herself, a marker of observation, a note that something here is worth noting. Then she moves on. The processions continue.

---

Inside a dwelling — you have to crouch to enter, and the stillroot frame brushes your shoulders, and the air is the same warm turpentine-clove thickness as outside, and the light is the same dim amber, and the sound is the same layered drone, slightly muted by the woven walls but not by much — an older man is lying on the smoothed amber floor with a child beside him. He is signing. The signs are large, slow, performed with both primary hands while the vestigial pair rests flat against his ribs. The child watches with the focused intensity of someone learning.

He is telling a story. You can tell it is a story because the signs repeat in patterns, because the child's body is oriented toward the teller with the particular stillness of an audience, because there is a rhythm to it that is not the rhythm of instruction or negotiation. It is the rhythm of a thing told because it has been told before, because it carries something that needs carrying.

The story, as near as you can read from the signs — which are not your language, which are not anyone's language except in the way that a gesture can be a language when it is all you have — the story is about the ceiling. About the amber overhead. About what was before the amber, which was something else, which was stone, which was a mountain that had a top on it and the top was open and there was something above the top that was not amber. The word for this something is a sign made by both hands opening upward, fingers spreading, and it is clearly important and clearly abstract and clearly very old. The child signs it back. The hands open upward. The fingers spread. Above them, the amber ceiling glows.

It is the closest thing they have to a word for sky.

---

The dead are not buried. There is no soil. The dead are given to the stillroot.

This is not ritual. It is waste management, and it is also grief, and the fact that these are the same thing here tells you something about the caldera that a long description could not. When a person stops — when the breathing stops and the hands stop and the vestigial limbs lie flat and the crystalline fuzz begins to lose its shimmer — the body is carried to a designated anchor point in the stillroot grove, a place where the tendrils are thick and the mycelial network is dense, and the body is placed against the root and the root grows over it. Quickly, by caldera standards. Over weeks. The stillroot metabolizes the body the way it metabolizes the amber substrate: slowly, completely, breaking it down into the components that feed new growth. The crystalline fuzz on the skin is already stillroot tissue. The transition is seamless.

This is not spoken of in the way that death is spoken of in places where death is an interruption. Here, death is a continuation. The person becomes a dwelling, or a graft union, or a path surface. The name they held becomes a property of the place they became. There is a dwelling on the eastern edge of the settlement whose walls are said to be a woman who was very good with amberfinches, and the flock that nests in those walls behaves as though it remembers her, which it may, because the mycelia in the walls are continuous with the mycelia in the birds' gut lining, and memory in this place is not a thing stored in a brain but a thing distributed across a substrate.

This is what the stories mean. The man signing to the child, the hands opening upward — he is not telling the child about a sky. He is telling the child about a time before the caldera was a caldera, before the amber was amber, when the world was open and the sound was not everywhere and the light came from somewhere other than the walls. These stories are not history. They are not myth. They are something else, something for which there is no category: a memory carried by a substrate, passed through channels that are chemical rather than neural, preserved in the slow metabolism of resin and mycelium. The people here are not telling their own history. They are telling the mountain's history, which is also their history, which is also the stillroot's history, and the boundaries between these are not real.

---

You would not call them happy. Happiness is a concept that requires a counterfactual — a state of not-happiness against which happiness is measured — and the caldera does not generate counterfactuals. The temperature is stable. The food is constant. The threat matrix is minimal: the weavers are territorial but not dangerous, the grub-herds are oblivious, the amberfinches are ornamental. There is no predator. There is no scarcity. There is no season. There is no change.

What they are is *continuous*. They are in a state of unbroken existence with their environment that has lasted so long that the state itself has become constitutional. A caldera-person removed from the caldera would not survive — the lung structures would fail, the skin symbionts would die, the inner ear would lose the drone and the loss of it would be as disorienting as the loss of gravity. They are not adapted to the caldera in the way that a desert animal is adapted to a desert, making do with what the environment provides. They are *constituted by* the caldera. They are an organ of it. The stillroot grows in them and they grow in the stillroot and the amber grows around both and the mycelia connect all three in a network that has no center and no edge.

Standing in the settlement at the hour that would be dusk if there were a sun — the amber light dims slightly on a long cycle, responding to some metabolic rhythm in the fungal networks that no one has mapped because mapping it would require a framework of inquiry that does not exist here — you see the people settle into their dwellings. The paths empty. The grub-herds continue their processions, indifferent to cycles. The amberfinches roost in the upper canopy, their clicking fading to occasional solitary notes. The drone continues. The rustling continues. The weavers' buzzing drops in pitch as their metabolisms slow.

The settlement breathes. Not metaphorically. The stillroot walls of the dwellings expand and contract by fractions of a millimeter as the metabolic cycle pulses, and the air inside the dwellings moves with this expansion, and the people inside the dwellings move with the air, and the sound moves with the people, and the amber moves with the sound, and everything is moving and nothing is going anywhere.

This is what lives in the caldera: a single organism that has forgotten it was ever separate parts, living in a room that is also part of the organism, making a sound that is the organism breathing, lit by its own persisting glow. It has been this way for longer than anything alive can remember. Everything alive remembers in the scale of millennia. The remembering and the living are the same act. The room and the organism and the remembering are the same thing.

The amberfinches click three times in the darkening light. Then silence, or what passes for silence here — the drone, the rustle, the buzz, the breath. Then three times more.
