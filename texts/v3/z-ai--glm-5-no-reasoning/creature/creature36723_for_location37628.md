# The People of the Drowned Spires

They are not born; they are germinated.

This is how they think of themselves, those few dozen families who have lived in Upper Vell longer than memory can credibly reach. A child arrives when the chamber decides it is time—not through any mystical process, but through the practical understanding that the people have developed over generations. They know which moss beds are ready for planting. They know which tuber nodules will produce the densest food and which should be set aside for seed. In the same way, they know when a dwelling has grown thick enough to shelter another body, when the bird populations can sustain another mouth, when the water flow is stable enough to promise years of calm. They do not plan families. They read the chamber, and they respond.

The children themselves emerge into light that has never known the sun, into air that is already half water. Their lungs open to it differently than a child born in some dry place above. They do not cry. There is no reason to; the sound would only be swallowed by the damp. Instead, they breathe—shallow at first, then deeper—and their small bodies begin the long process of learning what all bodies in Upper Vell must learn: how to hold warmth, how to move slowly, how to be patient.

---

They are not tall, the people of Upper Vell. Generations of limited nutrition and the constant, compressing damp have shaped them into something compact, dense, difficult to chill. Their bones are thick for their size. Their joints move with a smoothness that belies the cold; the cartilage has adapted, somehow, becoming more resilient, more resistant to the ache that all newcomers feel within hours of arriving. Their skin is pale—not the unhealthy pallor of illness, but the particular hue of something that has never needed to protect itself from direct sunlight. It has a grayish undertone, faintly green in certain lights, as if the moss itself has begun to claim them.

Their hair grows in ways that surprise visitors. On some, it forms thick mats that retain water like the moss beds, kept deliberately damp against their scalps. On others, it is sparse, almost absent, an adaptation that prevents the growth from trapping cold water against the skin. Children often have hair that falls in loose, dark curls, but as they age, it transforms—either thickening into something rope-like and absorbent or thinning to almost nothing. Both are considered beautiful. Both are functional.

Their hands tell the story of their lives. The fingers are long, the palms broad, and the skin there is toughened by decades of contact with stone and water and living plant matter. But it is the fingerprints that mark them most clearly: worn almost smooth in some places, especially the pads of the thumbs and forefingers, where decades of delicate work with moss and fungi have erased the ridges. They can identify each other by the patterns on their fingertips, or the lack thereof. A person who still has their whorls intact is either very young or very new.

Their eyes have adapted to the half-light. They cannot see in true darkness, but they can distinguish shades and shapes in illumination that would leave a surface-dweller blind. The pupils are larger than normal, the irises pale—gray, green, or a faint amber that catches what little light exists. They do not blink as often as they should. There is no need; the air itself keeps their eyes moist.

---

They speak, but not loudly.

The chamber's acoustics make sound behave strangely. A whisper at one end can be heard clearly at the other, while a shout may be swallowed entirely by the moss walls. The people have learned this geography of sound instinctively. They know which spots amplify, which ones absorb, which ones carry words to specific dwellings without anyone else hearing. Their ordinary speaking voice is soft, almost a murmur, and they use it rarely. Most communication happens through gesture, through expression, through the subtle language of posture that develops in any community where stillness is valued over action.

When they do speak aloud, it is often to the birds.

This is not something they discuss with visitors. It is too private, too easily misunderstood. But each person in Upper Vell has a relationship with the swallows that nest in the upper fissures. They know individual birds by their calls—or perhaps the birds know them. There is a dialogue that happens, a trading of sounds. The people have learned to mimic certain patterns, and the birds respond. They carry messages, after a fashion. They warn of changes. They announce arrivals. They mourn the dead.

The oldest among them—those who have passed sixty years, which is considered very old in Upper Vell—can summon specific birds to their dwellings simply by making the right sounds at the right time. They do not do this often. The birds are not pets. They are partners, of a sort, and the relationship must be maintained with respect.

The children learn the bird language early. They have no choice; it is simply part of the air they breathe, part of the constant soundscape of the chamber. By the time they are ten, they can distinguish a dozen different calls and know what each one signifies. By fifteen, they can produce at least three of them well enough to fool the birds themselves.

---

They do not name themselves.

Or rather, they do not use names in the way visitors expect. A child is not given a name at birth. Instead, they accumulate descriptors over the course of their life—attributes, achievements, relationships, locations. Someone might be called *the-one-who-tends-the-third-channel* or *she-of-the-thick-hair* or *he-who-was-first-to-hear-the-warning*. These shift over time. The same person might be known by five different identifiers across their lifespan, each one appropriate to a different context, a different relationship.

Visitors find this confusing and somewhat dehumanizing. The people of Upper Vell find the visitor's insistence on permanent, fixed names equally strange. Why would you call someone the same thing their whole life? They are not the same person. They have not done the same things. To name someone once and never change it is to deny that they have grown.

There are exceptions. Some descriptors become so attached to a person that they function as proper names, passed down even after death. *The-one-who-first-taught-the-moss-to-climb* has been dead for three generations, but everyone knows who that refers to. The person who currently tends the moss terraces is sometimes called by that name as an honorific, a recognition of continuity.

When someone dies, their descriptors are spoken aloud in the central clearing, one by one, while the community listens. It can take hours for someone who has lived a full life. The birds often go quiet during these recitations, as if they understand.

---

They do not love quickly.

This is what ends most visitor attempts to stay. The people of Upper Vell form attachments at the pace of their growing dwellings—slowly, deliberately, with decades of attention. A couple might live side by side for twenty years before they begin to share a structure. A friendship might take a decade to deepen into something either party would acknowledge aloud. This is not coldness. It is not reserve. It is simply the natural rhythm of a place where nothing happens fast, where patience is the only survival strategy worth having.

But when they do love, it is with a thoroughness that surface-dwellers rarely achieve. To know someone for sixty years is to know them in a way that no brief passion can replicate. The old couples in Upper Vell move like one organism, each anticipating the other's needs before they arise, each communicating through a glance or a shift in posture. They have watched each other age. They have watched each other's hair change texture, each other's skin grow thin. They have witnessed each other's griefs and triumphs and illnesses, and they have remained.

There is no marriage ceremony. There is only the gradual merging of lives—two dwellings growing together, two garden-platforms becoming one, two names beginning to be spoken in the same breath. When someone refers to *them-who-share-the-eastern-wall*, everyone knows exactly what that means.

The bonds between parents and children are somewhat different. Because children are raised collectively—not in a formal sense, but in the practical reality of a small community where everyone knows everyone—parentage is less significant than it might be elsewhere. A child calls all the adults by the same respectful term. A child learns from whoever is most skilled at the thing they need to learn. The woman who birthed them is important, but she is not uniquely important. She is simply the one who happened to bring them into the chamber.

---

They do not fear death.

This is what visitors find most unsettling. The people of Upper Vell are not suicidal, not resigned, not numb. They simply understand, with a clarity that only long isolation can produce, that death is a return rather than a departure. They have lived their whole lives in a place where water falls upward, where plants grow without sun, where stone and flesh and vegetation have merged into something indistinguishable. They do not see themselves as separate from the chamber. When their bodies stop working, they will become part of what they have always been part of.

The dead are placed in the moss beds that need the most enrichment. This is not spoken of as burial or interment. It is spoken of as *returning*. *She-has-gone-back-to-the-growing*. *He-is-lending-his-warmth-to-the-third-terrace*. The body is wrapped in woven fibers and laid in a shallow depression that has been prepared over the course of weeks, the moss there loosened and watered and readied. The living then gently cover the wrapped form with additional moss, and the slow process of integration begins.

Children are not shielded from this. They watch. They help. They learn that death is not frightening, not strange, not an interruption in the natural order. It is simply another transformation—the last one they will undergo, but continuous with all the others.

There is no belief in an afterlife, exactly. There is no doctrine, no ritual, no prayer. But there is a sense—rarely spoken of, almost never articulated—that some part of the person continues in the dwelling they helped grow, in the moss they helped tend, in the birds they learned to speak with. The oldest structures in Upper Vell are said to contain something of every person who ever lived in them. The walls remember. The roots hold traces. When the wind moves through the fissures above and the chamber sighs with its thousand tiny sounds, the people sometimes hear what they believe to be voices. Not messages. Not ghosts. Just... presence.

---

They do not travel.

This is not because they are forbidden from leaving. The fissures above are narrow but passable; with patience and skill, a person could climb to the surface and walk across the karst landscape to wherever they pleased. The water channels below are more dangerous but not impossible; a strong swimmer could follow them down into the deeper chambers and perhaps emerge somewhere else entirely.

But they do not go. They do not want to.

Visitors ask about this constantly. They do not understand how anyone could choose to stay in a cold, wet, dark cave when the world above holds sunlight and warmth and variety and possibility. The people of Upper Vell have learned to smile at this question, to give some polite non-answer, to let the subject drop.

The truth is harder to explain. The truth is that they would not survive.

A body raised in Upper Vell has adapted to its conditions in ways that go beyond the merely physical. The people here breathe differently, move differently, think differently. Their internal rhythms have synchronized with the slow pulse of the chamber itself—the rise and fall of the water, the growth and dormancy of the plants, the cycles of the birds above. They can feel these rhythms the way a surface-dweller can feel the sun on their skin. It is part of them.

To leave would be to tear themselves in half.

This is not metaphor. The few who have ever tried—attempts usually made in youth, in moments of restlessness or curiosity—have returned within days, sick and trembling, their bodies unable to process the dry air, the harsh light, the absence of the sounds they had never realized they depended on. One woman, a generation ago, made it three weeks on the surface before she began to lose her hair, her teeth, her ability to keep food down. She crawled back through the fissures, barely alive, and spent the next year recovering in her dwelling while the moss grew over her like a bandage.

They are not trapped. They are planted.

---

They are not unhappy.

This is the most important thing to understand, and the hardest for visitors to accept. The people of Upper Vell are not surviving. They are not enduring. They are not making the best of a difficult situation. They are living.

They take pleasure in small things: the taste of a well-grown tuber, the particular call of a favorite bird, the warmth of a dwelling that has been cultivated well. They tell stories, long rambling narratives that can take days to complete, passed down through generations, full of humor and tragedy and the slow evolution of their community. They sing, sometimes, when the acoustics are right, wordless melodies that rise and fall with the water. They laugh—surprisingly often, given their reputation for reserve—and their laughter echoes strangely in the chamber, bouncing off the damp walls, mixing with the bird calls and the splash.

They are curious about visitors, genuinely interested in the world above, full of questions that they ask softly, patiently, giving the answers time to emerge. They are not jealous of what they hear. They simply want to know. They file the information away, adding it to the collective knowledge that they have accumulated over centuries. They know more about the surface than most surface-dwellers know about themselves.

They are content in a way that visitors, who have seen sunlight and felt wind and known all the possibilities of the wider world, often have never experienced. This is what disturbs their guests most. Not the darkness or the damp or the strangeness, but the simple, undeniable fact of a people who have found what they need and have no desire for anything more.

---

They are old.

This is the final thing that visitors eventually realize, if they stay long enough to notice. The people of Upper Vell are not a new settlement, not a recent experiment, not a group that wandered in and decided to make do. They are a continuation. The chamber has held people for longer than any of them can say. They do not know how long. They do not have written records. But they have stories—stories that refer to events so far back that the surface-dwellers have no names for them, stories that speak of a time before the water rose, stories that describe the chamber as it once was.

They have changed, of course. They have adapted. The dwellings they grow now are different from the ones their ancestors tended. Their own bodies are different. But they are the same people, in some way that matters. They have held this space, and it has held them.

They do not know who carved the chamber. They have theories—some think it was natural, worn by water over unimaginable time; others believe it was shaped by hands, perhaps human, perhaps not. They do not know why the water rises. They have guesses, involving mineral compositions and pressure differentials and the peculiar physics of enclosed spaces, but nothing they could prove. They do not know if there are others like them, other chambers, other peoples living in the deep places of the world.

They do not need to know. They have their questions, and they have their answers, and the two categories do not overlap as much as visitors might expect.

---

The water rises. The plants grow. The stone endures.

And the people of Upper Vell endure with it—compact, pale, patient—tending their moss and speaking to their birds and waiting for whatever comes next, whenever it chooses to arrive.
