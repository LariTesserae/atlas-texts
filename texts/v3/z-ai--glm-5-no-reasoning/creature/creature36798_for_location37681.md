# The Maintenance-People

They are not tall. Generations of protein from channel-catfish and orchid-water and the pale cave-fern have made them compact, dense, the kind of bodies that the damp cannot quite penetrate further. Their skin has taken on a quality that the oldest among them call *slate-glow*—a faint luminescence, not enough to walk by, but enough that in the maintenance-darkness you can see the face of the person beside you as a soft grey smudge. They do not know why this happened. They do not think of it as strange.

They call themselves not by a single name. Each pump-station cluster has its own word for *us*, and the words are not translatable because they are not nouns—they are verbs. *We-who-tap-the-third-piston*. *We-who-hear-Station-Forty*. The language has evolved for the Works, not for the world; they have words for seven distinct types of drip, for the difference between a pump's healthy throb and its struggling throb, for the precise shade of mercury-blue that means the capacitor is failing. They have no word for *sun*. They have forty words for *mist*.

Their hands are extraordinary. Every maintenance-person develops, by adolescence, a touch-sensitivity that lets them identify alloy composition, stone porosity, the viscosity of water in a channel by the resistance it offers to a finger dipped and lifted. The brass hammers they carry—each one an inheritance, some of them centuries old, the handles worn to smooth organic curves by generations of palms—function as extensions of this sense. They tap walls and listen. They tap pipes and feel the vibration travel back through the brass into their wrist bones. They tap the ancient brick and know from the sound whether the heat-absorbing matrix within is still intact or whether that section has begun its slow, inevitable failure.

---

A child here learns the Works before it learns itself. The hum is the first thing—they hear it in the womb, the deep alto of the nearest pump station modulating the fetal heartbeat, and the children who are born during the maintenance-cycle have, their whole lives, a slightly different sense of rhythm than those born during full illumination. This is observed. It is not commented upon. It is simply another detail in the taxonomy of belonging.

Children do not play as outsiders would understand it. They follow. From the moment they can walk, they trail behind their parent—usually the mother, though the Works does not organize its families by any logic the outside world would recognize—through the daily rounds. They learn the paths not by instruction but by repetition, the body memorizing the route the way water memorizes its channel. *The path to Station Twelve is down the broad steps, past the orchid that blooms in the crack above the door, left at the junction where the water tastes of iron, through the vine-curtain that must be parted with the left hand because the right-hand vine has thorns.* The children learn to read the Works as a blind person reads a street—not as a layout but as a sequence of textures, temperatures, pressures, sounds.

They learn the birds early. A child who cannot distinguish the territory-song of a lamp-nester from the alarm-call of the same species is considered slow, not because it is a failing but because the birds are the Works' early warning system, and a maintenance-person who cannot hear what the birds are saying is one who will walk into danger. There are songs that mean *salamander surfacing nearby*. There are songs that mean *circuit-failure-three-stations-distant*. There are songs that the oldest maintenance-people, the ones whose hearing has begun to fail, say they can no longer hear but can feel in their jaw, a buzz that the bird's syrinx produces at a frequency below hearing but above bone-conduction.

---

Their clothing is made from the vine-fiber. The dark-leafed climbers are harvested in the maintenance-cycle, when the leaves have folded and the stems are accessible. The fiber is stripped, soaked in channel-water, beaten against the heat-brick until it becomes pliant. The cloth they make is not woven in any traditional sense—it is layered, the fibers pressed together while wet, the natural adhesive of the vine-bark bonding them into a dense fabric that sheds water without absorbing it. It is dark, almost black, and it moves with the body in a way that heavier cloth would not. A maintenance-person in full working dress looks, from a distance, like a shadow with hands.

They wear the brass hammers on their belts. The hammers are not tools, exactly—they are identity. A maintenance-person without a hammer is someone between stations, someone traveling, someone who has not yet been assigned or whose station has failed beyond their ability to repair. The hammers are passed parent to child, and each one carries the marks of its history: dents from particular pipes, the wear-pattern of a particular grip, the green patina of particular skin-chemistry. A maintenance-person can identify another's station by the shape of their hammer's head, by the wear on its face. They greet each other by touching hammer-handles, a brief contact that lets each feel the other's history.

---

They do not govern themselves in any way that would be familiar. The Works governs. The pumps need tending; this creates obligation. Station Seventeen's third piston must be tapped twice before startup, or it will seize, and the cascade-failure will affect Stations Eighteen and Nineteen within three days. This is not a law. It is a fact. The maintenance-person at Station Seventeen taps the piston because the alternative is failure, and failure in the Works is not abstract—it is the lights going dark, the water stagnating, the salamanders moving into the channels where people walk. Morality, here, is hydraulic. Ethics is the duty of maintaining flow.

Disputes are resolved by the stations themselves. When two maintenance-people disagree about a repair, they consult the pump. They listen to the hum together, standing in the same spot, and what they hear is generally enough to settle the matter. If it is not, they consult the water—divining by taste, by temperature, by the patterns of flow at a junction. This is not mysticism. It is the Works' own diagnostic system, the people who live in it serving as instruments of its self-assessment. When the Works is functioning well, disputes are rare. When something is failing, tempers flare, and the people who understand the system best are the ones who can feel the failure most acutely, and they are the ones who fight.

There are no leaders. There are elders—those who have tended the same station for forty years, who know the rhythms of a particular pump the way a musician knows an instrument. They are consulted. They are respected. They do not command. The concept of command is foreign to them; the Works does not command, it simply operates, and the people within it operate according to the same principle.

---

They eat the catfish-things from the channels—small, eyeless, pale-fleshed fish that have evolved to live in the artificial streams. They catch them with woven-fiber traps set at the junctions where the water slows. The fish are prepared simply: gutted, laid on heat-brick, eaten with the hands. The meat is soft and bland, with a faint sweetness that the maintenance-people identify as the taste of clean water. If the water goes bad—if a channel has been contaminated by a failing seal or a dead salamander—the fish taste of it, and the fish are not eaten, and the problem is traced and repaired.

They also eat the ferns that grow in the deeper ravines, and the orchid-buds before they open, and a kind of mushroom that grows in the perpetual damp on the north-facing walls of the pump stations. The mushroom is dark, almost black, with a dense texture and a flavor like the smell of wet stone. They dry it on the heat-brick and carry it in pouches for long journeys through the Works—a food that does not spoil, that provides energy for the long walks between stations.

They do not cook with fire. Fire is a foreign element here, in a world where everything is wet. The heat-bricks provide warmth for the body and for food, and the lamps provide light, and the concept of an open flame is exotic to them, something they have heard of but never experienced. The small lanterns they carry during the maintenance-cycle are not flames—they are fragments of the ancient glass that bends light, filled with captured glow from the nearest lamp, and they fade slowly through the maintenance period, so that a journey undertaken at the beginning of the dark-cycle is illuminated and a journey undertaken at the end is not.

---

They dream about water. This is not a guess; they talk about their dreams, because dreams are considered relevant information. A maintenance-person who dreams of a particular junction often goes to that junction and finds a problem beginning. A maintenance-person who dreams of the Lower Works—the deep levels, the unmapped places—is watched carefully afterward, because those who dream of the depths sometimes answer the intercom, sometimes descend. The dream is seen as a communication from the Works itself, the system speaking through the unconscious of the people who live within it.

Their funerals are simple. When a maintenance-person dies, their hammer is returned to their station and placed in a niche beside the pump. The body is taken to the deepest available channel—this varies by zone, by generation, by the current state of the Works—and committed to the water. The salamanders do not touch it; this is observed, and it is considered meaningful, though no one can say what it means. The body travels the channels and does not return. The person's name—their verb-name, their *we-who*—is spoken for the last time at the water's edge, and then it is not spoken again, because the person has rejoined the flow, and the flow does not name itself.

They believe, if they can be said to believe anything, in the Works. Not as a god—they have no gods, no spirits, no supernatural framework—but as a fact so large that it displaces the need for belief. The Works exists. It functions. It has functioned for longer than memory. It will function after they are gone. Their purpose is to maintain it, and in maintaining it, to be part of it, and in being part of it, to be sustained by it. This is not faith. It is weather. It is the condition of their existence, as fundamental as the mist, as inescapable as the hum.

---

The young ones are restless. They are always restless—the elders say this has been true for every generation, that the young always want to see more of the Works than their station-cluster, always want to know what lies beyond the next junction, the next ravine, the next darkening of the lamps. Most of them grow out of it. They find their station, their rhythm, their place in the flow. They learn the sounds of their particular stretch of channel and the particular frequency of their particular hum, and the restlessness becomes something they remember, like a childhood fever, a thing they had and recovered from.

Some do not. Some of them walk. They travel the Works from end to end, from the bright central pump-works where the vegetation is a forest and the water flows in channels ten feet wide, to the peripheral zones where the lamps are failing and the life is strange. They are not outcasts—the Works does not produce outcasts—but they are different, and the difference is marked by the hammers they carry, which are not inherited but made, brass being scarce but not absent, and a person who makes their own hammer is a person who has chosen to belong to the Works rather than to a station.

Some of these travelers go to the edges, where the Works fails and the plateau reasserts itself. They come back—most of them—with stories of the dark zones, the places where the moss grows over the lamps and the channels have dried and the stone is raw and unworked. They speak of silence there, an actual silence, the hum absent, the pump-throb absent, the water absent, and they say the silence is heavier than any sound the Works produces. They do not stay. They return to the hum, to the mist, to the paths between stations, and they do not speak of the silence again except to say: it is there.

Some of them go down. Into the Lower Works. Following the ancient intercom-voices, or the dreams, or simply the pull of the deep channels where the water flows with purpose that the upper maintenance-people cannot read. These are the ones who may not return. And when one of them does return—rarely, once in a generation—they come back changed. Not damaged, not broken, but altered. They hear frequencies that others cannot. They look at the water and see patterns that others cannot. They tap the walls and hear responses that others cannot hear, and they become, for the rest of their lives, people who stand at the edge of the known Works and listen to something below, something vast, something that the Works itself may be only the surface of.

---

And there are the things they do not discuss.

The shapes in the clouds. They see them. They see them every day, the slow movement above the lamp-layer, the dark forms passing through the mist. They do not look up. This is not avoidance; it is a kind of respect, or a kind of fear that has become so habitual it no longer feels like fear. The shapes are there. The shapes have always been there. The shapes do not descend. This is enough.

But the old ones—the ones who have tended the same station for fifty years, who have felt the same hum for fifty years—they say the shapes are different now. Slower. Lower. They do not say this to the young ones, because the young ones would look up, and looking up is not something you do in the Works. They say it to each other, in the maintenance-darkness, in the brief conversations that happen when hammers touch and hands brush and the water covers the sound of words. They say: the shapes are lower. They say: the hum is changing. They say: I dreamed of the depths, and the depths dreamed of me.

They continue to maintain. The pumps still throb. The water still flows. The lamps still mark their paths through the endless mist. And the maintenance-people tend their stations, and tap their pistons, and read their birds, and listen to their hum, and carry their hammers, and walk their paths, and do not look up, and do not go down, and live in the Works as the Works lives in them—an ancient, vast, incomprehensible collaboration between the built and the grown, the maintained and the evolved, the people and the place, all of it flowing together toward an equilibrium that none of them will live to see, and that the Works, in its patient, hydraulic, infinite way, is always approaching and never reaching, the way a river approaches the sea.
