Here, there are no kings and no prey. There are only the patient and the impatient, divided by how they answer the plateau's central problem: a surplus of life and a deficit of death.

**The Still-Shaped** live in the canopy of that false forest floor. They are not animals; they are geometries. If you could see them from above—they would never let you see them from above—they look like shadows of architecture that has not yet been built. Pyramids. Tetrahedrons. Frames of something that might later be flesh.

They are the plateau's eaters, and they are failing at their task.

A Still-Shaped moves by growing and ungrowing. It extends a face toward a burgundy leaf, adheres with a secretion that is warm to the touch, and digests the plant matter through its surface over the course of days. It does not consume the stalk—only the leaf, and only the oldest leaves, the ones already browning at the edges with that strange rust that shouldn't exist at this depth. It is a polite hunger. Careful. The plants grow faster than the Still-Shaped can clear them.

There are perhaps three hundred of them. They drift through the forest in formations that shift like slow crystals dissolving, and they communicate by the patterns of their arrangement. A line means *the warmth is rising here*. A circle means *this growth is new*. A scattered constellation means *I am dying and I know it*. They do not mourn. They simply rearrange.

Their bodies are translucent enough that you can watch the digestion happen—dark bilious green spreading through whatever passes for their architecture, fading to pale amber as they process. They have no organs. They have no center. They are nothing but appetite organized into shape, and they are visibly insufficient to the task asked of them. The plateau is winning. The plants are burying them. Every season the forest floor rises, and the Still-Shaped are pushed upward toward the light they cannot use.

They will not go extinct. They will simply become smaller, more efficient, more desperate. In another thousand years they may be no larger than coins, eating a single cell at a time, covering the leaves in a fine geometrical rash that passes for balance.

---

**The Veneer** swim in the spaces between the plant stalks, through that dense vertical water where the geothermal currents rise. They are long and flat and thin—anatomy reduced to ribbon, to route, to the space between two points made flesh. They are the color of the water around them, which means they are no color at all. Transparent muscle. Visible spine. Eyes that are just dark spots at the leading edge, barely differentiated from the shadows they chase through.

They eat the translucent shrimplike things. The shrimplike things eat the bioluminescent specks. The bioluminescent specks eat something nobody has named yet—perhaps the plants' waste, perhaps each other, perhaps some chemistry that rises from the vents. The Veneer are the apex of this small and tentative chain, and they are aware of their position, and it terrifies them.

Fear is the wrong word. It implies a mind that can hold the concept of future. But the Veneer carry something like it in their bodies. They swim in spirals, never straight lines—a path that suggests constant vigilance, constant readiness to change direction. Their bodies produce a faint electrical field that goes silent when they stop moving; to stop is to cease to exist, electromagnetically, and to cease to exist is to be found.

Nothing is hunting them. Not yet. But the plateau is young, and water carries signals, and eventually something larger will follow the chemical trail of all this warm impossible life down to the drowned topography. The Veneer already know this the way a wound knows it will scar. They reproduce in rushes—spawnings that empty them completely, leaving their ribbons torn and fading in the current while a hundred miniature Veneer scatter into the plant forest. Most are eaten by the specks, which are cannibalistic in their own small way. A few survive. It is enough.

---

**The Singing Stone** is what the plateau has instead of a god.

It lives in the deepest valley, where the ancient river carved a channel that now holds still water heated to the temperature of blood. It is not stone. It is also not not-stone. It is a colony—or a creature—or a process—that has colonized the bare rock and made it something else. From the valley's edge, it looks like the stone has grown organs: ridged columns that pulse slowly, sacs that inflate and deflate with the rhythm of the thermal vents, surfaces that glisten with mucus the color of rust-orange—a color stolen from the plants, or given to them, or shared in some covenant that predates both.

The Singing Stone is the source of the low rhythmic pulse that fills the drowned plateau. It breathes. The vents below push hot water and gas through channels in the rock, and the colony has shaped itself around those channels, built valves and chambers and resonant spaces that turn geothermal exhalation into sound. The pulse is its heartbeat, its voice, its way of making the water move in ways that benefit itself. The slow currents that rise from the valley carry its spores. The plants that carpet the plateau grew from its early offerings. The warmth that should have taken millennia to adapt to was engineered by something that needed the plateau to green, because the green is its extended body, its food, its breath.

It is not intelligent. Intelligence requires boundaries between self and world, and the Singing Stone has none. It is the plateau. It rose with the shelf buckling because it weakened the stone from below, because it needed to be close to the light, because its previous home was deeper and colder and running out of time. The plateau is its third attempt. The first two are lost somewhere in the ocean's memory, drowned too deep, failed experiments in reaching toward the sun.

It does not know the Still-Shaped exist, or the Veneer, or the specks and shrimps and unnamed chemistries. It does not know it has made a garden. It is simply growing, the way a fire grows, the way a crack grows, the way anything grows when the only law it follows is *more*. The colors of the plants—the impossible violets and burgundies and creams—are its colors, its chemistry, its way of marking territory in a medium where territory dissolves. From above, the bruise on the ocean's skin is its signature.

---

And there is **the thing that almost is**.

In the warmest vents, where the water carries the richest chemistry, something is learning to hold a shape longer than a moment. It is not yet alive the way the Still-Shaped are alive; it is not yet organized the way the Singing Stone is organized. It is the moment before a decision—the held breath before speech. It persists. It tries configurations and abandons them. It wants something—surface, air, light unfiltered—and it does not yet have the architecture to want, but the wanting is there, cellular and directionless and patient as geology.

The plateau does not know it is growing this. The Singing Stone does not know it is feeding this. In another few thousand years, if the water stays warm and the plants keep growing and the spores keep falling, this almost-thing will become a real thing. It will have wants it can articulate. It will have a shape it can keep. It will have hands, maybe, or something that serves the function of hands, and it will reach up through thirty meters of warm water toward the surface it has never seen but somehow knows is there.

And then the plateau will not be pristine anymore. Then it will be home.

But for now, it is only a thickness in the water. A warmth that lingers. A potential too young to have a name, learning to want in a place where wanting is the only law that matters.
