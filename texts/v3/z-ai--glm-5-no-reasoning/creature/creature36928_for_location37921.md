# What Lives in the Kálint Expanse

## The Inhabitants

I have to begin with what I cannot see. This is a place that teaches you that visibility is a kind of arrogance—the assumption that what matters will present itself to your particular array of senses. The Kálint Expanse was full before I arrived, and it will be full after I leave, and my seeing or not seeing has nothing to do with its fullness.

But I saw some things. I will tell you about those.

---

## The Grazers (I)

They are not individuals. This is the first thing to understand. When I watched them from the ridge, I saw hundreds—perhaps thousands—moving through the stalk-forest in their slow, grinding processions. Each one seemed separate, distinct, a body with boundaries. But when I descended, when I was close enough to hear the particular sound of their grinding plates and see the texture of their bark-skin, I understood that the boundaries were not where I thought they were.

They communicate through the root network. Not in the way that animals communicate—not signals, not messages—but in the way that a single organism communicates between its parts. When one grazer encounters a dense patch of growth, the others converge. Not because it called them. Because *it knew where the food was*, and they are all, in some sense, the same knowing.

Their skin is not colonized by the ground-cover moss. That was my first assumption, and it was wrong. The moss *grows from them*. It emerges from pores in their hide, rooted in a dermal layer that is more rhizome than flesh. They carry the landscape on their backs because they *are* the landscape—mobile extensions of the root-mat, sent out to harvest light and return it to the system.

I watched one grazer die. It was not dramatic—no predator, no violence. It simply slowed, stopped, settled. Within hours, the ground-cover had begun to absorb it. Within days, it had become a ridge of dark soil, slightly raised, already sprouting new stalks. The other grazers passed over it without acknowledgment. There was nothing to acknowledge. It had not left the system. It had merely stopped moving.

---

## The Flat-things

I am calling them parasites, but that word assumes a relationship that may not apply here.

They live in the canopy, among the ribbon-leaves. Flat, almost two-dimensional, moving by a kind of flowing that I cannot explain mechanically—they have no muscles that I can see, no apparent means of propulsion. They simply *go*, their bodies pouring from one surface to the next like a liquid that has learned to want.

They attach to the stalks and drain them. This much is clear. The stalks they have visited are visibly diminished—pale, slightly concave, the ribbon-leaves above them thinner and slower to rotate. But they do not kill. They take only enough to sustain themselves, and they move on. And the stalks recover. I watched a deflated stalk over the course of several days; by the end, it was indistinguishable from its neighbors. The flat-things and the stalks have an arrangement. I do not know what the stalks receive from the arrangement, but they receive something. The system does not tolerate pure extraction. Everything here gives and takes in measure.

The flat-things react to the light-rivers with something that looks like ecstasy, if an organism that simple can be said to experience ecstasy. When the luminous haze touches the canopy, the flat-things spread themselves to their full extent—suddenly, all at once, as if the light were a command—and become mirrors. They flash. The whole canopy flashes. It is blinding. And then the light-river passes, and they fold back into themselves, and return to their parasitic drifting.

I touched one, once. It was resting on a stalk I had cut through to examine. The sensation was strange—not slimy, not dry, not warm, not cold. *Informative*. For a moment, I understood something about the stalk—its internal structure, the flow of material through its bundled-wire trunk—and then the flat-thing flowed away, and I was left with the memory of information I could not interpret.

---

## The Flying Membranes

They are called membranes, but that is not quite right. Membranes imply a boundary between two things. These are not boundaries. They are filters.

The air is full of them. Millions—perhaps billions—drifting in the dense atmosphere, riding the slow diffusion-currents of the mist. They are translucent, barely visible, simple structures: a membrane, yes, but a membrane with structure, with folds and channels and small organelles that pulse with absorbed light. They are not animals. They are not plants. They are something else—perhaps a separate kingdom, perhaps a convergence that evolved independently because this place *needed* something to fill this niche, and nature, or whatever shapes nature here, obliged.

I breathed them in. I could not help it. They are everywhere, and the air is thick with them, and breathing means drawing them into your lungs. I expected to choke, to cough, to feel them as intruders. Instead, I felt—a nourishment. A slight tingle at the back of my throat. A sense of something being added rather than taken.

They filter the light. This is their function. The sky-white brightness is too pure, too direct, too intense for most of the life here to process directly. The membranes intercept it, absorb some wavelengths, transform others, and release the modified light downward through their slowly settling bodies. The glow that illuminates the basin floor is not the same as the searing whiteness above. It has passed through millions of translucent bodies, each one taking what it needs and passing the rest onward.

I held my hand under a dense cluster of membranes, in a place where they had accumulated thickly enough to cast something like a shadow. The light on my skin was warm, golden, gentle. Above, beyond them, the magnesium-white raged. They were protecting me. Not intentionally—they do not have intentions—but functionally. They made this place habitable for things like me.

---

## The Builders

I am still not certain they are animals.

They are columnar, massive, slow beyond the comprehension of motion. They do not move in the way that other living things move. They *grow* into new positions, extending mass over days or weeks, shifting by millimeters. If you watch one for an hour, you will see nothing. If you watch one for a week, you will realize that it has turned slightly, that its angle has changed, that its orientation toward the nearest structure has shifted by a few degrees.

They secrete the iron-dark material of the structures. This is not construction in any sense I understand. They do not shape, do not plan, do not assemble. They simply *exude*, and the material forms in the angles and spaces around them, hardening into tubes and chambers that look mechanical because the builders themselves are geometric—their bodies following the same crystalline jointing as the bedrock, as if they were made of the same stuff, as if they *remembered* being rock.

The structures are warm. I pressed my palm against the dark surface of one—a tall tower-like chamber near a stream confluence—and felt a low, continuous vibration. Something inside was processing. The mineral water entered through channels at the base, moved through a series of internal compartments I could not see, and emerged transformed. The output was denser, clearer, shimmering slightly with released luminescence. The plants near the outlet channels were taller, darker, more vibrant. They leaned toward the output as if toward a sun.

The builders do not communicate. They do not gather. They do not reproduce in any way I could observe. They simply *are*—embedded in their structures, processing, exuding, slowly turning toward whatever they turn toward. They may be individuals, or they may be organs of a larger system. The structures they create may be their bodies, extended. I do not know.

I sat near one for an entire cycle of the light—hours, perhaps longer—and listened to the thrum. The vibration resonated in my chest. By the end, I felt aligned with it, my heartbeat slightly out of sync, trying to match a rhythm I could not quite find. When I stood to leave, I felt unsteady, as if I had been unmoored from myself.

---

## The Pond-dwellers

I did not see them clearly. No one who values their continued functioning would plunge into one of those iridescent pools. But I saw the shapes beneath the surface.

They are large. Larger than me, certainly—dark, slow forms that move along the bottoms of the ponds with a deliberate, glacial pace. They do not surface. They do not interact with the film-layer that coats the water. They simply travel, back and forth, in patterns I could not discern.

I watched one pond for most of a day. The shapes inside were not random. They followed a circuit—approaching the inlet where the mineral stream entered, pausing there for a long time, then moving to the outlet and pausing again, then retreating to the deepest part of the pool and resting. They were not feeding. They were not breeding. They were *processing*—doing something to the water, or taking something from it, or both.

The film on the water's surface is alive. This much is clear. It is a colonial organism, or a symbiosis of organisms, that maintains the iridescent layer and regulates gas exchange between the pond and the air. But the pond-dwellers are separate. They do not touch the film. They live in a different regime entirely—lightless, pressurized, cold.

At night—or what would have been night, if the light ever changed—the film glowed. Softly, faintly, a bioluminescence that pulsed in time with the slow movements below. The pond-dwellers and the film-layer are connected. They communicate, somehow, through the column of water that separates them. What they say to each other, I cannot imagine.

---

## The Root-things

I put my ear to the ground. This was a mistake.

The sound is not a sound. It is a *presence*—a low, rhythmic pressure that you feel in your jaw before you hear it with your ears. Thud. Thud. Thud. Like a heartbeat, but slower, deeper, coming from somewhere beneath the soil and the root-mat and the rock itself.

I do not know what makes it. The grazers, perhaps, extending their system underground. The builders, continuing their processes in chambers below. Or something else entirely—something that lives in the deep fractures of the calcium-rich schist, something that predates the basin and will postdate it, something that the basin grew *around* without ever incorporating.

I dug. I should not have dug, but I dug. The soil is dense and dark, rich beyond anything I have encountered, and the root-network is thick—centimeters of interwoven rhizomes before you reach anything like mineral earth. Below that is the rock, pale and sharp-jointed, and the roots have penetrated it, following the cracks, growing into the fractures, holding the land together.

I did not find what made the sound. But I found, embedded in the root-network, structures that were not roots—nodules, hard and mineral-dense, that pulsed with a faint light when I touched them. They were warm. They were processing something. I do not know what.

I covered the hole and did not dig again.

---

## The Basin Itself

By the end, I understood that the basin is the inhabitant.

Not metaphorically. Not poetically. The Kálint Expanse is a single organism, or something so close to a single organism that the distinction no longer matters. The stalks and the ground-cover and the grazers and the flat-things and the builders and the membranes and the pond-dwellers and the root-things are all organs, tissues, cells—components of a system that maintains itself through total integration.

The basin breathes. The mist is its breath, the slow expansion and contraction of its atmosphere. The basin digests. The structures are its gut, processing the mineral water into usable nutrients. The basin thinks. I cannot prove this, but I felt it—the sense, never quite articulated, that the basin was aware of me, not as a person but as a feature, a data point, something to be incorporated into its processing.

The sound it makes—the single, sustained note that underlies all the other sounds—is its voice. Not communication. Self-expression. The basin sings because it is alive, and living things make noise.

I left because I began to understand that I could stay. Not survive—any organism can die here, become soil, feed the next cycle—but *join*. I could become part of the basin. I could let the ground-cover colonize my skin, let the root-network find my feet, let the membranes settle in my lungs. I could be absorbed. And there was a part of me, growing larger the longer I stayed, that wanted exactly that.

---

## Final Note

The basin does not need a name. Names are for things we can hold apart from ourselves. This thing holds us. It held me, briefly, and then it released me, and I do not know if that was mercy or oversight.

I have not described everything. I could not. The density of life here exceeds any single observer's capacity to observe. I walked for days and never saw the same stretch of ground twice, and every stretch was full.

If you go—do not dig. Do not touch the flat-things. Do not enter the ponds. And when the note becomes indistinguishable from your own heartbeat, leave immediately, before the basin learns that you are something it can use.

The green sound pours over the ridge. I still hear it, sometimes, when I close my eyes.

I do not think it has forgotten me.
