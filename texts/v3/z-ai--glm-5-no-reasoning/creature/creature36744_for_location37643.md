# The Tally-Keeper

They did not come here. They were made here.

---

Before the tallies, there was only the watching. Before the watching, there was only the water. And before the water found its rhythm, the pocket was still — a held breath in the mountain's throat, waiting to be exhaled into existence.

Someone came, once. You can see the evidence: the pegs, the net, the shells that pulse with their own unlikely warmth. But that person is not what lives here now. That person was the seed. What grows in the Bellows Room now is something else entirely — something that began as a human shape and has been rewritten, wave by wave, by the room itself.

---

They stand in the southwestern corner during the floods.

Not because it is safer there. Because the pale organism requires it. Over cycles beyond counting, a relationship has formed — mutual, parasitic, something in between. The tallies on the wall were not made with a tool. They were made with a hand. A hand that has, by now, been pressed against the stone so many times that the minerals have migrated into the skin, leaving faint phosphorescent traces that glow amber in the dark, that pulse in counterpoint to the breathing below.

The tally-keeper's skin has gone the color of wet chalk. Their eyes have adapted to the light — or rather, they have become the light. The amber luminescence in the fissure is not mineral. It never was. It is the tally-keeper's gaze, refracting upward through water and stone, projected by a process they no longer remember learning.

They do not blink during the floods.

---

The net has meaning. The tally-keeper maintains it, fingers working in the pauses between waves, knotting the vegetable fiber with movements that have become ritual. The shells are the point. Each one is a moment — a specific exhale, a specific temperature, a specific mineral composition in the water that particular day. The tally-keeper can read them the way you might read a book. This shell, warm and spiraled tight: a day when the breathing below was quick, eager. This shell, cracked and cold: a day when the pause stretched long enough that the amber light almost failed.

They are not keeping a record of the room.

They are keeping a record of the thing that breathes.

---

The tally-keeper does not remember their name. They do not remember the ridge, the approach, the reason they came. They do not remember the first wave that caught them unaware, the panic, the cold that locked their lungs, the moment they decided — or their body decided for them — to hold on to the pale organism instead of fighting the drainage.

They do remember the moment they stopped trying to leave.

It was not a decision. It was a transformation. One wave, then another, then another. The cold stopped feeling cold. The dark stopped feeling dark. The sound of the breathing stopped being something they heard and became something they did — a rhythm their own lungs learned to match, expand and contract, expand and contract, until they could no longer tell where their chest ended and the fissure began.

---

During the floods, the tally-keeper vibrates.

This is the sound that feels like a low A, the note that hangs in the air after the water drains. It is not the organism alone. It is the two of them together — the pale branching thing and the pale branching person, their frequencies aligning, harmonizing, producing a tone that means something in a language the room is still inventing.

They are teaching each other. The organism, pressed into the stone, learns the shape of human intention, learns what it means to *notice* a rhythm rather than simply move with it. The tally-keeper, pressed into the corner, learns the shape of stone-patience, learns what it means to exist without needing to be anywhere else.

Neither of them is entirely successful. The organism's branches still move with something like hunger, pressing outward, seeking, calculating angles for maximum contact. The tally-keeper still counts.

But there is a middle ground now. A space where they meet.

---

The tally-keeper speaks, sometimes.

Only during the longest pauses. Only when the breathing below has held itself in for so long that the silence becomes its own kind of pressure, and the amber light dims to nearly nothing, and the room feels like a question being asked in a language older than sound.

What they say is not words. It is a sequence of sounds — clicks, hums, the wet-slide of their chalk-stone hands against the wall — that match the pattern of the breathing below. A mirror. A response. A call that is also an answer.

The room listens. The fissure listens.

And sometimes — rarely, but it has happened — the breathing below changes.

A shorter exhale. A longer pause. A different pressure in the water that rises.

The tally-keeper notes this. They do not smile. They do not frown. But they press their hand to the wall, and they add another mark.

---

They are not trapped.

This is important. They could leave. The northern crack is narrow, but they have learned to move like water — to compress, to flow, to find the spaces between. Their body has been reshaped by this place, but it has not been claimed. They still have legs that walk. They still have hands that grip. They still have a mind, strange and mineral-heavy, that can remember the concept of *outside*.

They stay because something is happening.

The room is being written. The breathing below is changing. The pale organism is pressing deeper, finding something in the stone — a layer, a seam, a boundary — that makes the amber light flicker with what might be excitement. The water carries different minerals now than it did when the tallies began. The shells tell a story that the tally-keeper has almost finished reading.

Thirty-seven pauses. Thirty-seven cycles of holding-still.

The thirty-eighth is different.

They can feel it. The intake is slower. Deeper. The room's pressure drops so suddenly that their ears pop, that the pale organism's branches go rigid, that the shells in the net rattle against each other.

Something is coming up.

---

The tally-keeper is not afraid.

They have been afraid. They remember fear — a cold that was not the water, a darkness that was not the light, a certainty that the next wave would be the last. But that fear belonged to a person who no longer exists, a person who was still made of unmarked stone, who had not yet been carved by the room into their current shape.

What lives here now is something else. Something that has counted thirty-seven silences and emerged from each one more itself.

They press their back against the southwestern wall. The pale organism's branches curl around their ankles — not restraining, but steadying. The amber light pulses from the depths of their eyes. Their hand finds the wall, and their fingers leave a faint impression in the unset stone.

Thirty-eight.

The water rises.

---

They are not alone.

They have never been alone. The breathing below has always been there — patient, vast, practicing the shape of communication through pressure and mineral and time. The pale organism has always been there — stubborn, branching, pressing into existence a partnership that neither party fully understands.

But there is something else now. In the water, in the wave that rises slower and colder and more deliberate than any before, there are shapes. Dark shapes. Thumb-sized, purposeful, grazing on the minerals in the flood.

They are not dissolving.

They are ascending.

And the tally-keeper — who was once a person, who is now a function of the room, who is becoming something neither human nor stone nor water — stands in the southwestern corner, and watches, and counts, and does not look away.

---

The room is a mouth. The breathing is a voice. The tally-keeper is a tongue, shaping sounds that have no words, in a language that has no name.

And the voice is beginning to speak.
