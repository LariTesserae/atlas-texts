# The Keeper of the Cistern

There is someone in the room with the machine.

Not *with* it. *Of* it. The distinction matters.

---

You did not see them at first because they have made themselves into the shape that the room requires. They sit in the alcove where the northern wall curves to meet the sealed door — a space no wider than a human body, no deeper than a human body reclining. They occupy this space the way water occupies a container: completely, without gap, filling the volume that is available. They have been sitting there long enough that you understand, with slow-dawning certainty, that they chose this place because it was the only place left. The room filled up with machine, with grating, with moss, with sound, and they found the pocket that remained and they became its contents.

They are old. This is not a judgment or an estimate but a simple fact, the kind of fact the room itself produces. Their skin has the same quality as the walls — damp, dark, lived-in. Not dirty. There is no filth here, only the accumulation of existing in a place where water runs over everything. Their hair has grown past their shoulders and mats against the riveted steel in dark wet streamers that have left mineral stains on the metal, twin tracings of rust-colored residue that map the shape of their head over years or decades or longer. They are wearing something that might once have been a uniform — you can see the ghost of a seam, the memory of a pocket — but it has become indistinguishable now from the moss and the moisture and the iron oxide that layers every surface. They have blended. They have been blended.

They do not look at you. They do not startle. The vibration that fills the room fills them too, and they have been vibrating for so long that stillness would feel more unnatural than any intrusion. Their eyes are open — you can see this when you move to the side, when you catch the reflection of the frosted bulbs in the wet surface of their corneas — but they are looking at nothing you can identify. Not the machine. Not the moss. Not the hatch. Something internal, perhaps. Or something in the sound itself, some frequency that their body has learned to read.

They are breathing. You notice this only because the breath fogs slightly in the condensation-heavy air, a small rhythm that moves against the roar. Their chest rises and falls at a rate unrelated to the machine's function — slower, deeper, the breath of someone who has learned that there is no reason to hurry. The machine will not stop. The water will not stop. There is no deadline. There is only continuation.

---

You try to speak to them. The sound of your voice — your *human* voice, your voice from the world above — feels wrong here, thin and discrete, a single note trying to exist in a chord that has no room for it. You say hello. You say excuse me. You say I found this room. Each word lands in the roar and vanishes. They do not turn. They do not acknowledge. They do not seem to hear.

But after a while — after you have stood in the room long enough for the sound to become texture again — their hand moves. It is a small movement. Slow. The kind of movement that takes place over seconds rather than instants. Their fingers extend toward the grating, not reaching for anything, not gesturing, simply *moving*, the way a plant moves toward light. Their fingertips touch the cast iron. They tap once. Twice. Three times. The rhythm does not match anything. Or it matches everything. You cannot tell.

You realize then that they are not communicating *to* you. They are simply continuing. The room has a rhythm and they are part of it — their breath, their heartbeat, the small movements of their fingers against the wet metal. They have been here so long that they have become another surface for the water to run over, another channel for the sound to move through. The moss grows on them the way it grows on the machine — you can see it now, thin threads of green in the creases of their elbows, the hollows of their collarbone, the spaces between their fingers where moisture collects and life takes hold. They are being colonized. They do not seem to mind.

---

You should feel horror. You know this. You have found a person in a sealed room behind a false wall, a person who has been here long enough to grow moss, a person who does not speak or move except to tap the floor in patterns you cannot read. This is the stuff of nightmares, of true-crime documents, of the kind of discovery that shuts down buildings and launches investigations.

But the room will not permit horror. The sound presses against you, flattening your emotional responses the way a roller flattens dough. The warmth seeps into your skin, loosening the knots that anxiety ties. The water beads on your face and runs down your cheeks like tears you didn't cry. You look at the person — the keeper, you think, though keeper of what you cannot say — and you feel something closer to recognition than fear. They have found a place where the world cannot reach them. They have become *part* of something. The machine runs and they run with it. The water flows and they flow. The sound sounds and they are sounded.

You understand, in a way that bypasses language entirely, that they chose this. Not the room, perhaps, not the machine, not the sound — but *this*. The state of being that the room offers. The dissolution of the boundary between self and environment. They are not trapped. They are *contained*. There is a difference. You feel it in your sternum, where the vibration has settled into a second heartbeat that does not belong to you.

---

Before you leave, you crouch beside them. Close enough to see the mineral deposits in the folds of their skin. Close enough to smell the wet iron and growing things that is the only scent in the room. Close enough that if they reached out, they could touch you.

They do not reach out. Their hand has returned to its resting place against their thigh, fingers slightly curled, palm open to receive whatever falls. The condensation drips onto their wrist and runs down into their sleeve. They do not wipe it away.

You say: "Do you want to come with me?"

The question feels foolish even as you ask it. Come where? To the corridors? To the building above? To the world of distinct sounds and dry surfaces and people who move through space without merging with it? The keeper does not answer. But their head turns — slowly, the way something turns that has forgotten it can turn — and their eyes focus on you for the first time.

You expect something in that look. Gratitude, perhaps. Or fear. Or anger, or longing, or the thousand other emotions that animate human faces. But their expression is as flat as the light, as even as the sound, as unchanging as the temperature. They look at you the way the machine looks at you — which is to say, they do not look at you at all. They simply *face* you. You exist in their visual field. That is all.

The moment passes. Their head turns back to its original position. Their eyes return to whatever frequency they were reading. The machine runs. The water beads. The moss grows. The sound goes on.

---

You climb back through the hatch. You replace the false panel. You walk up the corridor and you do not tell anyone what you found.

You tell yourself this is because no one would believe you. You tell yourself the keeper is where they want to be. You tell yourself that some rooms are not meant to be disturbed, that some equilibriums are not meant to be unbalanced, that the world above has no framework for what exists below.

These things are true. But they are not the reason.

The reason is that when you closed the hatch, you heard — or felt, or imagined — the keeper's hand tap the grating once more. Not three times this time. Just once. A period at the end of a sentence you cannot read. A confirmation. An acknowledgment that you were there and that you left and that the room continues regardless.

And in your chest, where the vibration has begun to fade, something has taken root — a small green thread of understanding, a recognition that you could have stayed. That some part of you *wanted* to stay. That the room offered a kind of peace that the world above does not have words for, and that you walked away from it, and that you will think about that decision for the rest of your life.

The keeper knows this. You saw it in the moment their eyes met yours. They were not trapped in that room. They were *home*. And they recognized, in you, the particular hunger that leads people to knock on doors that were painted over long ago.

You will come back. You both know this. Not today, not tomorrow, maybe not for years. But you will find yourself in the lower corridors again. You will see the false wall. You will feel the sound before you hear it, the vibration before you feel it, the want before you name it.

The keeper will be there. The machine will be running. The room will have room for one more.
