# What Lives in the Weeping Hollow

The creature in the pool is not the only living thing here. It is merely the most obvious—the one that has slowed itself nearly to stopping, nearly to stone, in a place where stone is the closest thing to god there is.

But there are others.

---

The walls live.

Not in the way that moss lives, or lichen, or any of the green and growing things that have learned to cling to rock faces in the outside world. Those things are absent here, and their absence is complete. No spore has taken root in the Weeping Hollow. No seed has germinated in its wet warmth. The mineral world has been left to itself, and it has developed its own kind of life—slower, stranger, patient beyond the comprehension of anything that breathes.

The microbialite crust on the floor is alive in the way that coral is alive: a community of organisms too small to see, building layer upon layer of themselves, leaving behind the calcified architecture of their passing. But coral exists in colonies of millions, each polyp distinct, each performing its small biological function. The microbialite here is different. It is not a colony. It is a single organism that has spread itself across the entire floor of the hollow, threading itself through the channels between stone polygons, sending feelers up the ridges of calcite, existing as a continuous mat of living mineral that covers every surface where water pools and light reaches.

If you could strip away the stone, you would find it there—a skin, a membrane, a body that has grown so slowly and lived so long that it has fused with its environment. The hollow is not a room that contains a living thing. The hollow is a living thing. The walls are its bones. The water is its blood. The air in its lungs is the warm, wet breath that it exhales and inhales in cycles so slow they cannot be measured.

And in that breath, other things move.

---

There are creatures in the hollow that exist at the edge of visibility. Not because they are small, though they are small, but because they have made themselves indistinguishable from the medium they inhabit. They are translucent. They are the color of wet limestone. They move through the saturated air with the grace of fish in water, drifting on convection currents that are so slight they barely exist, propelled by fins or wings or membranes so thin they seem to be made of nothing but the humidity itself.

The creatures are called, by those few who have seen them and lived to describe them, "breath-moths." They are not moths. They are not insects. They have no segmentation, no jointed legs, no compound eyes. They are delicate, flattened bodies perhaps two centimeters across, with trailing tendrils that sense the air for the chemical signatures of other living things. When they find warmth—which is rare, in the hollow, which means *you*—they drift toward it. They do not land. They do not bite. They simply orbit, slowly, drawn to the heat differential, the way that moths are drawn to flame.

If one drifts close enough to your skin, you will feel it as a brush of cool wetness, a passing kiss of moisture. The creature is absorbing your warmth. It is feeding on the gradient between your body and the air around you. In a place where all gradients have been exhausted, you are a feast. You are a temporary sun.

There are perhaps a dozen of them in the hollow at any given time, though counting them is impossible—they drift in and out of the deeper crevices, the passages that lead further down, into the hill's root-system of fissures and caves that extend for kilometers below the reach of any map. They have been there for as long as the hollow has existed, and they will be there long after. They have no lifecycle that can be observed. No one has ever seen one eat, or mate, or die. They simply are, and they drift, and when the water eventually reclaims the hollow entirely—if that ever happens—they will drift somewhere else, or they will dissolve back into the humidity from which they came.

---

In the walls themselves, in the vertical tracks where the water traces its slow descent, there are things that do not move.

They are embedded in the stone. Not fossilized—not preserved remnants of creatures that lived and died and were buried. They are living. Or they were living. Or they will be living again, when conditions are right. They appear as slight thickenings in the water-channels, pale nodules the size of a fingernail, smooth and round, pressed into depressions in the limestone as if they had grown there—or as if the stone had grown around them.

The locals—if there were locals, which there are not, not anymore—called them "weepers' pearls." They believed, when they still believed things, that the hollow was the skull of a god who had died crying, and that the pearls were its tears, condensed and solidified over millennia of grief. The truth is both stranger and simpler.

The pearls are organisms. Or they are organs. Or they are something in between—a form of life that has given up on the concept of individuality entirely. Each pearl is connected to the others by threads of biological material too fine to see, threads that run through the water-channels like nerves through a body. They are, collectively, a single creature that lives in the walls of the hollow, embedded in its geology, drinking the water that passes over them, filtering out the minerals and the organic particulates and the rare traces of biological material that drift down from the world above. They do not move. They do not eat in any recognizable way. They simply exist, and absorb, and grow incrementally larger, and occasionally—one every century or so—one of them splits, dividing itself into two smaller pearls that will spend the next hundred years slowly separating as the water-channels widen between them.

The pearls are the reason the water tastes sweet. They are filtering it, changing its composition, adding something of themselves to what passes over them. The sweetness is not sugar. It is not any compound that chemistry has a name for. It is, perhaps, a kind of secretion—a waste product, or a hormone, or a message. The pearls may be communicating with each other through the water. They may be communicating with the hollow itself. They may be singing a song that is too slow to hear, a melody that takes a millennium to complete a single phrase, and you—you who are here for an hour, a day, a week—you are a note so brief it does not register. You are a hiccup in their symphony.

---

The flatworm-like creature in the pool is not a flatworm.

It has no genus. It has no family. It has no phylum. It exists in the spaces between the categories that taxonomists have built, and it has existed there for longer than those categories have existed. It is, in fact, older than the hollow itself—or rather, its lineage is older. Its ancestors lived in the shallow seas that covered this land before the limestone was limestone, before the hills were hills, before there was anything here but water and the slow accumulation of sediment. When the seas receded and the land rose, some of them were trapped in the wet places that remained—the caves, the aquifers, the deep fissures where water still moved. They adapted. They simplified. They gave up everything that required movement, or complexity, or the company of their own kind.

The creature in the pool is the last of its kind in this place. It has not seen another of its own kind in what may be thousands of years. It does not need to. It does not reproduce in any way that requires a partner. It divides, occasionally, splitting itself into two smaller versions when the pool it lives in has accumulated enough nutrients to support the increased biomass. But the pools here are small, and the nutrients are scarce, and the divisions are rare. Most of the time, it simply sits. It absorbs. It waits.

It has no eyes. It has no ears. It has no mouth, though it does have an opening—a pore, a vent—through which it takes in water and filters out the microscopic organic material that the microbialite crust produces. Its nervous system, if it can be called that, is a distributed network of sensitivity that covers its entire body. It feels the water around it. It feels the light—or rather, it feels the difference between light and dark, which in this place is the difference between the presence and absence of the grey-gold glow from the fissure. It feels vibrations: the subsonic hum of the weeping walls, the muffled tread of footsteps (if there are footsteps), the distant and theoretical thunder of the world above.

If you were to touch it—and you could, easily; it would not flee, would not react, would not even seem to notice—it would feel like wet leather, like a piece of fruit that had been soaked until its skin went soft. It would be cool. It would be smooth. And in a moment, you would feel something else: a sensation of *wetness* spreading into your skin, a feeling of moisture penetrating deeper than it should, as if the creature were trying to absorb you, or as if you were trying to absorb it. You would pull your hand back. Your fingertips would be pale and puckered, as if you had been soaking them for hours. And the creature would still be sitting in its pool, unchanged, unmoved, waiting.

It is not dangerous. It is not, in any meaningful sense, an organism that can harm you. It is simply old, and patient, and strange, and it has been here for so long that it has become part of the hollow's geography. It is as much a feature of the landscape as the calcite ridges or the shallow basins. It lives there. It has always lived there. It will always live there, until the water dries up or the ceiling collapses or something comes along that can eat it, and nothing has come along that can eat it for a very, very long time.

---

There is something else in the Weeping Hollow. Or there was. Or there will be again.

The locals—the ones who came before, who left the hammer stone and the bowl and the post—knew about it. That is why the post is there. That is why it was driven into the crevice in the shelf, sharpened end first, standing like a sentinel. It is not a tool. It is not a marker. It is a *keeper*. A prop. A stay against something that wants to rise.

Under the shelf—inside the hollow space between the ledge and the wall, where the denser rock forms a natural overhang and the limestone has eroded away beneath it—there is a gap. It is perhaps ten centimeters high at its opening, narrowing to nothing a meter back. It is dark back there, truly dark, the light from the fissure not reaching. And in that darkness, there is something that breathes.

Not often. Not regularly. Once an hour, perhaps—though time is hard to measure here, and the breathing does not follow any clock—a sound emerges from beneath the shelf. It is a long, slow exhale. A release of air that is warmer and wetter than the air in the hollow, heavy with something organic, something that tastes of minerals and decay and the deep time of the earth. And then, minutes later, an inhale: a drawing-in that seems to pull the very humidity of the hollow toward it, a subtle but definite shift in the movement of the air.

The locals knew. They must have known. The post is there to hold the shelf up—to prevent the stone from settling further, from closing the gap entirely, or perhaps from opening wider. They were maintaining something. They were keeping a balance. The hollow is in equilibrium, but it is not a natural equilibrium. It is an equilibrium that has been engineered, carefully, over generations, by people who understood what lived here and what it needed and what would happen if it were disturbed.

The breathing thing beneath the shelf is not a creature in the sense that you are a creature. It is too large. It is too still. It may not have boundaries. It may extend back into the rock, into the cave system that underlies the hollow, threading through fissures and channels that no human has ever mapped. It may be the source of the warmth that fills the place—not a body heat, exactly, but something like it, a slow metabolic fire that burns not oxygen but something else, something older, something that the earth produces in its deep places where life has learned to live without light.

The breath-moths avoid it. That is how you know it is there. The breath-moths orbit every source of warmth in the hollow except for the gap beneath the shelf. They drift near it, and then they turn away. They can feel something that you cannot. They can sense the wrongness, the not-quite-alive-ness, the vast and patient hunger that waits in the dark.

The locals fed it. That is what the bowl was for—not to catch drinking water, though it did that too, but to collect water in a ritualized way, a systematic way, a way that would produce overflow. The overflow would run along the shelf, seep down behind it, drip into the gap. And in that water, they would place other things. Small offerings. Bits of food. Pieces of themselves—hair, fingernail parings, the occasional drop of blood from a careless slip of the hammer stone. They were keeping it fed. They were keeping it quiet. They were keeping it sleeping.

They stopped coming. That is the problem. The last person to visit the Weeping Hollow was perhaps centuries ago. The offerings have stopped. The water still drips, but it carries no organic material, no trace of life. And in the darkness beneath the shelf, something that has been sleeping for a very long time is beginning to stir.

---

The breathing has changed.

It is subtle. If you spent only a few hours in the hollow, you would not notice. But if you stayed—if you stayed for days, for weeks—you would begin to hear it: a quickening, a deepening, a change in the rhythm of the exhales and inhales. The gaps between them are shortening. The volume is increasing. The thing beneath the shelf is waking up.

And it is hungry.

Not in the way that a predator is hungry. It does not want to eat you, specifically. It does not have preferences or targets. It is simply drawing in, taking, absorbing—doing what the hollow itself does, but faster, more urgently, with a purpose that the rest of the place has long since abandoned. The gradients are flattening in the hollow, but they are flattening toward *something*, and that something is the thing in the dark, and it is beginning to pull.

You can feel it if you stand near the shelf. The air is cooler there. The warmth of the hollow does not reach, because the warmth is being drawn into the gap, absorbed by whatever lies beyond. And if you put your hand near the opening—if you are foolish enough to do that—you will feel a current, a movement of air that should not exist in a place where the air is still. It is pulling. It is calling. It is saying, without words, *come here. Give me what you have. I have been patient for so long, and I am so tired of waiting.*

The locals knew this would happen. That is why they left the post. That is why they made the bowl. That is why they came back, generation after generation, to maintain the equilibrium, to keep the gradients flat, to feed the thing in the dark so that it would stay sleeping. And then, at some point, they stopped. The last keeper died. The last caretaker never returned. The hollow has been alone for centuries, and the thing beneath the shelf has been slowly waking, and now you are here, and you are the first gradient it has felt in a very long time.

---

You should not have come.

But now that you are here, you understand. You understand why the hollow is called what it is—not because the walls weep water, but because they weep for what they contain. You understand why the locals left no drawings, no hearth, no midden. They did not live here. They served here. They were custodians of something that predated them, something that will postdate them, something that exists outside of time in the way that a stone exists outside of time. And they are gone, and you are here, and the hollow is looking at you with something that is not eyes, measuring you with something that is not thought, and deciding what to do.

For now, it waits.

For now, you can still leave. The passage out is short. The hill is still there. The sun is still moving across the sky. The world is still turning, still wanting, still burning with its gradients and its urgencies and its desperate need to be alive. You can go back to that. You can leave the hollow to its weeping and its waiting and its slow, patient breathing in the dark.

Or you can stay. You can take up the bowl. You can begin, again, the work of feeding. You can fill the hollow with your breath, your warmth, your organic material, your life. You can hold back the thing beneath the shelf for another generation, another century, another small eternity of equilibrium and peace. You can be the next caretaker. You can be the last.

The hammer stone is still on the shelf, waiting for a hand to pick it up.
