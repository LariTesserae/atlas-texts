# The Drowned Quarter

## On What Lives Here

The first thing to understand is that nothing here remembers the surface.

Not the fish. Not the crab-things. Not the mat of brown-purple organisms that sheathes the walls like a living felt. Not the transparent drifters that own the open water between intersections. None of them carry a genetic memory of air, of light that comes from a direction rather than from all directions equally, of the moment when this place was not this place. Whatever existed here before the water — the city, the streets, the squares, the life that built and walked and spoke — is not remembered by the life that is here now. The water erased memory the same way it erased the wood: completely, without violence, through the patient application of time.

But.

The buildings remember. The cobbles remember. The ceramic drainpipes and the fitted stones and the archways that once spanned canals — these remember in the way that stone remembers, which is not through thought but through form. The shape of what was is still present in what is. The doorways still frame volumes of water that were once volumes of air. The windows still open onto nothing, which is what they always opened onto, in a sense — the space beyond the wall, the outside that is now also inside, the medium that fills everything and refuses to distinguish between the two. The streets still descend in terraces that were shaped for feet and are now shaped for currents, and the descent is the same descent, only the reason for it has changed.

What lives here lives in the shape of what was, the way hermit crabs live in the shells of other creatures. The life is not the building. The life is not the street. But the life arranges itself around these forms with such precision, such specific adaptation to the architectural zones the buildings provide, that it is impossible to say where the structure ends and the ecology begins. The upper-floor fish school in windows because windows exist. The wall-grazing things graze where they do because walls exist. The large dark fish cruise the streets because streets exist, and the streets provide corridors of current and visibility that open water does not. The life did not choose these zones. It grew into them the way a vine grows into a trellis — not by decision but by the simple mechanics of a living thing responding to the form it is given.

## The Fish, Properly

The word *fish* is a concession. Some of these things are fish in the way that a deep-sea anglerfish is a fish — technically, taxonomically, but the word feels inadequate, like calling a cathedral a building. Others are fish in no recognizable sense at all but merely occupy the same conceptual space: things with spines and fins and eyes that live in water, and the eye is the part that makes the word difficult, because some of these things have eyes and some of them do not, and the ones that do not have made other arrangements.

The large dark fish — the ones nearly a meter long, the ones that cruise the street level with unhurried confidence — these are the ones that most resemble what you would recognize. They are heavy-bodied, slow, dark in the way that deep-water creatures are dark, not black but a brown so deep it reads as black in the bio-luminescent gloom. Their scales, if they are scales, have a quality of dullness that suggests armor more than ornament. They move with a steady lateral compression of the body, a side-to-side motion that is so deeply right, so deeply *fish*, that watching them produces a recognition in the viewer that precedes thought — the body knows what it is seeing before the mind can name it.

But their eyes are wrong. Not in the way that deep-sea eyes are usually wrong — not the enormous light-gathering orbs, not the blind vestigial remnants. Their eyes are small, set high and forward on the head, and they are reflective. They catch the bio-luminescence and return it brighter, as if the eye itself contains light, as if seeing is an active process here, a projective process. What they see with those eyes is not known. What they look at is not always clear. They appear to navigate by the buildings rather than by sight alone, to know the streets the way a blind person knows a familiar hallway — by the spacing of the walls, the rhythm of the current, the pressure of the water against their lateral lines. The eyes may be for something else. The eyes may be for looking at each other. The eyes may be for looking at the things in the upper doorways that do not come down.

The small bright fish — the ones that flicker in the upper stories, that school in windows like thoughts passing through a mind — these are the bioluminescent ones, the source of the light. Or: they are *a* source of the light, one source among many, because in the Drowned Quarter the light comes from everywhere and nowhere, from organisms too small to see and organisms that might be fish and organisms that are not fish at all but that occupy a similar enough ecological role that the distinction becomes, as everything becomes down here, academic. These small fish — if they are fish — produce light from rows of organs along their bellies and flanks, organs that glow a blue-green that is not the blue-green of surface water but something slightly different, slightly warmer, slightly more alive. They brighten when the marine snow thickens. They brighten when something moves past them quickly. They brighten when the large dark fish pass below. They brighten, in other words, when they are stressed, and since they are always in some state of stress — this is the nature of being small and bright in a dark world — they are always glowing, and the Drowned Quarter is always lit by them, and the light is always the light of small bright creatures being afraid.

Between the large dark and the small bright are the middle fish, the ones that hover in doorways and hold position against the current with small adjustments of their fins. These are the ones that watch. They face into the current — which means they face upstream, which means they face the direction from which the water comes — and they watch for what the current brings. The marine snow is their food. The particulate matter drifting downward is their sustenance. They wait in doorways and they let the water bring them what they need, and the doorway is significant because the doorway creates an eddy, a small zone of slower current where the particulate matter accumulates more densely, and the fish that hover in doorways are not choosing doorways for architectural reasons but for hydrodynamic ones — the doorway concentrates the food, and the fish that learns to hover in a doorway eats better than the fish that hovers in open water, and this is how architecture becomes ecology, how the forms of the dead city become the structures of the living one.

## The Things on the Walls

The mat — the brown-purple carpet that covers the lower walls of every building — is the most important organism in the Drowned Quarter, though it does not look like an organism and does not behave like one and can be walked past a hundred times without being recognized as alive. It looks like felt. It looks like moss. It looks like the kind of growth that appears on any surface left submerged long enough — algae, mold, the inevitable colonization of stone by something that can eat stone or eat what settles on stone. But it is not any of these things.

It pulses.

The pulse is slow — once every several seconds, perhaps, though time is difficult to measure down here, where there are no clocks and no day and no night and the only rhythm is the current and the current has its own rhythm that is not a rhythm because it never varies. But the mat pulses, a contraction and expansion that is visible if you watch the same patch of wall long enough to notice that it has changed, that the surface that was slightly convex is now slightly concave, that the brown-purple has darkened and lightened and darkened again, and that the things grazing on it — the eyeless, leggy things that move along the walls with an undulating motion that produces in the viewer a deep and nameless unease — that these things react to the pulse, that they graze when the mat is expanded and still themselves when it contracts, as if they are reading it, as if the pulse is a signal, a communication, a word spoken very slowly in a language made of pressure and surface and the color purple.

The mat is one organism. This is the part that is difficult to accept. It is not many organisms growing in proximity. It is one organism that covers every lower wall in the district, that extends through every street and around every corner and into every doorway and up to the height where the bio-luminescence is still strong enough to sustain whatever process sustains it. It is one creature the size of a district. It has been growing for as long as the district has been submerged, and it has been growing *into* the district — into the mortar between the stones, into the ceramic of the drainpipes, into the gaps where the cobbles have buckled and the silt has settled. It is not on the buildings. It is in them. It is the building, now, as much as the stone is. The stone is the skeleton. The mat is the skin. And the things that graze on it are not parasites but part of the system — they are the mouthparts, perhaps, or the immune response, or something else for which no surface analogy exists, because on the surface nothing works like this, because on the surface no organism grows to the size of a city block and pulses and is eaten by things with too many legs and continues, continues, continues.

## The Things in the Upper Doorways

The dark is physical in the upper doorways. Not the absence of light but the presence of something else — a density, a weight, a quality of the water that is different from the water below. Things live in this dark. They are not fish. They are not the wall-grazing things. They are not the transparent drifters. They are something else.

They are known by their effects. The displacement of light. The disturbance of silt. The brief, momentary rearrangement of the bio-luminescent glow that suggests something has passed through it, something large enough to block or bend the light, something that moves and therefore lives but that does not come down and does not come closer and does not allow itself to be seen as anything other than a disturbance, an effect, a ripple in the medium of perception.

The speculation — and this is speculation, because nothing is known, because the upper doorways are the one zone of the Drowned Quarter that resists observation the way a black hole resists observation, by bending everything that approaches them — the speculation is that these things are old. Older than the fish. Older than the mat. Older than the crab-things and the transparent drifters and the leggy wall-grazers. The speculation is that these things were here before the water, or that they came with the water, or that they are the water, in the way that the mat is the buildings — not an organism in the water but an organization of the water, a pattern that the water has settled into after long enough, the way any fluid settles into a pattern if you leave it long enough in a container of the right shape.

They do not come down because they do not need to. The upper doorways are where the current is strongest, where the warm water rises from below and carries with it everything the district has produced — waste, spawn, the particulate residue of a thousand organisms living and dying in the streets. Whatever these things are, the current feeds them. The district feeds them. The life below feeds them. And they hang in the upper doorways and they do not move, except when they do, and when they do the light bends and the silt shifts and the fish below scatter for a moment and then regroup, because even the fish have accepted that the upper doorways are not their zone, that the rules of the Drowned Quarter extend in every direction including up, and that the things in the dark above are not to be disturbed, not because they are dangerous but because they are *fundamental*, because they are part of the architecture now, because removing them would be like removing the current or the pressure or the marine snow — the district would continue, but it would continue differently, and the difference would be a loss.

## The Crab-Things

The clicking is the only sound you can rely on. The current is felt, not heard. The silt-collapses are sporadic. But the clicking — the faint, rhythmic, clock-in-another-room clicking — is constant, and it comes from the crab-things, and the crab-things are everywhere on the cobbles, and they are the closest thing the Drowned Quarter has to a population.

They are crabs the way the fish are fish — technically, perhaps, or approximately, but the word does not capture what they are. They have shells, yes. They have legs — more than a crab should have, or perhaps the same number but arranged differently, or perhaps the same number arranged the same way but each leg is too long and too thin and moves with a fluidity that is wrong, that is too smooth, that suggests joints where there are no joints. They click because the tips of their legs strike the cobbles as they move, and the cobbles are stone and the legs are calcified and the sound is the sound of stone meeting stone, which is the sound of something that was not stone becoming stone, which is the sound of mineralization, which is the sound of life turning to geology, which is the only sound in the Drowned Quarter worth listening to.

They eat the silt. This is the simplest fact about them and the most important. The marine snow falls and settles and accumulates and the crab-things process it — they move across the cobbles and they eat what has landed there, and what they eat becomes what they are, and what they are is calcified, is mineral, is stone-and-chitin, is a creature that is becoming its own environment one bite at a time. When they die — and they die, everything dies, even in the Drowned Quarter, even in the warm bath of the thermal vent — when they die, their shells settle into the silt and become part of the silt and are eaten by the next generation of crab-things, and the cycle is so tight, so closed, so perfectly self-referential that it is difficult to say where the organism ends and the substrate begins. The crab-things eat the silt and become the silt and are eaten as silt by crab-things. They are their own ancestor. They are their own food. They are a closed loop in a place where everything is a closed loop, and they are the most closed of all, the most perfectly recursive, the most willing to admit that what they are is what the district is, which is a process, which is a continuation, which is a thing that happens rather than a thing that is.

There are hundreds of them. Thousands, perhaps. They cover the cobbles in a shifting, clicking mass, and when you step — if you step, if you are the kind of thing that steps — they part around your foot the way the small fish part around the large fish, with the same absence of alarm, the same species-level acceptance that a large thing has passed through and will pass through again and that the sensible response is to move and then to return and to continue.

## The Transparent Drifters

These are the ones that are almost not there. They drift in the open water between intersections, in the places where the current is strongest and where the buildings set back from the corners and the street becomes a square, becomes a volume, becomes a space that is not a corridor but a room — an outdoor room, a room with no ceiling, a room whose walls are current rather than stone. In these spaces the transparent drifters move, and their movement is not movement in the way that movement is usually understood. They do not propel themselves. They do not choose a direction. They are moved. They are in the current and the current carries them and their bodies are so nearly the density of the water itself that the current does not need to push them — they are already where the current is going, they are the current's passengers, they are the current's cargo, and they drift and they drift and they drift.

You see them only at the edges. The light bends at their margins — a refraction, a ripple, the ghost of a shape that is less a shape than a disturbance in the otherwise uniform field of the water. They are flat, roughly. Leaf-shaped, roughly. They have no features that can be described with confidence. They may have organs. They may have structures. But these structures, if they exist, are as transparent as the rest of them, are as nearly the density of water as the rest of them, are as close to not-being as a living thing can get while still being alive.

What they are for is a question that the Drowned Quarter does not answer. They are not food for the other things, or not obviously. They are not grazers. They are not predators. They do not interact with anything, as far as observation can determine. They simply drift. They exist in the open water and they move with the current and they are the closest thing the Drowned Quarter has to nothing — to the absence of life, to the pure medium, to water that is just water and not water that is full of things. But they are alive. They are alive the way a window is solid — technically, constitutionally, but in a way that strains the definition toward its breaking point. They are alive and they are almost nothing and they drift and they are the most peaceful things in the Drowned Quarter, the things that have made the deepest accommodation with the pressure and the dark and the warm water and the slow particulate rain, because they have become so nearly identical to their medium that the medium cannot touch them, cannot change them, cannot do anything to them that they have not already done to themselves.

They drift. The current carries them out of the square and into the next street and through the next intersection and into the next square, and they do not know this and do not care and are not capable of caring, and this is a kind of freedom, perhaps, the only kind available down here — the freedom of being so close to nothing that nothing can find you.

## On the Question of Intelligence

Nothing here is intelligent. Or: nothing here is intelligent in the way that intelligence is usually measured, which is by the standards of the surface, which is by the standards of air-breathers and tool-users and things that build and remember and plan. By those standards, the Drowned Quarter is empty of mind. The fish do not think. The crab-things do not think. The mat does not think. The things in the upper doorways — if they think, their thinking is so slow, so distributed, so unlike what thinking looks like when it happens in a skull that the word *think* does not apply and some other word is needed and no other word exists.

But.

The district itself thinks. Or: the district does something that is adjacent to thinking, something that occupies the same conceptual space, something that produces results that are indistinguishable from the results of thinking without necessarily involving anything that could be called a thought. The fish zone themselves by architectural feature. The mat pulses in synchrony with the current. The crab-things process the silt in a pattern that mirrors the pattern of the streets — they move along the cobbles in lines that are not random, that follow the seams between the stones, that trace the grid the way a pen traces the lines of a drawing, as if the streets are still being used for navigation by organisms that do not know what navigation is. The transparent drifters collect in the squares and disperse in the corridors, not by choice but by physics, because the current shapes itself to the architecture and the drifters are shaped by the current and the architecture is still there, still organizing, still doing what it was built to do, which was to organize the movement of things through space.

This is not intelligence. This is something else. This is the ghost of intelligence, the residue of design persisting in a medium that has dissolved the designer, the way a mold persists after the object that made it has been removed. The district organizes life the way a riverbed organizes water — not by intention but by form, not by decision but by the simple fact of being shaped the way it is shaped. And the life that fills it is organized, and the organization looks like purpose, and the purpose looks like intelligence, and maybe that is enough, maybe that is all intelligence ever was — the persistence of form after the form-giver is gone, the shape of something continuing to shape things, the dead hand of design still arranging the living world into zones and patterns and rules that are observed not because they are enforced but because they are the path of least resistance, which is the only path water knows, which is the only path anything knows, down here, in the dark, in the warm, in the pressure that has been happening so long it has become the normal condition.

## What Lives Here, Finally

What lives here is the district. Not in it. *As* it. The fish and the crabs and the mat and the drifters and the things in the upper doorways are not inhabitants of the Drowned Quarter; they are the Drowned Quarter, the way a forest's trees and birds and insects and soil are not inhabitants of a forest but the forest itself, distributed across a thousand species and a million individual organisms, all of which are the forest and none of which is the forest, and the forest is what lives, the forest is the organism, and the trees are its bones and the birds are its blood and the insects are its nerves and the soil is its skin.

The Drowned Quarter is alive. The streets are its veins. The current is its blood. The buildings are its skeleton, and the mat is its skin, and the fish are its thoughts — the bright small ones flickering like neurons in the upper stories, the large dark ones moving through the streets like impulses traveling down a nerve, the middle ones hovering in doorways like words held in the mouth, considered, released. The crab-things are its immune system, processing the falling debris, breaking it down, returning it to the substrate. The transparent drifters are its breath, moving in and out of the squares like air in lungs, if lungs were made of water and air were made of almost-nothing. And the things in the upper doorways — those unseeable, fundamental things — they are whatever part of a living thing is closest to consciousness, the part that watches, the part that does not act but that knows, in some deep and untranslatable way, that it is.

The district is alive, and it does not know it is alive, and this is the condition of most living things, and the pressure is the condition of being alive, and the dark is the condition of being alive, and the warm water is the condition of being alive, and the marine snow falling in its steady, endless, patient rain is the condition of being alive, and everything here is alive, and everything here has been alive for longer than the surface can imagine, and everything here will continue to be alive, in this form or in whatever form comes next, because the water does not end and the stone does not end and the form does not end, it only changes, and the change is the life, and the life is the change, and the clicking of the crab-things on the cobbles is the heartbeat, and the pulse of the mat on the walls is the breathing, and the district turns in the dark like a sleeper in a bed, and the sleeper does not wake, and the sleeper does not need to wake, and the sleeper is home.
