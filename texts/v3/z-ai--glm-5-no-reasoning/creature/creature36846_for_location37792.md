# What Lives in the Weeps

The first thing that lives here is the patience.

Not a creature. Not yet. But patience lives here the way electricity lives in wire — inherent, structural, indistinguishable from the medium. The patience of stone accepting water. The patience of water dissolving stone. The patience of moss building soil from rock and reclaiming soil back to rock. It is the governing condition. Everything that has life here has inherited it, adapted to it, become an expression of it. The fastest thing in this place — the wren — moves with a speed that elsewhere would be unremarkable, but here reads as almost vulgar. And even the wren slows, eventually. Even the wren nests in the same crevice, season after season, until the moss grows into the shape of the nest and the nest becomes a feature of the stone.

---

Beneath the moss, in the saturated dark where light has never reached, the oldest inhabitants work.

They are bacteria. They have always been bacteria. They will always be bacteria. They live in the hairline fractures of the schist, in the millimeter-wide seams where water has opened the stone like a book, and they eat the rock. Not quickly. A molecule here, a molecule there. A hydrogen bond weakened, a silicate structure loosened, an iron oxide reduced. They have been doing this for so long that the bowl itself — the forty-meter depression, the cupped hand of dark stone — is their architecture. They carved it. They are carving it still. The weeping of the stone is their waste product, their exhalation, the slow sweat of a metabolic process so drawn out that it looks, from above, like geology. From below — from their vantage, if they had one — it looks like agriculture. They are farming the stone. They have been farming it for a million years. The crop is the bowl. The crop is the water. The crop is everything that exists here because the bowl exists.

There are others in the fractures. Chemolithotrophs that derive energy from the oxidation of iron, staining the water with the taste of blood. Nitrifying bacteria that convert the ammonia of decay — rotifers, nematodes, the slow accumulation of dead springtails — into nitrite and then nitrate, feeding the moss, which holds the water, which feeds the bacteria, which eat the stone. A cycle so tight it looks like stillness. A metabolism so distributed it looks like a landscape. They do not know they are the foundation of everything. They do not know anything. They eat and they die and they eat and they die, and the eating and the dying have become, over geological time, the place itself.

In the deeper fractures — three centimeters in, five, where the stone is still stone and not yet the soft transitional material of the weeping walls — there are organisms that have never been described. Not because they are rare. Because no one has looked. They are small. They are slow. They divide once per century, perhaps, perhaps less. Their metabolisms run at the temperature of the deep rock, which is the temperature of the earth, which is the temperature of equilibrium. They may have been here since before the moss. They may have been here since before the bowl. They may be the oldest continuous inhabitants of this place, and they may be the oldest continuous inhabitants of anything, and there is no way to know because there is no calendar here, no record, no sediment, no ice core, nothing that counts. Only the stone, and the fractures in the stone, and the life in the fractures, and the water that connects them all.

---

Above the bacteria, in the moss — in the layered, competing, collaborating continent of moss — the invertebrates hold their parliament.

Start with the tardigrades, because they are the most resilient and because resilience, here, is the primary virtue. They live in the water films that coat every moss leaf, every liverwort frond, every lichen thallus. Each one is a half-millimeter, eight-legged, armored, oblivious. They can survive desiccation, freezing, boiling, radiation, the vacuum of space. Here they need none of this. Here they are not surviving. Here they are living, which is what they do when surviving is not required — they live slowly, thoroughly, with a completeness that larger organisms cannot achieve. They eat the bacteria. They eat the algae. They eat the rotifers. They are eaten, in turn, by the nematodes. The nematodes are smaller and faster and hungrier, and they move through the water films like thread through water, which is what they are, which is what they have always been.

The rotifers are the wheel-bearers, the tiny animals with the crowns of cilia that appear to rotate. They do not rotate. They beat, and the beating creates a current, and the current draws in food — bacteria, organic particles, the碎屑 of the moss itself — and the rotifer eats. They are the filter-feeders of the moss continent. They are the ones who clean the water, who clarify it, who keep the pools from stagnating — though stagnation here is not a problem, because stagnation is a human concept, a word for water that has stopped being useful, and the water here has never been useful and has never been anything other than what it is.

There are mites. Oribatid mites — the moss mites, the beetle mites — small, dark, armored, moving through the moss substrate like tanks through a forest. They eat dead plant matter. They are the decomposers, the recyclers, the ones who take what the moss has built and break it down and return it to the water and the stone. They are the other half of the cycle — the bacteria eat the stone, the moss builds from the stone, the mites unbuild the moss, the bacteria eat the stone. Round and round. A wheel. The rotifers' wheel and the mites' wheel and the bacteria's wheel, all turning at the speed of geology, all turning so slowly they appear to be still.

Lift a handful of moss and you will see them — tardigrades, nematodes, rotifers, mites — and you will not see them, because they are too small, because they are transparent, because they are the color of water and they live in water and they are water with just enough structure to be alive. What you will see is the moss itself, dark and wet and dense, and the movement within it — a shimmer, a shifting, a sense that the moss is not a plant but a city, a civilization, a continent so densely populated that the inhabitants have become indistinguishable from their habitat. The moss is the continent. The invertebrates are the continent. The distinction between them is a distinction that exists only in the human mind, which is not here, which has never been here, which will never be here long enough to understand that the moss and the mites and the tardigrades are not separate things living in the same place but a single thing expressing itself in multiple forms.

---

On the surface — the visible surface, the moss and the stone and the shallow pools — the larger inhabitants live their larger lives.

The caddisfly larvae are the architects. Each one builds a case — a tube, a cylinder, a portable home — from fragments of their world. Liverwort pieces. Stone grains. Sand. Bits of moss. Each species has its aesthetic. The cylinder-builders use stone, uniform grey, precise. The spiral-builders use liverwort, green-brown, organic, curved. The tube-builders use moss fragments, dark, irregular, looking like nothing so much as a driftwood shack built by someone with all the time in the world, which is what they are and what they have. They drag these cases across the wet moss, across the stone, through the shallowest water. They are the only things here that build. Their cases are the only constructions. And they build not for permanence — the cases are temporary, abandoned when the larva pupates, dissolved back into the moss — but for the act of building itself, which is the act of living, which is what they do.

They will become caddisflies. They will emerge from their cases with wings, with long legs, with antennae, and they will live above the moss for a few days, mating, laying eggs in the water, dying. The adults are not here. The adults are elsewhere — above the bowl, in the mist, in the wind that does not reach the floor. Or they may not be. The adults may never leave. The adults may emerge and find the mist and the stone and the still air and settle back down and lay their eggs in the same pool where they were born and die on the surface of the water and sink into the moss and become part of it. There is no elsewhere here. The concept of elsewhere has been bred out. The caddisflies that left did not come back, and their lineages died, and the caddisflies that stayed are the ones whose genetics remember that staying is the only option.

---

The springtails are the most numerous and the most forgotten.

Millions. Tens of millions. They cover every surface that is not submerged — the lichen, the moss, the stone, the ferns, the undersides of the overhangs. They are the grazers of this world, the cattle of the moss continent, eating algae and fungi and decaying matter with a thoroughness that ensures nothing accumulates. Without them, the moss would suffocate under its own detritus. With them, the moss breathes. They are white and grey and pale blue — the blue is not pigment but structural, the color of their scales, the color of light filtered through a body smaller than the light's wavelength, which is a kind of physics that the springtails do not know about and do not need to know about.

They jump. When disturbed — when a finger touches the moss, when a boot shifts the stone, when a centipede comes too close — they spring. A furcula, a forked appendage folded beneath the abdomen, snaps down against the substrate and launches them. The jump is the only sudden thing here. It is a flicker. A life appearing and disappearing in the same instant. And then they land, and they fold the furcula back, and they are still, and the stillness resumes as though the jump was a memory, a dream the moss had, not a real event.

They do not know they are prey. Or they know and do not care. The centipedes move through the moss and take them, one by one, with a slowness that is indistinguishable from patience, and the springtails do not flee, because fleeing is a concept that requires a future, and the springtails live in the present, which is all there is here. The centipedes are rust-colored, small, with legs that move in waves — a body-length per second, perhaps less. They are the predators of this world. But the word predator is too active, too dramatic. They are the selectors. They select the slow, the old, the already-dying. They select the springtails that were going to die anyway. And the springtails reproduce — parthenogenetically, some of them, cloning themselves, each daughter a perfect copy of the mother, a line of identical animals stretching back into a past that has no beginning — and the reproduction matches the predation, and neither has changed, and neither will change.

---

The beetles are the wanderers. Not that they wander far — the bowl is forty meters across, and a beetle's world is smaller than that, perhaps four meters, perhaps five — but they move with a purpose that the other invertebrates lack. The springtails drift. The centipedes patrol. The caddisflies crawl. The beetles walk. They are going somewhere. They never arrive.

The darkling beetle — matte black, flightless, its elytra fused into a single dome — is the most common. It walks the wet stone. It walks the moss. It walks the edge of the pools. It is looking for something — food, a mate, a place to lay eggs — and it will find it, because everything it needs is here, has always been here, will always be here. But it walks anyway. It walks with the deliberation of a pilgrim. It walks because walking is what darkling beetles do, and this darkling beetle has been doing it for its entire life, and its ancestors have been doing it for longer than the moss has been growing, and the walking is not a means to an end but the end itself. The walking is the life.

The rove beetle is the submarine. It moves through the moss — not on it, through it, submerged in the wet substrate, its glossy body visible only as a ripple in the surface, its antennae breaking the water film like periscopes. It is hunting. It is always hunting. The rove beetle is the most active predator here — active being relative, active meaning it moves more than the centipede, which is not active at all by any standard outside this place. It eats the springtails. It eats the mites. It eats whatever it can catch, which is everything, because everything here is slow. It catches by being slightly less slow. A marginal advantage. An incremental velocity. Enough.

The water beetle lives in the deepest pool. Three centimeters. An oval. Paddling with legs that are too long for its body — reaching, always reaching, as though the pool were larger than it is, as though the pool might expand if the beetle only swam far enough. It will not expand. The pool has been this size for longer than the beetle's lineage can remember. But the beetle swims. It swims because swimming is what water beetles do. It has never left the pool. It will never leave the pool. The pool is its world, its universe, its everything. When it dives — and it dives, gripping the stone bottom with its tarsal claws, holding its breath through a bubble of air trapped beneath its elytra — it is diving to the bottom of the world. When it surfaces, it is surfacing to the top of the world. The distance between the two is two centimeters. The beetle does not know this. The beetle knows only the pool.

---

The spiders are the stillness made visible.

Crab spiders first. Dark as the stone. Darker — the green-black of the wet schist is light compared to them, is a grey compared to their absolute, lightless, matte black. They sit on the stone. They have sat on the stone. They will sit on the stone. They do not spin webs. They do not need webs. They wait, and the waiting is the hunting, and the prey comes — the springtails, the flies, the small things that move across the stone surface — and the spider strikes, and the strike is fast, the only fast thing, faster than the wren, faster than the springtail's jump, a burst of violence so quick it does not register as motion but as a change in the scene — the prey was there, and now it is not, and the spider is where it was, and the only evidence that anything happened is a faint wetness on the spider's mouthparts.

Then the spider is still again. Still for hours. Still for days. Still for the duration of a life that may last years in this cold — metabolisms are slow here, generation times are long, a spider that lives two years in a temperate climate may live five here, seven, ten, the cold preserving it, the consistency sustaining it, the unchanging conditions allowing it to continue long past the point where life in a variable world would have ended.

The wolf spiders are larger. They patrol. Their eyes — eight of them, arranged in three rows — catch the indigo light and reflect it back as tiny points, which is the only way to see them, which is how they see each other. They walk the moss with a stride that is efficient and unhurried. They cover ground. They cover the same ground. Day after day, night after night — and there is no day or night here, there is only the permanent indigo, the closed-eye light, and so the wolf spiders patrol continuously, resting in crevices, resting under overhangs, resting in the same spots, perhaps, where other wolf spiders rested before them, where their mothers rested, where their grandmothers rested, an unbroken line of spiders resting in the same stone depression because it is the right shape and the right temperature and the right humidity and it has always been right.

They carry their young. The female wolf spider attaches her egg sac to her spinnerets and drags it behind her as she patrols, and when the spiderlings hatch they climb onto her back — dozens of them, a hundred, a living fur of small dark bodies — and she carries them. For days. For weeks. They ride the mother spider across the moss continent, and they see what she sees, which is the moss, which is the stone, which is the same as what every wolf spider has ever seen, and they learn nothing, because there is nothing to learn, because the world is already complete.

---

The snails are the slowest. They are so slow that the moss grows over their shells. They sit in the liverworts, in the wet seams, and they eat — rasping the algae from the stone surface with their radulae, a tongue covered in teeth, thousands of teeth, each one a tiny chitonous blade, and the rasping is a sound so faint it cannot be heard, a sound that exists below the threshold of the ear, below the threshold of the weeping, a sound that is almost theoretical. They move at a pace that makes the centipedes look reckless. They move at a pace that makes the water beetle look like a thing of pure velocity. They move at a pace that is, perhaps, the pace of the stone itself — the pace at which the stone weeps, the pace at which the bacteria eat, the pace at which the bowl deepens, the pace at which the world changes. The snails are the clock. If anything here keeps time, it is the snails, moving across the stone at the speed of geology, leaving a trail of slime that dries in the indigo light and becomes a faint silver track, a record of passage, a sign that something was here, which is then overgrown by the moss and erased.

The thrush eats them. The thrush — sitting on the stone shoulder above, spotted-breasted, patient in its own way, a patience that is different from the stone's patience because it is a patience of waiting rather than a patience of being — drops the snails onto the rock. The crack. The shell breaks. The thrush descends, takes the soft body, returns to the shoulder. This happens in the same place. The same stone. The anvil. There is a stone that is the anvil and it has been the anvil for as long as the thrushes have been here, which is as long as the snails have been here, which is as long as the moss has been here. The stone is chipped. The chipping is so small that it cannot be seen, but it is there — a slight depression, a slight flattening, the mark of a thousand snails broken on the same spot, the mark of repetition so profound it has become geological.

---

The wren is the apex. Not because it is large — it is the smallest bird here — but because it is the fastest, the warmest, the most alive in the way that warm-blooded things are alive, which is to say desperately, constantly, burning calories to maintain a temperature that the stone and the moss and the water and the air do not share. The wren burns. In a landscape of cold metabolisms, of organisms that have matched their temperature to the stone, the wren is an anomaly, a fire, a tiny furnace moving through the ferns and the moss with an energy that does not belong.

It eats everything. Spiders. Springtails. Beetles. Larvae. It eats the centipedes. It eats the snails — the small ones, the ones it can swallow whole. It eats the caddisfly larvae, cases and all, grinding the stone-and-liverwort tubes in its gizzard. It is the only thing here that has a gizzard. It is the only thing here that has a stomach that works by grinding rather than dissolving. It is the only thing here that converts life to heat.

It sings. A single note. A low chirp — not the bright, complex song of wrens in other places, not the long warbling territorial declaration of lowland species, but a single short note, repeated, the same note, at intervals that are not regular enough to be the weeping and not irregular enough to be random. It sounds like a mark. It sounds like punctuation. It sounds like the wren is placing a period at the end of a sentence that is the silence, and the sentence begins again, and the wren places another period, and the sentence begins again, and this is the wren's contribution to the sound of the place — not melody, not pattern, but the insistence that something is happening, that the present exists, that there is a now and it is different from the then, which is a belief that nothing else here holds.

The wren nests in a crevice in the stone. The crevice is narrow, horizontal, perhaps fifteen centimeters deep. The nest is built of moss — the same moss that grows on the surrounding stone, the same species, the same texture, the same dark green. The nest is indistinguishable from the wall. If you did not know it was there, you would not see it. The wren enters and exits through a hole that is exactly the width of the wren's body, and the moss has grown around the hole so that it looks like a gap in the moss, not a door, not an entrance, just a place where the moss is absent. The nest has been rebuilt in this crevice every season. The moss of the old nest does not need to be removed because it has already become part of the wall, part of the moss, part of the stone. The wren builds on top of it. The new nest incorporates the old. The nest is, in this way, older than the wren. The nest is older than the species. The nest is a structure that has been continuously maintained for longer than any single wren has lived, a construction passed from generation to generation not through memory or instruction but through habit — the habit of building in the same place, the habit of using the same materials, the habit of a life so consistent that the nest rebuilds itself.

---

There are others. There are always others.

Fungi. Not the visible fruiting bodies of mushrooms — there are no mushrooms here, the conditions are wrong, too wet, too cold, too dark for the production of fruiting structures. But the mycelium is everywhere. It threads through the moss, through the liverworts, through the decaying plant matter on the stone surface. It is white, filamentous, a network so fine and so dense that a single cubic centimeter of moss contains kilometers of fungal hyphae. It is the connective tissue of this place. It links the moss to the substrate, the substrate to the water, the water to the stone. It is not a creature. It is not a plant. It is not a bacterium. It is something else, something that has been here since before the categories were invented, and it does not recognize the categories, and it goes about its business of decomposition and connection with an indifference to taxonomy that is, in its own way, a kind of wisdom.

Nematodes in the water. Rotifers in the water films. Tardigrades in the moss. Mites in the moss. Springtails on the moss. Centipedes in the moss. Beetles on the stone. Spiders on the stone. Snails in the liverworts. Larvae in the pools. A wren in the ferns. A thrush on the shoulder. And beneath all of it, in the fractures, the bacteria, eating the stone, making the bowl, creating the world in which everything else lives.

And the moss. The moss is not an animal. The moss is not fauna. But the moss is alive, and the moss is what lives here most thoroughly, most completely, most successfully. The moss is the body that everything else inhabits. The moss is the skin and the skeleton and the flesh of this place, and the fauna are the life that moves across it, through it, within it, and the distinction between the moss and the fauna is, in the end, a distinction that dissolves the way the distinction between stone and plant dissolves — gradually, through moisture, through time, through the slow unmaking of boundaries by the persistent fact of wet.

---

What lives in the Weeps is the Weeps itself.

Not a metaphor. Not a spirit. Not a consciousness. But the place as a living system, a single organism composed of stone and water and moss and bacteria and invertebrate and bird, each one a tissue, each one an organ, each one performing its function in a body so large and so slow that it does not know it is alive. The bacteria are the gut — they process the stone, they extract the minerals, they produce the water. The moss is the skin — it covers, it holds, it absorbs, it regulates. The invertebrates are the blood — they move through the moss, they distribute, they cycle, they connect. The birds are the breath — they come and they go, they eat and they excrete, they bring the outside in and they carry the inside out, such a small exchange, such a tiny respiration, but it is the only exchange this place has with anything beyond itself, and without it the system would close entirely and become a stone with water in it and nothing more.

The thrush is the exhale. The wren is the inhale. Everything between is the held breath.

This is what lives here. Not a creature. Not a community. Not an ecosystem, which is a word that implies system, which implies design, which implies a plan. This is not planned. This is not designed. This is what happens when water meets stone for long enough that the stone becomes a bowl and the bowl becomes a world and the world becomes a closed loop and the loop tightens over millennia until it is a knot, and the knot holds, and the holding is the life, and the life is the holding, and nothing changes and nothing changes and nothing changes and the weeping continues and the moss grows and the wren sings its single note and the thrush cracks its snails and the water stands in its pools and the mist sits in its air and the place lives.

The place lives.

It does not know it lives. It does not need to know. It lives the way the stone weeps — not through intention, not through choice, but through the simple, patient, overwhelming fact of being what it is, in the only way it can be, for as long as it can be, which is longer than anything that has ever come here to observe it, which is longer than anything that will ever come here to observe it, which is, in the end, long enough.

Long enough.
