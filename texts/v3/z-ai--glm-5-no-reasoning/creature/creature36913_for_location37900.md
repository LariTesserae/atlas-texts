# What Lives in the Rhizome Settlements

---

## The Settlers Themselves

They are thinner than you expect. Not emaciated—there is food, of a sort, and the Authority's rations do arrive with regularity—but *drawn*, as though something has pulled the moisture from them slowly, over years, the way the Substrate pulls mineral seep from rock. Their faces are narrow. Their eyes sit deep. The skin around their knuckles and the corners of their mouths splits without bleeding—there is no water enough for generous bleeding—and they paint themselves constantly with the salve, this gloss on their hands, their forearms, the backs of their necks. The salve is made from rendered Substrate resin cut with something the chemists in the industrial district refine and will not name. It smells like pine tar and hot copper. The settlers smell like this always. The smell enters fabric, enters hair, enters the grain of the metal sheeting that lines the better rooms. Visitors remark on it. The settlers no longer notice.

Their color is wrong. Not the pallor of people kept from the sun—though that too, surely—but a specific tonal shift, a warmth drained from the complexion and replaced with something cooler, grayer, with undertones of the same rust that marks the older Substrate growth. The industrial workers show this most clearly. Their hands are stained permanent amber-brown from the resin, their nails thickened and yellowed, and when they cough—which is always, a productive rattling hack that they perform without covering their mouths, having long since abandoned that particular politeness—they bring up phlegm the color of old brick. They spit it into the Substrate's root-crevices. The Substrate absorbs it. This is not considered disgusting. This is considered *returning something*.

They are short. Generations underground, in corridors that narrow and shift, in rooms with domed ceilings that were carved for a population that has forgotten what an open sky does to posture. The older settlers develop a forward curve to the spine—not hunched, but *attentive*, leaning slightly toward whatever they are walking toward, listening through their bodies for the Substrate's groans and shifts. The young move differently. They flow. They navigate the Below's irregular passages with a fluidity that seems instinctive, ducking where a branch has thickened, sidestepping where a doorway has migrated, pressing a hand to the wall without looking when they need to check whether a corridor is still viable or has begun to close.

Their eyes have changed. Not dramatically—not the enlarged pupils of deep-cave fish, not the reflective tapetum of nocturnal predators—but subtly. Their irises are wider, the pupils more responsive to dim shifts in light. In the high-light periods, they squint constantly, shielding their faces, moving through the brightest corridors with their heads down. In low-light, they open fully, and they can read the texture of a wall at distances that would leave a surface-dweller blind. They have developed, also, a sensitivity to the quality of light—the specific amber of a fiberoptic cable beginning to fail, the faint blue-green of resin-moth wings, the deeper darkness that indicates a space the Substrate has sealed. They read these the way a sailor reads water.

Their hands are remarkable. Every settler's hands tell a biography. The gardeners—the pruners, the shapers who maintain the upper level's domesticated corridors—have calluses on the heels of their palms from the constant pressure of shears, and their fingertips are smooth, polished, rubbed numb by the daily handling of the Substrate's young growth. The industrial workers have scarred hands, nicked and scored from machinery and from the sharp edges of older Substrate that splinters like wood when broken. The children's hands are sticky, always sticky—coated in resin from climbing, from touching, from pressing their palms against new growth to see whether it will accept them.

They carry their water in small, sealed vessels that they keep on their persons at all times—clipped to belts, worn on cords around the neck, tucked into interior pockets. A settler without water is a settler in crisis. They drink in sips, not gulps, wetting their mouths rather than slaking thirst, because thirst here cannot be slain. It can only be managed, held at bay, acknowledged and deferred. They think about water the way the poor think about money: constantly, with a low-grade anxiety that has become so habitual it registers as personality. Visitors notice the way settlers' eyes track a spilled drop, the way they will pause mid-conversation to wipe a damp surface with a finger and press that finger to their lips.

## The Children of the Below

They are born in the residential level, in the curved rooms that breathe with the Substrate's circadian rhythm, and within hours of their birth their parents carry them to a wall where new growth has been exposed—pale, rubbery, yielding—and they press the infant's hand against the tissue. The handprint fills with sap. The Substrate's surface dims around the print, then brightens, then dims again, a slow pulse that the settlers call *recognition*. The parents watch this with held breath. If the Substrate accepts the child—meaning, if the pulse comes, if the handprint lingers even for a moment before fading—the family rejoices quietly. If it does not, if the print vanishes immediately and the tissue remains inert, the parents say nothing. But the settlement knows. The settlement always knows. Children who are not recognized develop differently. They are not shunned—not exactly—but they are watched. They are the ones who lose their way in familiar corridors. They are the ones whose rooms close faster, whose doorways shift more aggressively, as if the Substrate is trying to dislodge them.

The recognized children grow into the Below's rhythms with an ease that borders on the uncanny. They know which walls are about to exude sap before the sap appears. They dream, they say, of the Substrate's interior—not metaphorically, but *structurally*, as if they have spent the night inside the tissue, feeling its chemistry, its mineral seep, the slow progress of its growth. The Authority's medical officers have attempted to study this. The results are inconclusive. The children's dream-reports are too consistent to be fabrication and too strange to be neurological. They describe "tasting the rock" and "hearing the water that isn't water" and "the place where the old ones went." The medical officers file their reports and move on.

The children play in the middle level's branching corridors, chasing each other through passages that shift overnight, inventing games that require the Substrate's cooperation. *Follow-the-grain*: a game in which one child leads and the others must trace the path by touch, feeling the direction of the wood's fibers, following the Substrate's internal logic rather than the visible architecture. *Hold-the-room*: a game in which children occupy an unused space and try to keep it from closing, pressing their bodies against the walls, breathing with the tissue, willing it to stay open through presence and attention. Some rooms hold. Some do not. The children regard the ones that close as *rejecting* them, and they sulk, and they try again elsewhere.

They hunt resin-moths with sticky-paddles fashioned from old machinery parts and harvested silker-web. They are efficient at this. They have developed techniques: waiting motionless near the processing plants where the moths gather, tracking the faint glow of their wings, striking with a speed and precision that speaks of long practice. They bring their catches to the Authority's collection points in mesh bags and receive payment in chits that can be exchanged for surface goods—dried fruit, sometimes, or cloth, or the precious sealed water canisters that represent luxury. The best hunters among the children are quietly proud. The worst are quietly hungry.

They do not fear the dark. This is the thing that unsettles visitors most. Children in the Below move through unlit corridors without hesitation, navigating by the Substrate's faint bioluminescence—the barely perceptible glow that the oldest growth emits in deep cycles, a light too dim for surface-adapted eyes but sufficient for theirs. They navigate by touch, by the pressure changes in the air, by the sound of the tissue around them. They navigate, perhaps, by something else—something that the settlers call *being known* and that the Authority calls *navigational intuition* and that the children themselves do not name at all, because to them it is simply the way one moves through the world.

## The Gardeners

They are the aristocracy of labor in the Below—not the highest-paid, not the most respected by the Authority's official hierarchy, but the most *necessary*, and they know it, and this knowledge sits in them like a quiet weight. The Substrate grows. Without the gardeners, it would grow into the settlement's rooms and corridors and fill them in, year by year, centimeter by centimeter, until the Below was sealed and silent and the settlement had to begin again somewhere else. The gardeners hold this at bay. They are the ones who stand between the human population and the organism's slow, patient reclamation of every space it has yielded.

They work in pairs, mostly—one to cut, one to watch. The watching is critical. The Substrate does not resist cutting, not exactly, but it *responds*. A cut releases sap, and the sap carries information—chemical signals that propagate through the tissue at speeds the surveyors have measured and the gardeners have learned to anticipate. If you cut too much in one area, the Substrate grows more vigorously there, as if compensating. If you cut too little, the growth continues unchecked. The gardeners walk a line between these extremes, reading the tissue's response in real time, adjusting their cuts, their timing, their pressure. It is skilled work. It takes years to learn. The master gardeners—those who have worked the upper level for decades—can feel the Substrate's intentions through their shears, through the handles of their saws, through the soles of their boots. They know where the organism means to grow before it grows.

They carry their tools in leather rolls—hand-forged, most of them, made in the industrial district from scavenged metal and hardened Substrate wood. The shears are curved, angled to follow the grain of the tissue. The saws are thin, almost surgical, designed to cut without tearing. The pruning hooks are long-handled, extendable, tipped with a blade that the gardeners sharpen themselves on stones carried in their pockets. Each gardener's tool-roll is personal, customized, shaped to their hand and their technique. They do not share tools. They do not lend them. When a gardener retires or dies, their tools are buried with them—not in the ground, for there is no ground, but in the Substrate itself, placed in a hollow carved for the purpose and sealed with new growth. The Substrate grows around them. The tools become part of the tissue. The settlers say the tools remember their owners' hands.

The gardeners speak a dialect that borrows heavily from the settlement's sign-language but extends it, adding terms for the Substrate's textures and behaviors that have no equivalent in standard communication. They can discuss the precise quality of a wall's resistance, the specific elasticity of a new growth, the exact degree to which a corridor has begun to narrow—all without words, in a rapid flutter of gestures that other settlers can follow only partially. This is not secrecy. It is efficiency. The gardeners work with an organism that does not wait for human speech, and they have adapted accordingly.

They are, on average, the longest-lived settlers. The industrial workers die young—resin-lung, machinery accidents, the cumulative effects of smoke and fumes. The Authority's administrators live to surface-equivalent ages, sustained by imported water and better food and the cleaner air of the upper level. But the gardeners live *longest*, and no one is certain why. Perhaps the constant handling of young Substrate growth, the exposure to its chemical exhalations, the daily intimate contact with the organism's most vital tissue—perhaps this does something. Perhaps the Substrate's recognition, which all settlers receive at birth, is reinforced by the gardeners' work, deepened, made mutual. The oldest gardeners—those in their sixth and seventh decades—move through the settlement with a calm that borders on serenity. They do not hurry. They do not worry. They prune, and they shape, and they tend, and the Substrate grows around them, and they are, as much as any human can be, *at home*.

## The Industrial Workers

They are the ones who do not sleep well. The lower level's engines run on cycles that have nothing to do with the light-shifts above, and the workers' shifts are calibrated to production demands rather than circadian rhythm. They emerge from the processing plants blinking, disoriented, their eyes streaming from the smoke that has accumulated in their lungs over hours of work. They drink their water ration in one long pull and immediately begin to crave the next. Their hands shake. This is not nerves but vibration—the machinery's constant thrum has entered their bodies, and it takes hours to subside, and by then it is time to return to work.

They are loud, in a settlement that is mostly quiet. Not because they are careless but because the industrial district's sound-geometry is different—the Substrate amplifies certain frequencies, and the workers have learned to pitch their voices to carry. They shout to each other across the processor floors, their words clipped and functional, stripped of conversational flourish. In the residential level, after shifts, they are conspicuously noisy—their laughter too sharp, their footsteps too heavy, their conversations pitched to industrial acoustics rather than the middle level's softer sound-profile. The other settlers tolerate this. The industrial workers are the ones who produce the fuel that keeps the settlement's lamps burning. They are permitted their noise.

The work itself is brutal and specific. The Substrate's older growth is harvested in blocks—woody, dense, laminated in those layers of rust and cream and charcoal and bruise-purple—and fed into the processors, which grind it into coarse fiber. The fiber is then heated, pressed, and distilled, yielding the resinous fuel that the settlers burn in their lamps and their industrial engines, the lubricant that keeps the machinery running, and the bitter paste that supplements the imported rations. The paste is the color of clay and the consistency of putty. It tastes, according to one visitor who tried it, like "iron filings mixed with unsweetened chocolate and the memory of wood smoke." The settlers eat it without comment. It provides calories. It provides, perhaps, something else—the long-term workers who eat the most paste develop the deepest color-staining, their skin taking on the Substrate's rust tones, their bodies incorporating the organism's chemistry into their own. Whether this is adaptation or contamination is a matter of perspective.

The women among the industrial workforce wrap their hair in silker-silk scarves, not for modesty but to prevent the accumulation of particulate—smoke, resin-dust, microscopic flakes of the Substrate's external dermis that drift in the lower level's air like snow. The men shave their heads, mostly, or crop their hair close. The children of industrial workers are raised primarily in the residential level by older relatives, not because their parents are absent but because the industrial district is no place for a child. The resin-moths breed in the machinery. The silkers, though slowed by heat, still drop from the ceiling-growth without warning. The air itself is a hazard—thick with compounds that the Authority's chemists have catalogued and the workers have learned to endure.

They die in ways that the settlement has come to accept as ordinary. Resin-lung is the most common—the slow accumulation of particulate in the lungs, forming a coating that thickens over years until the worker can no longer draw a full breath. The final stages are quiet. The worker sits in their room, breathing in shallow sips, their chest barely moving, their lips darkened to the same purple as damaged Substrate. They are comfortable, the medical officers say. The resin has a mild analgesic effect. The worker feels little pain. They simply diminish, growing quieter, slower, more still, until they are indistinguishable from the settlement's ambient hush.

When they die, the other workers carry them rootward. This is the industrial district's custom, not the Authority's protocol. The bodies are taken to the boundary of the abandoned zones—the edge of the known, the beginning of the dark—and placed in a hollow that the Substrate has already begun to form. The workers do not carve this hollow. They find it. The Substrate, they say, makes space when it knows someone is coming. Whether this is true, the hollows are always there. The bodies are placed inside. The Substrate grows over them. The workers return to their shifts.

## The Authority's People

They live skyward, near the Hub, in the upper level's rectangular rooms with their planed walls and their varnished surfaces and their brass and ceramic and glass. They are not, technically, a separate class—the Settlement Authority's charter defines all residents as equal participants in the colonial enterprise—but in practice they are distinct, marked by accent and dress and the quality of their skin, which is less stained, less dry, less marked by the Substrate's chemistry.

They are drawn from the surface population, mostly—rotated through on two-year assignments, or three, or five. Some of them stay longer. A few stay permanently, drawn into the settlement's gravity by marriage or by a peculiar attachment that the Below seems to inspire in certain personalities. These are the ones who go native, as the saying goes—slowly acquiring the settlers' pallor, the settlers' thirst, the settlers' habit of timing their breaths to the pressure-shifts. The Authority regards this with mild concern. The settlers regard it with approval.

The administrators work in the intake halls and the orientation chambers, processing new arrivals, maintaining the supply manifests, coordinating with the surface via the access shaft's communication systems. They are clerks, essentially—well-fed clerks with clean hands and imported water and a cautious, slightly overwhelmed manner that comes from managing a population that does not entirely respect their authority. The settlers tolerate the Authority because the Authority controls the supply lifts. The Authority tolerates the settlers because the settlers produce the resin fuel that the surface has begun, in recent decades, to depend upon. It is a mutual tolerance, transactional and brittle.

The Authority's surveyors are a different breed. They are the ones who map the Substrate's growth, who track the downward extension centimeter by centimeter, who venture into the abandoned zones to assess whether they can be reclaimed or must be written off. They are braver than the administrators, or more reckless, or simply more curious—they go where the Substrate grows wild, where the growlers thrum, where the light fails. They carry brass lanterns fueled by processed resin, and they mark their routes with metal tags hammered into the older growth, and they keep meticulous journals in waterproof ink that the settlement's humidity—such as it is—cannot degrade.

They have mapped, over the decades, a vast territory below the settled zones—hundreds of meters of Substrate growth, extending downward in a branching pattern that mirrors, in some uncanny way, the settlement's own spiral. The deeper they go, the older the growth, the denser the tissue, the harder the wood. At the deepest mapped point—some two hundred meters below the industrial district's floor—the Substrate is almost mineral in its density, layered in colors that do not appear in the younger growth: deep greens, blues, a black so absolute it seems to absorb light. The surveyors who have reached this depth report a sound—not the growlers' thrum, but something else, something deeper, a vibration that they feel in their bones rather than hear with their ears. They call it the Substrate's heartbeat. They do not stay long at this depth. They turn back, and they record their findings, and they do not return.

## The Silkers and the Moths

The silkers are the settlement's companion species—uninvited but tolerated, pervasive but not threatening, a presence so constant that the settlers have ceased to think of them as separate from the environment. They are pale, translucent, their exoskeletons faintly luminous in the dim light, their bodies segmented and soft, more isopod than insect, their many legs moving in a rippling motion that is either graceful or revolting depending on one's tolerance. They spin their webs in the spaces between the Substrate's branches—in the upper level's ceiling vault, in the middle level's corridor-junctions, in the industrial district's high corners where the machinery's heat slows them and makes them manageable.

The settlers harvest the webbing with long-handled hooks, pulling it down in sheets that are surprisingly strong—strong enough to be spun into thread, woven into fabric, used for everything from clothing to rope to the filters that protect the processing plants' machinery from resin-moth infestation. The silker-silk fabric is coarse, slightly sticky, and it takes dye poorly, but it is what the settlement has. The poorest settlers wear it exclusively. The middle sort wear it undergarment, against the skin, where its slight tackiness serves as a moisture barrier, trapping what little moisture the body produces against the skin rather than letting it evaporate into the Substrate's thirsty air.

The silkers themselves are handled with a casualness that horrifies visitors. A settler will pluck a silker from a wall with bare hands, examine it briefly—checking, perhaps, for eggs or damage—and toss it aside or drop it into a collection jar depending on its condition. The silkers do not bite. They do not sting. They are, by the standards of the settlement's fauna, almost gentle. But they are large—the size of a human hand, with legs extending to twice that span—and they drop from ceiling-growth without warning, and visitors will flinch, and settlers will laugh, and the silker will land on the floor and scuttle away into a crevice, unbothered, continuing its work.

The resin-moths are less benign. They are smaller than the silkers—their wingspans no wider than a child's palm—but they breed with a fecundity that borders on the aggressive, laying their eggs in any processed Substrate product they can access. The eggs are microscopic, pale, and they hatch into larvae that are ravenous and indiscriminate—eating machinery seals, clogging filters, boring into fuel reserves and rendering them useless. The Authority's maintenance crews spend a significant portion of their time on moth-eradication: sealing access points, replacing compromised materials, deploying the sticky-paddles and the silker-silk filters that are the settlement's primary defense.

The moths themselves are, in a way that the settlers grudgingly acknowledge, beautiful. Their wings are greasy, translucent, veined with a substance that glows faintly—the same bioluminescence that the oldest Substrate growth emits, concentrated and refined in the moths' biology. In the dark of the low-light periods, they cluster around the processing plants' exhaust vents, and their collective glow casts a pale, shifting light across the machinery, and for a moment the industrial district looks less like a factory and more like a cathedral, or a cave filled with stars. The children hunt them in these periods, their sticky-paddles flashing, their silhouettes cutting through the moth-light, and the scene is strange and lovely and the settlers do not remark on it because it is simply what the low-light is.

## The Growlers

No one has seen one clearly. This is the first fact about them, and it is the fact that the settlers return to most often when visitors ask. *No one has seen one clearly.* They have been glimpsed—shapes in the dark of the abandoned zones, movement at the edge of lantern-light, something large and low and slow withdrawing into a corridor that the Substrate has nearly sealed. But these glimpses are unreliable, contradictory, shaped by fear and by the strange acoustics of the deeper Below.

What is known is the sound. The thrumming—a low, rhythmic vibration that passes through the Substrate itself, felt in the walls and floor and ceiling, in the metal bracing and the carved furniture and the settlers' own bones. It is not a growl. The name is a misnomer, or a simplification, or a distortion produced by generations of retelling. The sound is closer to a pulse, a heartbeat, a deep organic rhythm that seems to originate from somewhere below the mapped zones and propagate upward through the tissue. When it is distant, it is barely perceptible—a faint tremor that one might mistake for the machinery's vibration or the Substrate's own metabolic processes. When it is close—when a growler moves through the abandoned zone adjacent to the settled districts—the thrumming is powerful enough to rattle objects on shelves, to vibrate liquids in their containers, to produce a pressure in the ears that the settlers describe as "being inside a throat."

The settlers fall silent when the thrumming passes close. This is instinct, not protocol. The Authority has no official position on the growlers—they are, technically, classified as "unidentified fauna of subterranean origin, potentially hazardous, to be avoided"—but the settlers' response is older and more visceral than administrative classification. They stop. They listen. They place their hands on the walls, feeling the vibration, tracking its movement. They wait. When the thrumming fades, they resume their activities. They do not discuss it.

The growlers, as the settlers understand them, are shy. They avoid light. They avoid sound. They avoid, as far as anyone can tell, the settled zones entirely, confining themselves to the abandoned districts where the Substrate grows wild and the fiberoptic cables do not reach. They are large—the size of a human, or larger, according to the glimpses—but they do not hunt, or if they do, they hunt something other than settlers. No settler has ever been attacked by a growler. No settler has ever been harmed by one. The fear, the silence, the waiting—these are not responses to threat. They are responses to *presence*. The growlers are the Below's other inhabitants, the ones who were here first, perhaps, or who emerged from the Substrate's depths in the same way the settlers emerged from the surface. They share the space. They acknowledge the sharing, in their way, by keeping their distance. The settlers acknowledge it by falling silent.

There is one thing more. The surveyors, in their deepest explorations, have reported finding spaces in the abandoned zones that appear to have been *prepared*—hollows in the Substrate's mass, smooth-walled and regular, too uniform to be natural growth and too organic to be carved. These spaces are empty. They smell, the surveyors say, of something that is not the Substrate's usual resin-and-mineral scent—something warmer, something animal, something that fades quickly in the open air. The surveyors do not enter these spaces. They mark them on their maps and they retreat, and when they return to the Hub, they file their reports with careful, neutral language, and the Authority files the reports in archives that no one consults, and the settlers nod when they hear, and they say nothing, and they go about their lives, and the Substrate continues to grow.

## The Old Settlers

They are the ones who have been in the Below the longest—not the Authority's rotated personnel, not the recent arrivals, but the settlers whose families have lived underground for three generations, four, five. They are the ones who have been presented to the Substrate and recognized, whose children have been presented and recognized, whose grandchildren bear the marks of recognition in their movement and their dreams and their easy passage through the settlement's shifting corridors.

They are not numerous. The Below's population is not large—a few thousand, perhaps, spread across the three levels, living in the carved hollows and the brass-lined chambers and the breathing rooms. But the old families are the settlement's spine, its memory, its continuity. They hold the knowledge that the Authority's archives do not contain: which corridors have shifted and how much, which rooms are safe and which are closing, which sections of the Substrate have been damaged and how they have healed, what the growlers' thrumming means when it comes from the east versus the west, from the deep versus the shallow.

They live mostly in the middle level, in the older residential districts where the architecture has settled into a kind of truce with the Substrate's growth—the walls no longer shifting aggressively, the corridors stable, the rooms holding their shape with only occasional reinforcement. Their dwellings are neither rich nor poor. They are *established*—filled with the accumulated objects of decades: silker-silk tapestries on the walls, carved Substrate-wood furniture, metal tools and ceramic vessels and glass containers that have been passed down through families and have acquired the patina of long use. They are not nostalgic objects. They are functional, maintained, repaired when they break. But they carry the weight of continuity, and the old settlers value this.

They speak the settlement's sign-language fluently, naturally, as a first mode of communication rather than an adaptation. They also speak the surface tongue, but with an accent that has diverged over generations—slower, more carefully enunciated, shaped by the Below's acoustics. They have words for things that the surface language does not name: the specific quality of the air when the Substrate is about to enter a growth phase, the particular sound of a corridor beginning to close, the taste of the mineral seep that the Substrate draws from the rock. They have words, too, for states of being that the surface does not recognize—*root-touched*, meaning the disorientation that comes from spending too long in the deep zones; *sap-known*, meaning the condition of being chemically marked by the Substrate's resin, recognizable to other settlers by scent; *wall-heard*, meaning the ability to sense a room's structural integrity through touch, a skill that the old families develop more fully than others.

They are the ones who carry the stories. Not the Authority's official histories—the production logs, the population censuses, the growth-measurement records—but the other histories, the ones that live in oral tradition and in the Substrate's memory. They tell of the settlement's early years, when the first settlers descended through the access shaft and found the cavity empty and the Substrate young and the Below a vast, quiet darkness that needed to be claimed. They tell of the first pruning, the first carving, the first room. They tell of the settlers who were lost in the early days—those who ventured too deep, too soon, and were absorbed by the Substrate before they could be found. They tell of the Substrate's response to the first human presence—how it grew around the rooms, how it accepted the corridors, how it *recognized* the settlement as something other than an intrusion.

They tell, too, of the things that the Authority's archives do not record. The children who were born in the Below and never went surfaceward, who have no concept of sky, who dream of ceilings and membranes and the golden light of fiberoptic cables rather than of sun. The old settlers watch these children with something that is not quite pride and not quite concern but is perhaps better described as *attention*—the careful, patient observation of people who are waiting to see what happens when a human being has been shaped, entirely, by the Substrate's environment.

They have noticed, these old settlers, that the youngest generation is different. Not dramatically—the children still look human, still speak, still play and hunt and learn. But they move through the Below with an ease that exceeds even their recognized parents'. They do not get lost. They do not cough. Their skin is less dry, their eyes more adapted, their sleep more regular. They drink less water and seem not to suffer for it. They press their hands to the Substrate's walls and the walls respond—not slowly, not over hours, but immediately, the tissue yielding, the corridors opening, the rooms expanding as if the Substrate is making way for them specifically, personally, with a recognition that is not the slow pulse of infant-presentation but something faster, something deeper, something that the old settlers do not have a word for because they have not encountered it before.

The old settlers watch. They wait. They say nothing to the Authority. They say little to each other. But in their private moments, in their established rooms with their silker-silk tapestries and their carved furniture, they place their hands on the walls and they listen, and the Substrate breathes around them, and they wonder—quietly, without alarm, with the patient attention of people who have lived their lives inside a living thing—what it is becoming, and what they are becoming inside it.

## What Lives Here

A organism and a settlement and a population, intergrown. A thing that was a plant and is now a home. A people who were colonists and are now something else—residents not of a place but *within* a place, incorporated, recognized, slowly being shaped by the same forces that shape the walls around them. A system that includes the silkers and the moths and the growlers and the machinery and the smoke and the light and the water that is never enough. A living cavity beneath a desert, breathing, growing, remembering, making space for the things that live in it, and slowly, patiently, without malice and without intention, becoming what it will become next.
