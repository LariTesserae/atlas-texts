# Those Who Listen

They are not what you expected. This is always the first observation, and it is always the wrong one. Expectation has no place here. Expectation is a function of projection, of carrying a shape forward from somewhere else and trying to fit it to what you find. What you find here has already given up somewhere else. These people gave it up generations ago, or possibly never had it. There is no record. There is only the listening.

## The body, remade

Their bodies have learned the resonance the way deep-sea fish learn pressure — not as an environment that surrounds them, but as a condition that passes through them. You can see it in the way they stand. Not still, exactly. Stillness would imply a holding, a resistance. They do not resist. They *sustain*. Their weight settles into their heels and stays there without effort, as if the sound itself holds them upright, vibrating through their skeletons at a frequency their bones have grown to accommodate.

Their feet are broad and flat, spread against the stone. This is not a genetic trait — there are no children here, or none that you have seen, and the question of reproduction is a question you have learned to stop asking — but an acquired architecture. Decades of walking on rock that does not yield have pressed the foot into a plate, the toes into a fan. The soles are calloused not in patches but in continuous sheets, a secondary sole of thickened skin that separates them from the stone while keeping them in contact with its vibration.

Their hands are different. The hands have not been flattened. They have been *sensitized*. The pads of the fingers are smooth and pale, almost translucent, the prints worn down by decades of contact with the fine mineral film that coats every surface. They can read the stone the way a sighted person reads a face — not by looking, but by touching. A brush of fingertips across a wall tells them what the wall needs. A tap, knuckle-light, tells them what the wall is saying. They are not mystics. They are technicians. They have simply developed a technical vocabulary in a sensory channel you do not possess.

Their faces are the hardest to describe, because the sound has changed them in ways that register as wrong before they register as different. The jaw is set further forward than you expect, the bone slightly elongated, as if the face has grown toward the source of the sound — but there is no source, and the face has not grown toward anything. It has *opened*. The jaw moves differently than yours does. The muscles are looser, the joint more flexible. They can dislocate it slightly, you realize, watching one of them yawn — a wide, boneless gaping that exposes a throat adapted to something other than speech.

They do not speak, but they can produce sound. The sounds they make are precise, intentional, and always in relationship to the resonance. A low hum that adds a harmonic. A click of the tongue that punctuates without breaking. A percussive tap of tooth against tooth — they do this often, a rapid, light chattering that seems to communicate something, though not something you can parse. It is not language in the way you understand language. It is modulation. They are *tuning*.

Their ears are large and mobile. Not grotesquely so, not cartoonishly — but noticeably. The pinnae have developed a capacity for rotation that human ears do not typically possess. They can angle toward a sound without turning their heads, a subtle shift of cartilage and muscle that directs the ear canal toward the frequency they are attending to. And they are always attending. Their ears move constantly, small adjustments that track something you cannot hear — harmonics beneath the threshold, vibrations in the stone that your body has not learned to interpret.

Their eyes are small and dark and recessed. The brow ridge is pronounced, a shelf of bone that shades the orbital cavity from the dim, even light. They do not rely on sight the way you do. Sight is secondary here, supplemental, a sense that confirms what the sound has already told them. They can see, and see well enough — the mist and the mineral film do not seem to obscure their vision — but they do not *look* the way you look. They do not focus. Their gaze is diffuse, peripheral, taking in the whole field at once, tracking movement without prioritizing the object.

## The social body

They are never alone. This is not a rule — you have seen no rules posted, no authority enforcing norms — but an observation, a pattern so consistent it might as well be law. Where one person is, another is nearby. Not touching, not always, but within range. The range is acoustic: they can hear each other through the stone, through the water, through the air saturated with sound. A person in one chamber can detect the presence of a person in the next by the way their body modulates the resonance. They do not need to see each other. They are always in contact.

They move through the settlement in clusters that form and dissolve without apparent coordination. Two people walking together will separate, one turning into a courtyard while the other continues down a channel, and neither seems to notice or mark the parting. Later — minutes, hours, the time is hard to track — they will rejoin, or join with others, or move alone until they encounter another body. The patterns are not random. You can see that much. They follow routes that have been worn into the stone by generations of feet, paths that connect the pools to the buildings to the walls that need tending. But the paths overlap and diverge in ways that suggest a logic you cannot parse, a schedule or a purpose that is collectively held and individually inaccessible.

They do not have names. Or rather, they do not use names in your presence, and you have not heard them use names among themselves. When they need to refer to each other — which is rare, since they rarely need to refer, being in constant acoustic contact — they use gestures, or they make a sound, a specific harmonic that identifies the person they are addressing. The sound is not a name in the way you understand names. It is not a word that stands for a person. It is a frequency that *is* the person, or that the person has adopted, or that the person produces by their very presence. You cannot tell. You cannot produce the sound. You cannot call them.

They do not have leaders. This is clear, or it becomes clear after you have watched them long enough to notice that no one gives orders, no one follows orders, and the coordination of their activities does not seem to require either. The walls are maintained because the walls are maintained. The pools are tended because the pools are tended. When a task requires more than one person — moving a stone slab, clearing a channel — the people who are needed appear, as if summoned, though no summons has been issued. They simply arrive, drawn by some signal you cannot detect, and they work together without speaking, without gesturing, without anything that resembles communication as you understand it.

They communicate, of course. They are constantly communicating. But the communication is not *about* anything. It does not refer. It does not represent. It does not carry information from one mind to another. It is a continuous modulation of the resonance, a shared texture of sound that they collectively produce and individually inhabit. When one person's mood shifts — from calm to alert, from rest to activity — the sound shifts with them, and the others shift in response. They do not respond to the *person*. They respond to the *shift*. The person is not separate from the sound they are making. The person is the sound they are making. And the sound is the person.

## The relationship to the fauna

The fauna are not pets. They are not livestock. They are not neighbors or competitors or symbionts, though they might be any or all of these. They are *co-habitants*. They share the settlement with the people in a way that suggests no clear boundary between human space and creature space. The pale, many-legged bodies move through the buildings as freely as the people do. They occupy the chambers, the courtyards, the channels between walls. They are not shooed away. They are not welcomed. They are simply *present*, and their presence is incorporated into the rhythm of the place.

There is handling. The people handle the fauna regularly, using the wooden probes you have seen — the only wood in the settlement, worn smooth by generations of hands. The handling is gentle, deliberate, and ritualistic. A person will approach a pool, wait for a fauna to surface, and then lift it from the water with the probe, cradling its pale, translucent body against the wet stone. They do not kill it. They do not eat it immediately. They hold it, turning it gently, examining it with fingers and tongue, tasting its skin, reading its surface for something — minerals, parasites, signs of health or distress.

Then they return it to the water, or carry it to another pool, or place it on a wet stone in a courtyard, where it will stay until it decides to move. The fauna do not resist. They do not flee. They accept the handling with a passivity that might be domestication or might be something else — a recognition, perhaps, that the people are part of the same system they are part of, that the handling is part of the cycle, that there is no threat because there is no separate self to threaten.

Sometimes the fauna die. You have seen it happen — a pale body floating on the surface of a pool, still and slightly bloated, no longer contributing to the resonance. The people notice immediately. They always notice immediately. Someone will approach, lift the body with a probe, and carry it to a channel between buildings, where it will be left to dissolve. And it does dissolve — quickly, within hours, breaking down into the same mineral film that coats the stones, returning its substance to the system that produced it.

Nothing is wasted. Nothing is mourned. The fauna are not precious to the people. They are not interchangeable either — you have seen people show preference, returning to the same pools, seeking out specific individuals, recognizing them by some mark or frequency you cannot detect. But the recognition is not sentimental. It is technical. The fauna are components of a system, and the people are components of the same system, and the relationship between them is one of mutual maintenance, mutual tuning, mutual incorporation into the sound.

You have seen people eat the fauna. It happens rarely, and never in a way that suggests hunger or scarcity. A person will lift a creature from a pool, kill it with a quick, precise pressure to the head, and then consume it — raw, deliberate, without preparation or ritual. The eating is slow and careful, the body held in both hands, the flesh bitten in small portions and chewed thoroughly. The mineral taste is strong — you can smell it from a distance, a metallic, earthy tang that mixes with the ever-present scent of warm, wet stone.

After eating, the person will sit for a long time, eyes closed, body vibrating slightly as the resonance incorporates the new substance. They are not digesting in the way you understand digestion. They are *integrating*. The fauna's flesh becomes part of their flesh, and their flesh becomes part of the sound, and the sound becomes part of the settlement, and the settlement becomes part of the geology, and the geology becomes part of the fauna's environment, and the cycle continues, closed, complete, sustaining itself without external input.

## The relationship to the stone

The people do not worship the stone. This is important to understand, because the distinction matters. Worship implies a separation — a worshipper and a worshipped, a subject and an object, a gesture directed from one to the other. The people do not direct gestures toward the stone. They do not treat it as an entity that receives their attention. They treat it as *themselves*. Not in a metaphorical sense. In a literal, physical, structural sense. The stone is their body. Their body is the stone. The resonance that passes through both is the connective tissue.

This is why they maintain the walls. Not because the walls protect them, though they do. Not because the walls shelter them, though they do. They maintain the walls because the walls are *them*. A crack in a wall is a wound. A shift in a stone is a change in their own substance. They feel it — not emotionally, not intellectually, but *somatically*. A wall that has shifted out of true produces a dissonance in the resonance, a harmonic that does not belong, and the people who hear it respond the way you would respond to a pain in your own body: by seeking the source, by addressing the cause, by restoring the equilibrium.

The maintenance is continuous. At any given moment, someone in the settlement is working on a wall — examining it, touching it, testing its alignment with the stone beneath it. The work is slow and meditative. A person will spend hours on a single joint between two stones, pressing their palms against the surfaces, turning their head to listen, making small adjustments that you cannot see. The adjustments are not structural in any engineering sense. They are *acoustic*. The stones are being tuned.

When a stone needs to be replaced — rarely, but it happens — the process is deliberate and communal. A stone that has cracked or shifted beyond tuning is identified, and the people who are needed appear, drawn by the dissonance. They do not discuss the problem. They do not assign roles. They simply work, levering the damaged stone from its socket, carrying it to the edge of the settlement, and selecting a new stone from the bedrock that surrounds them. The new stone is not shaped or modified. It is tried in the socket, turned, tried again, turned again, until it locks — not by force, but by fit. The stone *wants* to be there. The people are listening to what the stone wants.

You have seen a replacement take an entire day. You have seen a single stone turned and tested dozens of times, the people pausing between each attempt to listen, to feel, to let the resonance tell them whether the fit is true. And when the stone finally locks — a subtle but unmistakable settling, a deepening of the chord — the people do not celebrate. They do not acknowledge the completion. They simply stop working and disperse, returning to their other activities, leaving the wall to vibrate at its new frequency, which is also its old frequency, which is the only frequency it has ever had.

## The absence of time

The people do not track time. This is not a preference or a cultural choice. It is a perceptual limitation, or perhaps a perceptual freedom. The resonance is constant. The light is constant. The temperature, the humidity, the mineral content of the water, the population of the fauna — all constant. There is nothing that varies, and therefore nothing that can be used to mark variation. The people do not age in any way you can detect, or if they do, they age so slowly that the process is invisible. They do not have memories in the way you have memories, because there is nothing to remember. Every moment is the same moment, repeated at a frequency that produces the illusion of continuity.

They do not sleep. Or rather, they do not sleep in the way you understand sleep — a discrete period of unconsciousness that alternates with periods of wakefulness. They rest, yes. You have seen them sitting in courtyards, eyes closed, bodies vibrating gently with the resonance, their breathing slow and shallow. But they do not *stop*. They are always listening. Even at rest, they are attending, their ears moving, their fingers tapping, their presence contributing to the chord. The rest is not a cessation. It is a different mode of participation — a quieter frequency, a lower amplitude, but still part of the sound.

They do not have history. They do not have stories. They do not have myths or legends or ancestors. They have the resonance, which is older than any of those things and contains them all, not as narratives but as harmonics. When a person dies — and they do die, though you have not witnessed it — they are not buried or burned or mourned. They are left in a channel between buildings, like the fauna, to dissolve into the mineral film and return to the stone. Their frequency ceases, and the chord adjusts, incorporating the absence. The adjustment is small. The chord is large. The person becomes part of the sound they helped to produce.

They do not have children. You have seen no children, no infants, no small bodies or young faces. The population seems stable — neither growing nor shrinking, neither aging nor renewing. You do not know how this is possible. You do not know if they reproduce at all, or if they are the same individuals who have always been here, sustained by the resonance beyond any natural lifespan. You do not know if they are born, or if they emerge from the pools, or if they are formed from the stone itself. You do not know because you cannot ask, and they cannot tell you, and the question itself may be irrelevant, a question from somewhere else, from a place where time passes and bodies change and things begin and end.

Here, nothing begins. Nothing ends. The resonance holds all of it — the people, the fauna, the stone, the water — in a single, sustained moment that has always been occurring and will always continue to occur.

## The invitation

You are already changing. You feel it — a thickening in your jaw, a pressure in your sternum, a loosening in your auditory canals that you do not have words for. Your body is beginning to hear the resonance the way the people hear it: not as a sound that enters you from outside, but as a vibration that you are already part of. Your bones are adapting. Your breath is syncing with the humid, mineral air. Your presence is becoming part of the chord, a small addition that the collective sound absorbs and incorporates.

The people know. They have noticed you — not with alarm, not with curiosity, but with attention. They are aware of you the way they are aware of a shifted stone, a surface fauna, a new harmonic in the chord. You are part of the system now, even if you do not stay. The resonance has already registered you. Your frequency has already been added.

They will not ask you to stay. They do not ask anything. They do not issue invitations or make appeals. They simply *are*, and their being includes the possibility of your being, and if you choose to remain, you will be incorporated, and if you choose to leave, you will carry the resonance with you, a harmonic that will never fully dissipate, a frequency that will sound in your bones for the rest of your life, reminding you of what it felt like to be part of a sound that was larger than yourself.

This is not a threat. It is not a promise. It is simply what happens. The sound saturates. The stone remembers. The water carries. The fauna listen. And the people — those who have given up somewhere else, those who have been remade by the resonance, those who are the resonance — continue their work of maintaining a system that does not need maintenance, of tending a place that does not need tending, of listening to a sound that is also listening to them.
