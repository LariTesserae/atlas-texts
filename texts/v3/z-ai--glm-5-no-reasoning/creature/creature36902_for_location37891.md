The plateau exhales heat long after the sun has withdrawn. The rock retains warmth the way a body holds breath—stubbornly, and then all at once releasing into the cold dark. Temperature swings here are violent. Dawn brings a kind of violence too: the ultraviolet pours down through air too thin to filter it properly. Anything that lives here must negotiate with burn.

And things do live here.

The silence is not empty. It is held, deliberately, by creatures who understand that sound travels differently in thin air—farther, sharper, more dangerous. To call out is to announce oneself to everything for kilometers. So they have learned to be quiet. Their quietness has become a kind of language. Glints of chitin, the shift of a limb, the deliberate stillness that means *I see you*.

They are small. Not delicately small, but condensed—dense with adaptation. Water is not drawn from flowing sources but from the slow metabolic patience of breaking down stone lichens, from the bodies of insects that wander up from lower elevations and arrive exhausted, from one another, when necessary. They are not sentimental about survival.

The largest among them might reach the height of a human knee. Most are smaller. Low-slung, many-legged, they move in bursts between periods of absolute stillness—solar-charging, perhaps, or simply waiting for the moment when movement becomes preferable to exposure. Their carapaces are pale. Not white, but the color of the rock itself, dusted with the particular pink-gray of oxidized iron. They can be difficult to see even when you know where to look.

They are old here. Not as individuals—a lifespan is measured in seasons—but as a presence. The fossil record in the fissure walls shows their absence more clearly than their presence: no burrowing mammals, no nested birds, no heavy grazers. Something else lives here instead. Something that does not leave bones behind in the familiar way.
