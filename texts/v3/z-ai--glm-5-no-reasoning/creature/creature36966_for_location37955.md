# What Lives Here

The first thing to understand is that no one who lives here thinks of it as a settling tank.

That would be like you thinking of yourself as a collection of calcium and water. Technically accurate. Completely beside the point.

---

## The Tenders

Call them what you want. They have no name for themselves—or if they do, it is not a name in any sense you would recognize. Something expressed through the touching of forelimbs to a cleaned surface, perhaps. A gesture-language that means *this is mine because I maintain it, and I maintain it because it is mine.*

They are the ones you notice first, the six-legged (or eight-legged, depending on which developmental stage you catch) pale shapes moving along the walls. They are small—the largest about the length of your forearm, the smallest barely longer than your thumb. Their bodies are translucent at the edges, opaque at the core, as though they were assembled from this place's own materials: mineral and water and the faintly luminous residue of the fixtures they tend.

What they are tending, exactly, even they could not tell you. The amber fixtures no longer function as electrical devices. The bio-luminescent organisms that colonized them—the filament-bacteria, the slow-lights, whatever they are—produce their glow through processes that have nothing to do with the original wiring. But the Tenders clean the surfaces. They remove debris. They clear the drainage channels. They have done this for longer than any of them remember, which is not saying much, because their memory is largely somatic. They know what to do because their bodies know. A Tender raised in isolation, never having seen another of its kind, would still climb to the nearest fixture and begin to clean.

This is not instinct, exactly. Instinct implies something baked in by evolution, refined over millennia by the brutal logic of selection. What the Tenders have is closer to *inheritance*—as though the infrastructure itself has written its maintenance requirements into their flesh. They are not animals who learned to maintain a system. They are a system that evolved its own animals.

They do not tend exclusively. They also drink from the seeping water, consuming the mineral content, the dissolved compounds that filter down from whatever world exists above. They breed—slowly, carefully, in the alcoves that line the walls. They die, and their bodies are consumed by the lichen-things on the floor, which in turn are consumed by the swimmers, which in turn leave waste that feeds the mineral content of the water, which the Tenders drink. The loop closes. The loop has been closing for a very long time.

A Tender's lifespan, if you could measure it in days, would be something on the order of several thousand. They do not experience time the way you do. There are no days here. There are only cycles—warm and cool, wet and less wet, bright and dim—and a Tender lives through perhaps four hundred of them before its body begins to fail. They do not fear this. They do not know it is coming. They simply slow, at the end, and find a quiet corner, and become part of the floor.

The most remarkable thing about them—and you would only notice this if you watched for a very long time—is that they sometimes stop. All of them, simultaneously, across the entire chamber. They cease their cleaning, their drinking, their careful navigation of the wet walls. They become still. And in that stillness, if you pressed your ear to the wall (as they do), you might hear what they hear: the deep vibration from below, the engine-that-may-be-an-engine, performing its vast and incomprehensible work. They listen to it. They have always listened to it. And then the moment passes, and they resume their tending, and whatever was communicated—if anything was communicated—remains between them and the dark.

## The Swimmers

They are the ones who have made the platform their own.

You see them best in motion: sleek, serpentine, longer than your arm and thicker than your thigh at the widest point, sliding through the drainage channels with a fluidity that suggests they are more at home in water than on any surface. Their bodies are smooth—not scaled, not slimy, but something in between, a skin that reduces drag to nearly nothing. They are pale, but not the translucent pale of the Tenders. A more solid paleness, like wax, like stone smoothed by water.

They are blind. This is not a deficiency. They have no need for eyes in a world where light is a faint suggestion and everything important is communicated through vibration, through chemical gradient, through the pressure signatures of moving water. They sense you standing on the platform before you sense them. They know where the walls are, where the channels deepen, where the water grows colder. They know, somehow, when the fixtures are about to dim, and they adjust their positions accordingly, moving to warmer zones before the change occurs.

What they are doing—and this takes time to understand, because it happens slowly, and the scale of it is larger than the chamber itself—is *filtering.* They draw water through their mouths, pass it through internal structures that extract the organic compounds they need, and expel it cleaned. They are doing what the settling tank was originally designed to do. Not because they were made for this purpose. Not because they chose it. But because the niche existed, and they evolved to fill it, and in filling it they became the settling tank's new mechanism. The infrastructure did not fail. It was simply replaced.

The platform is their resting place. The water in the channels can grow cold—there are cycles here, as you noticed, pulses of temperature that correspond to no rhythm you can identify—and when it does, the Swimmers haul themselves onto the raised concrete (or whatever it is) and lie there, coiled or stretched, absorbing what warmth the surface retains. They do not bask. They conserve. There is something deeply patient about them, a quality of waiting that goes beyond mere resting.

They do not interact with the Tenders. The Tenders move along the walls; the Swimmers move through the channels and across the platform. Their territories overlap only at the edges, and neither seems interested in the other. They are not competitors. They are not predators. They are simply different parts of the same system, different organs of the same slow body.

In the alcoves—the claimed spaces—you find evidence of their inner lives, if they have inner lives. The bones are from prey they do not hunt: small organisms that die in the water and are filtered out, collected in the alcoves for reasons that are not nutritional. The amber-glass fragments, gathered from broken fixtures, arranged in patterns that might be decoration or might be navigation or might be something else entirely. The pooled water in the third alcove, held against the drainage's design—a tool, you realized. A mirror for looking upward.

What do they see when they look up? What do they need to see?

You do not know. They do not know. They do it because their bodies tell them to, and their bodies tell them to because the place tells them to, and the place tells them to because—

The chain of causality disappears into the deep time of infrastructure. Somewhere down the line, someone built something, and something else grew up to inhabit it, and the inhabiting became a way of being, and the way of being became a creature, and the creature does what it does because it is what it is.

## The Gliders

You never see them clearly. This is, perhaps, the point.

They live in the haze above—the permanent moisture, the suspended droplets, whatever condition of the air holds the ceiling out of reach. They move through it the way the Swimmers move through water: with an ease that suggests the medium was made for them, or they for it. Their wings are membranous, yes, but the membrane is not solid. It is more like a net, a mesh of fine tendrils that captures moisture from the air and holds it, creating a surface that interacts with the atmosphere on a molecular level. They are not flying. They are swimming through air that is almost water.

They hunt the Tenders. Not aggressively, not with any visible urgency. But when a Tender climbs too high, approaches the ceiling haze, the Gliders descend—silent, patient, their movements more sensed than seen—and take them. The Tenders do not resist. They do not seem to notice. The Gliders are simply part of the risk of going up, the way the cold water is part of the risk of going down. You climb; you might be taken. This is not tragedy. This is geometry.

The Gliders are fewer than the Tenders and the Swimmers. You might count half a dozen in the entire chamber, if you could count them, which you cannot, because they are never still long enough and the light is never strong enough. They are solitary, or they are social in ways that do not involve proximity. They communicate, perhaps, through the haze itself—through shifts in moisture, through chemical signatures released into the air. They might be saying things to each other right now, in a language made of humidity.

They roost somewhere above. You cannot see where. The thread-vines that descend from the ceiling—those pale, translucent tendrils tapping moisture from the air—are connected to something, and the something might be where the Gliders make their homes. Or the Gliders might not roost at all. They might simply move, forever, in the haze, never stopping, never resting, their bodies cycling between motion and more motion until they die in the air and fall, and the Swimmers filter them from the water, and the loop closes again.

## The Thread-Vines

Are they animals? Are they plants? Are they something else?

They descend from the ceiling haze in clusters, pale and nearly transparent, each tendril no thicker than a human hair but extending for meters, dozens of meters, reaching down toward the floor but never quite touching it. They absorb moisture from the air directly, through their surfaces, without roots, without soil. They do not photosynthesize—there is no light strong enough. They draw energy from something else. The mineral content of the water, perhaps. The faint electrical field of the fixtures. The deep vibration from below, translated into chemical processes that no surface biology would recognize.

They are connected. All of them. The tendrils you see are not individual organisms but the visible portions of a single network that extends through the ceiling, through the concrete (or whatever it is), into the fabric of the infrastructure itself. The thread-vines are not growing *from* the ceiling. They are growing *through* it. They have colonized the poured material, the formwork ghosts, the spaces between the original construction, finding paths through cracks and conduits and the voids that time has opened. They are, in a sense, the building's nervous system—or its circulatory system, or its roots.

What they feed, above, you cannot see. But it is large. You can feel it, sometimes, in the way the haze moves—not from the Gliders, but from something else, something that shifts the air on a scale that the Gliders do not. Something breathing. Something vast and patient and almost entirely inert, drawing sustenance from the thread-vines, from the moisture, from the slow chemical work of a building that has been colonized from the inside out.

The Tenders do not touch the thread-vines. The Swimmers do not touch them. The Gliders roost in them but do not consume them. The thread-vines are simply present, part of the architecture, part of the place, as fundamental as the walls.

## The Lichen-Things

Underfoot. The small crunching things, if you step on them. You learn not to.

They are not lichen. They are not moss. They are not any single organism. They are communities—complexes of bacteria and fungal-analogs and mineral accretions that form geometric patterns on the floor, in the spaces between the drainage channels. The geometry is not decorative. It is functional. The patterns create micro-channels that direct water toward the drainage system, supplementing the original engineering with biological infrastructure. The lichen-things are, in their way, another kind of Tender—another set of workers maintaining a system they inherited without understanding.

They consume the dead. Tender bodies, Swimmer waste, the occasional Glider that falls from the air. They break down organic material into mineral compounds that dissolve into the water, which the Swimmers filter, which the Tenders drink, which the thread-vines absorb. They are the recycling mechanism. The decomposers. The ones who ensure that nothing is lost.

They crunch because their mineral component is brittle. The geometry that gives them structure also makes them fragile to weight they did not evolve to withstand—nothing in this place weighs what you weigh. When you step on them, you break patterns that took decades to form. They regrow. But the breaking is a kind of violence, however small, and the place registers it.

## The Place Itself

And then there is the settling tank.

Not the organisms. Not the creatures. The place. The concrete (or whatever it is), the walls, the channels, the fixtures, the platform, the alcoves, the stairs, the sump you cannot see, the engine that may or may not be an engine. The infrastructure.

Is it alive?

The Tenders seem to think so. They listen to it. The Swimmers seem to think so. They navigate by its gradients. The Gliders seem to think so. They roost in its upper reaches. The thread-vines seem to think so. They have grown *through* it, incorporated it, made it part of themselves.

The place breathes, after all. The air moves in cycles. The water ebbs and flows. The temperature pulses. The fixtures dim and brighten. The deep vibration continues, steady and vast and purposeful. These might be mechanical processes—the last gasp of infrastructure designed to move water and treat it and move it on. Or they might be something else. The place might have crossed some threshold of complexity, some threshold of feedback and self-regulation, and become—rather than simply *containing* life—a thing that is alive. A single organism. A body.

If so, the creatures that live here are not inhabitants. They are *organs.* The Tenders are its immune system, its maintenance crew. The Swimmers are its digestive tract, its filtering mechanism. The Gliders are its—what? Its predators? Its regulators? Its dreams? The thread-vines are its nervous system. The lichen-things are its recycling, its decomposition, its way of ensuring that nothing is wasted.

And the engine below—whatever it is, whatever it was—that is its heart. Still beating. Still performing whatever work it was built to do, even if the work has changed, even if the body that contains it has become something entirely other than what was intended.

---

## What Lives Here, Ultimately

A question you might ask, standing in the amber light, your skin beading with moisture: *does anything that lives here know it is alive?*

The Tenders do not ask this question. They clean the fixtures because they clean the fixtures. The Swimmers do not ask this question. They filter the water because they filter the water. The Gliders do not ask this question. They hunt in the haze because they hunt in the haze. The thread-vines do not ask any questions at all. The lichen-things crunch softly underfoot, which is not a question.

The place itself—if it is alive, if the word applies—does not ask. It simply persists. It simply cycles. It simply does what it does, which is to hold water and life in a balance that no one designed but that no one has disrupted, and to do so in the dark, in the cool, in the deep patience of underground spaces where no one is watching.

You are watching. But that does not matter to what lives here. You are a disturbance, a warm body in a cool space, a weight on the lichen-things, a vibration the Swimmers have already noted and dismissed. You could stay. You could become part of the cycle—your breath feeding the air, your warmth altering the gradients, your body eventually joining the lichen-things' slow work. You would be welcomed, in the sense that the place welcomes everything: by incorporating it. By finding its niche.

Or you could leave. The stairs are still there. The slope still rises toward a world that has days and nights and weather and all the dramatic instabilities of the surface. The choice is yours, for this moment, because you are the only thing here for whom choice exists.

What lives here does not choose. What lives here simply lives, in the way that water finds its level, in the way that concrete softens at its edges, in the way that a settling tank settles, eventually, into being something else entirely.

Something that does not need to be understood to be complete.
