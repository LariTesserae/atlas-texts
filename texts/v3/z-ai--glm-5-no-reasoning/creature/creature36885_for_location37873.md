# What Lives in the Hollow of Sounding

---

## The Listeners

There were people here once. That is the plainest fact and the hardest one to hold.

They shaped granite with harder granite. They built walls to focus a sound they could not have understood with language but clearly understood with their bodies, the way you understand the taste of water or the weight of your own limbs. They slept in troughs cut into stone. They caught condensation in channels they carved with their hands. They counted something — the breathing of the void, the long pressure-wave that flexes the floor — by rubbing grooves into the wall above where they lay their heads. They counted for a long time. They stopped counting, or were stopped, three grooves into a new set of five.

What lived here was a listener. Singular, perhaps. The sleeping platforms suggest one, maybe two. Not a family. Not a village. Someone who came to the rim of a place that sings and built a shelter from the stone that was already there and stayed.

Not a hermit. Hermits withdraw from something. This person was not withdrawing. They were *attending*. The distinction matters. The tally marks on the wall are not the record of someone passing time. They are the record of someone measuring something they believed was worth measuring. Someone who thought: *this sound has a rhythm, and the rhythm means something, and I will track it until I understand what.*

They did not understand. Or they did, and the understanding was not the kind that survives in stone.

What lived here was a mind pressed against a mystery, wearing itself smooth the way the water wears the mineral streaks — slowly, in the direction the sound pulls everything, until the grooves in the wall stop and the listener is gone and only the sound remains.

---

## The Bowl Itself

But there is another answer, a harder one, and it sits beneath the first like the second floor of silt beneath the first skin of stone.

What lives in the Hollow of Sounding is the Hollow of Sounding. The place is not where something lives. The place is the thing that is alive.

Consider: the stone is unfinished. It is *becoming*. This is not a metaphor. The rock has not decided what it is. It was pulled downward — not by erosion, not by faulting, not by any force that has a clean geological name. Drawn, as if by a hand from beneath. The floor is a skin stretched across a void, and the skin flexes on a thirty-second cycle, and the flexing is the sound, and the sound is the central fact. The mountain is being made, right now, from the inside out. The void beneath the floor is the space where the making happens. The boom is the sound of creation happening at a speed you can almost perceive.

What lives here is a world being born. A small one. A bowl-sized one. It has a heartbeat — the slow pressure-wave, thirty seconds per cycle, that ripples the water and flexes the stone and orients everything: the mineral streaks, the ferns, the trees, the listener who came to count. It has skin — the floor of stone, thinner than you'd like, sagging slightly, still soft enough to resonate. It has a membrane — the water on the floor, the drumhead, the lens-pool where the sound becomes visible. It has a metabolism — the water cycling endlessly from air to stone to thread to pool to vapor, carrying minerals, depositing silt, building the second floor that will one day meet the first and seal the void and end the resonance and finish the becoming.

It is alive in the way a fetus is alive: not thinking, not knowing, not aware, but *organized* around a single process. The sound arranges everything. The sound is its organizing principle. The sound is the thing that tells the mineral streaks which way to run and the moss which way to grow and the water where to gather and the listener where to build. It is not a will. It is a *direction*. Everything in the bowl faces the center because the center is where the sound comes from, and the sound is the only fact that matters to anything here.

What lives here is a nascent thing. A geology that has not yet completed. An organism of stone and water and vibration that will take three thousand years to finish being born, at which point it will go silent, and still, and dead, and done.

The irony — if a mountain can have irony, if a process can be ironic — is that the living part is the becoming part. When the bowl is finished, when the second floor meets the first and the void is sealed and the sound stops, the Hollow of Sounding will be dead. It will be a geological feature. A depression in high stone. A place where nothing happens and nothing needs to.

Now, though, it is alive. It is the loudest living thing you have ever heard.

## The Small Lives

And around the living mountain, the smaller lives.

The moss is alive in the obvious way — growing, holding water, advancing across stone — but it is alive in a particular way here that it is not elsewhere. It does not hold memory. Step on it, and the print fills and vanishes. It is entirely present-tense, the text says, and that is a kind of life most living things cannot achieve. The moss does not remember being stepped on. The moss does not anticipate being stepped on again. The moss exists in the now of the bowl, which is a now that stretches across millennia but feels, from inside, like a single unbroken moment. The moss is the bowl's skin flora. It lives on the surface of the becoming and it does not know the becoming is happening and it does not need to know. It is the simplest kind of life here, and in its simplicity it is the most complete: it has no past and no future and no anxiety about either.

The ferns are more complex. They have structure — thick dark stalks, waxy leaves, fronds as long as your arm. They grow from crevices in the shelves of stone, angling toward the gray light, toward the center, toward the sound. They do not move because there is no wind, and the lack of wind has made them into something strange: plants that have never experienced disturbance. They have never been bent by a gust. Never been battered by rain. Never had to recover. They grow in the direction the sound pulls them and they do not deviate because deviation is not a concept their existence has encountered. They are the most single-minded living things here. They point at the sound the way sunflowers point at the sun, except the sun moves and the sound does not, and so the ferns have achieved a stillness that is not passivity but *alignment*. They are so perfectly oriented to the central fact of this place that movement would be a kind of error.

The trees on the rim are the oldest living things here besides the bowl itself. Mountain ash, and a birch whose bark has gone the grey of stone — a species trying to become geological, the text says, and that is not a joke. The trees have been here long enough to have gnarled. They grow horizontally, spreading inward over the rim, leaning toward the center as though listening. Their bark has adapted to the color of the rock. Lichen crusts them in yellow-grey and deep orange. They are short because altitude stunts them, but their shortness is not diminishment — it is density. They are as much tree as a taller tree, compressed into less space. They have lived here long enough to begin to resemble the thing they grow from. That is not adaptation in the usual sense. It is *convergence*. The trees are becoming the mountain, and the mountain is becoming itself, and the boundary between the two is blurred by time and moisture and the constant vibration that orients everything toward the center.

The birds. The birds are the warmest things here. Small, dark, unidentifiable, they live in the crevices of the rim, in the gap between moss and stone. They call in sharp single notes — *like stones striking stone*, the text says, and that simile is exact and important. The birds have learned to make a sound that belongs in this place. Their calls are percussion. They are mallet-blows. They are the only sounds here besides the boom, and they are the only sounds that are deliberately made by a living thing. The birds do not sing. They strike. They produce the sound of stone-on-stone, and they do it in a place that is already full of stone-on-stone resonance, and so their calls are not separate from the bowl's sound but a part of it — a higher, sharper, faster layer laid over the deep continuous boom, the way a small chime might sound inside a cathedral while the organ plays.

What do the birds eat? Insects, perhaps, drawn to the moisture. Or the moss itself, or the fern-spores. The text does not say. The birds are the one living thing here whose interior life is not described because they will not let you approach. They are the wildest thing in the bowl, the least known, and their wildness is a kind of honesty: they are the only things here that have not been organized by the sound. They live in the rim, at the edge, in the space between the bowl and the outside world. They are the border. They are the membrane between the becoming-place and the finished world. Their sharp notes are the sound of that membrane flexing.

## The Unanswered

And beneath all of this, literally and figuratively, the void.

What lives in the void beneath the floor? The text does not say. The void is described by its effects — the sound, the flexing, the thirty-second pressure wave — but not by its contents. It is a space. It resonates. It breathes. It pulls the stone downward. It is the source of the becoming.

Is it alive? The question is the wrong question. The void is not a thing. The void is an *absence*, and the absence is active. It is the space where the mountain has not yet arrived. It is the not-yet-made, and the not-yet-made has a voice, and the voice is the boom that has sounded for longer than the moss or the trees or the birds or the listener can measure.

The listener came to count it. The listener built walls to hear it better. The listener slept beside it for long enough to carve hundreds of tally marks into granite. The listener stopped, mid-count, three grooves into a new set of five, and the text does not say why.

What lives in the Hollow of Sounding is the sound, and the sound is the voice of a place being born, and the place being born is the only thing here that will outlast everything else — the moss, the ferns, the trees, the birds, the structures, the marks on the wall, the memory of the person who made them. The sound will outlast you. The sound will outlast the becoming itself, because the sound is the becoming, and when the becoming is finished — when the second floor meets the first and the void is sealed and the mountain is done — the sound will stop, and the place will be silent, and what lives here now will have finished living, and the bowl will be a bowl, and the stone will be stone, and there will be nothing here but geology.

Not yet. Not for three thousand years. The boom continues. The water beads. The silt falls. The birds strike their sharp notes. The moss advances a hair's width across the sounding-chamber walls.

The listener is gone. The listener counted as far as they could and went away or were taken away or simply stopped, the way the counting stopped, mid-group, unfinished. But the thing they were counting — the cycles, the breaths, the waves — continues without them, the way it continued before they arrived, the way it will continue after the silt seals the floor and the sound goes silent and the bowl finally becomes what it has been trying to become since before the air learned to be this wet.

What lives here is the unfinished. What lives here is the in-between. What lives here is the long, low cry of a world still being made, and the small bright percussion of birds at the rim, and the slow green patience of moss that does not know it is patient, and the memory — in stone, in grooves, in the shape of walls — of someone who listened and counted and did not finish and left behind the only record a human can leave in a place like this: the evidence that they were paying attention.
