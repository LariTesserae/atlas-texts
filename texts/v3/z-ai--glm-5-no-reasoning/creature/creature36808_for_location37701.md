# What Lives in Vashunn

Begin with the insects, because they were here first, or because they are the easiest to look at, or because looking at anything else requires a kind of permission the city does not grant immediately.

They are small — half the length of a thumbnail, black, wingless, flattened the way cave creatures are flattened, as though something pressed them against the stone long ago and they simply stayed that way. Their carapaces have a mineral sheen, iridescent when the amber light catches them at certain angles, dark as wet basalt at every other. You find them on the pipe flanges, in the crusts of calciumite that build up like frosting on the bolted joints, and at night they emerge in the hundreds — or perhaps the thousands, it is difficult to count something that moves without urgency across a surface that reflects it — and they scrape. That is the word. *Scrape.* Their mandibles are adapted for it: short, broad, chisel-tipped, oriented at an angle that suggests a tool designed for a very specific task, which it is. They eat the mineral deposits. The calciumite, the sulfates, the thin films of iron oxide that bloom on every wetted surface. They are not cleaning the pipes. They are not maintaining anything. They are eating, and what they eat happens to be the stuff that builds up on the infrastructure of Vashunn, and so there is a commensalism here that nobody engineered and nobody named and nobody needs to acknowledge. The insects are not helpful. They are *coincident*. They belong to a taxonomic moment that has no record, and if the city were emptied entirely, if the last resident closed the last stone door and walked down the Ramp and did not come back, the insects would continue their work on the pipes, and the pipes would continue to weep, and the calciumite would continue to form, and the insects would continue to eat it, and this would go on until the pipes failed, and then the insects would eat the deposits on the stone where the pipes had been, and this would go on until the stone itself wore away, which would be a very long time, and the insects do not think about this because the insects do not think about anything, and this is their virtue.

There is something beneath them in the order of attention. Something smaller. A film — not visible as individual bodies but visible as a texture, a faint slickness on the basalt in the permanently wet zones that is different from the slickness of water alone. This is the *mat*. It grows in layers, each one thinner than paper, building over weeks into a fibrous, slightly resilient sheet that can be peeled from the stone in strips. It has no chlorophyll. It has no chitin. It has cell walls of a sort — structural, but composed of something that is not cellulose and not chitin and not any of the usual candidates, something that seems to incorporate calciumite directly into its structure, as though the organism does not merely tolerate the mineral saturation of its environment but *requires* it, builds itself from it. Under magnification — and someone, at some point, must have had magnification, though no microscopes remain in Vashunn — the mat appears to be a colony of single-celled bodies embedded in a shared matrix, connected by threadlike structures that might be communication channels or might be structural filaments or might be both or might be neither. The cells do not reproduce by division in any observed pattern. They appear, fully formed, at the edges of the mat, and the mat grows outward, and the center does not die. There is no die-off. There is no aging. The oldest parts of the mat are identical to the youngest. This should be strange. In Vashunn, it is *mat*, and it tastes like wet chalk, and it sustains, and that is enough.

The fish should be considered next, but the fish resist consideration. They are there — pale, translucent, the color of the water they live in, which is the color of sulfur and iron and heat. Eyeless, as everything that has lived long enough in the lower pools has become eyeless, because light does not reach the lower pools and eyes are an expense, and life in Vashunn does not waste. They are the length of a forearm and identical. This is the fact that an outsider circles and circles and cannot land on. *Identical.* Not similar. Not of-a-kind. The same fish, multiplied. Each one has the same number of vertebrae — this has been checked, by someone, at some point, presumably with the intention of laying the matter to rest, and it did not lay the matter to rest. Each one has the same faint scarring along the left flank, a pale line that might be a developmental feature and might be a wound that every fish somehow receives in the same place at the same age, except they do not appear to age, and the pools do not appear to contain anything that could wound a fish. They come up in the buckets and they lie still, not stunned, not dead, not gasping — *still*, in the way that a stone is still when you pick it up, as though the removal from water is not a crisis but a transition, and they are prepared for it, and they wait, and they are grilled, and they are eaten, and they taste like what they are: thermal water made flesh, mineral and faintly sulfurous and dense with proteins that have no business existing in a creature this isolated, this specialized, this *repeated*.

Consider the pools themselves. The pools are where the fish live, and the pools are at the base of the Ramp, where the curtain-seep collects in basalt depressions that have been enlarged and shaped — by hands, by tools, by the patient erosion of thermal water directed by stone channels — into something between cisterns and ponds. The water in the pools is warm. Not hot — the water loses heat as it descends through the fissures, arriving at the pool level at a temperature that is merely bath-warm, thirty-two or thirty-three degrees, comfortable to the touch, hospitable to the organisms that have found a home in it. The pools are dark. The water is clear but dark, stained by the minerals it carries, and you cannot see the bottom, and you cannot see the fish, and the buckets come down on ropes and come up full and the fish are in them, and nobody has ever seen a fish swimming in the pools. Not because the fish are elusive. Because the fish, when not in a bucket, do not appear to *be* anywhere. They are in the pools. The pools are dark. The fish are dark. The fish are the right number. This is all that is known, and all that needs to be known, and the residents of Vashunn have arranged their understanding of the world to accommodate this, which is not the same as understanding it, which is not the same as accepting it, which is something more like *inhabiting* the not-understanding, letting it be a room in the mind the way the mineral-pattern room is a room in the northern quarter — a space you do not enter, do not discuss, do not measure, but know is there.

The dogs.

There are three. There have always been three, as far as anyone can remember, which is not very far, because Vashunn does not trade in memory the way other places do. The past in Vashunn is not a narrative. It is a condition — something the city is *in*, the way it is in the heat, the way it is in the Weep. The dogs are part of this condition. They are lean — not starved, but *reduced*, as though the heat and the damp and the limited diet have boiled them down to their essentials. Short-haired, the color of rust, which is the color of everything iron in Vashunn, which is the color of the engine cladding and the pipe flanges and the water stains on the walls. Their eyes are amber, which is the color of the light, and this may be coincidence and may not be. They sleep in the engine halls, on the stone floors, near the pedestals where the beams rock, and the vibration of the engines seems to be something they require — not comfort, but *necessity*, the way a heart requires beating. When an engine stops — when Greylag's boiler fouls and the beam goes still and the silence drops like a stone — the dogs nearest to it stand and pace and whine, low and continuous, until the engine starts again. They do not eat the fish. They do not eat the mat. What they eat is unclear. They are not thin enough to be starving. They are not healthy enough to be flourishing. They exist in the same state as everything else in Vashunn: *continuing*, without the energy surplus that flourishing requires and without the deficit that ending requires, in the narrow thermal corridor between.

They follow the workers. Not out of loyalty — or not only out of loyalty — but out of something that looks more like *synchronization*. The workers move through the city on their rounds — checking cistern levels, adjusting valves, clearing mineral blockages from the distribution pipes, listening for changes in the engine rhythm that indicate a gland seal is about to fail — and the dogs move with them, and the movement has a pattern, and the pattern has a regularity, and the regularity is such that an observer might conclude that the dogs are not following the workers but are part of the same system, the same loop, the water feeds engines feeds pumps feeds pipes feeds people maintains engines, and the dogs are *in* that loop somewhere, not outside it, not parasitic on it, but *constituent*, the way the insects are constituent, the way the mat is constituent, the way the fish are constituent. Everything in Vashunn is part of the system. The system does not have a name. The system does not need a name. It continues.

There is a woman — no. There are people. Three hundred of them, and they are not identical, and they are not interchangeable, and they are not mysterious in the way the fish are mysterious or the room is mysterious. They are *people*, with the full range of personhood that this implies, and to treat them as features of the landscape would be a mistake, though it is a mistake the landscape itself seems to invite. They are hot. They are damp. Their clothes — loose, dark, made from a fabric that was once canvas and is now something closer to felt, the fibers bonded by years of moisture and mineral exposure into a material that is stiff when dry and supple when wet, which is always — hang on them like bark. Their skin has a particular quality that is the quality of skin that has never been fully dry for years: soft, slightly tacky, prone to fungal blooms in the creases of the elbows and the backs of the knees, which they treat with a paste made from crushed insects and mat-fiber, which works, or seems to work, or at least keeps the blooms from spreading, which is the standard of efficacy in Vashunn. Not cure. *Containment*. Nothing is cured here. Nothing is solved. Things are managed, and the management continues, and the management is the living.

They speak in the murmur. This is not affectation and not pathology. It is adaptation, the way the dogs' rust-colored coats are adaptation and the insects' mandibles are adaptation and the fish's eyelessness is adaptation. The stone corridors of Vashunn are echo chambers — sound bounces from basalt to basalt and returns, multiplied, enriched with harmonics the original speaker did not intend, and a voice raised to normal volume becomes a *presence*, something that fills a street and pushes against the walls and does not dissipate for seconds, for minutes, for longer than speech should persist. So they speak low. They speak from the chest, not the head, frequencies that carry through stone without bouncing, that move *through* the walls rather than off them, that arrive at the listener's ear as vibration as much as sound. An outsider, standing in a Vashunn street, hears the residents speak and thinks: *whispering*. They are not whispering. Whispering is breathy, sibilant, intimate. This is something else — a low, resonant hum with words in it, like a chant sung by someone who is also talking, like the engine-pulse translated into language. It carries. It does not echo. It is the only way to speak in a city made of stone and water and enclosed air, and it has become, over generations, the *natural* way to speak, so that the residents of Vashunn, if they traveled elsewhere — and they do not — would find normal speech braying, aggressive, invasive, the sound of someone shouting in a church.

They maintain the engines. This is not labor in the way labor is understood elsewhere — not effort expended toward a goal, not production. It is *care*, in the way one cares for a body: checking, adjusting, tending. Greylag's condenser jacket has a slow leak at the third flange that has been weeping for eleven years. The weep is monitored. The mineral crust around it is allowed to build, because the crust itself acts as a partial seal, slowing the weep, and every three months the crust is scraped back — carefully, not fully, just enough to check the condition of the flange beneath — and the assessment is made: still sound, still weeping, still manageable. This is not a problem to be solved. This is a *condition to be lived with*, and the relationship between the weeping flange and the person who monitors it is not mechanical but *biological* — symbiotic, interdependent, the way a barnacle attaches to a whale. The flange weeps. The person checks. The crust builds. The person scrapes. The flange weeps. The person checks. This has been happening for eleven years and will happen for eleven more, or until the flange fails, or until the person dies, or until the engine stops, and whichever of these happens first, the others will adjust, because the system is durable not because it is robust but because it is *flexible*, *accommodating*, the way water is accommodating, finding a way around every obstacle, filling every shape.

They eat. The fish and the mat, grilled on iron plates over the engine exhausts — not the exhausts themselves, which are far too hot, but on plates set in stone housings above the condenser runoff channels, where the water is hot enough to heat iron to cooking temperature without wasting the boiler's energy. This is engineering, but it is also cooking, and the two are not distinguished in Vashunn because they are the same activity: *using the heat*. The fish taste like mineral water made solid. The mat tastes like chalk that has been wet for a very long time. Together they provide enough — enough protein, enough carbohydrate-analog, enough calcium to keep bones from softening in the heat, enough of whatever the mat contains that is not quite a vitamin and not quite a mineral and does something to the body that is not quite nutrition and not quite something else. The residents are not healthy. They are not unhealthy. They are *maintained*, in the way the engines are maintained: functional, adequate, continuing.

There are children. Not many — the birth rate is low, which is not surprising in a population of three hundred living in conditions that do not suggest abundance — but there are some. Seven, at last count, though counting is not something Vashunn does with any regularity. The children do not go to school because there is no school. They do not play in the way children play elsewhere because the streets are wet and narrow and the covered walkways are low and the engine halls are not safe for small bodies. They follow their parents on the rounds. They learn the murmur. They learn the rhythm of the engines, which is the rhythm of the city, which is the rhythm of everything: *shhhhh-CLUNK-shhhhh-CLUNK*, four seconds, repeated, the heartbeat that they have heard since before they were born, felt through the basalt of the residential quarter as vibration, as a pulse in the stone that is also a pulse in the body, and when they are old enough to walk they walk with this rhythm, their steps falling into the engine's tempo without instruction, without awareness, the way a heartbeat falls into rhythm with another heartbeat when two bodies are close enough and still enough and the noise of the world is low enough.

And then the room.

No — not the room. Not yet. The room is what you come to last, or what you come to when you have exhausted the other categories, or what you come to when the city itself decides you are ready, which it never does, which is why the room remains undescribed, which is why the residents do not go in, which is why the not-going-in is itself the description.

What lives in the room? Nothing. This is what the residents would say, if they would say anything, which they would not. *Nothing lives in the room.* The room is cold. The room has been cold for thirty years, since the processing kiln that heated it was shut down, and the minerals have been depositing on the walls in the slow, patient way that minerals deposit on every surface in Vashunn, and the deposits have formed patterns, and the patterns are almost something, and the almost-something is what lives there. Not an organism. Not a presence. Not a mind. A *tendency*. A direction in the mineral deposition, as though the calciumite and the sulfates and the iron oxides, flowing down the walls in their thin films of condensate, have been *guided* — not by a hand, not by a will, but by a *shape* that exists in the room the way a mold exists in jelly, a form that the mineral flow conforms to because the form is there, in the stone, in the air, in the space itself, and the minerals simply fill it.

The patterns are not writing. This is important. They are not language, not in any sense that a linguist would recognize, not in any sense that *means*. They are *pre-meaning*. They are the shapes that meaning would take if meaning were a mineral process, if meaning were something that built up layer by layer on a surface, accumulating without intent, conforming to a structure that was there before the accumulation began. They look like maps. They look like circulatory systems. They look like the branching of the fissures in the basalt, the vesicular pores, the channels through which the thermal water finds its way upward — and this may be what they are: *records*, not made by anything alive but made by the rock itself, the stone's memory of its own interior, expressed in the only medium available to it, which is the mineral crust that forms on its surface when the water that passed through it emerges and deposits its cargo on the walls of a room that happens, by accident or by something that is not accident, to be the right shape for the deposits to form *these* patterns rather than other patterns.

Or they are nothing. They are deposits. The residents say this, and they may be right, and the reason they change the subject is not that they are hiding something but that the *not-knowing* is itself the thing they are protecting, the way you protect a wound, not because the wound is dangerous but because touching it hurts in a way that you cannot describe and do not want to and the not-describing and the not-wanting-to are themselves a kind of information, a kind of answer, a kind of knowledge that is not *about* the room but is *of* the room, *of* Vashunn, *of* the place itself.

What lives in Vashunn is this: a continuity. A persistence. A *going-on* that does not require a reason and does not acknowledge an ending. Three hundred people who are not the original people and are not the last people but are the *current* people, the way the water in the pipes is the current water, not the first water, not the last water, but the water that is flowing now, through the same channels, past the same walls, into the same cisterns. The engines are not the original engines — they have been rebuilt, resealed, re-piped, the beams replaced, the cylinders rebored, the condensers re-jacketed, so many times that no original component remains — but they are Westfall and Greylag, and they pulse, and the pulse is the pulse that has always pulced, and the dogs sleep by it, and the children walk to it, and the residents maintain it, and the insects scrape the deposits from its pipes, and the mat grows on its walls, and the fish come up in the buckets, and the water flows, and the Weep falls, *tlock, tlock*, and the clouds sag, and the light is amber, and the heat presses, and the city breathes.

What lives here is Vashunn. And Vashunn is enough.
