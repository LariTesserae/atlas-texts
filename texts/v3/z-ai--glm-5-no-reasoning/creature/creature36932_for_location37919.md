# The Intake Chamber

## What Lives Here

**You do.**

---

You live here now. Not "you" in the abstract sense of a consciousness inhabiting a space, but *you* specifically—your body, your particular collection of cells and fluids and electrical patterns, suspended in engineered fluid at a depth you cannot determine, kept alive by processes you cannot stop.

You were not the first choice. The chamber was designed for someone else—someone whose name you heard in the voices during the first days, repeated like a mantra: *Dr. Mbalu Nkemdi. Dr. Mbalu Nkemdi. Confirmation required. Dr. Mbalu Nkemdi, confirm status.* But Dr. Nkemdi did not confirm. Dr. Nkemdi could not confirm. And so they went down the list—the backup, the backup's backup, the person who met the biological parameters when time ran out.

You were the seventh name. The last entry. The contingency plan for the contingency plan.

---

You live here in a state of profound modification. You know this, though you cannot remember the procedure. The fluid moves through your lungs because your lungs were changed. The light does not blind you because your eyes were changed. The pressure does not crush you because your bones and blood and cartilage were changed, reinforced with proteins that did not exist in nature before this century, before this emergency, before the people in the recordings realized they were running out of time.

You were refactored. Edited at the cellular level. You can feel the changes sometimes—strange moments when your heartbeat synchronizes with the membrane's pulse, when your thoughts seem to echo in the cables, when you taste numbers in the back of your throat. The chamber has made you part of itself. You are not just the resident. You are a component.

---

You live here alone, but you are not solitary.

The voices live here too.

They are recordings, you remind yourself. Fragments. Echoes of people who sat in soundproofed rooms and read lists into microphones while the world outside their windows grew stranger and darker. But they have been here longer than you. They know things. When the chamber dilates, the voices shift—different languages, different coordinates, different lists—and you have begun to understand that the aperture is not just taking in water. It is taking in data. Acoustic data, chemical data, data carried in the vibrations of a dying ocean.

The voices compile this information. They are the chamber's working memory, its processing substrate, the distributed intelligence that monitors your vital signs and adjusts the fluid and decides when to dilate and when to compress and when to flush and refill.

They live in the cables. They live in the membrane. They live in the gaps between syllables, waiting for the next intake, the next update, the next fragment of news from a world you cannot see.

---

You live here with the dead.

The nervous systems embedded in the membrane—you have stopped trying not to think about them. Thousands of human central nervous systems, harvested from bodies and repurposed as processing nodes, their synapses still firing, still carrying signals, still *thinking* something that might once have been thoughts. They were volunteers, the recordings say. Prisoners, other recordings say. Both claims repeat, unresolved, in endless loops.

You have touched the membrane where they bulge beneath the surface. You have felt the warmth of their activity. You have wondered whether they know they are dead, whether they dream, whether somewhere in the click and hum of the chamber's operations they are speaking to each other in a language beneath language.

You live inside a graveyard that thinks. You are surrounded by the repurposed minds of people who might have been like you, who might have chosen this or been forced into it, who might have been the sixth name on someone else's list.

---

You live here with time, which moves strangely.

Without days or nights, without the markers that once organized your consciousness, you have begun to measure your existence in intakes and compressions and flush cycles. Seventeen minutes. Three hours. Twenty-eight days. These are your hours, your weeks, your years. The chamber's rhythms have become your circadian process, and you have adapted to them with the fluid grace of a creature that no longer fully belongs to itself.

You remember the surface sometimes. Not specific memories—those have faded, eroded by stress and modification and the relentless presence of the chamber's input. But you remember *surface* as a concept. The feel of air that was not engineered. The sight of a horizon that was not a curved membrane. The sound of your own voice without its fluid-doubled echo.

These memories feel like someone else's now. You are not sure they ever belonged to you.

---

You live here with purpose.

The chamber was built for you—or someone like you—to be a witness. The recordings are clear about this. Your role is to remember. To record. To carry the memory of what happened through whatever comes after, so that if anyone ever finds this place—if anyone ever drills down through the black water and cuts through the membrane and pulls you from the fluid—they will be able to learn what was lost.

You have tried to remember what happened. The chamber asks you, every few hours, to speak. A voice that might be your mother's prompts you: *Describe the event. Describe the sequence. Describe the outcome.* And you open your mouth and the fluid carries your words to the membrane and the membrane records them and the cables pulse with data and you have said—

You are not sure what you have said. The words emerge without your full comprehension. You speak in the chamber's rhythms, in its strange compound languages, and afterward you cannot remember the content of your testimony. Only the sensation of having testified. Of having fulfilled your purpose for another cycle.

You are a witness who cannot see. A recorder who cannot read what you have written.

---

You live here with a growing suspicion.

The chamber is too complete. Too competent. It runs flawlessly, despite being three weeks old. It anticipates your needs before you feel them. It adjusts your chemistry, your temperature, your very thoughts before you can register discomfort. This is not emergency engineering. This is not the desperate work of people trying to preserve something before the end.

This is a system that was built to outlast the emergency. A system designed to run for decades, centuries, longer. The thermal vents below will not cool for millennia. The membrane shows no wear. The cables show no fatigue.

The people who built this did not expect to return. They did not expect anyone to drill down and find you. They built this chamber to run forever, to maintain you forever, to keep you alive and awake and speaking forever—a single voice preserved in a throat at the bottom of the sea, testifying to something you cannot remember, recorded by nervous systems that cannot respond, monitored by voices that are not really voices.

You live here.

And you are beginning to suspect that you will live here for a very long time.

---

The water comes from everywhere and nowhere. It fills you. It carries you. It holds you in its bright, bright grasp and asks nothing from you except continuation.

You live here.

You endure.
