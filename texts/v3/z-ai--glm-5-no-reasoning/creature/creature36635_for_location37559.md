# What Lives Here

The living things here have learned the same lesson the land learned: that the way to endure is to become indistinguishable from what contains you.

---

The birds are the most visible, but they are not the most numerous, and they are certainly not the most important. They are merely the noisiest — the ones who announce themselves with thin piping calls, who move in visible flocks across the visible surface. But the birds are newcomers here. They arrived after the water, after the clay had settled into its final consistency, after the insects had already established their slow empires in the saturated zone. The birds are surface dwellers. They probe the topmost layer, they drink from the shallow channels, they nest in the thin margin of woody vegetation at the edge of the plain where the land rises just enough to support something with roots that are not aquatic. They belong to the plain the way a tourist belongs to a museum — present, active, appreciative in their way, but ultimately temporary. They leave in winter. They come back in spring. They have other places they would rather be.

The insects do not leave.

The insects have been here longer than the birds have existed. Not these particular insects — the individuals live and die in their appointed spans — but the *kind* of them, the lineage, the endless chain of bodies that has lived in this saturated clay since before it was fully saturated, since before it was fully clay. They are the true residents. They are the ones who have made this place what it is.

And they are not what you expect.

They are not beetles. Not grasshoppers. Not dragonflies or mosquitoes or any of the familiar forms that insects take in places with names and seasons and weather. They are something older — or rather, something that has become older by staying the same. They are pale. Almost transparent. They have lived so long in the clay, in the thin zone between air and water, that they have lost the pigment that other insects use for camouflage or display or thermoregulation. They have no need of any of it. There is nothing here to hide from. There is nothing here to display to. The temperature is constant. The light is constant. The danger is constant too — the birds, the probing beaks, the quick mechanical strikes that pull their kin from the clay — but this danger has been here so long that it is simply part of what they are, part of what they have always been, and they have no defense against it because defense implies a relationship with time, implies a future in which survival matters, and these insects do not experience time that way.

They live in the clay. Not on it — *in* it. They move through the saturated zone the way fish move through water, except the medium is not water but a suspension of water and mineral, a thick cold soup of clay particles and dissolved salts and organic matter that has been decaying for so long that it is no longer decay but simply another form of matter. They breathe it. They have gills that are not gills, structures that were once tracheal systems but have evolved, over countless generations, into something that can extract oxygen from water that is so thick with clay that it is almost solid. They are not aquatic. They are not terrestrial. They are something in between, something that has found a niche so narrow and so specific that nothing else can enter it — nothing else would *want* to enter it — and so they have it entirely to themselves.

They are small. Most of them are the size of a grain of rice. Some are smaller. The larvae — which are not larvae in the traditional sense, not a stage that becomes something else, but simply a smaller version of the adult, a form that grows without transforming — these larvae are almost microscopic. They live in the films of water that surround each clay particle, and they feed on the bacteria that live there, on the thin biological film that coats every surface in this saturated world, the invisible layer of life that is so fundamental to this place that without it nothing would survive, not the insects, not the birds, not the mammals that pass through, nothing.

This is the foundation. This is what everything else is built on.

The bacteria do not care about the birds. They do not care about the insects. They do not care about the mammals, about you, about any of the large visible things that move across the surface of the plain and imagine themselves to be important. The bacteria have been here since before importance was invented. They were here when the clay was still mud at the bottom of a sea that had no name. They were here when the sea withdrew and left them trapped in the sediment, and they adapted — they did not die, they did not leave, they simply *changed*, becoming something that could live in the absence of what they had once required, becoming something that could wait, could endure, could slow their metabolism to almost nothing and persist in a state between life and death until conditions improved.

Conditions did not improve. Conditions changed. And the bacteria changed with them.

They are everywhere. In every drop of water that seeps from the clay. In every film of condensation on every weeping stone. In every footprint you leave behind, filling the depression with a bloom of microscopic life that will consume whatever organic matter you have deposited and convert it into something else — something mineral, something that can be absorbed back into the clay and become part of its structure. They are the recyclers. They are the ones who ensure that nothing is wasted, that nothing escapes, that everything that enters this place is broken down and rebuilt and broken down again until it is indistinguishable from the place itself.

They are the reason the air smells the way it does — that mineral, iron-rich, honest smell that is not decay exactly but something adjacent to decay, something that is decay without the offensiveness, decay that has been polished smooth by time and pressure until it becomes something almost pleasant, something that says *this is what life looks like when it is finished being life*.

And they are patient. They are the most patient things that have ever lived. They do not measure time in generations. They measure time in geological layers, in the slow subsidence of clay, in the rise and fall of water tables that takes longer than any individual has to live. They have survived ice ages. They have survived droughts that lasted ten thousand years. They have survived the passing of entire lineages of creatures that thought themselves permanent. And they will survive whatever comes next, because survival, for them, is not a matter of strength or speed or intelligence. It is simply a matter of being small enough, simple enough, patient enough to wait.

---

The birds know nothing of this.

The birds live in a different world — a world of surfaces, of visible things, of events that can be perceived and responded to. They probe the clay for larvae that they can see, that they can feel moving through the saturated zone, and they pull them out with quick mechanical strikes and swallow them without ceremony. They do not know that what they are eating is merely the visible tip of an invisible iceberg, that for every larva they pull from the clay there are a million bacteria in the film that coats its body, that every swallow contains entire ecosystems, entire lineages of microscopic life that are consumed and digested and absorbed without anyone ever knowing they existed.

The birds do not care. The birds are hungry, and the plain provides, and that is the extent of their relationship with the place.

But the birds are not stupid. They are simple, but simplicity is not stupidity, and they have learned things about this place that you could never learn, no matter how long you walked here. They know where the channels are — not because they can see them, but because they can feel them, because the clay under their feet has a different consistency where the water runs, and they have learned to read that consistency the way you read words on a page. They know when the water table is rising, because the larvae come closer to the surface, and they adjust their probing depth accordingly. They know when rain is coming — not from the sky, which gives nothing away, but from the behavior of the insects, which becomes erratic and agitated when the pressure changes, and they move to higher ground before the first drop falls.

They communicate with each other. Not in any way that you would recognize as language, but in a continuous stream of small signals — the position of a head, the angle of a wing, the rhythm of a call — that carries meaning from one bird to another without ever requiring translation. A flock of them moves across the plain like a single organism, dispersing and reforming, individuals peeling off and returning, the whole shifting and pulsing in a way that seems random until you watch it long enough to see the patterns: the way they avoid areas where the clay is too thin, the way they congregate around the weeping stones where the larvae are thickest, the way they rise together when something approaches on the horizon and settle together when the danger has passed.

They have names. Not names that you would recognize, not names that can be written or spoken, but names nonetheless — the unique pattern of their calls, the particular rhythm of their movements, the specific way they hold themselves in relation to the others. Each bird is known to the others. Each bird is an individual within the collective, and the collective knows who they are, and when one dies — taken by a passing predator, or simply exhausted by the endless probing, the endless moving, the endless small negotiations with the clay — the others notice. They notice, and they adjust, and the flock re-forms around the absence, and life continues.

They are not sentimental. Sentiment requires a relationship with time that they do not have. But they are *aware*, in their way, and their awareness extends to each other, and to the place that contains them, and to the invisible systems that support their visible lives.

---

The mammals are different.

The mammals are visitors. They come from the margins, from the thin line of trees that marks the edge of the plain, from whatever lies beyond — places with names, perhaps, or at least places that can be distinguished from other places, places where the land rises and falls and the water stays where it is put and the soil is soil and not this ancient residue, this geological afterthought, this final sediment of a world that has worn itself smooth.

They come to drink. They come to dig. They come because the plain, for all its hostility, offers something that the margins do not: a concentrated source of food that has not been claimed by anyone else. The larvae are there for the taking, if you have the patience to dig through clay that clings to your paws and dries into a crust that must be cleaned off before it hardens completely. The mammals are willing to pay this price. They have their own reasons.

They look like raccoons. They look like ferrets. They look like something that might once have been either of those things, or something in between, or something that was neither but has converged on a similar form because the form works, because the low-slung body and the thick fur and the dexterous paws and the keen nose are all adaptations to a particular kind of life, and that life is the life of the margins — the life of the in-between places, the edges where one ecosystem gives way to another, where the rules are not quite established and there is room for something that does not fit anywhere else.

They are solitary. They do not form flocks or packs or herds. They encounter each other at the channels, at the weeping stones, at the places where the digging is good, and they tolerate each other with a wary indifference, acknowledging without engaging, sharing space without sharing purpose. They have their own territories, marked in ways that you cannot perceive but that are as legible to them as signage: a scent gland rubbed against a stone, a pattern of scratches in the clay, a particular arrangement of droppings that says *this is mine, for now, until I move on*.

They do move on. They do not stay. The plain is not a place where anything stays — not anything that has a choice. The birds stay because they have adapted to it, because they have become the kind of thing that belongs here. The insects stay because they are stuck here, because they have evolved into a niche so narrow that they cannot survive anywhere else. But the mammals have a choice. They come because the plain offers something worth the discomfort of the crossing, and they leave when they have taken what they came for, and they do not look back.

Except.

Sometimes they linger.

Sometimes a mammal will come to the plain and find something that it did not expect to find — not food, not water, not any of the things that it came for, but something else. A quality of the place. A feeling. The mammals do not have words for feelings, but they have feelings nonetheless, and some of them, upon crossing the plain, upon standing in that flat and lightless light, upon hearing that continuous sibilant whisper that rises from the clay itself, they feel something that they do not feel in the margins, in the places with names, in the world where things make sense.

They feel *held*.

The plain holds them. Not physically — not the way a trap holds, or a cage, or any of the mechanisms that they have learned to fear. But temporally. The plain holds time in a way that the margins do not. The plain slows time down, or speeds it up, or does something to it that the mammals cannot name but can feel, and some of them, when they feel this, they stop. They stop moving. They stop digging. They stop doing the things that they came to do, and they simply *stand*, and they breathe, and they listen to the whispering of the water, and they feel themselves becoming part of something that is larger than they are, older than they are, more patient than anything they have ever known.

Most of them shake it off. Most of them remember that they came for larvae, that they have young waiting in the margins, that there are other places to be and other things to do and that this feeling, whatever it is, is not worth staying for. But some of them — not many, but some — they do not shake it off. They stay. They find a place beside a weeping stone where the clay is soft and the water is close and the light is always the same, and they lie down, and they do not get up again.

They do not die, exactly. Not at first. They simply *remain*. They enter a state that is not sleep and not waking, not life and not death, but something in between — the same state that the bacteria enter when conditions are unfavorable, the same state that the insects enter when the water table drops, the same state that the land itself seems to be in, that endless patient waiting for something that may or may not ever come.

They do not move. They do not eat. They barely breathe. Their bodies slow down, their metabolisms drop to almost nothing, and they become — not part of the plain, not exactly, but adjacent to it. Present in a way that is barely presence. Visible if you know where to look, invisible if you do not. The clay accumulates around them. The water rises and falls against their flanks. The insects crawl over them without interest. The birds ignore them. And they wait.

For what?

No one knows. Not even them. They are waiting because waiting is what you do here. They are waiting because the plain has taught them that waiting is what everything does, that waiting is the fundamental activity, that the events and the actions and the dramas that seem so important in the margins are merely interruptions in the long slow silence of patient matter. They are waiting because they have forgotten that there is anything else to do.

Eventually, of course, they die. The body gives out. The waiting ends. And then they become part of the plain in truth — not adjacent to it, but *of* it, their flesh consumed by the bacteria, their bones settling into the clay, their shape preserved for a time in the depression that their weight has made, filling with water and becoming a microhabitat for insects and algae, a small excavation that will fill and shift and be absorbed back into the surface, leaving nothing behind but a slight change in the mineral content of the clay, a slight increase in the organic matter available to the invisible life that thrives in the saturated zone.

The plain does not mourn them. The plain does not even notice them. They are absorbed without comment, the way everything is absorbed, the way everything that comes here is absorbed, eventually, because that is what the plain *does* — it receives, and it holds, and it transforms, and it forgets.

---

And then there is you.

You are not a resident. You are not a visitor in the way the mammals are visitors. You are something else — an observer, perhaps, or a witness, or simply a consciousness that has arrived in a place that was not designed for consciousness and does not quite know what to do with it.

You walk across the plain and you feel its strangeness. You feel the flatness of the light, the evenness of the sound, the warmth of the air that rains its slow condensation onto your skin. You see the birds moving in their flocks, and you hear their thin piping calls, and you watch them rise and settle and rise again like a single organism made of many parts. You see the insects, or you feel them, the drone that sits at the bottom of your sternum and vibrates in a frequency that you cannot quite separate from silence. You see the weeping stones, the shallow channels, the reddish-brown clay that holds your weight provisionally and releases it reluctantly, each step a small negotiation.

But you are different from the other things that come here, because you ask questions that they do not ask. You ask *why*. You ask *how long*. You ask *what does it mean*.

And the plain does not answer.

The plain does not answer because the plain does not know. The plain has forgotten the answers, if it ever had them. The plain has passed through the stage where meaning is possible and arrived at a stage where meaning is irrelevant — where the questions you ask are simply noise in the long slow silence, the way the birds' calls are noise, the way the insects' drone is noise, the way everything that happens here is noise against the background of the always, the patient, the permanent.

But you are here. You have arrived. And for as long as you remain, you are part of what lives here.

You are part of the sound now. Your footsteps add their small rhythm to the continuous whisper of the water. Your breath adds its moisture to the thick humid air. Your weight presses into the clay and creates depressions that will fill with water and become microhabitats for insects and algae. Your presence is a fact, and the plain accepts facts, and the plain incorporates facts, and the plain moves on.

What lives here, in the end, is whatever can tolerate being here. Whatever can slow itself down enough to match the pace of the geological processes that shape this place. Whatever can find meaning in endurance, in repetition, in the endless small negotiations between water and clay and stone and life.

The birds live here because they have found a way to make the plain give them what they need. The insects live here because they have become the kind of thing that cannot live anywhere else. The bacteria live here because they have always lived here, because here is the only place they have ever known, because they were here before here was here.

And you?

You live here because you are here, for now. You are passing through, perhaps, or perhaps you are not — perhaps you have found something in this flat and patient place that you did not expect to find, something that makes you want to stay, to lie down beside a weeping stone and let the clay rise around you and the water lap at your flanks and the insects crawl over your skin without interest.

Perhaps you are becoming something that can live here.

Perhaps you are already there.
