# The Anchored

They are not swimmers. They do not glide or school or hunt. They exist in the manner of the vegetation—rooted, patient, arranged.

A body has adapted to the pressure rather than resisting it. Their forms are soft in the way deep-water forms are soft: no air bladders that might compress, no rigid structures that might shatter. Cartilage where surface-dwellers have bone. Dense, slow muscle that does not tire because it does not exert. Skin that has become something like the algae itself—dark, absorptive, faintly luminous in patches along the forearms and spine.

They do not breathe water. They do not breathe at all, not in the way the surface understands it. Oxygen enters through the skin, drawn from the dissolved gas the vegetation releases during its bright cycles. When the algae dims, they dim with it. Their own luminescence fades. They rest against the pillars, hands pressed to the cold stone, and wait for the light to return.

---

## The Shape of a Body

Their hands are worth describing first. The hands are the tools of this world, the only tools that remain. Long fingers, webbed halfway, sensitive to vibration and to temperature shifts. The palms have developed ridges—calluses formed over generations of gripping pillar stone, of pulling through kelp forests, of holding fast against currents that would otherwise carry a body into the unlit regions.

The fingers end in something that is not quite a nail. A hardened plate, translucent, used for scraping algae from stone, for prying open the seed-pods of the vine-plants, for tracing the grooves in the basalt that are too worn for eyes to read.

They are not large. Mass is expensive in deep water. They are slight, compact, dense—a body that sinks when it stops moving and stays sunk. They weigh themselves against the stone, wrapping legs around pillar bases, anchoring with hands and feet. To be untethered is to drift, and to drift is to be lost.

Their eyes are small and dark, adapted to the green-black glow. They see in gradients rather than shapes. They read the light the way surface-dwellers read text: patterns of brightness and shadow that tell them where the algae is thickest, where the currents are moving, where another body has passed recently and left a warmth in the water.

They do not see far. Distance is not useful here. The grid extends beyond comprehension; what matters is the nearest pillar, the nearest vine, the nearest patch of luminescence.

## The Way They Live

They are not social. They are not solitary. They are distributed.

The grid shapes everything. Each body occupies a sector—perhaps twenty pillars, perhaps fifty, depending on the density of the vegetation and the strength of the current. They know their sector the way a body knows its own skin: every groove in the stone, every thicket of kelp, every current that moves through at a particular angle during a particular phase of the algae's breathing cycle.

They do not speak. There is no air to carry words, and the water dampens sound into uselessness. They communicate through touch—pressing palms together when they meet at sector boundaries, lingering in contact long enough to share warmth, to share the subtle vibrations that travel through skin and bone.

Sometimes they travel. A body will leave its sector, swimming the slow stroke of the deep places, hands trailing along pillar surfaces. They move from stone to stone, never crossing the open dark between. The journey can take cycles—sleeping wrapped around pillars during the dim phases, moving only when the algae brightens. They arrive at another sector, another body, and they rest together in the kelp, sharing warmth and the thin sustenance of the seed-pods.

They do not stay long. To leave a sector untended is to lose it—the vegetation grows differently without the shaping hands, the algae thickens or thins unpredictably, the stone accumulates sediment in patterns that take cycles to read. So they return. They tend.

---

## The Shape of a Life

A body lives for a long time. There is nothing here to kill it. The predators are long extinct, if they ever existed. Disease requires a density of population that this world does not have. The cold slows the metabolism, stretches the pulse, extends the span of days until a single lifetime becomes difficult to measure.

They do not count. They have no numbers. The concept of "years" has no referent here—no seasons, no orbits, no light and dark except the slow breathing of the algae. They measure in growth cycles: a kelp ribbon reaching a particular length. A vine connecting two pillars that were previously separate. An algae mat thickening enough to change the color of the light from black-green to green-black.

They do not have names. A name requires differentiation, and differentiation requires comparison. They are not the same—they have different growths of algae on their skin, different callus patterns on their hands, different sectors they know as intimately as their own bodies—but they do not have the language to say how. When they press palms together, they share what cannot be spoken: the particular quality of a pillar's cold, the taste of a current that has traveled from below, the memory of a journey taken cycles ago.

---

## The Work

They are not builders. They are not gardeners. They are tenders.

The work is difficult to describe because it is so slow. A hand pressed to a pillar, feeling the growth of algae beneath the palm. A finger tracing a groove, clearing sediment, exposing the stone to the water. A body pulling a vine toward a particular pillar, wrapping it around the basalt, leaving it to grow into the shape that was intended.

They do not know who intended it. They do not know if intention was involved. They know that the vegetation grows in certain ways when tended and in other ways when left alone, and they prefer the tended growth. It produces more seed-pods. It creates zones of calm water. It maintains the channels that allow travel between sectors without crossing the open dark.

Sometimes they find things. A piece of stone that is not a pillar—a fragment, carved with marks too worn to read. A formation in the sediment that suggests a shape, a purpose, a hand that was here before. They do not collect these things. They leave them where they lie. To move them would be to disturb the pattern, and the pattern is all they have.

---

## The Question of Origin

They do not remember the builders.

This is not loss. This is not grief. They do not know that there were builders, do not conceive of a time when the pillars were not, when the water moved through an unbuilt place. The stone has always been here. The algae has always grown on it. The hands have always tended it.

They have no myth of creation. They have no myth at all. The past is not a story; it is a quality of the stone, a depth in the grooves, a heaviness in the sediment. The future is not a destination; it is a direction of growth, a thickening of the algae, a slow shift in the color of the light.

When they press palms together, they share the only thing that can be shared: the present. The cold of the water now. The taste of the current now. The quality of the light in this cycle, in this sector, in this moment of contact between two bodies that will separate and return to their pillars and wait for the next journey, the next touch, the next brief warmth in the endless cold green dark.

---

## The End State

They will be here until the stone dissolves.

Not these bodies—these bodies will end, will sink into the sediment, will become the mounds at the pillar bases that the algae colonizes. But the hands, the work, the tending: these will continue. A body learns its sector, lives its cycles, and then releases. Another body grows into the space—born from a vine-pod, nurtured in the kelp forests, taught the sector by touch and presence and the slow accumulation of knowing.

They do not fear ending. They do not fear anything. Fear requires the possibility of change, and change here is so slow that it registers only as growth—algal, vegetal, mineral. The water does not change. The stone does not change. The light breathes and dims and breathes again, and the hands move through it, tending, waiting, living in the only way that anything lives here:

Patiently. Completely. Without beginning or end.
