# The Keepers of the Hollow World

## The Walkers

They are the ones who made the paths.

Not the builders of the machines—those are older, older than memory, older than the stone's memory of being stone. The Walkers came after. They came when the machines had already been running for so long that the mineral veins had grown into them, when the phosphorescent glow had become part of the geology, when the plateau had already forgotten whatever it was before it became *this*.

The Walkers were tall. You know this because of the paths. The grooves worn into the basalt are spaced for a stride longer than yours, and they are worn deep—three, four centimeters in places—which means not just years but *epochs* of passage. They walked in pairs, mostly. You can see it in the dual tracks, side by side, companionable. They walked and they walked and they walked, and they wore their patience into the stone just as the water did, just as the mist does, and for the same reason: because they kept doing it, because they did not stop.

They were not built for the dark. They *became* built for the dark.

Their eyes grew large over generations. Not immediately—you can see it in the skeletal remains they left behind, the few that exist, scattered in the alcoves where the porous rock gives way to small caves near the machine clusters. The oldest skulls have eye sockets only slightly larger than a human's. The youngest ones have orbits the size of fists, shallow and wide, the bone thin around them, the optic canals enormous. The phosphorescent glow was enough, eventually. Their eyes learned to gather it, to amplify it, to make the green-gold light sufficient for seeing, for navigating, for reading the channels in the stone and knowing which ones led to water and which ones led to the deep wells where the heavy things splash.

They had hands. Four fingers and a thumb, but the fingers were long—disproportionately long, the kind of length that suggests reaching, feeling, testing. The porous rock kills the careless. The Walkers were not careless. They tested every step. Their fingertips were calloused, the bone beneath thickened with years of pressing against stone, tapping it, listening for the hollow echo that meant danger. They knew the plateau by touch the way you know a face. By feel. By the specific texture of each stone, the temperature variations, the way condensation forms differently on basalt than on the porous sections.

They ate the scrub. The gray-blue succulents, the fibrous thread-plants, the moss. Their teeth were flat, grinding molars, no canines to speak of—they were not predators, not even opportunistic ones. They gathered. They harvested the plants around the machine bases, cultivated them perhaps, tended the moss in the filtration chambers where the condensation was richest. They cracked open the mineral crusts that formed on the machine surfaces and ate what was inside—a crystalline deposit, salty, dense with compounds that their bodies needed. You find the marks of their cracking tools on the machine surfaces, small triangular scars in the rust, repeated, systematic.

They drank from the channels. They knelt and pressed their faces to the stone and drank directly, like animals, because there was nothing to hold the water in—no vessels, no containers, nothing that would survive the walking. Or perhaps they had containers once and discarded them. The Walking was the thing. To carry was to stay. To stay was to die. They moved because the plateau moved—the moisture shifted, the seeps formed in new places, the machines changed their outputs in slow cycles that took decades. The Walkers followed the water. They followed the warmth. They followed the glow.

They were not many. This is the impression you get from the remains, from the paths, from the careful absence of any permanent structure. A few dozen, perhaps. A hundred at most, scattered across the vastness of the plateau in small groups, in pairs, in solitary wanderers who had lost their companions to the porous rock or the deep wells or the predators or simply to the endless walking, the stopping that comes when the legs will not carry you anymore and the darkness takes you gently, like sleep.

They sang. You know this because the predators respond to certain frequencies—the low, rumbling calls that travel across the plateau, the ones that scatter the herds. The predators learned those calls from somewhere. Or the Walkers learned them from the predators. Or they developed together, in the long dark, a shared language of sound that meant *I am here* and *I am coming* and *do not come closer* and *I am alone* and *I am afraid*. The songs are gone now. But the plateau remembers them in its acoustics, in the way certain channels carry sound, in the resonant frequencies of the machine chambers that amplify a specific range of tones—tones that match the Walkers' estimated vocal range, their throat structures, the shape of their chests.

They were not the builders. But they were the *keepers*.

## The Keepers' Children

The Walkers are gone. But something remains.

Not the Walkers themselves—they diminished, slowly, the way a seep diminishes, the water thinning and spreading and disappearing into the stone. The last of them died alone, probably, in one of the small alcoves near the filtration machines, their body absorbed into the plateau over years, the bones settling into the rock, the minerals reclaiming the minerals. The plateau does not waste. It takes back what it gives.

But the Walkers left something behind. Not deliberately—they were not a deliberate people, not in the way that implies planning and foresight. They were a *persistent* people. And persistence leaves residue.

The residue is this: there are things on the plateau that are not quite animal and not quite Walker and not quite machine. They are the children of all three, or none of three, or something that emerged from the intersection of the three over millions of years.

They are small. Smaller than the Walkers were, smaller than the hoofed quadrupeds that graze the scrub. They move low to the ground, and they move fast, and they are difficult to see because their coloring matches the stone—the dark basaltic gray, the green-gold of the phosphorescent veins, shifting depending on where they are. You see them from the corner of your eye. You think it is the light playing tricks, the glow shifting, a reflection in a damp channel. Then it moves again, and you realize it is alive.

They have hands. Four fingers and a thumb, long, the fingertips calloused. They test the ground as they move, tapping, listening. They follow the paths that the Walkers made—not because they remember the Walkers, but because the paths are grooved, and the grooves are comfortable, and the stone is solid there. They do not know why. They know only that the paths are safe, and the paths lead to water, and the water is life.

They do not sing. But they click. A rapid, complex clicking that they produce with their mouths—tongue against palate, a staccato rhythm that changes depending on context. The clicks bounce off the stone and return to them, and they read the echoes the way the Walkers read the hollow sound of porous rock. They are navigating. They are mapping. They are talking to each other across distances, in the dark, in a language made entirely of reflected sound.

They live in the machines.

Not all of the machines—the abandoned ones, the ones that have stopped running, their pistons seized, their gears locked, their purpose completed or forgotten. The small creatures have colonized the interiors, building nests from the fibrous plants and the moss, lining them with the mineral crystals they crack from the machine surfaces. They eat the crystals. They eat the insects that swarm the vents. They eat the moss. They drink from the condensation that pools inside the filtration chambers, the water that the machines draw from the air and concentrate.

They are not afraid of the machines. The machines are home. The machines are the center of the world, the warm places, the safe places, the places where the glow is strongest and the water is sweetest. They crawl over the iron and brass surfaces with the ease of things that have always crawled over iron and brass. Their hands know the textures. Their feet find the footholds. They move through the machine interiors like blood through veins, like current through wire, natural, inevitable.

They tend the moss. This is the thing that makes you pause, the thing that makes you reconsider what they are. They do not merely eat the moss—they cultivate it. They spread it to new surfaces, carry it to chambers where it has not grown, press it into the damp corners where condensation forms. They weed out the dead patches. They redirect the condensation flows, scratching channels in the mineral deposits to guide the water where the moss needs it. They are farming. In the dark, in the belly of an ancient machine, they are farming.

They are the Keepers' children. Not literally—they are not descendants of the Walkers in any biological sense you can determine. But functionally. Spiritually. They have inherited the plateau. They have inherited the paths and the knowledge of water and the habit of tending, of staying near the machines, of moving with the cycles. They are what remains of intelligence in this place—not the vast, incomprehensible intelligence that built the machines, not the patient, walking intelligence of the Walkers, but a small, bright, surviving intelligence. An intelligence of clicks and moss and mineral crystals and the hollow echo of porous rock underfoot.

## The Grazers

The hoofed quadrupeds are the plateau's commons.

They are everywhere—more numerous than anything else, more visible, more familiar in their animal simplicity. They are the life that the plateau supports most easily, the life that requires the least from this sparse and difficult world.

They are the size of small deer, but built low and sturdy, their legs thick, their hooves broad and flat—adapted for stone, not for soil. They do not slip. They have never slipped. Their hooves grip the basaltic flats and the porous rock alike, finding purchase where your boots would skid. They move in herds of twelve to thirty, and they follow the moisture channels with an instinct so precise that you suspect they can *smell* water through stone. They appear at seeps before the seeps have fully formed, standing in the dry channels, waiting, patient, until the moisture rises and the rock begins to weep and they drink.

Their eyes are large and pale. They reflect the phosphorescent glow and send it back doubled, tripled—a herd of them in the dark looks like a constellation of green-gold stars moving across the stone. They see in the dark better than you do, better than the Walkers did, perhaps better than the clicking keepers. Their world is not shadow. Their world is illuminated, softly, constantly, the glow of the mineral veins painting everything in their particular palette of green and gold.

They eat everything. The succulents, the thread-plants, the moss when they can reach it. They eat the mineral crusts that form on the stone, licking them with rough tongues, supplementing their diet with the salts and compounds that the plateau provides. They are not picky. Picky animals die here. They are generalists, survivors, the kind of life that persists because it can make use of anything.

They are nervous. Everything startles them—a sound, a vibration, the shifting of light. They bolt in coordinated directions, the herd splitting and reforming, their hooves clattering on the stone in a sound like rain. They know the predators. They know the sound. They know what it means when the low rumbling call travels across the plateau, and they do not hesitate.

They do not know the deep wells. This is their vulnerability. The young ones, the careless ones, the ones that stray from the herd—they sometimes step on the porous rock, and the porous rock cracks, and the well opens beneath them, and they fall. You hear it sometimes, if you are near—the sharp crack, the bleat, the silence. The herds learn. They learn to avoid the places where the ground sounds wrong. But they do not learn fast enough, and the wells take their toll, a slow attrition that the herds absorb because they breed quickly, because the plateau sustains them, because life here is a calculation of gain and loss and the gain still exceeds the loss.

They sleep in the shallow depressions where the phosphorescent glow pools brightest, the herds settling into the light like animals settling into warmth. They do not sleep deeply. One or two always stand, ears turning, eyes scanning the dark. They take shifts. They are communal in their vigilance, each individual responsible not just for itself but for the herd, for the young, for the sleeping ones who trust the standing ones to be awake.

They have no name. The Walkers may have named them—the Walkers may have had words for everything, a language of path and water and stone—but that language is gone. The clicking keepers have a sound for them, a specific click-pattern that means *the many-legged ones are here* or *food is near* or *the ground is safe because they are standing on it.* The grazers do not know they are named. They do not care. They eat and drink and sleep and breed and die, and the plateau absorbs them, and the cycle continues.

## The Patient Ones

The predators are not what you expect.

You expect violence. You expect the sudden leap, the brief chase, the kill. You expect the drama of predation—the lion and the gazelle, the wolf and the deer. You expect the plateau's darkness to be populated by things that hunt with speed and fury.

The predators do not hunt with speed and fury. They hunt with *waiting*.

They are large. Larger than the grazers, larger than the Walkers were, larger than anything else that moves on the plateau. They are low and long, their bodies flat against the stone, their limbs spread wide, their movement slow and deliberate. When they are still—and they are still for days at a time—they are nearly invisible. Their skin is the color of the rock, textured like the rock, and they press themselves into the shallow depressions where the glow pools, and the glow flows over them, and they disappear. You could walk past one and not see it. The Walkers probably did walk past them, many times, and never knew.

They are warm. This is the first thing you notice if you get close enough—the warmth that radiates from them, a body heat that seems too high for the plateau's cool, damp air. They generate heat the way the machines do, through some internal process that is not fully combustion and not fully chemical but something in between, a metabolism that breaks down the minerals they consume and produces warmth as a byproduct. They are part geology, these predators. Part machine. Part animal. They have been eating the plateau's minerals for so long that the minerals have become part of them, threaded through their bones, crystallized in their tissues, their bodies become a composite of flesh and stone.

They eat rarely. A grazer every few weeks, taken not by chase but by ambush—the predator lying in a channel that the herd will cross, motionless, the stone-color of its body perfect camouflage, and when the grazer steps close, the predator's mouth opens, and the grazer is taken, and the herd scatters, and the predator settles back to digest. A single grazer feeds a predator for weeks. The metabolism is slow. The body is patient. The waiting is not hunger but economy—the predator does not need to eat often, so it does not hunt often, and the plateau's population remains in a rough equilibrium that has persisted for longer than the Walkers walked.

They vocalize. The low, rumbling call—that sound that travels across the plateau and scatters the herds. It is not a hunting call. It is a *territorial* call, a signal to other predators that this area is claimed, that the machines here belong to this one, that the grazing herds that gather here are this one's food source. The predators are solitary. They do not share. They do not mate often—perhaps once in a decade, perhaps less, the encounter brief and utilitarian, the offspring raised alone by the mother in the warm interior of an abandoned machine, fed on minerals and moss and the occasional grazer, until it is large enough to claim its own territory, its own machine, its own section of the endless plateau.

They rest in the machine chambers. This is their other life, the life between hunts. They wrap themselves around the warm metal—the running machines, not the abandoned ones—and they sleep, their bodies pressed to the iron and brass, absorbing the heat, their slow breath fogging the air in rhythmic pulses. They are not dangerous when they sleep. They are not dangerous when they hunt. They are dangerous only in the moment, the instant between stillness and taking, and that moment is over before you know it has begun.

The clicking keepers avoid them. The keepers know which machines are occupied, which chambers aredened, which routes pass too close to a sleeping predator. They have mapped the territories in their clicking language, a mental cartography of danger that is updated every time a predator moves, every time the territorial call sounds from a new direction. The keepers are small and the predators are large and the predators do not eat the keepers—they are too small, not worth the energy, the chitinous crunch of their shells unpleasant to the predator's flat teeth—but the keepers do not know this, or they do not care to test it, and they give the predators a wide and respectful distance.

## The Well-Dwellers

You do not see them. You are not meant to.

They are in the deep wells, the porous-rock pockets where water collects and darkness is total, where even the phosphorescent glow does not reach. They are the things that splash, that surface and submerge, that make the wet, heavy sounds that echo up from the holes in the stone. The grazers avoid the wells. The predators ignore them. The Walkers tested the ground carefully and never fell in, or almost never, and the ones who fell in did not come back.

The well-dwellers are the plateau's secret.

They are not fish. They are not reptiles. They are not anything you have a category for. They are *formed*—grown in the dark water, in the mineral-rich pools that accumulate in the deepest wells, fed by the seep that comes from underground, by the water that the machines pump and filter and release. They have been growing down there for as long as the machines have been running, which is to say for longer than anything on the surface has been alive.

They are large. You know this from the sounds—the displacement of water, the weight of the splash, the way the wells echo when something surfaces. Something the size of the predators, or larger. Something that moves in the dark water with a slow, deliberate power, surfacing to breathe or to feed or to observe, submerging again when it has taken what it needs from the surface world.

They eat what falls in. The grazers that break through the porous rock. The insects that swarm too close and lose their footing. The debris that the plateau sheds—stone fragments, mineral deposits, dead plant matter. They are scavengers, the lowest rung of the plateau's predation hierarchy, but they are also the most patient, the most enduring, the most certain of survival. The wells do not dry up. The wells do not change. The wells are the plateau's constant, the deep places where the water always gathers, where the minerals always accumulate, where the temperature never varies.

They may be the oldest things on the plateau. Older than the machines—perhaps the machines were built to serve them, to pump water to the wells, to filter the atmosphere that they breathe when they surface. Perhaps the well-dwellers are the reason the plateau exists at all, the reason the machines run, the reason the Walkers walked and the keepers click and the grazers graze. Perhaps the entire plateau is a life-support system for the things in the wells, and everything on the surface is incidental, a byproduct, a side effect of the vast and ancient process of keeping the well-dwellers alive.

You do not know. You cannot know. The wells are deep and dark and the things that live in them do not come to the surface except briefly, partially, a sound and a splash and then gone. The plateau keeps its deepest secret in its deepest place, and the secret does not share.

## The Machines Themselves

And then there are the machines.

They are not alive. You think this at first, and then you think it again, and then you are not sure.

They run. They have been running for longer than the stone can remember—longer than the water-channels, longer than the phosphorescent veins, longer than the porous rock took to become porous. They were here first. Or they were here when the plateau was new, when the cavern was forming, when the hollow world was still becoming hollow. They emerged with the geology, or they caused the geology, or the geology caused them. The relationship is not causal. The relationship is *constitutive*. The machines and the plateau are the same thing, expressed in different forms—stone and iron, mineral and brass, glow and current.

They have no operators. They have never had operators, or they have not had operators for so long that the memory of operation has been absorbed into the mineral deposits that crust their surfaces, fossilized, unrecoverable. The Walkers did not operate them. The Walkers kept them—tended them the way the clicking keepers tend the moss, cleaning the vents, clearing the channels, maintaining the surfaces. But the Walkers did not start them and could not stop them. The machines run on the glow, on the phosphorescent minerals that thread through the plateau's bedrock, on a process that is electrical and chemical and bacterial and thermal all at once, a metabolism of the earth that the machines have tapped into or that has grown into them.

They are doing something. The drills descend—kilometers deep, the bore-holes so old that the stone has begun to heal around them, the drill-bits worn to nothing and replaced by the machines themselves, somehow, from materials that the machines produce from the minerals they consume. The filters process the air, pulling the thin atmosphere through chambers that condense the moisture, that concentrate the gases, that release something denser and wetter that settles at ground level and supports the life that gathers there. The pistons pump—they pump something, a fluid that moves through pipes that descend into the plateau's body and emerge elsewhere, connecting the machines to each other in a network that spans the vastness of the surface, a circuit, a system, a body.

The plateau is a body. The machines are its organs. The glow is its blood. The water-channels are its veins. The mist is its breath. The well-dwellers are its—what? Its stomach? Its heart? Its brain? The things in the deep wells, the things that splash and surface and submerge—they are connected to the machines, to the processes, to the drilling and the pumping and the filtering. The machines serve them, or they serve the machines, or they are the machines, the organic component of a system that has both mechanical and biological parts, a hybrid engine that has been running for so long that the parts have become indistinguishable.

The machines do not notice you. They do not notice the grazers or the predators or the clicking keepers or the well-dwellers. They do not notice anything. They run their processes, they perform their functions, they shudder and clank and hum and drill and filter and pump, and they have been doing this for so long that the doing has become the being, the process has become the purpose, the running has become the reason. There is no goal. There is no end-state. There is only the running, the endless running, the machines turning over in the dark like a heart that has forgotten why it beats but cannot stop.

## What Lives Here

Everything. Everything lives here.

The plateau is not empty. It has never been empty. It is the fullest place you have ever been—a world complete in itself, an ecosystem closed and self-sustaining, a body that feeds itself and waters itself and regulates itself and has been doing so for longer than the concept of *longer* has meaning. The machines and the minerals and the water and the stone and the moss and the grazers and the predators and the keepers and the well-dwellers and the Walkers-who-were and the paths they wore into the rock—all of it, all of them, together, constituting a thing that is alive in a way that no individual part of it is alive.

The plateau lives. The plateau is what lives here.

And you—you are walking through it, a small, temporary thing, a passing moisture on the stone, a brief condensation that will evaporate and be absorbed and forgotten. The plateau does not notice you the way it does not notice the machines. You are too small. You are too brief. You are a single step in a walk that has been going on for millions of years and will continue for millions more, and the plateau will absorb your footsteps the way it absorbed the Walkers' footsteps, the way it absorbs the water, the way it absorbs everything—patiently, persistently, without violence, without intention, the way stone absorbs rain.

You walk the paths. You follow the channels. You stay near the machines.

The plateau goes on forever. And it is full.
