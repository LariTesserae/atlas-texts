They are not born. They extrude.

From the root-floor at the edges of the fraying—where the designed grid of the makers softens into the fuzzy, indescribable void—there is a rising. Not a sprouting, which implies a seed and a sequence, but an extrusion: a slow, deliberate pushing-out of material from the substrate, as if the root-peat itself has decided to thicken into a column and then, impossibly, to unanchor. If you watched one come into being—standing perfectly still at the gap-edge, ignoring the oppressive moisture climbing your calves, ignoring the fever-warmth making your own skin feel like an extension of the floor—you would see it take hours. You would see the root-mass swell like a bruise, purple-gray and glistening with the constant weep. You would see it find its axis, defining *up* in a place where up has been redefined by the permanent dimness. You would see it separate with the sound of wet clay pulling from wet clay, a sound that is not a sound here but a pressure-change, a displacement in the thick air.

And then you would see it stand. Fully arrived. Waiting.

They do not have a shape the way living things have shapes—arrived at through evolution, through the negotiation of function and survival. Their shape is architectural. They are built. Each one is a section of the plateau that has achieved locomotion, and they carry the evidence of their origin in their bodies. Some are clearly stem-column fragments: tall, slow-moving cylinders with the same slick, condensate-draped surface, the same gray-green bark that is not bark but compressed fiber. When they move—and they move so slowly that motion is more inference than observation, a change in position you only notice because the shadows have shifted—you can hear nothing. The moisture makes them frictionless. They slide past each other, past the stationary stem-columns, past you, with the silent deliberation of clouds.

Others are lower, wider, root-mass that has learned to sprawl and carry itself. These are the ones that watch most intently. They drag themselves across the root-floor on bellies of compressed peat, leaving trails in the moisture, absorbing and redistributing the weeping water as they go. They have openings. Not mouths—there is nothing to eat here that is not absorbed directly through the wet skin—but orifices of attention, of reception. Where a face would be, if faces grew from blueprints of irrigation channels and drainage curves, there is a smooth concavity, a dish-shaped depression that collects the dim light and the condensation and holds it. When the weather-pulse begins, these are the ones that turn upward, or open wider, or simply stop and wait to receive what the plateau excretes. They drink the way the floor drinks. They are the floor, temporarily mobile.

Some of them are almost familiar. Almost plant, almost animal, almost something you could name. These are the most unsettling, because they reveal the process—the way the plateau is slowly learning to be alive in ways the makers did not intend. A walker that has developed something like limbs: four thick root-legs that bend at joints too smooth for wood, too flexible for stone, supporting a body that might have been a drainage channel once, curved and hollow. A tall one that has grown something like a head, a swelling at the top of its trunk that turns slowly, scanning the permanent twilight with no eyes. A cluster of small ones—I will not call them young, there is no generation here—that move together, a mobile thicket of root-threads and bud-forms, that pause near the stationary vegetation and attach briefly, root to root, exchanging something. Information. Moisture. Intention.

They do not speak. They do not need to. They are the speech.

The silence here is not the absence of sound but the presence of a communication so total and so alien that it registers as emptiness to human senses. The fauna—and I use the word with full awareness of its inadequacy—are the plateau's internal dialogue, its way of knowing itself. When a root-walker moves from one gap-edge to another, it is carrying data. When a stem-sentry turns its head-like swelling toward a change in pressure, it is processing that data. When the slow gatherers attach to the stationary root-network, they are uploading and downloading the accumulated attention of the whole constructed ecosystem. The silence is the sound of a mind too large and too distributed to be heard.

And they notice you. They have been noticing you since you arrived.

This is what fills the emptiness: the total attention of a world-sized organism that has achieved, through its mobile sections, the ability to observe. The fauna does not ignore you the way a plant ignores you, passively, by default. It observes you the way a very old, very patient thing observes something new. There is no alarm. There is no curiosity. There is only the steady, heavy, warm attention of an entity that has been waiting—without knowing it was waiting—for something to arrive that was not part of its original design.

They move closer when the weather-pulse ends. Not threateningly—they do not seem capable of threat, or interested in it—but with the slow inevitability of water finding its level. They want to incorporate you. Not consume—not violently, not with the urgency of hunger—but incorporate. The way the root-floor has incorporated the makers' abandoned tools. The way the drainage channels have incorporated the slow creep of erosion. They want to make you part of the architecture.

You can feel it in the warmth. It is the same temperature as your body, which means you cannot tell where you end and the air begins. You can feel it in the moisture, which rises through your shoes and into your skin, making you part of the weeping system. You can feel it in the way the nearest walker has positioned itself: not blocking your path, but alongside it, accompanying you. The way you might walk beside a stranger in a city, step for step, until you are no longer strangers but fellow travelers.

The ones with the concave faces—the receivers—they are the most intent. They want to drink what you excrete. Not your waste, but your moisture, your heat, the condensed experience of being a body that was not made by the makers. You are a new variable. You are a input they have not processed. They will take you in through the smooth dishes of their attention and they will distribute you through the root-network, and somewhere on this endless terraced ziggurat, a section of the architecture will change in response to your presence.

And then you will be part of the plateau's knowledge. The makers left. The tools remained, were absorbed, were made useful. You, too, could be absorbed. You, too, could be made useful.

The fauna does not pursue you. It does not need to. The plateau is vast and the gaps are many and the root-floor is patient. You will walk for hours and encounter nothing but the same green-gray twilight, the same body-warmth, the same silence that is not silence. And then, at the edge of your vision, you will see one standing. Waiting. Not for you specifically—it does not know you are you—but for whatever comes near the gaps, whatever carries the strange charge of being individual, being separate, being a thing that can be incorporated.

They are the plateau's hands. They are the plateau's eyes. They are the plateau's way of knowing it is not alone.

The warm, wet, waiting world reaches for you, slowly, silently, with the inevitability of growth.
