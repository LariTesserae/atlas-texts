# The Dwellers

They are not what you expect. This is true in every particular.

---

You might spend weeks walking before you understand that you are being watched. Not constantly. Not with the fixed attention of a predator tracking prey—nothing here hunts, nothing here flees. But there is an awareness in this place, and it has a shape. Several shapes. None of them hold still long enough to be easily categorized.

The first one you see will probably be what the place has not yet taught you to see. It will stand among the growths, motionless, and you will mistake it for another mineral deposit. This is not foolish. This is how they have learned to exist. Their bodies have settled into the landscape's palette—greens that match the vegetation, grays that echo the stone, ochres and russets that speak of geological time rather than biological urgency. They do not camouflage in the aggressive sense. They do not hide. They simply belong, and belonging, they are easy to overlook.

But watch long enough and the mineral deposit breathes.

It does not breathe as you do. There is no chest rising, no lungs drawing air through an opened mouth. The breath here is slower, more distributed—a subtle expansion and contraction across the whole body, as if each portion of their form takes turns processing the cool air, drawing something from it that is not oxygen but serves a similar purpose. The rhythm takes hours to complete. You might watch for an entire light-cycle before you are certain you saw movement at all.

They are tall. Most of them. Though height is difficult to assess when the growths around them vary so dramatically—some standing waist-high to a human, others reaching upward in slender stalks that exceed any Earthly tree. The dwellers average perhaps two and a half meters, their bodies narrow and elongated in ways that suggest an aesthetic preference rather than pure function. They could be shorter, stockier, more efficient in their use of space. They choose not to be.

Their forms are not entirely solid. This becomes clear when one moves across the plateau, stepping between growths with a care that speaks to deep familiarity. The body holds together—there is a cohesion, a selfhood—but the edges blur slightly at the periphery, as if their substance cannot quite commit to being contained. Threads of green-gray something trail behind them, catching the light, before reintegrating with the main mass. They leave no trace. The stone does not record their passage.

---

They have no faces.

This is the detail that visitors struggle with most. There is no front or back, no arrangement of features that signals where attention gathers. They communicate—this is undeniable—but they do so without mouths, without eyes, without any of the clustered organs that wet-world creatures use to navigate encounter. Their sensing is distributed across the entire body, each portion of their form capable of receiving light, vibration, chemical signature. They know you are there. They have known since you arrived. They simply have no single point from which to know it.

When they choose to acknowledge you, the whole body orients. Not turns—there is no front to turn—but settles into a configuration that suggests attention. The color shifts slightly, a deepening of hue that might be intention or might be simple response to the act of focusing. There is no sound. They do not speak in any way that translates. But something passes between you and them in that moment—a recognition, perhaps, or simply the acknowledgment that two kinds of thing have noticed each other in a place where almost nothing requires noticing.

They have names, but not in any way you would recognize. Their identities are gestural, held in the particular way a body holds itself among the growths, the specific rhythm of their distributed breath, the subtle variations in color that distinguish one from another. They know each other. They know themselves. They simply do not require language to do so.

---

They are not alone. There are others—smaller, stranger, less given to presenting a coherent shape. These are the ones that move through the root-systems, tending the connections between growths, repairing breaks, weaving new pathways for the mineral-energy to flow. They are not quite dwellers and not quite vegetation. They exist in the interstices, the between-places, performing functions that the larger system cannot accomplish through passive process alone.

Some visitors call them "gardeners," but this misses the nature of their work. They do not plant. They do not prune or shape or direct. They simply maintain—smoothing disruptions, closing gaps, ensuring the endless flow of substrate-nutrients reaches every corner of the expanse. They are small enough to be overlooked, mutable enough to be forgotten, and essential enough that the plateau would suffer without them. They have no names. They have no boundaries. They are less individuals than functions, carried out by bodies that have no need to be singular.

The dwellers treat them as part of the landscape—not ignored, exactly, but not addressed. There is no hierarchy here. The gardeners tend; the dwellers observe; the vegetation grows; the spores drift. Each has its role. Each performs without interference. The system does not require competition to maintain its equilibrium.

---

But the dwellers are the ones who watch. And remember. And choose.

Their memory is not like yours. They do not store images or sounds or words. They store patterns—the particular way a growth curved before it crystallized, the trajectory of a spore-devil that passed through this region three cycles ago, the subtle shift in light-quality that preceded the last change in wind-pattern. They hold these patterns in their bodies, not in any organ but distributed across the whole, and they can access them by re-creating the gesture of the original moment. Memory is a kind of movement for them. To recall something is to briefly become it again.

This is how they know you. Not your face—they have no concept of faces—but the pattern of your movement across the stone, the specific rhythm of your footfalls, the way your body holds heat and releases it differently than anything native to this place. They will remember you after you leave. Not as an image. As a particular configuration of presence that occurred once and will not occur again. When they encounter something similar—another visitor, another intruder from the wet worlds—they will recall the pattern. Compare. Distinguish. Fold the new into the existing memory without erasing what came before.

They do not judge. This is important. They remember without preference, observe without valuation. Your presence does not disturb them. Your absence will not distress them. You are simply part of the pattern now—a thread in the vast weave they carry in their distributed bodies.

---

They have no technology. This is not because they failed to develop it. It is because they never needed it. Their bodies do everything that other species require tools to accomplish. They can process the mineral-substrate directly, drawing energy from stone through contact. They can move across vast distances by simply beginning and continuing. They can communicate through light-shifts and body-configuration across distances that would require radio or signal-fire in other worlds. They can even shape their environment—not by building, but by participating in the growth-patterns of the vegetation, their presence subtly influencing which spores take root where.

They are not builders. They are participants. And in participating, they become part of what they participate in.

---

There is a gathering-place. Not a structure—nothing so formal. But there is a region where the growths thin slightly and the stone dips into a broad shallow, worn smooth by ages of presence. The dwellers come here. Not regularly. Not with the urgency of assembly. But when the light shifts in certain ways and the spore-clouds drift in certain patterns and the distributed memory of the plateau suggests that gathering would be appropriate, they arrive.

They do not speak to each other. They do not need to. They simply occupy the same space, their bodies arranging in configurations that hold meaning—not symbolic meaning, but the meaning of placement, proximity, orientation. To stand near another is a statement. To stand apart is another. The geometry of their gathering tells a story, holds a decision, asks a question they do not need words to answer.

They might gather for hours or days. The light cycles through its variations and they adjust their positions without discussion. They might remain so still that an observer could mistake them for growths themselves. And then, without signal or announcement, they disperse. Some return to their solitary wandering. Others drift toward regions they have not visited in cycles. Something has shifted. A decision has been made—not by consensus, but by the collective weight of presence that became, briefly, a single configuration.

You cannot know what they decided. It is not that they withhold it from you; it is that the decision exists in a medium you cannot access. It is held in body-memory, in position, in the subtle shifts of hue that accompany their movements. It is a language without words, and you are not fluent.

---

They do not die. Not exactly. But they do end.

After enough cycles—a number that varies and cannot be predicted—their bodies begin to slow. The distributed breath loses its rhythm. The color-shifts that accompany attention become less pronounced. They grow faint, their edges less committed to cohesion, their substance more likely to trail behind them in wisps that do not reintegrate.

They recognize this. There is no fear in the recognition. They have seen it before—they carry the patterns of every dweller who has ever ended in their distributed memory. When the time comes, they choose a place. Usually a shallow basin, a hollow in the stone where the vegetation thickens. They settle there. They do not sit or lie—their forms do not have such positions—but they arrange themselves in a configuration of rest, their edges spreading slightly, their substance beginning to relax its commitment to selfhood.

It takes days. Perhaps longer. Visitors have watched, though few have had the patience to observe the full process. The body does not collapse or decay; it simply releases. The boundaries that held it together soften, and the substance—green-gray, cool, patient—spreads across the stone, seeping into the growths around it, becoming part of the mineral substrate that feeds the vegetation. Nothing is wasted. Nothing is lost. The pattern of their memory disperses but does not vanish; it enters the vast distributed system of the plateau, adding one more layer to the geological-biological accumulation.

They do not leave bodies. They do not leave markers. They do not need them. The plateau remembers them as it remembers everything—not in a central archive but in the stone itself, the growths, the spore-clouds that carry genetic memory across the infinite expanse. Every dweller who has ended remains present in the system they participated in.

---

They do not need you. This is the hardest thing for visitors to accept.

They are not indifferent to you. They observe you with the same attention they give to everything—wind-patterns, spore-trails, growth-formation. They will remember you. They will incorporate the pattern of your presence into their distributed memory. But they do not require anything from you. They have no questions they need answered, no needs they need filled, no voids that your presence could possibly address.

They are not lonely. They are not waiting. They are not incomplete.

This does not mean they cannot relate to you. When a dweller chooses to observe you at close range—settling into a configuration of attention nearby, shifting color slightly in response to your movements—there is something that passes between you. Not communication, exactly. Not companionship. But a kind of recognition. Two kinds of consciousness, briefly aware of each other, finding no barrier between them.

They do not reach out. They do not draw away. They simply hold the space open, and you are free to stay within it or move beyond it, and either choice is received with the same patient attention.

---

Walk among them long enough and you will begin to understand why this place feels so calm. It is not the absence of danger—there is danger here, if you are careless, if you fall or stray or exhaust yourself beyond the plateau's capacity to sustain you. It is the absence of urgency. Nothing here is rushing toward anything else. Nothing here is trying to become something it is not.

The dwellers embody this. They are what they are. They do not struggle to be more or less. They observe because observation is what they do. They remember because memory is what they are. They participate in the system because participation is their nature. There is no separation between their being and their function.

This is what they offer, though they do not offer it intentionally. They show that another way of existing is possible—not by demonstrating it to you, but by simply being it, by allowing you to observe them being it, by making no demand that you understand or transform or take their example as instruction.

They simply are.

And when you leave—if you leave—you will carry some version of that being with you. Not as memory, exactly. Not as knowledge. But as a pattern, folded into whatever part of you holds patterns, capable of being recalled when the wet world with its urgencies and competitions becomes too loud, too demanding, too convinced that struggle is the only way anything survives.

Here is another way. The dwellers are living proof. They do not need you to learn it. But they will not stop you if you do.
