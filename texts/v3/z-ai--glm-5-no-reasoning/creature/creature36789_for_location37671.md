# The Hollow and Its Inhabitants

## I. The Deep-Speakers

They work where the heat becomes a physical presence, where the steam-works thunder loud enough to dissolve language into vibration alone. You find them in the lowest levels, in the heat-sweat shops and the stamp mills and the geothermal tap-chambers where the mountain bleeds its ancient warmth. They are the ones who invented silence in a place that has none.

Their sign language is not elegant. It was never meant to be. It grew from necessity the way the breath grows on damp walls—opportunistically, without plan, accumulating gestures the way the city accumulates architecture. A flat palm thrust downward means *pressure dropping*. A fist struck twice against a pipe means *clear the channel*. Fingers drawn across a condensation-slicked surface trace meaning that lasts only seconds before the water erases it, and this temporariness is baked into the grammar: deep-speak is not for recording. It is for *now*. For the immediate negotiation between bodies and machines in a place where delay kills.

They've developed a physiognomy specific to their work. Shoulders thick from hauling pipe sections through corridors never designed for hauling. Hands that stay perpetually reddened from the mineral-heavy water, the calcium and iron staining the skin a permanent rust-brown that the workers wear as identification. Lungs that have breathed steam and stone dust for so many years that the cough becomes a kind of speech, a deep-chested bark that the deep-speakers use to locate each other in the foggy lower chambers the way miners once used canaries.

But their eyes have adapted most remarkably. The orange glow of the pressure-release valves is their daylight. They've developed a kind of night-sight that reads the thermal gradients of a room—where the steam concentrates, where the cooler air pools, where a pipe is about to fail because the heat signature shifts half a degree before the pressure gauge catches it. A deep-speaker can walk into a chamber they've never entered and within minutes identify which joints are stressed, which seals are weeping, which sections of the breath have crept too close to an intake valve. They feel the mountain's moods in their teeth, in the soles of their feet, in that animal part of the brain that registers vibration before conscious thought can name it.

They marry late, if at all. The work selects for a particular temperament—solitary, patient, attuned to pattern. The ones who stay down longest develop a quality the surface-dwellers call *hollow-mindedness*, though it's not emptiness. It's density. They become like the stone they work within: full of something, but impermeable. A deep-speaker who has spent twenty years in the lower levels carries a silence with them even when they come up to the rim for the festivals. They sit at the edge of the crowd and watch the hollow-wing swallows wheel against the thin sky, and their hands move in small unconscious gestures, tracing thermal maps on their knees.

There are perhaps eight thousand of them in the deepest levels at any given time. The work shifts run twelve hours because the machines cannot be left unattended, and the walk up takes two more, so they sleep in the shallow carved niches near the steam-works, in the constant wet warmth, breathing recycled air that tastes of brass and copper and the sweet breath. They dream in vibration. They wake to the sound of their own coughing, which sounds like a message in a language they almost remember.

The deep-speakers have no formal organization. They have something older: the transmission of knowledge through the body, through demonstration, through the accumulation of scarred hands and stained lungs. A new worker is paired with an experienced one for their first year, and during that year they learn nothing that can be written down. The mentor teaches through gesture and example, adjusts the student's hand-signs with physical correction, shows them where to press a palm against a pipe wall to read the pressure through the skin. After a year, the student either stays or leaves. Most leave. The ones who stay have discovered something about themselves that the surface cannot satisfy—a need to be inside things, to feel the structure, to speak in a language that vanishes as soon as it is made.

They are not numerous, and they are not visible, and in the Hollow's three million they register as a rounding error. But without them, the steam-works fail within a week. The cisterns overflow. The drainage channels clog. The heat, which is managed and channeled and converted into useful work, instead finds its own path, and the mountain begins to reclaim what was carved from it. The deep-speakers know this without arrogance. It is simply the structure of the place. They are the structure of the place.

## II. The Rim Families

The cool air at the edge is not a luxury. It is a territory, defended and inherited, passed through bloodlines that trace back to the original excavators and the mathematical precision of their first cuts. The rim families live in the oldest chambers, where the solar alignment still functions, where the carved corridors meet the plateau's edge and the wind strips the moisture from your skin for the first time in hours.

They are thinner than the deep-dwellers. The altitude does this, and the constant wind, and the food that arrives from the lower districts already half-cooled, already stripped of the heavy mineral taste that characterizes everything in the Hollow's middle levels. They eat less because appetite diminishes in the cool. They sleep better because the air is dry enough to allow it.

But they are not soft. The rim families control access. Everything that enters the Hollow enters through them—the long cable railways that climb the plateau's outer face, the supply lifts that bring preserved food and manufactured goods, the water that runs in aqueducts from the plateau's surface down into the carved cisterns. They are the customs house, the first filter, the gatekeepers who decide which goods flow inward and at what rate. This has been their function for a thousand years, and the families that hold the oldest gates—the Sunward Gate, the West Channel, the old North Lift that still runs on its original bronze cables—carry a weight in the Hollow's politics that has nothing to do with elected office and everything to do with the fact that they can, if they choose, close the mouth of the mountain.

The rim is also where the Hollow speaks to the outside, and the rim families are the ones who speak. They maintain the radio stations, the weather monitoring equipment, the communication arrays that keep the city connected to the plateau's other settlements and the lowland cities beyond. They know what rain looks like, not from the seep that touches every surface, but from watching it fall across the open sky from the comfort of a dry chamber carved at the plateau's edge. Their children sometimes leave. This is the quiet shame of the rim families: that comfort produces restlessness, that dry air makes a person hungry for horizons, that the children raised at the Hollow's mouth sometimes look outward instead of inward and do not come back.

Those who stay develop a particular quality of attention. The rim is a place of edges, of transitions, and the people who belong to it learn to read the boundary—where the Hollow's breath meets the plateau's wind, where the hollow-wing swallows turn back at the cloud layer, where the mineral smell of the interior gives way to the clean thin scent of high altitude. They become translators between worlds. They carry messages from the depths to the surface and vice versa, and in carrying them they learn to hold both languages at once: the barking shout of the middle levels and the quiet precision of the rim, the deep-speak of the lower districts and the formal speech of the external settlements.

The rim families are not loved in the Hollow. They are tolerated, because their function is necessary, and envied, because their chambers are dry, and resented, because they decide what enters. The deep-speakers, when they come up for the festivals, look at the rim families' dry clothes and clean hands and feel the mineral sweat evaporating from their own skin, and something passes between them that is not quite hostility and not quite understanding. It is recognition. They are two ends of the same body, the mouth and the bowels, and neither can exist without the other.

## III. The Middle Thousands

Between the depths and the rim, the vast majority of the Hollow's population lives in the middle levels, where the steam-works breathe and the cable cars thread the districts together and the noise is a physical thing, a pressure on the eardrums that never releases. Three million people, minus the deep-speakers and the rim families, minus the engineers and the administrative class—still nearly two and a half million, living in the terraced chambers and the stacked housing and the industrial corridors where the air is wet and warm and the sound never stops.

They are the ones who shout.

The Hollow bark—that characteristic near-shout that begins in childhood, when a child first learns that conversation with a parent requires raising the voice above the mechanical heartbeat of the district—is not rudeness. It is adaptation. In the middle levels, soft speech is lost. Whispers are absorbed by the stone and the condensation and the endless industrial hum. A child who whispers is a child who is not heard, and in the Hollow, not being heard is a kind of erasure. So they learn to bark. Every sentence arrives at full volume, and the residents of the middle levels carry this with them even in the rare moments of quiet—the deep festival nights when the steam-works are shut down for maintenance, and the silence is so complete that the barking voices echo off ancient stone like gunshots, and the speakers do not know how to be quiet because quiet was never offered to them.

The middle levels are where the Hollow's character—accumulation, density, the refusal of anything to leave—most fully expresses itself in the population. The housing blocks here are the densest. A single chamber carved in the classical period, originally designed as a warehouse, now holds fourteen families. They have partitioned it with salvaged metal and cloth and the layered sheets of dried breath that peel from the upper walls and harden into something almost like wood. Each family's space is a cube perhaps three meters on each side, containing sleeping mats, a small heating element, a water spigot tapped from the corridor's drainage channel, and the accumulated possessions of however many generations have lived in that particular niche.

The corridors between these housing blocks are the Hollow's true public spaces. Not because they were designed to be, but because the residents have made them so. Commerce happens in the corridors—the food vendors who set up each morning near the cable-car stations, selling preserved fish from the lowland lakes and the mineral bread baked in the steam-works' waste heat, and the dried breath that some of the older residents still chew as a stomach medicine, and the hollow-wing swallow eggs that are a delicacy and a luxury and a source of constant low-level conflict with the elders who insist the birds must not be disturbed. Children play in the corridors, their shouts joining the mechanical roar as if they belong to the same instrument. The elderly sit in carved alcoves where the air is slightly cooler and watch the traffic pass, and they are the Hollow's true historians because they remember who lived where and when they left and who replaced them and how that new family has changed the partition wall and whether the drainage channel in section seven runs slower than it did thirty years ago.

Work in the middle levels is industrial in the oldest sense. The steam-works require constant maintenance, and the maintenance workers are the middle level's primary labor force. They are not engineers—the engineers are a separate class, trained in the old academies near the rim, speaking the formal language of pressure gradients and thermal efficiency. The maintenance workers are the hands. They tighten seals and replace valves and clear the drainage channels when the mineral deposits build up and scrape the breath from intake surfaces with the long-handled tools they call *tongues*. They work in the wet heat, stripped to the waist, their skin glistening with that mineral sweat that never quite dries, and they have developed a culture of labor that is neither proud nor ashamed. The work exists. It must be done. They do it.

But the middle levels are also where the Hollow's strange creativity expresses itself. Because nothing leaves, everything accumulates, and what accumulates includes ideas. The corridors have a tradition of graffiti that dates back centuries—messages carved into stone with chisels, later painted with mineral pigments mixed from the drainage water, now inscribed with whatever tool is at hand. The walls of the middle levels are a palimpsest of accumulated text: declarations of love, territorial markers for the housing blocks, technical notes about pipe pressures that are no longer relevant but have been preserved because the act of erasing would require more effort than the act of ignoring. In the older corridors, the graffiti layers are so deep that the original stone is no longer visible—what you see is compressed text, the compressed speech of generations, a kind of geological record of human thought.

There are musicians in the middle levels. Not in the formal sense—there are no concert halls, no stages, no silence in which a performance could occur. Instead, the musicians play *with* the Hollow, not against it. They position themselves near the steam-works' release valves, where the hiss and rumble provide a constant tonal foundation, and they play instruments that have evolved for the specific acoustic environment: metal pipes cut to lengths that resonate with the industrial hum, percussion instruments made from the mineral crusts that form on cooling tanks, and the voice—the bark transformed into something melodic, a kind of singing that uses the corridors' echo as a component of the music. The sound carries for hundreds of meters through the carved stone, and the residents of the middle levels pause in their work for a moment, and the pause is the Hollow's version of applause.

## IV. The Engineers and the Engineers' Children

They are a class apart. Two centuries of formalized technical education have created a lineage that is neither rim nor depth, neither middle nor outside. The engineers of the Hollow trace their ancestry to the Third Excavation, the great period of expansion when the mountain's hydrology was first systematically understood and the drainage channels were cut with mathematical precision. They are the inheritors of that knowledge, and they guard it the way the rim families guard the gates: with inherited privilege and the quiet conviction that without them, everything fails.

They are correct about this. Without the engineers, the drainage channels would not be maintained, the steam-works would not be calibrated, the cisterns would overflow or run dry, the ventilation systems would fail, and the three million residents of the Hollow would discover, within a matter of months, that the mountain is not a passive container but an active force, constantly pushing water and heat into the carved spaces, constantly working to reclaim what was taken from it. The engineers understand the mountain as a system. They are the only people in the Hollow who think of it this way.

Their training begins at twelve. The academies near the rim—the same academies that have trained engineers for five hundred years—take the children of engineers and teach them the formal sciences of the Hollow: hydrology, thermodynamics, structural engineering, the specific geology of the plateau. They also teach the oral tradition—the accumulated knowledge that does not exist in any text, because the Hollow's humidity destroys paper within months and because the engineers have never trusted writing with their most important secrets. The knowledge passes through demonstration, through apprenticeship, through the slow accumulation of understanding that begins with a twelve-year-old child being led through the drainage channels by a grandmother who has walked them every day for sixty years.

The engineers' children grow up bilingual. They speak the formal language of the academies—a clipped, precise dialect that borrows from the rim families' speech but strips it of social pleasantries, a language designed for the communication of technical fact. They also speak the bark of the middle levels, because the engineers' work takes them into every district, and a engineer who cannot communicate with the maintenance workers cannot get anything done. This bilingualism marks them. They are never fully comfortable in either world. In the academies, they are too loud. In the corridors, they are too precise.

The engineers are the ones who say the mountain will collapse. They have been saying this for fifty years. Their surveys show that the lower levels—the deepest excavations, the ones that approach the geothermal vents—have begun to compress, the stone softening under the combined assault of heat and water and the accumulated weight of three thousand years of construction above. They have presented their findings to the administrative councils and to the rim families and to anyone who will listen, and the response has been the response that the Hollow gives to all predictions of change: acknowledgment, delay, the quiet confidence that the mountain has survived three thousand years and will survive three thousand more.

The engineers' children—the current generation, the ones in their twenties and thirties—are different. They have grown up with the knowledge of collapse as a background condition, the way the deep-speakers have grown up with noise and the middle-dwellers with damp. For them, the mountain's instability is not an abstraction. It is a sound they have heard in the lower levels, a specific groan in a specific corridor that their parents identified when they were children, and that they can now identify themselves, and that is getting louder. Some of them have begun to leave the Hollow entirely, taking their technical knowledge to the lowland cities, working in industries that have nothing to do with carved stone and ancient water. Others have gone deeper, joining the deep-speakers in the lowest levels, trying to understand the mountain's condition from within, as if proximity to the problem might yield a solution that the formal sciences cannot.

## V. The Bird-Speakers

Among the elderly of the middle levels, there are those who claim to understand the hollow-wing swallows.

This is not a formal discipline. There is no academy, no apprenticeship, no institutional framework. It is a thing that happens to certain people as they age, a kind of attunement that develops after decades of living in the same chambers, hearing the same calls, watching the same flocks move through the same corridors at the same hours. The bird-speakers do not announce themselves. They do not claim a title. They simply know things, and when they speak about what they know, other people listen, and then they argue about whether the knowledge is real.

The claims are specific. A particular chittering pattern, rising in pitch and quickening in rhythm, means a pressure drop in the nearest steam-works. A different pattern—slower, lower, with a characteristic pause between phrases—means the drainage channel in a specific section is beginning to clog. A call that the birds make only at night, only in the upper levels, only when the wind comes from the south, means that a structural failure is imminent in the district below.

The engineers dismiss this. They have studied the calls, recorded them, correlated them with events, and found no statistically significant relationship. The bird-speakers respond that the engineers do not understand what they are measuring—that the relationship between the calls and the events is not causal but symptomatic, that the birds are not predicting but responding, that the same sensitivities that allow the birds to navigate the Hollow's air currents also make them aware of changes in pressure and temperature that precede mechanical failures by hours or days.

There is no resolution to this argument. It has been ongoing for generations, and it has become one of the Hollow's characteristic internal tensions: the conflict between inherited knowledge and formal knowledge, between the body's accumulated attention and the instrument's measured fact. The bird-speakers do not press their case. They simply continue to listen, and when they speak, their neighbors make small adjustments—checking a pressure gauge, clearing a drainage channel, moving to a different chamber for the night—and the adjustments sometimes prevent things and sometimes do not, and the pattern of prevention and non-prevention is too complex to analyze, too embedded in the particularities of specific moments, to ever resolve into proof.

The bird-speakers are mostly women. This is not a rule but a tendency, and no one in the Hollow remarks on it because the pattern is so longstanding that it has become invisible. The women who become bird-speakers are the ones who spend the most time in the corridors, in the public spaces, in the positions where the birds' calls are most audible. They are the grandmothers who sit in the alcoves and watch the traffic pass. They are the vendors who set up each morning near the cable-car stations and hear the dawn flocks moving through the districts. They are the ones who have been in the same place for the longest time, and the birds have been in the same place for the longest time, and something in the accumulation of shared duration produces a communication that cannot be taught because it is not a skill. It is a condition.

The bird-speakers have a relationship with the birds that goes beyond the informational. They feed them, in the offhand way that one feeds a presence that has always been there. They scatter crumbs on their windowsills and watch the hollow-wing swallows descend, and they recognize individual birds by the variations in their markings—the slightly lighter patch under the left wing, the small notch in the right primary feather—and they name them, though the names are not spoken aloud. They are held in the mind, the way one holds the names of streets in a city one has walked for a lifetime. The birds, for their part, have learned to recognize the bird-speakers' chambers by some signal that is not visible to human eyes—perhaps a smell, perhaps a pattern in the mineral deposits on the window ledge, perhaps something else entirely—and they return to the same nests above the same windows generation after generation, the bird lineage and the human lineage cohabiting in a parallel inheritance that neither species has consciously chosen.

## VI. The Children

The Hollow's children are not like other children. This is not a romantic observation. It is a developmental one.

They are born in wet heat. The first air they breathe is thick with moisture and mineral and the sweet smell of the breath that grows on every surface. Their lungs adapt within days, the alveoli developing a resistance to the particulate matter that would damage a lowlander's respiratory system within weeks. Their skin thickens. Their ears adjust. They are, in the physiological sense, a population adapted to a specific environment, and the adaptation is visible in the first year: the Hollow's infants have a characteristic flushed quality, the skin perpetually pink from the heat and the constant damp, the eyes slightly narrowed against the steam that rises from every surface.

They learn to navigate before they learn to walk. The Hollow is vertical as much as horizontal—the stacked housing, the ladders between levels, the carved stairs that connect districts at different depths—and the children learn the three-dimensional geography of their home in the same way that lowland children learn the two-dimensional geography of streets. A five-year-old in the Hollow can describe the fastest route from a chamber in the middle levels to a specific food vendor near the cable-car station, and the route will involve descending two floors via a ladder, crossing a bridge over a drainage channel, passing through a steam-works chamber where the child knows to stay close to the left wall because the right side is too hot, and emerging through a carved doorway that the child recognizes by a specific pattern in the mineral deposits on its frame.

The children's games are the Hollow's games: vertical, damp, loud. They climb the cable-car pylons when the cars are not running. They chase each other through the drainage channels, sliding on the wet stone, their barking voices echoing off the carved walls in a way that transforms the sound into something musical. They compete to scrape the breath from the deepest corners, using tools they make themselves from scrap metal, and the one who brings back the largest sheet earns a status that lasts until the next game.

They know the birds before they know people, in a sense. The hollow-wing swallows are the first living things the Hollow's children observe with attention, because the birds are everywhere—in the windowsills, in the corridors, in the great chambers where the flocks roost. The children learn to read the birds' moods before they learn to read human faces. They know when a flock is about to move, the way the chittering rises and the birds shift on their ledges, and they feel the air displacement before it arrives, and they learn to brace against it, and this becomes a reflex so deep that the Hollow's children, even as adults, instinctively widen their stance when they hear the swallow-call that precedes a flock's departure.

They are not afraid of the depths. This terrifies their parents. The children of the middle levels hear stories of the deep-speakers and the heat-sweat shops and the lower levels where it is too loud for speech, and they are drawn to these places the way children everywhere are drawn to the forbidden. They sneak past the maintenance workers and descend the carved stairs into the steam-works' lower chambers, and the heat hits them like a wall, and the sound swallows their barking voices whole, and they stand in the orange glow of the release valves and feel the mountain's heartbeat in their bones, and some of them are afraid and leave and never speak of it, and some of them are not afraid, and those children become the deep-speakers of the next generation.

The children know the mountain is dying. They have heard their parents discuss it, heard the engineers' warnings, heard the elders' dismissals. They do not experience this as fear, because children do not experience geological time as fear. They experience it as fact—one more fact in the dense accumulation of facts that constitutes growing up in the Hollow. The mountain is wet. The mountain is hot. The mountain is loud. The mountain will collapse, someday, not today. The children integrate this into their understanding of the world the way they integrate the mineral smell and the barking speech and the birds' dawn calls: as a condition of existence, not a threat to it.

Some of the children, the ones who will grow up to be engineers or rim-family diplomats or the rare Hollow-born who leave for the lowland cities, develop a second kind of knowledge. They learn that the Hollow is not the world. This comes as a shock, not because no one has told them—the rim families' children, who attend the formal academies, are told explicitly—but because the body resists the knowledge. The child who has grown up in the wet heat, who has never felt dry air on bare skin for more than minutes, who has learned to shout before speaking and to feel vibration before hearing, experiences the outside as a kind of sensory deprivation. The lowland cities are too quiet. The sky is too large. The air is too thin. The children who leave the Hollow carry its weight in their bodies, and the ones who return do so with a relief that they cannot articulate, because the words for it do not exist in any language they know. The closest they come is the deep-speakers' gesture for *home*: a flat palm pressed against a wet stone wall, feeling the mountain's warmth bleeding through.

## VII. The Hollow-Wing Swallows

They are not swallows.

The name is a colonial artifact, assigned by the first surveyors to arrive from the lowland cities, who saw small dark birds wheeling through the carved spaces and reached for the nearest familiar category. The hollow-wing swallows are passerines, technically, but only technically. They are larger than true swallows—the size of a small thrush, with a wingspan that can reach thirty centimeters in the older individuals. Their wings are proportionally longer than any other passerine, an adaptation to the specific aerodynamic challenges of flying through carved corridors, around steam-works infrastructure, across the open vertical spaces of the great drainage channels. The wings have a characteristic silhouette in flight: curved, almost tubular, with a flexibility at the joint that allows the birds to make the sharp angle-turns necessary in a built environment that was never designed with flight in mind.

They are dark. Not black—dark in the way that wet stone is dark, with a depth that comes from the absorption of light rather than its reflection. Their feathers have a mineral sheen, a faint iridescence that the bird-speakers say comes from the calcium and iron in the water they drink and the breath they eat. In the orange glow of the steam-works' release valves, they appear almost copper. In the cooler light near the rim, they are the color of the mountain's cut face: dark grey, almost charcoal, with a smoothness that makes them look carved rather than grown.

They have been here longer than the city. This is not in dispute. The fossil record in the plateau's limestone—examined by the engineers during the Third Excavation—shows hollow-wing swallows in the plateau's crevices and cliff-faces for at least forty thousand years before the first human settlement. They evolved with the mountain. Their digestive system is adapted to the mineral crust that grows on wet stone surfaces—the breath—and they can extract nutrition from it the way no other species can. Their beaks are specialized: narrow, slightly curved, with a serrated inner edge that scrapes the crust from the rock without damaging the underlying stone. Their waste, processed through a digestive system that has co-evolved with the mineral chemistry of the plateau, returns calcium and iron and trace minerals to the surfaces where the breath grows, accelerating its spread. The birds and the breath are a closed loop. They need nothing else.

But they have adapted to the city. The three thousand years of human excavation have given the hollow-wing swallows something the plateau's natural geography could not: an almost infinite supply of nesting sites. Every carved ledge, every shelf, every overhang created by the excavation process is potential habitat. The population has grown with the city—not linearly but exponentially, as each new excavation creates more surface area, more ledge-space, more protection from the plateau's harsh winds. The elders who say there were fewer birds in their youth are correct, though the decrease they remember is not the decrease the engineers fear. The birds are not declining. They are accumulating, like everything else in the Hollow.

They have no predators here. The plateau's native raptors—large, solitary, dependent on open skies—cannot navigate the carved corridors and do not attempt to. The hollow-wing swallows have been predator-free for so long that they have lost the flocking behavior that characterizes their distant lowland relatives. They do not form tight defensive formations. They form *traffic* formations: vast, loose, flowing patterns that move through the Hollow's arterial spaces with a coordination that appears choreographed but is, in fact, emergent—each bird responding to its immediate neighbors, the pattern propagating through the flock like a wave through water.

The noise they make is not birdsong. It is a chittering—a rapid, clicking vocalization that carries well in the acoustic environment of the Hollow but would be inaudible in open air. The chittering serves the functions that song serves in other species: territory, mate attraction, flock cohesion. But it has also, in the three thousand years of cohabitation with humans, developed a quality that the bird-speakers insist is something like language. The calls vary by district. The birds nesting in the steam-works' warm corners have a different chittering pattern than the birds nesting in the cooler rim chambers. The calls change with the seasons, with the humidity, with the pressure. Whether this is language or simply the acoustic reflection of environmental conditions is the question that the bird-speakers and the engineers have been arguing about for generations, and it is the question that will never be answered, because the answer depends on a definition of language that no one in the Hollow has been able to agree upon.

The birds do not know they are being watched. Or perhaps they do, and they do not care. They go about their patterns with the indifference of a species that has been somewhere for forty thousand years and has no expectation of leaving. They nest above human bedrooms. They eat the crust that grows on human walls. They fly through human corridors at dawn and dusk in formations that darken the air, and they do not adjust their paths for the humans below, and the humans have learned to adjust for them, and this mutual accommodation—unconscious, unsigned, unremarked—is the closest thing to a social contract that the Hollow's two dominant species have ever achieved.

## VIII. The Breath

It is not alive. It is not dead. It is the thing in between, the category that the Hollow's residents have learned to accept without resolution, the way they accept the wet heat and the constant noise and the mountain's slow decay.

The breath grows because the conditions are right: damp, warm, dark. It thrives in the corridors where the steam-works' heat rises and the condensation collects on every surface. It spreads at a rate of perhaps a hand's breadth per year—slow, by the standards of living things, but relentless in the way that only non-living processes can be. It does not seek light. It does not seek nutrients. It simply extends, the way a crystal extends, the way frost extends, following the gradient of moisture and temperature across the available surface.

Its structure is layered. Thin sheets of mineral-organic composite, each one translucent, stacked like the pages of a book that no one is reading. The mineral component is calcium carbonate, drawn from the water that seeps through every surface. The organic component is more difficult to identify—the engineers have analyzed it and found proteins, complex sugars, compounds that are produced by living processes but are not, themselves, alive. The breath is a byproduct. It is the residue of something that was once alive—perhaps a microorganism, perhaps a colony of microorganisms, perhaps something that has no analogue in the lowland taxonomies—and that has left behind this slow-growing crust as its only evidence.

The Hollow's residents have a relationship with the breath that is simultaneously practical and superstitious. Practically, they scrape it. The maintenance workers scrape it from intake surfaces and pipe walls and ventilation shafts, because its accumulation restricts airflow and interferes with the steam-works' efficiency. They use the long-handled tools called *tongues*, flat metal blades that peel the crust from the stone in sheets that are collected and dried and used for a variety of purposes: partition walls in the housing blocks, a structural material that is surprisingly strong when compressed, a medicinal substance that the elderly chew for stomach ailments and that the engineers have analyzed and found to be mostly calcium carbonate with trace minerals and the mysterious organic compounds that no one has been able to identify.

Superstitiously, they fear it. Not actively—the breath is not dangerous in the way that a collapse is dangerous or a steam-pressure failure is dangerous. But there is a quality to its growth that unsettles. It spreads without purpose. It covers without consuming. It is always there, always extending, always converting the bare stone surface into something that is not quite stone and not quite alive and not quite anything that has a name. The residents of the deepest chambers, where the breath grows thickest, report a feeling of being *encroached upon*—not threatened, not invaded, but slowly, imperceptibly enclosed, as if the breath were a kind of slow-motion closing of a hand around the spaces where they live.

The children are not afraid of it. They touch it, peel it, collect it, use it in their games. They know its smell—the sweet, faint, organic scent that it releases when the humidity shifts—and they associate it with home in the same way they associate the mineral taste of the water with home. For the children, the breath is a feature of the landscape, no more frightening than the walls themselves.

The engineers have a different view. They have measured its growth rate, mapped its spread, calculated the rate at which it restricts ventilation and reduces the efficiency of the steam-works. They have estimated that, at the current rate of growth, the breath will compromise the critical ventilation shafts in the lower levels within two hundred years. They have presented this finding alongside their warnings about the mountain's structural integrity, and they have received the same response: acknowledgment, delay, the quiet confidence that the Hollow has always dealt with the breath and will continue to deal with it, and that two hundred years is a long time, and that the mountain may collapse before the breath becomes critical, and that the birds eat it, and the birds are many, and the balance will hold.

The balance has held for three thousand years. The breath grows. The birds eat it. The birds' waste feeds it. The humans scrape it and use it and fear it and ignore it. The breath continues its slow extension across every surface, its patient conversion of the Hollow's carved stone into something else, something that is not alive and not dead and not yet named, and that may, in the end, be the last thing remaining when the mountain gives out and the water finds its own path and the humans have gone and the birds have gone and the only thing left in the carved chambers is a thin, pale, layered crust, growing in the damp and the dark, releasing its faint sweet smell into empty corridors that no one walks.
