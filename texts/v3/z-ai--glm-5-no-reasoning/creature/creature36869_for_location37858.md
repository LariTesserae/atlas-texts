# What Lives in the Rootmass

## The Hollow-Tenders

They are not short, though they seem it at first—their posture an accumulated response to surfaces that require reading. Spines curved slightly forward, weight carried on the balls of their feet, hands often touching what they walk on. This is not caution. This is literacy. They read the root mass the way you might read a page, feeling forGive tension, for moisture gradients, for the subtle vibration of something growing underneath.

Their skin has taken on the quality of the place: damp, slightly cool to the touch, colonized by a microbiome that began as foreign and became symbiotic. They do not dry out here. They have stopped expecting to. The pale fine hair that covers their arms and legs has thickened into something like moss—slow-growing, moisture-holding, a second skin that mediates between them and the wet air.

They do not wear clothing in any way you would recognize. What covers them is grown: layers of cultivated fungus that conforms to body shape, shed and regrown seasonally, patterned with lichen colonies that indicate age and status and lineage. A person's history can be read on their surface. Old growth means wisdom. Bare patches mean recent passage through difficult terrain.

Their eyes are the most startling thing. Large, adapted to the diffuse light, they seem to gather illumination rather than reflect it. The pupils do not contract in the ordinary way—they dilate and narrow slowly, like plants orienting toward or away from light. In true darkness, which they rarely encounter, their eyes seem to faintly glow. Not bioluminescence. Something else. A quality of absorption that has become so complete it registers as emission.

They speak, but rarely. Their language is largely gestural—hands moving in ways that indicate direction, quantity, quality, relationship. Sound draws attention here, and attention is not always desirable. When they do use their voices, it is a low harmonic singing that travels through the root mass itself, vibrations conducted through the dense plant matter to listeners kilometers away. They are patient with communication. A message sent may take days to receive. They have learned to live in the space between sending and receiving.

## The Threaders

Not human. Not precisely animal. The threaders exist in the category of "things that have not been named because they bridge categories that were never meant to touch."

They are small—the largest specimen ever observed was the size of a child's hand, most are considerably smaller. Their bodies are segmented, soft, pale, almost translucent. They move through the root mass like water through soil, threading themselves through gaps and cracks and the tiny tunnels that form as bark expands and contracts with moisture. They are the only thing here that seems truly at home.

What they do is difficult to describe as behavior. They connect. They find loose endings—broken root tips, severed stems, torn fungal networks—and they join them. Not all the way. Not permanently. Just enough. A filament here, a cell bridge there. They are living sutures. The root mass heals because of them, and it heals in specific ways, directed patterns, as if something is being built according to a plan that no single threader understands.

They have no apparent sensory organs and yet they navigate unerringly toward damage. They have no apparent means of communication and yet they coordinate—two threaders will approach the same break from opposite sides, meeting in the middle, joining their work with a precision that implies intention.

They are rarely seen. You might pass thousands without knowing it, their pale bodies invisible against the pale wood, their movements too slow to register as movement. But if you stay very still in one place for very long—if you become patient enough—you might feel them: a faint tickle across your ankle, a soft pressure against your palm. They are curious about you in the way that engineers are curious about anomalies. They will investigate. They may even try to connect you.

They cannot, of course. Your skin is the wrong kind of surface. Your edges do not want to join. But they will try, gently, persistently, their soft bodies pressing against yours with a questioning pressure that feels almost like affection.

No one knows where they came from. No one knows what they want. They may not want anything. They may simply be doing what they are, the way a river flows or a fire burns. They connect. That may be all.

## The Drift

You will not see the drift. No one sees the drift. But you will know when it has passed.

The temperature drops, slightly. The condensation pauses in its gathering. The ambient sound—the drip, the creak, the soft rustle—goes silent. And then you feel something, not against your skin but *through* it, a pressure wave of coolness that moves through the air and through you at the same time.

The drift is large. Judging by the area of effect, judging by the way the root mass itself seems to recoil slightly in its passage, the drift may be the largest animal here. Or it may not be an animal at all. It may be a phenomenon. A mobile cold-spot. A living weather pattern.

The hollow-tenders speak of it with a kind of reverence that contains no fear. They say it clears pathways. They say it brings quiet. They say that following a drift's passage is the safest way to travel, because everything else has moved aside.

What does it eat? Where does it go? How does it survive in a place that seems to have so little to offer to something so large? The answers may not exist. The drift may not survive in the way you understand surviving. It may be part of something else—a circulatory system, a planetary function, a process that only looks like an organism because you cannot see what it connects to.

You will not see the drift. But you will remember its passing. The silence it leaves behind is different from ordinary silence. Emptier and fuller at the same time.

## The Pale Ones

They come from below. Sometimes they return there. Sometimes they stay.

They are human-shaped, human-sized, but the word "human" does not fit them anymore. Their skin is white—not pale, not fair, but the white of roots that have never seen light, of fungi that grow in darkness, of things that have exchanged pigmentation for some other adaptation. Their eyes are sealed shut, or perhaps they were never meant to open. They navigate by touch, by smell, by something else that has no name in any surface language.

They come up from the deep places where the root mass meets what might be stone. They do not speak. They do not build. They are sometimes seen standing very still in places where the hollow-tenders gather, as if listening, as if waiting for something to be said that they can understand.

The hollow-tenders do not fear them, but they do not approach them either. There is an agreement here, unspoken and very old. The surface is for those who breathe the air. The depths are for those who do not. When the pale ones rise, they are passing through. They are allowed to pass.

What do they want? What do they know? The hollow-tenders have theories. The pale ones carry things with them sometimes—objects from below, fragments of compressed plant-mass stone, pieces of something harder and older than anything that grows. They leave these things in certain places. Arranged. Deliberate. Messages? Offerings? Trash?

No one knows. The pale ones are patient in a way that suggests their sense of time operates on a different scale. A pause of years might be, for them, a pause of moments. They stand and they wait and they listen to something that no one else can hear.

And then they descend, and they are gone.

## The Unseen Congregation

There are more. There must be more. The root mass is too large, too complex, to support only this scant handful of forms. You sense them in the turbulence—the moving shadows that are not shadows, the sounds that stop when you turn toward them, the feeling of being observed from angles that should not exist.

The root mass itself may be the population. The individual plants may have more awareness than you assume. The boundary between "what lives here" and "what is here" may be thinner than language can express.

The hollow-tenders have a word for this. It does not translate. The closest approximation is "the listening," but that implies a separation between listener and listened-to that the original word rejects. There is no listener. There is only listening. The root mass listens. The air listens. You listen, and in listening, you become part of what is heard.

You are being observed. Not by eyes. By something else. By pressure and moisture and the slow chemical gradients of growth. You leave traces in the air, in the water that condenses on your skin, in the vibrations of your footfall. You are legible here. You are being read.

And whatever reads you—whatever parses the meaning of your passage—is patient enough to wait for you to finish being written.

---

## What Lives Here

What lives here is relationship. What lives here is process. The hollow-tenders and the threaders and the drift and the pale ones and all the things you will never see—they are not separate populations. They are expressions. Outcroppings. Places where the vast vegetative intelligence surfaces long enough to be named.

You came here looking for inhabitants. You found them. But you are also being found. The root mass has been cataloging you since you arrived—your moisture, your temperature, your sound, your chemical signature. It knows where you are. It knows what you are. It is deciding what to do with you.

So far, it has decided to let you pass.

So far.
