# The Distiller

She is not what you would expect, if you expected anything.

Nothing about her suggests the hermit or the mad alchemist or the religious recluse. She is not those things. She is a woman in her middle sixties who walks up the mountain every ninth day with a canvas sack of charcoal on her back and a box of matches in her coat pocket and leaves again before the furnace has stopped smoking. Her name, if it matters, is something the people in the valley below have stopped using — they call her the Distiller, or more often just *her*, as in *her smoke's up again*, or *I saw her on the track this morning.* She has been doing this for — she isn't certain. Twenty-three years. Twenty-four. The counting went early.

She was not the first. The apparatus was here when she arrived, though it was in worse repair. The furnace was cold. The gutters were clogged with moss. The lead pipe had separated from the lower tank and a slow mineral drip had built a stalagmite of rust and calcium on the stone below it, maybe a decade's growth. She found the works the way you find a tool in a shed that someone else left — recognizable in its purpose, unclear in its provenance. She was a hydrologist. She was doing survey work for a mining concern that had lost interest in the Vraal massif, taking water samples from the seeps and vents along the escarpment. She found the spur by accident, following a trickle of warm water up a fracture line, and she found the apparatus, and she found the beaker, and in the beaker she found a residue — a faint crystalline ring, dried, marking where the last batch had evaporated over the years.

She tasted it. She would tell you this without embarrassment. A hydrologist tastes things. She put her finger to the residue and touched it to her tongue and stood in the grotto for a long time, feeling the mineral sweetness of it dissolve and spread, and she thought: *someone was making something here.* And then she thought: *I could make it again.*

She cleaned the gutters. She reamed the lead pipe. She relined the furnace with fire clay from a deposit she found further down the escarpment, where the granodiorite gave way to older metamorphics. She found the charcoal — a stand of dead mountain ash in a cirque valley two kilometers below, lightning-killed, slowly carbonizing on its own — and she started carrying it up. She lit the furnace. She waited.

---

What she distills is not water.

Or it is water, in the sense that it is the condensate of heated vapor, and the vapor is the weep of the mountain, and the weep is water. But the water that rises through the granodiorite along the Vraal escarpment has passed through stone that carries — she has tested this, with instruments, with reagents, in a lab she no longer has access to — a dissolved mineral profile unlike anything in the standard tables. Strontium. Barium. A lithium isotope she could not identify with the equipment she had. Trace amounts of something that registered on her mass spectrometer as a molecular weight she had never seen and could not match to any reference compound. The concentration of these elements in the raw weep-water is low. In the distillate, after the furnace has driven off the volatile fraction and the coil has condensed what remains, the concentration is still low. But it is there. And the taste — she will tell you this — the taste is unlike anything. Not sweet, exactly. Not mineral, exactly. A brightness on the tongue. A clarity. The faintest suggestion of something floral, though nothing here flowers except the rhododendrons and this does not taste like them.

She does not know what it is. She has been distilling it for twenty-three years, or twenty-four, and she does not know what it is.

This is the part that matters, and the part she would not explain to you, because she does not have an explanation. She takes the distillate. She fills small glass vials she carries in padded pockets inside her coat. She brings them down the mountain. She — here it becomes unclear — she does not sell them. She does not drink them, not exactly. She has, occasionally, in the early years, before she understood the weight of what she was doing. She does not anymore. The vials go — somewhere. To someone. There is a man in the valley town who keeps a shop that sells nothing, a shop with a counter and a back room, and she leaves the vials on the counter and he leaves payment in a tin, and the payment is not money, it is supplies: charcoal, copper sheeting, lead solder, fire brick. The arrangement was made before her. The man in the shop knew her predecessor — she has never learned who that was — and he treated her, at their first meeting, as though he had been expecting her. He was not surprised. He was courteous. He gave her a list of what the apparatus required, and she has been fulfilling it ever since.

She does not know where the vials go after the shop. She has not asked. She does not want to know. This is the part of the arrangement she understands least clearly and guards most carefully — the sense that she is part of a chain, a link, and that the chain extends in directions she cannot see, and that the not-seeing is itself a kind of service. She is the Distiller. The grotto is the Condensery. The mountain provides. The apparatus runs. What happens beyond the shop is not her concern, or at least she tells herself so on the walk down, every ninth day, the empty sack folded under her arm, the vials secure against her ribs.

---

Her body has changed with the work, the way a body changes with any labor performed long enough.

Her legs are extraordinary. She does not think of them this way — she thinks of them as legs, as the things that carry her up the mountain — but they are extraordinary. The climb from the valley to the spur takes her three hours, over broken ground, through a scree field that shifts underfoot, up a series of ledges where she has bolted iron rungs into the rock where the grade exceeds what her legs can manage unaided, though it rarely does. She carries twenty-five kilograms of charcoal. She has done this, conservatively, nine hundred times. Her calves are dense, roped, the muscles visible through the skin of her lower legs when she rests and rolls her trousers to soak her feet in a rivulet. Her knees ache, as knees will, but they have not failed. Her back is broad and slightly stooped — not from age, from years of carrying the sack — and her hands are the hands of someone who works with metal and stone and fire: cracked-nailed, scarred across the knuckles, the skin of her fingertips thickened and faintly yellowed from the heat of the furnace and the acid tang of the copper.

Her face is unremarkable and she is glad of it. She has a broad, weathered face, brown from altitude exposure, with the fine creases of someone who squints into vapor and wind. Her hair is gray, cut short, irregular — she cuts it herself with the shears she uses for the copper sheeting, and it shows. She wears layers: a wool base, a canvas jacket she has patched a hundred times, a shell of waxed cotton that she re-waxes every spring. She is not poor. The supplies from the shop cover what she needs. She does not want more than she needs. She lives in a stone cottage in the valley with a stove and a table and a shelf of journals — twenty-three years, or twenty-four, of daily entries, mostly observations of the apparatus, the flow rates, the distillate volume, the color of the furnace flame, the weather, the growth of the moss, the slow migration of a crack across the face of the lower tank that she has monitored and patched and monitored again. The journals are not diaries. They are records. They are the closest thing she has to a document of what the Condensery produces, and she writes in them with the care of someone who knows that what she is doing is not understandable to her and may need to be understood by someone else, later, after her.

She is not lonely. This is the thing people get wrong, when they imagine someone alone on a mountain. She has the apparatus, which requires her attention and rewards it. She has the mountain, which is not a companion but is a presence, a system, a thing whose processes she has come to know with the intimacy of long observation — where the water enters the rock, where it heats, where it rises, how the flow changes with the season (it does not change much, but it changes: the weep is slower in winter, thicker in summer, and once, eight years ago, it turned briefly, startlingly, milky, and the distillate that batch was cloudy and she kept it separate and sent it down with a note, and the man in the shop gave her a look she had not seen before and doubled the supplies that month). She has the ferns. She has the wren. She has the spider. She has the grotto itself, the warm wet cathedral of it, the sound of it, the way the light moves through the vapor on afternoons when the overcast thins and the sun briefly finds the spur and every surface ignites with small bright points and the whole place becomes, for a moment, a chamber of lenses, of light held in water, of water held in stone.

She does not own this place. She does not feel she belongs to it. She feels — and this is the word she uses, in the journals, the only word that fits — that she *attends* it. She attends the apparatus. She attends the process. She is present for it, necessary to it, but the process is not hers. The mountain does the work. The stone weeps. The heat rises. The vapor condenses. She lights a fire and cleans the gutters and checks the beaker and fills the vials, and this is her part, and her part is small, and she is content with its smallness.

---

Sometimes — not often, perhaps twice a year — she stays overnight.

She brings a bedroll and a small stove and she sleeps in the alcove behind the tanks, where the wall curves and the stone is warm and the sound of the water and the apparatus and the wind through the rhododendrons fills the dark with a continuous soft mechanical roar that is, to her, the sound of a thing working as it should. She sleeps well there. Better than in the cottage. She wakes in the dark and lies still and listens to the drip of the condensing coil and the tick of the cooling furnace and the deeper, slower sound of the mountain breathing through its vents, and she thinks of nothing, and the nothing is full.

In the morning she checks the beaker. It is fuller than when she lay down. It is always fuller. The mountain weeps and the apparatus gathers and the beaker fills and the process is steady and slow and the slowness is the point. One drop every three seconds. Twenty drops a minute. Twelve hundred drops an hour. She has calculated this, of course. She has a column in her journals: *Yield, mL, per 24 hr.* The number varies. The mountain varies. The apparatus varies. But the beaker fills, and she decants it, and the vials go down, and the supplies come up, and the furnace is lit again, and the grotto hums.

She will do this until she cannot. When she cannot, someone else will come. She does not know who. The man in the shop does not know who. But the apparatus is maintained, and the instructions are in the journals, and the mountain will still be weeping, and the chain will find its next link, because that is what chains do. That is what the Distiller believes, or has decided to believe, standing in the warm wet light at the edge of the cliff, holding a vial of clear liquid up to the vapor-thick air, watching the faint mineral sweetness catch what light there is.

One drop every three seconds.

The beaker fills.
