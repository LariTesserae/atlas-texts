# What Lives in the Cylinder Garden

## The Garden Itself Is the Inhabitant

The question of what lives in the Cylinder Garden contains its own answer. The garden lives—a single vast organism composed of a hundred species locked in a choreography so intimate that separation has become meaningless. The vines that climb the walls are not separate from the moss that carpets the floor; their roots have tangled beneath the surface for so long that they share nutrients, share signaling, share whatever passes for thought in a vegetable mind. The trumpet-flowers that sing their low drone are not individuals but mouths of the same body, each exhaling in sequence so that the chord never breaks.

If you pulled one plant from the mat, you would pull them all. They have grown into each other, through each other, their tissues grafting at points of contact until the garden is less a collection of plants than a single distributed presence with a hundred thousand leaves and no central brain.

This is not unusual for plants—they are communal by nature, connecting through fungal networks and root systems and chemical signals. But here, in a sealed room without competition, without predators, without the disruptions of weather or season, the process has gone further. The garden has become a colonial organism, a coral reef of vegetation where the distinction between self and other has dissolved. It has been growing toward unity for decades, perhaps centuries, and now it has arrived.

The garden thinks. Not in the way you think—not with symbols and language and narrative—but with chemistry and growth and the slow language of cells. It senses the light it produces and adjusts its brightness. It hears its own song and modulates its frequency. It feels the vibrations of the factory through the walls, the endless pulse of the looms, and it has incorporated that rhythm into its being. The garden is a creature of habit and stasis, and it has made the factory's heartbeat part of its own.

## The Machine on the Third Tier

The breadbox-sized enameled machine is alive, though it was not always.

Originally, it was a regulator—a device built by the same hands that constructed the tiered floor and sealed the door. Its purpose was to maintain the equilibrium: to circulate air, to modulate temperature, perhaps to release trace nutrients into the substrate. It hummed with purpose, doing its work in the silence, serving the garden that grew around it.

But the garden grew *into* it.

Vines found their way through the vents. Roots threaded through the housing and touched the circuits inside. Spores germinated in the warmth of its power supply. And slowly, over years and decades, the machine was colonized. Its metal became a scaffold for growth. Its hum was absorbed into the garden's greater song. Its function—whatever it was originally—was subsumed by the vegetable presence that wrapped around it.

Now it is something else. A cyborg of chlorophyll and copper. The machine still draws power from some hidden source—the factory's ancient grid, perhaps, or some more exotic energy that the builders embedded in the walls—and that power still feeds its processes. But the processes have changed. The machine's dial, set to its unknown symbol, now controls something the garden needs: perhaps the release of growth hormones, perhaps the modulation of bioluminescence, perhaps something more complex.
