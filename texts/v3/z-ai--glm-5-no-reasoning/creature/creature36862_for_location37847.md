# What Lives Here

There is nothing that *owns* this place. That is the first thing to understand. No creature sits at the center of it, no keystone predator, no sentient steward tending the machinery or guiding the growth. The Capillary Works has no lord. It has no caretaker. It has only **inhabitants**, and they are inhabitants the way a man is the inhabitant of his own bloodstream — present, functional, incidental to the larger circulation.

---

## The Beetles

Call them the **mat-dwellers**. They are the most numerous and the most legible.

Dark-shelled, round-bodied, the size of a thumbnail. They move through the root-fiber in patterns that appear random but are not random — they are *algorithmic*, driven by moisture gradients and chemical signals too subtle for human senses. They are always moving toward the damp. Toward the seams. Toward the places where decomposition is most advanced and the fiber is softest and most edible. They feed on decay, and in doing so they accelerate the decay, and in accelerating the decay they produce the very conditions for new growth, which will eventually decay in turn, which they will feed on in turn.

They do not know this. They are beetles. They are following moisture and nothing else.

Their shells are not merely dark. In the bioluminescent light they carry a faint oily iridescence, a sheen that shifts between deep amber and black depending on the angle, and this sheen is not decorative — it is a byproduct of the biofilm that coats their exoskeletons, a thin layer of microorganisms that live on their shells the way lichen lives on the steel housings. Each beetle carries its own microscopic ecosystem. Each beetle is a corridor in miniature.

They do not fly. Their wing cases are fused — have been fused for uncounted generations. There is nowhere to fly to. The corridor is all. The mat is all. Flight would be an expenditure of energy with no return, and the equilibrium does not tolerate expenditures with no return. So they walk. They walk constantly, ceaselessly, in their steady purposeful patterns, and when they die they die where they walked, and their bodies soften and sink into the fiber and are consumed by the organisms on their own shells, and the moisture carries what remains into the seams, and the cycle does not pause to mark the transition.

---

## The Pale Arthropods

They live on the walls. They are the things that feed on the beetles, and they are the things that most resemble something *designed* for this place, though they were not designed — they *became*.

Translucent bodies. Visible organs — dark tracings under a skin that has no use for pigment, that has not seen light in evolutionary time, that has adapted to a world where vision is secondary and touch is primary and chemical sensing is primary and sound is useless because the drone fills every acoustic niche and leaves no bandwidth for anything else. They are long — three centimeters, four — and many-legged, their legs distributed along a segmented body that moves with a fluid, rippling gait, each leg placing itself with a precision that seems mechanical but is merely thorough.

They hunt by chemical trail. The beetles leave traces — pheromonal, metabolic, the exhaust of their living — and the pale arthropods follow these traces along the bark ridges, through the darker patches where the luminescence is weakest, moving with a patience that is not patience but metabolism, a slow-burning engine that cannot afford speed. They catch the beetles in the seams. They eat them there. The moisture washes the remains away.

They have no name. They have no need for one. They have been here long enough that their ancestry is indistinguishable from the ancestry of the place itself.

## The Vibration-Hunters

The spiders — if they are spiders — are the largest animals here, and they are the ones that most unsettle.

Palm-sized. Flat. Splayed. Their legs are not the slender jointed limbs of surface-dwelling spiders but broad, flattened appendages, edged with hooked setae so fine they are almost invisible, designed not for silk-spinning — they build no webs, have never built webs, the spinnerets are vestigial, long since repurposed as sensory organs — but for grip. For purchase on the bark surface. For holding position in a world where the air moves in slow tidal pulses and the ground vibrates constantly with the machinery's drone.

They hunt by vibration. This is their adaptation, their singular expertise. The drone is omnipresent, but it is also *uniform* — a flat background of mechanical frequency against which any irregularity registers as signal. The footfall of a beetle in the root-mat at three meters' distance produces a vibration that is, to the spider's setae, as distinct as a shout in a silent room. The spider orients. The spider moves. One leg, testing. Another leg, confirming. A pause — long, still, the body pressed flat against the bark, every setae tuned to the substrate. Then another step. Then another. The approach takes minutes. The strike, when it comes, is the only fast thing the spider ever does.

They do not eat where they kill. They carry their prey to the darker patches, the opaque zones where the bark is densest and the light is lowest, and they feed there, in private, in the places where the pale arthropods also hide. These darker patches are the territory of the slow. The places where the quick things — the flying things, the dust-motes — do not go.

Each spider occupies a territory of roughly fifteen meters of corridor. They do not overlap. They do not compete. The spacing is natural — a function of prey density, of metabolic need, of the simple mathematics of a closed system in which every predator is allotted exactly enough to survive and no more. They do not fight. Fighting would waste energy. They simply *occupy*, and the occupation is balanced, and the balance has been balanced for longer than the concept of imbalance has had any meaning here.

## The Winged Motes

They are the only things that fly, and they fly only in the warm zones, near the pipe arrays, where the copper tubing radiates heat into the air and the condensation beads on metal surfaces and the light catches their wings.

They are tiny. Smaller than gnats. Their bodies are barely visible — dark specks, dots of matter — and it is their wings that define them, wings that are not membranous but feathery, fringed with hair-like structures that catch the bioluminescence and scatter it in brief prismatic flashes as they move. They appear and disappear. They materialize in the light and vanish in the shadow, their presence inferred more than observed, a flicker at the edge of vision, a movement that might be imagination.

They drink the condensation from the copper tubes. They feed on the biofilm that grows on the patina. They mate in the air, briefly, in clusters of two or three, and then separate, and the females lay eggs in the crevices where the pipe arrays meet the bark, and the eggs hatch into larvae that bore into the interface between metal and tissue, living there, in that junction, in the place where machine becomes organism, feeding on both, belonging to neither.

They are the only things in this space that move with speed. They are the only things that seem *alive* in the way that surface-dwellers recognize life — quick, reactive, fragile. They die in the air sometimes, exhausted, and their bodies drift down like dust and land on the warm copper and are absorbed by the condensation and carried into the tubes and become part of the fluid and the fluid becomes part of the plant and the plant becomes part of the machine and the machine pumps on.

## The Things That Are Not Animals

There are organisms here that do not fit any category. They are not insects. They are not arachnids. They are not vertebrates. They may not be animals at all.

In the crevices between the steel housings and the bark — in those narrow seams where the machine meets the tissue, where the corrosion has created micro-environments of iron oxide and moisture and organic decay — there are growths. Flat, branching, almost crystalline in their regularity, they extend across the corroded metal in fractal patterns that mirror the branching of the capillary pipes themselves. They are not lichen. They are not fungus. They are something that has no name because no one has ever named them, because no one has ever been here, because this place has been closed and running and complete for longer than taxonomy has existed as a human practice.

They are slightly warm. They respond to touch — not by retracting, but by *changing texture*, firming under pressure, softening when the pressure is removed. They may be sensory. They may be part of the system — extensions of the plant's monitoring network, distributed sensors embedded in the machine's skin, reporting back to something. Or they may be independent. They may be organisms in their own right, exploiting the seam, finding their own equilibrium in the micro-environment between iron and bark.

They do not move. They grow — so slowly that growth is imperceptible, measurable only in the way that geological processes are measurable, by comparing states separated by long intervals. They have been growing for as long as the machinery has been running. They will continue growing for as long as it continues. They are the closest thing in this place to *character* — to *presence* — to the sense that something here is not merely functioning but *inhabiting*, *experiencing*, *being* in some way that transcends the mechanical and the biological and enters into some third category for which no word exists.

---

## The Absence

And then there is the thing that does not live here but whose absence shapes everything.

The corridor has no architect. The machinery has no operator. The system has no *mind*. There is nothing here that experiences the corridor as a corridor, that hears the drone as sound, that sees the luminescence as light. The beetles do not know they are in a pipe. The spiders do not know they are hunting on a wall. The pale arthropods do not know they are translucent. The winged motes do not know their wings catch the light. Everything here is *in* the Capillary Works but nothing is *of* it — nothing experiences it as a whole, as a place, as the thing that you experience when you walk its length and feel the thrumming in your bones and smell the tannin and the iron and the cellulose and the cool damp air that breathes in and out over the span of minutes.

You are the only thing that has experienced this place as a place. You are the only thing that has walked its length and felt its emptiness and recognized its finality. You are the only thing that has noticed that it does not notice you.

And this — your noticing, your walking, your eventual leaving — this is the only thing that has happened here in an uncountably long time that is not part of the cycle.

The cycle will not incorporate your passing. The moisture you displaced with your feet will settle back. The beetles you disturbed will resume their patterns. The spiders will re-orient to the vibrations that remain. The air you breathed will be recycled by the plant. The heat you left will dissipate into the copper. The corridor will close behind you the way water closes behind a hand drawn through it — without effort, without memory, without any change that persists.

You are the anomaly. And the anomaly is already fading.
