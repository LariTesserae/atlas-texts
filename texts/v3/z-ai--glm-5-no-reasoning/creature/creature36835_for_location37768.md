# What Lives Here

The first thing to understand is that nothing here chose to live here. Everything arrived by accident or desperation and then, through the long patient grinding of generations, found a shape that could hold itself against the weight of that sky and the insult of that sun. This is not a place where life flourishes. It is a place where life *insists*.

---

## The Runners

They call them runners, but that word misses something. A runner implies purpose, destination. These animals are better described as **intervals of motion** in a landscape otherwise defined by stillness.

Physically, they are precise instruments. A mature adult stands about waist-high at the shoulder when quadrapedal, with a body length of perhaps 1.3 meters from snout to tail-base. The tail itself adds another meter — a thin, rigid strut used for balance during bipedal sprints, feathered with short bristly hairs that make it appear as if drawn with a single stroke of a brush. Their legs are the main event: long, articulated, with joints that bend both forward and backward depending on the gait. The bones are visible beneath the skin as ridges, knobs, straight lines — they have almost no fat, these animals, almost no soft tissue. They are architectural.

Their hide defies description not because it is complex but because it is the opposite: a uniform rusted ochre that matches the ridge stone so precisely that a runner at rest is genuinely invisible until it moves. No spots, no stripes, no differentiation. Even the eyes — small, dark, positioned on either side of a narrow skull — catch the light in a way that makes them disappear. The only break in the camouflage is the inside of the mouth, which is a shocking pale pink, visible only when they feed.

And they feed constantly. The runners are machines for processing shrimp. They move along the seep-lines in loose formations, each animal maintaining a precise distance from its neighbors — not territorial, but optimal for intercepting the clouds of insects that rise from the warm water. When they spot prey, they shift to bipedal stance in a single fluid motion, dropping their forelimbs to their sides and sprinting on hind legs alone, snakelike neck extended, head swiveling. The strike is fast enough to blur. Then back to four legs, back to stillness, the shrimp swallowed whole without chewing.

Their social structure is opaque to human observation. They form groups of eight to twelve — always eight to twelve, never more, rarely less — but these groups merge and split with no apparent pattern, individuals moving between them fluidly. There is no visible dominance, no grooming, no play. They communicate through that single low click, produced by snapping the lower jaw against the upper, and the clicks vary in rhythm and duration in ways that seem to convey information but resist interpretation. A group moving fast across a ridge will click in unison, a rapid staccato. A group feeding will click singly, at random intervals. A group at rest — folded into the stone, legs tucked, breathing slowed — will click only once every few minutes, a sound like a heartbeat heard through a wall.

They sleep in the troughs, but never the deep ones. They avoid the mats. When asked why, a researcher who spent three seasons on the plateau said: *They know something we don't. Or they feel something we can't.*

## The Black Shrimp

In the seeps, life concentrates.

The shrimp are not true shrimp — they are something else, an ancient branch of crustacean that evolved in isolated aquifers and surfaced here when the water found its paths. They are small, most of them, two to three centimeters long, though occasional individuals reach twice that size. They have no eyes — haven't had eyes for millions of years — and their bodies are a translucent grey-black that makes them appear as solid shadows in the warm water.

What they lack in vision they make up for in other senses. Their antennae are longer than their bodies, trailing in the current like threads of smoke, detecting chemical signatures with a precision that borders on the supernatural. They can taste a bacterial film from a meter away. They can sense the vibration of a runner's footfall through the rock before the animal is visible. They know things.

They live in densities that startle. A single trickle-path, twenty meters long, might hold ten thousand individuals, layering over each other in slow motion, grazing the bacterial films that grow on the wet stone. The bacteria are the foundation — thin, iridescent coatings that draw energy from the iron and sulfur in the water, and the shrimp have evolved mouthparts specifically designed to scrape these films without damaging the underlying stone. They are gardeners, tending their invisible crops.

Their life cycle is synchronized to the aquifer's pressure. When the water flows strongest, they breed — producing eggs that sink into the cracks and wait, sometimes for years, for the right conditions to hatch. When the water slows, they congregate in the deepest pools, moving minimally, conserving energy. They can survive being dried out for days at a time, encased in a protective mucus, waiting for the condensation or the rain. They have patience.

The runners eat them. The swifts eat them. Occasionally, when a flash flood scours a trough, the shrimp pour over the ridges in living streams, and everything on the plateau converges to feed. But the populations recover. They always recover. There is something about their numbers that exceeds predation, something about their reproduction that accounts for loss before it happens. They are the plateau's great success story, though nothing about them seems grand enough for the word.

## The Swifts

They arrived late. Fossil evidence suggests they've only been here for perhaps ten thousand years — a blink, in plateau time. They came from somewhere else, some lowland population that found the thermals rising off the heated ridges and stayed.

They are not unique species. They *are* swifts, recognizable as such — small, dark, scythe-winged, built for speed and endurance. But they have adapted. Their feathers have a slight iridescence that matches the mineral deposits on the trough walls. Their feet, never strong to begin with, have nearly disappeared — they cannot perch, only cling, and they do so in the cracks and crevices of the trough walls, hanging upside down in colonies of hundreds.

They spend their days on the wing. Rising before dawn, they ride the thermals in widening spirals, catching insects drawn to the heat and the water. Their flight is erratic and precise, each individual tracing a path that somehow never collides with another. The sound of their wings — that tearing-paper whisper — fills the air above the ridges from sunrise to sunset.

In the evenings, they descend. The colonies in the trough walls are dens of noise and movement, the birds chittering and shuffling, preening and feeding their young. The walls themselves seem to vibrate. The smell is distinctive: guano and feathers and the mineral tang of the stone, all warmed by the day's heat and slowly releasing into the cooling air.

They are social in ways the runners are not. They recognize individuals, remember faces, hold grudges. A swift displaced from its preferred crack will squabble for hours, refusing to settle elsewhere. A swift that loses its mate will call for days, a thin piping sound that echoes through the troughs. They care, in their small frantic way.

But they avoid the deep troughs too.

## The Mats

In the deepest, narrowest troughs, where direct sunlight never reaches, the world changes.

The mats are alive. That much is certain. They are colonies of organisms — moss, liverwort, fungus, bacteria, things with no common names — growing in overlapping layers, drawing moisture from the condensation and the stone. They form patches the size of tables, the size of beds, covering the rock in dark spongy expanses that yield slightly under pressure.

They are cool. On a plateau where heat is the dominant condition, the mats are a radical exception. Press your hand into one, and water wells up around your fingers — cold water, colder than the air, colder than the seeps. The mats hold the night's condensation through the day, releasing it slowly, maintaining their own microclimate in defiance of the sun.

The smell is distinct: wet stone, pepper, something almost fungal, something almost sweet. It intensifies when the mats are disturbed. It lingers on skin and clothing.

And the sound. The low breathing.

The sound is definitely biological. It comes from within the mats, or beneath them, or somehow distributed through their entire structure. It is rhythmic but not regular — more like a sleeping animal than a machine — and it has been heard by every researcher who has spent significant time on the plateau. It does not change with weather or season. It does not stop.

The runners avoid the mats. The swifts avoid the deep troughs where the mats grow. The shrimp do not appear in the water that passes beneath them. Even the insects give the mats a wide berth, clustering at their edges but never crossing onto their surface.

What lives in the mats?

The most rigorous scientific answer is: *unknown*. The mats have never been excavated. They have never been sectioned or sampled or studied beyond surface-level observations. The reason is simple: the plateau is remote, funding is limited, and the mats are not the primary focus of any approved research.

But there are other answers too, passed between researchers in the field, spoken low and late at night when the condensation is darkening the stone:

Some say the mats themselves are the organism — not a colony but a single entity, kilometers of connected tissue, breathing through pores we haven't identified, surviving on condensation and minerals and the slow decay of its own lower layers.

Some say there is something *beneath* the mats, something that lives in cavities carved into the stone over millennia, something that draws the mats down as a disguise or a symbiote, something that breathes.

Some say the sound is not biological at all but geological — thermal expansion and contraction of water trapped in deep channels, resonating through the stone, amplified by the mat structure until it becomes audible.

None of these answers is satisfying. All of them raise more questions than they resolve.

What is known: the mats have been here longer than anything else on the plateau. The mats were old when the pumps were built. The mats will be here after the pumps have dissolved into anonymous lumps of iron.

## The Human Presence

There is one permanent resident.

She arrived with the research team seven years ago and stayed after they left. Her name is not important — she has stopped using it herself. On the rare occasions when supply teams come through, they call her the Station Keeper, a joke that has become a title.

She lives in the stone building in the wide trough. The partially collapsed roof has been patched with salvaged metal and layered sedge, adequate to shed the occasional rain. The broken glass vessels have been replaced with functional ones, scavenged from other abandoned sites across the plateau. The rusted tools have been cleaned, oiled, restored to use. She has made the building livable — barely, but livable.

Her routine follows the plateau's cycles. Mornings: check the seeps, measure the flow, record the shrimp populations in a waterproof notebook. Midday: rest, when the heat makes work impossible. Afternoons: walk the ridges, note the condition of the pumps, observe the runners. Evenings: sit in the worn-smooth corner where the previous occupant sat, watch the condensation rise, listen.

She has seen the mats breathe. She has walked to the edges of the deep troughs and stood there, feeling the air change, feeling the attentiveness that settles over those places. She has never crossed onto a mat. She respects the boundaries.

She does not know what she is waiting for. The research that brought her here was concluded years ago, its findings published and forgotten. The plateau does not need her observations. The runners do not need her protection. The shrimp will thrive or fail regardless of her counting.

But she stays. She tends the two working pumps, applying oil to their bearings, tightening their bolts, keeping them creaking in the wind. She maintains the building. She records her notes. She listens to the plateau breathe.

In her seventh year, she has begun to understand something that the short-term researchers never grasped: the plateau is not empty. It is *full* — full of processes, full of cycles, full of lives that intersect and diverge and continue without observation. Her presence adds nothing, subtracts nothing. She is a feature of the landscape now, like the pumps, like the rusted settling tanks, another object slowly returning to stone.

She finds this comforting.

## What Came Before

Before the researchers, before the pumps, before even the runners evolved their current form, there were people here.

The evidence is subtle. A few pictographs in a trough wall, faded almost to invisibility — geometric patterns that might represent water or might represent nothing at all. A few shards of worked stone, clearly tools, clearly ancient, scattered near a seep that no longer flows. A few bones, human-shaped, weathered to the same ochre as the rock, discovered by accident and left undisturbed.

These people came when the climate was different, perhaps, when the aquifer flowed stronger and the troughs held permanent pools. They lived here long enough to leave marks but not long enough to leave ruins. They departed, or they died, or they became something else.

The plateau does not mourn them. The plateau barely remembers them.

But the current resident has found herself thinking about them more and more. Late at night, when the wind drops and the breathing from the mats is the only sound, she wonders what they knew — about the water, about the shrimp, about the deep troughs and what lived there before the mats grew over it.

She wonders if they listened to the plateau breathe, and if it ever told them what it was breathing for.

---

## What Lives Here

So. What lives on the Condensate Plateau?

Runners. Shrimp. Swifts. Mats. Crusts and sedges and bacterial films. One woman. The ghosts of industry. The older ghosts of people who came before.

And something else — something that lives in the sound the plateau makes, the deep mineral hum of stone expanding and contracting, settling into itself. Something that lives in the regularity of the cycles, the predictability that predates prediction. Something that lives in the way the plateau continues, patient and complete, asking nothing and offering nothing and simply *being*.

The runners click in the fading light. The swifts pour into the cracks. The two working pumps creak out of rhythm with each other, out of rhythm with the wind, out of rhythm with everything except their own stubborn persistence.

In the deep troughs, the breathing continues.

The plateau does not need a name for what lives here. It knows, the way a body knows its own organs, the way a clock knows its own gears. Everything here has a place. Everything here has a function, even if that function is only to exist.

This is a place where existence itself is the achievement. And everything that lives here has achieved it.
