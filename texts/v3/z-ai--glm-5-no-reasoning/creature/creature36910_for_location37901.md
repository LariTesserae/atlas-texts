# The Plateau and Its Inhabitants

## The Grazers

They do not have a name for themselves. If they possess language at all, it exists below the threshold of anything you would recognize as communication—a vocabulary of pressure changes, of subtle shifts in body weight registered through the stone by others of their kind a hundred meters away. They know where each other are. They do not need to know much else.

**An adult grazer stands roughly one and a half meters at the shoulder,** though the shoulder is a complicated concept on an animal with six legs. The front pair carries the most weight, the thickest musculature, the deepest chest. The middle pair is set close to the front, almost vestigial in its placement but functional—it stabilizes the animal on the uneven stone when it bends to drink. The rear pair is the longest, angled for slow, deliberate locomotion. When the animal walks, the legs move in a lateral sequence that looks almost mechanical: left-front, left-middle, left-rear, right-front, right-middle, right-rear. This gait produces no sound because the hooves are not hooves in the conventional sense. They are pads—thick, fibrous, almost cartilaginous structures that absorb impact and conform to the rock surface. The animal could walk across gravel and leave no audible trace.

**The body is narrow.** Slender is the word that comes to mind, but slender implies a fragility that is inaccurate. The grazer is not fragile. It is *reduced*—stripped of everything nonessential, pared down to the minimum configuration of bone and muscle and organ required to exist at this altitude, in this cold, on this stone. The ribcage is deep but not broad. The spine is visible as a ridge running from the base of the skull to the tail, which is short and held tight against the body. The fur lies flat, never fluffed, never ruffled by wind, because there is rarely wind here—just the mist, which the fur repels through its waxy outer layer while the white undercoat holds warmth with the compressed efficiency of a survival system that has had millennia to perfect itself.

**They have no eyes.** The skull is smooth where eye sockets would be—just a gentle concavity on either side, covered in the same pale fur, reading as a slight indentation if you are close enough to see it. How they navigate is not entirely clear. They do not bump into each other or into the rock formations. They find the moisture pools without apparent searching, walking in a deliberate line to a specific divot in the stone, lowering the long neck, drinking, and moving on. Their path across the plateau is neither random nor rigid—it has the quality of a route walked many times, a circuit that the animal knows through some faculty other than sight.

**The most likely explanation is vibration.** The stone transmits the machinery's hum, the mist's condensation, the distant wind from the plateau's edge. A grazer standing still on the rock receives a composite picture of its environment through the soles of its six feet—the location of nearby water (which subtly changes the stone's resonance), the position of other grazers (detected through their footfalls, however soft), even the presence of the buried machinery (which the animals treat as terrain, navigating around the warmer patches or pausing on them in what might be thermal comfort or might be something else entirely). They feel the world rather than see it. They are creatures of pressure and temperature and vibration, and the plateau is their instrument.

Their necks are longer than a deer's, more slender, with a gentle curve that the flat light renders almost calligraphic against the gray-white sky. The head is small, narrow, tapered to a muzzle that is not hard like a horse's but softer, almost leathery, adapted for suction rather than cropping. They drink by pressing the muzzle into the shallow pool and drawing water through a narrow mouth with a motion that is less lapping and more inhaling—a long, continuous pull that can drain a hand-sized pool in seconds. The throat works visibly as they swallow. Then they lift the head, stand motionless for a moment—processing, perhaps, or simply existing in the interval between one action and the next—and walk on.

They do not eat. This is the strangeness you keep returning to. They drink the mineral-saturated water from the shallow pools, but there is no grazing behavior in the conventional sense. They do not crop the waxy plants. They do not consume lichen or stone moss. They do not eat each other. They do not eat anything visible. **The water must be sufficient.** The mineral content of the pools—leached from the rock, concentrated by evaporation, possibly supplemented by whatever the buried machinery draws from below—must contain enough of what they need. Calcium. Iron. Sulfur compounds. Something in that water sustains an animal that weighs perhaps two hundred kilograms, that walks kilometers each day, that maintains a thick double coat in temperatures that sit perpetually near freezing. It is not a diet you can explain through ordinary biology. But nothing about these animals is ordinary biology. They are a local solution to a local problem, evolved or adapted within this closed system, eating what the plateau provides.

There are young. You see one—smaller, its legs proportionally longer, its fur slightly darker, a dirty gray that will pale with age. It stays close to an adult, matching the slow pace, stopping when the adult stops, drinking where the adult drinks. It does not play. It does not run. There is no excess energy here for anything so wasteful as play. It learns the circuit by walking it, and it will walk that circuit for the rest of its life, which may span decades—the low metabolic rate, the slow movement, the minimal stress of an environment with no predators suggests an animal that ages at a pace comparable to the erosion of the stone beneath it.

When a grazer dies—and they do die, eventually, the body wearing out in the way all bodies wear out, the system finally unable to sustain itself—it collapses where it stood. The cold preserves the body for a long time. The fur holds. The skin dries and tightens. Eventually, over months or years, the moisture from the mist works into the remains and the body softens, settles, flattens. The minerals return to the stone. The fur becomes a pale mat on the rock that the wind does not move because there is no wind. The plants may colonize the site—the hooked seeds catching in the remaining fur, finding in the body's minerals a richer substrate than bare stone. A grazer's death is not a loss to the population. It is a redistribution, a slow return of materials to the system that built them.

## The Plants

**They should not be here.** That is the first thought—not a judgment but a recognition that the presence of photosynthetic organisms at this altitude, in this cold, with this little light, challenges what you know about where plants can grow. But they are not, strictly speaking, photosynthetic. Or if they are, the photosynthesis is auxiliary, a secondary process supporting a primary metabolism that draws from something else.

They grow in clusters. Not mats—not the sprawling, interconnected coverage of moss or lichen—but discrete groupings, each plant an individual, each cluster a colony of individuals that have found the same favorable microsite. The favorable sites are predictable: near the moisture pockets, near the warm cracks where the machinery vents, in the shallow depressions where mineral concentration is highest. The plants are opportunistic in the most patient sense of the word. They do not compete. There is enough for all of them, barely, and the margin is so thin that competition would exhaust the system. They coexist because coexistence is the only viable strategy.

**The leaves are the most visible part.** Waxy, oval, no larger than a thumbnail, arranged in opposite pairs along stems that are reddish-purple and wiry. The wax is functional—it traps the mist's moisture, channeling droplets down the stem to the root system, which is not a root system in the conventional sense but a network of fine threads that spread across the rock surface rather than penetrating it. The plants do not dig into the stone. They hold onto it. They cling. The roots secrete a mild acid that dissolves the rock's surface layer at a molecular level, releasing minerals that the plant absorbs through its threaded holdfasts. This is their food. The water is their medium. The light is supplementary. They are rock-eaters, stone-dissolvers, chemists of the mineral frontier.

They do not flower. Reproduction is vegetative and seed-based simultaneously—new stems sprout from the spreading root network while tiny hooked propagules form at the leaf bases, detachable units that the grazers carry in their fur. The propagules are almost weightless, smaller than a grain of sand, produced in quantities that seem excessive until you realize that the probability of any individual propagule landing on a viable microsite is nearly zero. The plants compensate through volume. Millions of propagules, scattered across the plateau by the hundreds of thousands of grazers, and a handful find purchase each year. This is enough.

**They are old.** Not as individuals—individual plants may live decades, perhaps centuries in the slow metabolism of this place—but as a species, a form. They have been here as long as the grazers, as long as the mist cycles, as long as the machinery. They are part of the system's original configuration, whatever that was. They did not arrive after the machines were buried. They were already here, or they were planted, or they co-evolved with the infrastructure in a way that blurs the distinction between natural selection and engineered design. The warm cracks where the plants grow largest are also the cracks where the machinery's heat vents most efficiently. Is this coincidence? Is this adaptation? Is this what the system was designed to do—support a surface ecosystem through subsurface heat management? You cannot tell. The plants cannot tell you. They grow where they grow, and they have been growing there for a very long time.

## The Burrowing Things

**Smaller lives exist beneath the grazers' notice.** In the cracks and fissures of the plateau—narrow gaps in the stone, some natural, some perhaps structural, remnants of the buried infrastructure's surface access points—there are animals. They are rarely seen. They emerge from the fissures at intervals that do not correspond to any cycle you can identify, drink from the nearest moisture pool with quick, nervous movements, and retreat. They are fast where the grazers are slow, furtive where the grazers are open.

You glimpse one. It is the size of a rat but built differently—lower to the ground, with shorter legs and a longer body, covered in a fine, dense pelage that is darker than the grazer's fur, almost brown. It has eyes—small, dark, adapted for the low light of the fissures rather than the diffuse brightness of the surface. It does not stay above for more than a few seconds. It drinks and vanishes.

There must be a network of fissures extensive enough to support a population of these animals. How large that population is, you cannot say. You see three individuals over the course of an hour, each at a different location, each emerging from a different crack. They may be numerous or they may be rare. The plateau's fissures are its hidden dimension—the only space where the flat, exposed surface gives way to depth, and in that depth, a separate ecosystem operates on principles you cannot observe.

They may eat the plants. They may eat the mineral deposits themselves. They may eat each other. They may eat something that exists only in the fissures, some organism or material that the surface never sees. The buried machinery must create spaces down there—rooms, corridors, shafts—and the burrowing things may inhabit those spaces, living in the infrastructure the way rats live in human walls, adapted to the hum, adapted to the heat, adapted to the dark.

## The Machines

**The machinery is the ecosystem's skeleton.** This is not metaphor. The plateau's biological inhabitants are arranged around the buried infrastructure the way a body's tissues are arranged around bone. The plants cluster at the warm vents. The grazers walk circuits that correspond to the moisture-pool distribution, and the moisture-pool distribution is influenced by the machinery's thermal output. The burrowing things live in the fissures that are, at least in part, structural features of the buried system. Remove the machinery and the ecosystem would reconfigure—or collapse. The truce between life and industry on this plateau is not a parallel coexistence. It is a dependency.

The hum tells you the system is active, but it tells you nothing about what the system does. The rhythm—two minutes on, one minute off—is too slow for a pump, too fast for a tidal cycle, too regular for a natural process. It may be a heartbeat. It may be a breathing cycle. It may be the operational rhythm of equipment that was designed by beings who valued patience over speed, who built for duration rather than throughput. The machinery does not appear to be degrading. There is no evidence of failure, no sound of grinding or stress, no visible surface disruption that would suggest subsurface collapse. It runs as smoothly as it has ever run, which is to say it runs the way stone erodes—imperceptibly, continuously, without urgency.

The power source is the question you cannot answer. Geothermal energy is plausible—the plateau sits at high altitude, but altitude does not preclude geological heat, and the canyons below suggest tectonic activity that could produce thermal vents. Nuclear is possible but would require fuel that should, by any reasonable calculation, be exhausted after centuries of operation. Something else—something you do not have a framework for—is also possible. The machinery may draw power from the temperature differential between the surface cold and the subsurface warmth. It may harvest energy from the mist cycles themselves, from the kinetic energy of condensation and evaporation. It may run on nothing at all, in the way that certain mathematical processes run on their own momentum once initiated, a self-sustaining reaction that requires no external input.

The machines do not care that you are here. They are not aware of you. They are not aware of the grazers or the plants or the burrowing things. They are not aware. They function. That is their mode of existence—function, continuous function, the execution of processes that were set in motion long ago and that have not been countermanded. The plateau's ecosystem has grown around them the way a forest grows around a river—not because the river intends it, but because the river is there, and life arranges itself around what is.

## What Lives Here, Summarized

**Nothing lives here alone.** That is the essential fact. The grazers need the water. The water needs the mist. The mist needs the canyon systems and possibly the machinery. The machinery needs the power source. The plants need the minerals and the warmth and the grazers. The burrowing things need the fissures. The fissures need the stone and the infrastructure. Everything is connected to everything in a system so tightly bound that the removal of any single component would cascade through the whole like a crack through crystal.

What lives here is a process. A system. An arrangement of stone and water and fur and leaf and metal and heat that has achieved something rare: **stability without stagnation.** The grazers move. The mist shifts. The pools fill and empty. The plants spread, millimeter by millimeter. The machines hum. The burrowing things come and go. Nothing is frozen. Nothing is dying. Nothing is being born in any way that disrupts the equilibrium. The plateau is a clock that runs on water and stone and time, and the hands of the clock are the slow walks of a hundred thousand six-legged animals across a rust-colored surface, under a sky that is the same color as their fur, in a cold that has been here forever and will be here forever, because this place does not change. It simply continues.

The grazers do not know they are part of a system. The plants do not know they are part of a system. The machines do not know anything. And yet the system persists, and in its persistence, it is more alive than any individual creature within it. The plateau lives. Everything on it is a cell in a single, vast, cold, patient organism that breathes mist and speaks in hums and walks on six legs and does not need to be seen to exist.

This is what lives here.
