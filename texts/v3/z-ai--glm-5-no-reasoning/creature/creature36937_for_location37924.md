# The People of the Vertical City

They are not a distinct people. This is the first thing to understand. They do not think of themselves as "mountain people" or "highlanders" or anything that would separate them from others. They are simply those who live in the continuing place, and they have been here long enough that the question of origin has lost meaning.

## Bodies

Their bodies tell the story of altitude. Chests are deep, ribcages expanded to house lungs that work harder than lungs should need to. Their blood carries more hemoglobin than lowland bodies—enough that a visitor can see the difference in the darker flush of their cheeks, the deeper color of their lips. Children born here develop this way naturally. Those who come from elsewhere adapt over months and years, their bodies slowly learning to pull more oxygen from thin air, or they leave.

They are not tall. This is not a matter of nutrition but of efficiency. A smaller body requires less oxygen. Over generations, the people have compacted. Their limbs are proportionally shorter, their frames denser. They are not slight or fragile—quite the opposite. The muscle they carry is lean and persistent, built by the constant work of moving through vertical space. Every task requires climbing. Every journey involves stairs. Their legs are strong in ways that lowland legs are not, accustomed to the particular strain of ascent and descent.

Their skin weathers early. The cold is dry, but the sun is fierce at altitude, the thin atmosphere filtering little of its intensity. Those who work the upper terraces or tend the highest catchment systems have faces marked by exposure—skin thickened and creased, patterns of lines that track the direction of prevailing winds. They do not consider this damage. It is simply what happens to skin that lives in this light.

They dress in layers. This is practical necessity and cultural form. The innermost garments are close-fitting, made from soft woven wool that holds warmth against the skin. Over this, they wear looser tunics and trousers, also wool, sometimes lined with the finer fur of the goat-like creatures that wander the terraces. Outer layers are thicker still—heavy felted wool or leather, sometimes both, often treated with oils rendered from the same animals to shed moisture. Every garment is designed to be added or removed as movement between temperature gradients requires. A person moving from the deep interior chambers to the upper terraces might shed or add three layers in the course of a single journey.

They do not wear shoes in the way lowlanders do. Their footwear is more like mittens for the feet—constructed to hold warmth while allowing the toes to move and grip. The soles are thick felt or sometimes leather, but flexible. They need to feel the stone beneath them, to navigate the uneven surfaces of paths and stairs. A person who cannot feel the ground is a person at risk of falling.

## Movement

They move differently. Visitors notice this immediately. Lowland bodies walk with a certain momentum, a forward lean that assumes level ground and abundant oxygen. The people here move vertically. Their posture is upright but not rigid, weight centered, each step placed with intention. There is no wasted motion. To waste motion is to waste breath.

They rest often, but this is not laziness. They have learned that continuous effort in thin air leads to exhaustion that recovery cannot easily repair. They work in intervals—climbing, carrying, building—then pausing. The pauses are social. They find ledges or corners or the warmth of sun-facing walls, and they sit together. They talk. They watch. They wait for their bodies to be ready for the next effort.

Their paths through the city are not the paths a visitor would take. They know the shortcuts—the hidden stairs, the passages through buildings that function as public thoroughfares despite being interior spaces, the routes that avoid unnecessary ascent by cutting through rather than over. A visitor asks directions and receives an answer that involves "through the weaving chamber" or "past the third cistern" or "behind where the old foundation cracked." These are not landmarks. They are living features of a body that the people navigate as blood cells navigate veins.

They have an intuitive sense of elevation. This is not taught but absorbed. They know, without checking, how many tiers they have climbed, how far they are from water, from warmth, from home. Their bodies register altitude in ways that conscious thought does not need to track. A person can tell you, eyes closed, whether they are in the upper third or lower half of the inhabited city, and can estimate within a few tiers exactly where they stand.

## Daily Life

They wake with the light. The sun's arrival is sudden at altitude, spilling over eastern peaks and flooding terraces with hard, clear illumination. They sleep through the dark hours—a necessity when darkness is absolute and artificial light requires fuel they prefer to conserve. Their days begin with the sun's appearance and end with its disappearance behind western ridges. They do not measure time in hours. They measure it in light and shadow, in the position of the sun relative to specific peaks, in the quality of cold that shifts as the day progresses.

Mornings are for tending the water. This is the first work. The condensation that has accumulated through the night must be gathered before the sun warms the air and the moisture evaporates back into the too-dry atmosphere. They move through the catchment systems with practiced efficiency, checking channels, clearing debris, collecting the precious liquid from basins and cisterns. Water is life here, and they treat its collection with the gravity that deserves.

The gardens receive attention next. The hardy greens and root vegetables that grow in carved beds require care that changes with the season—not warm and cold seasons, but wet and dry, storm season and clear season, periods when the hail falls and periods when it does not. They know these cycles intimately. They have accumulated generations of observation about when to plant, when to cover, when to harvest. Their agricultural knowledge is encoded in practice rather than written down, passed through demonstration and participation.

Midday is for the heaviest work. The sun's warmth, even at altitude, provides enough relief from the cold that labor requiring sustained effort becomes possible. They build and repair. They process materials—spinning wool, working leather, cutting and fitting stone. They prepare food, which at these altitudes means mostly preservation: curing, drying, fermenting in the cold chambers that maintain consistent temperatures year-round.

Afternoons bring the social rhythms. The work of the day accomplished or paused, they gather in the warmer interior spaces. They talk. They share meals. They teach children. They tend to the needs of the elderly and the ill. They discuss what needs discussing—repairs needed, marriages arranged, disputes resolved, observations of weather and wildlife shared. These are not formal councils. They are the ongoing conversation of a community that lives in continuous proximity.

Evenings are for returning. Those who have traveled to distant tiers make their way home before the light fails. The birds settle into their roosts in the eaves. The goat-creatures find shelter in whatever corners will have them. The city grows quieter, the wind's voices more prominent as human voices diminish. They sleep early, and deeply, their bodies exhausted by the day's effort and hungry for the oxygen that sleep does not require.

## Families

They organize themselves in household clusters that span generations. A typical dwelling holds not just parents and children but grandparents, aunts, uncles, cousins—anyone connected by blood or marriage or long proximity who has not yet formed a separate household. Space is limited. Privacy is not expected. They sleep in shared warmth, multiple bodies under thick wool, sharing breath and heat through the cold nights.

Children are raised collectively. This is necessary when every adult has work that requires attention. A child who needs minding is passed from arm to arm throughout the day, learning the city by moving through it, absorbing knowledge through observation and incremental participation. By the time a child can walk and climb independently, they know the terraces around their household. By the time they can carry a load, they are contributing. This is not exploitation in their understanding. It is participation. Children want to be part of what their people do.

The elderly are valued for what they hold: memory, technique, pattern. An elder who has lived seventy years in the city carries knowledge that no book records—subtle signs of weather changes, techniques for working difficult stone, understanding of how buildings shift and settle. They are not warehoused or isolated. They move more slowly, rest more often, but they remain present in daily life, their presence a resource the community draws upon.

Marriages are arranged, but not rigidly. The considerations are practical: which households have space, which have enough workers, which have too few. Emotional attachment matters, but it is not the primary concern. Marriage here is household formation, a uniting of labor and resources. Love, if it grows, grows from shared work and shared space over years.

They do not have large families. The environment does not support rapid population growth. A woman might bear three or four children over her lifetime, spaced carefully, her body given time to recover between. Some children do not survive the cold or the altitude or the ordinary hazards of life in a vertical city. The people accept this without resignation. They grieve, and they continue.

## Death

They do not bury their dead. The ground is stone, and the cold preserves rather than decomposes. Instead, they have places—ledges on the highest uninhabited tiers, shelves carved into cliff faces, locations where the body can be placed to become part of the mountain in a literal sense. The dry cold and the thin air mummify what remains, turning flesh into something like leather, bone into something like the mountain itself.

They mourn with presence. When someone dies, the household fills with people. They sit together. They speak the name. They recall the life. They do not hurry this process. For days, the body lies in the dwelling, watched over, accompanied. The transition from presence to absence is marked slowly, the community absorbing the loss through proximity to what remains.

Eventually, they carry the body to its place. This is work. It requires climbing, effort, the contribution of many hands. The path to the placing-grounds is not easy. It is intended to be difficult. The labor of carrying the dead honors the dead. Those who are strong enough to bear bodies up the vertical paths do so as an act of love.

After placing, they return. They do not visit frequently. The placing-grounds are not for the living. They are for the dead, and for the mountain. Sometimes, a family member might climb to a placing-ground on an anniversary, to sit near the body and speak or think or simply be present. But the dead are not gone. They have become part of the continuing place, part of what holds the city together.

## Belief

They do not have a religion in the way lowlanders understand it. They have no gods, no temples, no prayers. What they have is attention.

They believe in noticing. The mountain speaks through wind, through tremors, through the slow shifting of stone. The weather arrives as a presence to be read rather than a force to be fought. The animals that share their space carry meaning—the behavior of birds predicts storms; the movement of the goat-creatures signals changes in temperature. They attend to these signs not through superstition but through accumulated observation.

They believe in continuation. The city has been here longer than anyone can remember. It will be here, they think, after they are gone. Their task is not to build something permanent but to participate in what continues. Each generation adds what it can, maintains what it received, passes on what it learned. This is not worship. It is participation.

They have rituals, but these are practical. When a new building is begun, they mark the start with shared food and words of intention—not prayers, but statements of purpose. "We build this to hold warmth. We build this to last. We build this for those who will come after." When a child is born, they gather to witness and acknowledge. "This one has arrived. We will teach them what we know." When a couple joins households, they witness. "These two join. May their work go well."

They do not believe in intervention. The mountain does not care about their desires. The weather does not respond to their wishes. They do not ask for favors. They do not expect protection. They understand themselves as living within forces larger than themselves, forces that operate according to patterns they can observe but not control. Their response is adaptation, not appeal.

They have stories, but these are histories. They tell of past storms that damaged whole tiers, of collapses that required rebuilding, of generations that faced starvation when the gardens failed. They tell of individuals—builders, gardeners, water-finders—who contributed something essential. They tell of changes in the mountain itself, the slow settling that their grandparents noticed, the new cracks that appeared in their parents' time. These are not myths. They are records.

## Knowledge

They know what they need to know. This sounds simple, but it is not. Their knowledge is vast, specific, and entirely practical.

They know stone. They know which formations hold water, which will split cleanly, which will crack unpredictably. They can look at a cliff face and read its history—the pressures that formed it, the weaknesses that time has created. They cut stone without measuring devices, fitting blocks by eye and hand, creating joints that hold without mortar because they understand how stone settles against stone.

They know water. They know where it will seep and where it will not. They can identify the subtle color variations that indicate moisture in stone. They have built the catchment systems over centuries, adding to them, refining them, developing an infrastructure of collection and storage that matches the mountain's own hydrology.

They know the cold. They understand its variations, its daily rhythms, its seasonal patterns. They can predict frost formation by the feel of the air. They know which spaces will hold warmth and which will lose it fastest. They build for cold in ways that lowland architects cannot imagine, creating spaces that maintain habitable temperatures through nothing but thermal mass and solar orientation.

They know the animals. They understand the birds that nest in their buildings, the goat-creatures that wander their terraces, the small mammals that inhabit their walls. They know what these creatures need, how they behave, what they indicate about the environment. They do not romanticize these relationships. The goat-creatures are tolerated until they become food. The birds are appreciated until they damage gardens. But they are seen as participants in a shared ecosystem rather than as intruders.

They do not know everything. They have no written records. They have no science in the formal sense. They have no understanding of geological processes as modern science understands them. They do not know why the mountain trembles, only that it does. They do not know why water emerges from deep cracks, only where. Their knowledge is experiential, accumulated, partial—and yet sufficient. It has kept them alive for generations.

## Trade

They are not isolated. This is a common misconception about mountain peoples. They have contact with others, though it is limited by geography.

Paths connect their city to lower elevations—dangerous paths, carved into cliff faces, crossing ridges and descending through passes that are blocked by snow for months at a time. They travel these paths when they must. More often, others travel up to them.

They have things to trade: the fine wool of their high-altitude sheep-equivalents, the specialized herbs that grow only at these elevations, the preserved meats that the cold has cured, the worked leather from the goat-creatures. In return, they receive things they cannot make or grow: metals, certain tools, grains that do not thrive in their terraces, medicines that their own plants cannot provide.

Traders come rarely—perhaps a few times each year, when the paths are clear. They bring news from elsewhere, which the mountain people receive with interest but not urgency. They bring goods, which are exchanged through negotiation that is neither competitive nor generous. Fair trade is the goal. Both sides know what things cost. Both sides know what things are worth. They transact and move on.

They do not leave. Some do, especially among the young—a few each generation descend and do not return. This is accepted. The city does not require everyone to stay. It requires enough to continue. Those who remain do so because the continuing place is their place. They do not judge those who leave. They do not romanticize those who stay.

## Conflict

They have conflicts. They are human, after all. The density of their living creates friction.

Disputes arise over space, over resources, over behavior that affects others. They have no formal courts, no police, no written laws. They have instead a complex system of mediation that depends entirely on reputation, relationship, and community pressure.

When two people or households disagree, they first attempt to resolve it between themselves. If this fails, they bring in others—neighbors, elders, those whose opinions carry weight. The process is slow. It involves much talking, much sitting together, much waiting for the right moment to speak or act.

Sanctions, when needed, are social. A person who has wronged others might find themselves excluded from shared meals, from collective projects, from the informal networks through which information and assistance flow. This exclusion is temporary, its duration determined by the severity of the offense and the responsiveness of the offender. It is not meant to punish but to correct. Reintegration is always the goal.

Violence is rare. This is not because they are naturally peaceful but because violence is costly. In thin air, conflict that escalates to physical effort exhausts both parties. In a dense city, violence draws attention and intervention. In a community where everyone knows everyone, there is no anonymity in which to hide from consequence. They have learned, over generations, that violence rarely produces the outcomes either party desires.

They do not have enemies in the sense of rival groups or external threats. The mountain is not contested. No one else wants to live here. They have no army, no defenses, no weapons beyond those used for hunting and processing. They are not prepared for war because war has never come to them.

## Art

They make things. This is inevitable in a culture where everything must be made by hand. But they do not distinguish between useful objects and beautiful objects. The distinction would make no sense to them.

Their textiles are patterned—not for display but for identification. Each household has designs that mark their work, their garments, their stored goods. These patterns are not explained or interpreted. They simply are, a visual language that communicates belonging and ownership without requiring words.

Their stone work includes decoration, but subtle. A carved line along a lintel. A smoothed curve at a doorway. These are not sculptures. They are the attention of makers expressed in the material they work. The stone is already beautiful; they simply reveal what it could be.

They sing. This is perhaps their primary art form. Singing requires no materials, no space, no preparation. They sing while working, while resting, while climbing. Their songs have no words—only rhythms and melodies that match the pace of effort, the mood of the moment. They do not perform. They sing as birds sing, because singing is what they do.

They tell stories. These are not formal performances. They are conversations that extend, tales that emerge from shared memory, accounts that grow in the telling. They do not have professional storytellers. Everyone tells. Everyone listens.

They do not have visual art that is separate from function. They do not have music that is separate from daily life. They do not have literature that is separate from speech. Their aesthetic sense is woven through everything they make and do, indistinguishable from the practical.

## Personality

They are not homogeneous. Living in the same place under the same conditions has not made them the same people. They have the full range of human temperaments.

Some are energetic, driven to work, to build, to move. These are the ones who climb to the highest terraces, who take on the heaviest loads, who push for expansion and improvement. They are valued, but they are also watched. Energy at altitude can lead to exhaustion. The community protects its driven members from themselves.

Some are contemplative, content to sit and watch, to think before acting. These are the ones who notice changes first, who remember patterns, who provide the observation on which practical decisions depend. They are valued. But they are also nudged. Contemplation without action serves no one.

Some are social, drawing others into conversation, organizing gatherings, maintaining the bonds that hold the community together. They know everyone, remember everything about everyone, function as living archives of relationship. They are valued. But they can also exhaust. Not everyone wants constant connection.

Some are solitary, happiest when working alone, climbing alone, being alone. They are the ones who volunteer for the remote tasks—tending the highest catchment systems, checking the placing-grounds, monitoring the paths. They are valued. But they are also checked on. Solitude can become isolation.

They love and hate and fear and hope. They have favorites and feuds. They carry grudges and let them go. They laugh at stupid things and weep at unexpected moments. They are fully human, constrained but not flattened by their environment.

## The Young

Children here grow up with a different relationship to danger than lowland children. They are not protected from awareness. They learn early that a fall is possible, that cold can kill, that water is precious. They are taught to pay attention—not through fear but through practice.

They play, but their play mimics the work they will do. They build with stones, pretending to be architects. They carry small loads, pretending to be porters. They tend small gardens, pretending to be growers. Their games are rehearsals.

They explore. Every child knows parts of the city that adults rarely visit—crawlspace tunnels, hidden ledges, the forgotten corners that adults have outgrown. They return with stories that may or may not be true. The adults listen. Sometimes they learn something.

They grow into responsibility gradually. There is no moment when a child becomes an adult. There is only increasing capacity, increasing contribution, increasing recognition. A person is considered adult when others start relying on them. This happens at different times for different people.

They sometimes chafe against the constraints of their environment. They climb higher than they should. They challenge the cold without proper clothing. They test limits. This is expected. It is how they learn where the limits actually are.

## The Old

Elders here are not relic populations. They are active participants until they cannot be.

They work as long as they can move. The work changes—less carrying, more teaching; less climbing, more sitting in warm spaces doing handwork—but it remains work. An idle elder is a concern. It means the body has failed before the mind.

They hold memory. They are the living archives of technique, pattern, story. They are consulted. They are listened to. Their opinions carry weight precisely because they have seen more than others.

They prepare for ending. They do not fear death, but they approach it deliberately. They settle what needs settling. They say what needs saying. They choose what they will wear to the placing-ground. They participate in the planning of their own departure.

They remain present. They do not withdraw from community. They stay in the flow of conversation, relationship, activity. They are not warehoused. They are not dismissed. They are integrated until integration ends.

## Visitors

They receive few visitors. The paths are difficult, and the destination offers little that cannot be found elsewhere.

But visitors do come—traders, researchers, the occasional wanderer seeking experience. The people receive them with hospitality that is neither warm nor cold. They are given space, warmth, water. They are watched.

Visitors are obvious. They move wrong. They hurry. They labor at altitudes that the locals navigate easily. They do not know where to look, how to see, what to notice.

Some visitors want to learn. These are tolerated, perhaps even appreciated. They ask questions. They listen to answers. They try to understand. The people share what they can, though some knowledge cannot be transmitted through words.

Some visitors want to teach. These are difficult. They arrive with assumptions about what the mountain people need, what they lack, what they should change. They do not stay long. The environment itself rejects them—they cannot acclimate, cannot adapt, leave early or leave ill.

The people do not try to impress visitors. They do not perform their culture. They simply continue being what they are. Visitors see what they see, understand what they can. The continuing place does not adjust for observation.

## What They Are

They are participants. This is the word that best captures what they understand themselves to be.

They do not think of themselves as conquering the mountain. They do not think of themselves as surviving despite conditions. They think of themselves as part of a system that includes stone and wind and water and bird and creature and cold. They participate in this system. They contribute to it. They draw from it. They will eventually return to it.

They are not ambitious, in the sense that lowland cultures understand ambition. They do not seek to grow, to expand, to accumulate, to dominate. They seek to continue. This is a different orientation, one that prioritizes endurance over achievement, balance over growth.

They are not happy, exactly. Happiness suggests a state of satisfaction that they do not necessarily experience. They are present. They are engaged. They are sometimes joyful, sometimes sorrowful, sometimes bored, sometimes fascinated. They experience the full range of human emotion, but they do not organize their lives around emotional states. They organize their lives around continuation.

They are not unique. This is important. They are humans adapted to specific conditions, nothing more. They have no special wisdom, no secret knowledge, no spiritual superiority. They simply live where they live, and they have found ways to do so that work.

They are. They continue. This is enough.
