They were here before the rock opened.

That is the fact you have to sit with first, before anything else about them makes sense. Before the plateau sheared away and let the light in, before the granite split and fell and left this vast breathless hollow, before the mineral water pooled and the pale fungus ringed it in perfect circles—before any of that existed, they were already here. In smaller places. In narrower cracks. In the deep, slow fissures that thread through all bedrock if you go deep enough, the capillaries of the geologic body. They lived in those, populations isolated from each other by walls of solid stone, each crack containing its own small genetic universe, its own dialect of click and chirp, its own variations on a body plan that was already, in those cramped early days, becoming something strange.

They were smaller then. They had to be.

You have to understand what a colony like this remembers. Not in the human sense—not narrative, not event. But the body remembers. The wing membrane remembers the dimensions of the crack it was born in, adjusts its span in the next generation, and the next. The claw remembers the texture of the stone it grips. The ear—those extraordinary ears, so much more complex than a bat's, so much more precise—remembers what it needs to hear in order to survive, and it shapes itself around that requirement across hundred of generations, thousands, until the listening has become an act indistinguishable from being. Each isolated fissure-population evolved along its own trajectory. Drift. Selection. The slow blind artistry of deep time working on creatures that had nothing to do but adapt to the particular shape of their particular darkness.

And then the rock opened.

---

It would be wrong to call it a catastrophe. Catastrophe implies something that shouldn't have happened, something destructive. For them, the rupture was simply... event. A sudden change in available volume. The ceiling came off their world. The walls fell away. A space the size of—what? You have no good comparison. A cathedral is too small. A canyon is the wrong shape. It was simply *room*, suddenly, after millennia of none, and they poured into it the way water pours into a channel that has been dry for a thousand years. Not frantically. Not desperately. Just inevitably.

The first ones to arrive were the deep-wall populations, the ones from the fissures closest to the rupture. They found the hollow empty—or nearly so, just the damp stone and the mineral water already pooling in the fresh fractures—and they settled into it with the ease of creatures recognizing a home they hadn't known they were waiting for. The hollows in the wall, those thousands of small crevices and shelves, weren't carved by water or geology. They were carved by bodies. By the same bodies, returning to the same places, generation after generation, the slight acid of their skin oils and the pressure of their grip slowly reshaping the stone. The walls are ribbed because *they* ribbed them.

Then the other populations came.

This is where it gets remarkable, and you have to think about it slowly. The fissure-populations had been separated for—how long? You can't know. Long enough to have become distinct. Different sizes. Different fur densities. Different dialects of sound. Different ear structures, different wing-fold patterns, different metabolisms tuned to different temperatures and different mineral contents in their drinking water. They had become, in the quiet isolation of the deep rock, almost different species.

And they came together in the hollow, and they did not compete.

---

You have to understand what they are. Not bats. The body is wrong for bats—too large, too dense in the chest, the wing membrane too thick, the legs too articulated, too capable of walking, of climbing, of gripping. The face is wrong too. Bats have a particular look, a particular architecture of nose and jaw that says *bat* to the human eye. These don't. Their faces are longer. The features more distributed. The noseleaf—if you can call it that—isn't a single blade but a complex of folds and ridges, like a topographic map of a place that exists only in sound. The ears are enormous, not cupped like a bat's but flattened and ribbed, extending back along the skull in a way that makes the head look almost flat, almost spread. They hear in dimensions you can't conceptualize. Not just echo, not just direction. Texture, density, temperature, the water content of the air ahead of them, the specific mineral signature of each pool they pass over. They hear the world the way you see it—in full, in color, in depth.

And the sound they make. The sound is not the screech of a bat, not the chirp of a bird. It is something else entirely. Each individual produces a continuous low-frequency emission, barely audible to human ears, that overlays and interlaces with the emissions of every other individual in the colony. A million voices, each slightly different in pitch, in rhythm, in harmonic structure, combining into a single field of sound so dense and so complex that it constitutes, in aggregate, something like a shared perceptual space. They don't just hear their own echoes. They hear each other's echoes. They hear the echoes of echoes, the interference patterns where one voice's sound bounces off another body and returns modified, carrying information about that body's position, its movement, its health.

They are, in a sense that is almost but not quite metaphorical, a single organism.

Not like bees. Bees are individuals in service to a collective. These are more like neurons—each one a processing node in a sensory network so vast that no single member can perceive its full scope. The colony knows things that no individual in it knows. It knows the shape of the entire hollow, the volume of every pool, the location of every crack and fissure and stress fracture in the walls. It knows where the condensation is thickest, where the stone is warmest, where the insects breed in greatest density. It knows these things the way your body knows the position of its limbs—not through any single act of perception but through the accumulated, integrated, constantly-updated input of a million separate sense organs, all feeding into a system that has no center, no brain, no single point of integration.

Just sound. Just the endless, layered, harmonically complex murmur of a million bodies making small adjustments in the dark.

---

They are pale. You notice this when one passes close enough to feel the air from its wings—that particular cool displacement, damp, carrying the faint mineral smell of the stone. The fur is not white but *absent of pigment*, the way deep-cave organisms are absent of pigment, the body having long since abandoned the unnecessary expense of manufacturing color in a world that has no light to see it in. Against the dark stone, they look almost luminescent, though they are not. They simply register against the background as *lighter*, and in the flat dim glow filtering down from above, that is enough to make them seem to glow.

The wings are the strangest part. They fold against the body in ways that seem to involve too many joints, too many possible configurations. You watch one settle into a wall hollow and the wings don't simply fold—they *collapse*, each membrane segment finding its own position against the body, against the stone, against the wings beside it, until the whole creature looks less like an animal and more like a piece of the wall itself, a slight protrusion, a fold in the rock. They are camouflage, but not visual. The camouflage is acoustic. Folded like that, the body absorbs sound rather than reflecting it. The individual disappears into the wall, into the colony, into the field. Only when it unfolds—spreading those pale membranes, launching itself into the air—does it become, acoustically, a distinct object. A node in the network. A voice in the chorus.

They sleep, or something like sleep. Not all at once. A colony this large doesn't sleep all at once—it can't afford to. The perceptual field must be maintained. So they cycle, individuals going quiet for periods—minutes, hours—and then resuming, their voices re-entering the chorus at slightly different frequencies, slightly different rhythms, as if they are reporting back what they learned in the silence. You wonder if they dream. You wonder what a creature that perceives the world primarily through sound would dream *of*. Perhaps silence. Perhaps the shape of the hollow, rendered in the absence of its own voice. Perhaps the other colonies, the ones that stayed behind in the deep fissures, the ones that didn't come to the hollow, the ones that are still there, still small, still isolated, still listening to the particular dimensions of their particular cracks.

They hunt the pale insects that breed in the mineral pools—small, nearly transparent things that skim the water's surface, that cluster at the edges where fungus meets stone. The hunting is not individual. A group of fifty will rise together, fly in a formation that isn't a formation but a *shape*—a shape determined by the acoustic properties of the space they're entering, the density of the prey, the movements of other groups—and they will sweep through a corridor of air, taking what they take, and return to the wall. The efficiency is extraordinary. Nothing is wasted. No effort is expended on competition, on display, on the territorial posturing that characterizes so many colonial species. They have moved beyond that, or beneath it, or sideways to it. They are a system, and systems don't compete with themselves.

---

You wonder about the young. You don't see them, but you know they must exist—the colony is not declining, not aging toward extinction. Somewhere in the wall, in the deeper hollows, in the crevices too small for you to notice, there are young. Born pale and small and already listening. Already adding their voices to the chorus. The young are important not because they represent the future—the concept of futurity means nothing to a creature that lives in a world without seasons, without change, without the pressure of time's arrow—but because they represent *new input*. Each new voice in the colony is a new frequency, a new harmonic, a new node in the network. The colony's perceptual resolution increases with each addition. It sees more clearly. It hears more precisely. The world becomes more itself with each birth, and this, perhaps, is the closest they come to a purpose: not survival, not reproduction, but *resolution*. The sharpening of a collective sense so vast and so fine that the stone itself must know it is being perceived.

They do not know you. You are not in their history. Nothing your size has ever entered this place, has ever walked the plain between the pools, has ever displaced the air in the corridors they fly through. You are an anomaly in a system that has spent its entire existence eliminating anomalies. And yet they do not react to you with fear, with aggression, with the startled flight that characterizes so many colonial animals when confronted with the unfamiliar. They react with *incorporation*. Your body, walking across the stone, becomes a feature of their acoustic landscape. They fly around you not because they fear you but because you are an obstacle, and the system accounts for obstacles the way a river accounts for a stone—by flowing around it, immediately, without effort, without consideration.

A few come close. You feel the air from their wings. You smell the mineral damp of their bodies. You hear, up close, the individual voice—so small, so thin, so different from the overwhelming aggregate that you realize the colony's sound is not a sum but a *synthesis*. No single voice could produce what you hear. What you hear is an emergent property, a thing that exists only in the interrelation, only in the between.

They look at you. You think they look at you. Their eyes—if those are eyes, those pale, recessed, barely-differentiated structures on either side of the noseleaf complex—are not vestigial but they are not primary. They function at the very bottom of the luminance range, in the flat gray glow that filters down from above. They see, if they see, a shape. A density. A thing that is not stone and not water and not colony. They register you. They do not interpret you. There is nothing in their perceptual vocabulary that corresponds to *human*, to *visitor*, to *stranger*. You are simply a new kind of object in a world that has, until now, contained only the objects it has always contained.

You change nothing, and this is the most important thing to understand about them. They are not waiting to be discovered. They are not living in expectation of contact. They are not incomplete without your observation, your naming, your taxonomy. They are, in the fullest sense of the word, *sufficient*. A world that has decided what it is. A life that has found its equilibrium and holds it with a stubbornness that feels almost geological, as if they have become part of the stone not by dying but by living so long, so completely, so perfectly adapted to this place that the distinction between organism and environment has ceased to mean anything.

They are the Lower March. The Lower March is them. And you are passing through.
