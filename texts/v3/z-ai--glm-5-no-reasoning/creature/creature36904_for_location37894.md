# What Lives in the Seep

I have to be careful with this word, *lives*. The Seep has made it unreliable.

---

The first thing to understand is that nothing here chose to be here. The salamanders did not migrate into the darkness. The thread-spinners did not descend from some surface ecosystem that lost its light. The reeds did not have ancestors that once photosynthesized. These things emerged *from* the Seep the way the water emerges from the fissures — slowly, under pressure, already shaped by the place they would inhabit. They are not adaptations. They are secretions.

This is the first wrongness, and it is the one that matters most.

The blind salamanders — pale, elongated, their limbs nearly vestigial — move through the ink-dark pools with the patience of circulation. They do not hunt. They do not flee. They press themselves against the roots of the reeds and absorb what they need, directly, through their skin. Their bodies are permeable. They are filters shaped like animals, and they process the water as it moves through them, taking what the Seep has dissolved and passing the rest along. Their waste is not waste. It is a stage in a chemical sequence. They live for years — decades, possibly — and they grow slowly, adding segments the way a stalactite adds layers. When they die, they dissolve. Rapidly. Their bodies come apart in the warm water and become part of the substrate again, and the dissolution is not decay. It is delivery.

There are larger things in the deeper pools. The flashes of actinic light catch them sometimes — shapes that hold still during illumination, as if they know that stillness is invisibility. They are not salamanders, though they share something with them. Their bodies are longer. Their segmentation is more pronounced. Some of them appear to have structures along their flanks that move — gill-like, but rhythmic, mechanical, operating at a pace that does not match respiration. They may be the adult form of the salamanders. They may be something else that the Seep produces when it needs a different kind of processing. They do not come to the surface. They do not need to. The deep pools feed them, and they process what the shallow pools cannot.

They are not eyes. None of these things have eyes. Eyes would be useless here, and the Seep does not produce what it does not need.

---

The thread-spinners are harder to classify because they occupy a category that does not exist outside the Seep.

They cling to the undersides of stone shelves by hooks at the ends of their limbs — four limbs, six, eight, the count varies, and it varies *within individuals*, as if the number is not fixed but responsive to need. Their bodies are soft, compressible, able to flatten into the crevices of the rock until they are nearly two-dimensional. They are warm to the touch. Not the ambient warmth of the Seep's heated air, but their own warmth, metabolic, the warmth of something that is converting energy.

They spin threads. The threads are protein-based but chemically active — they bond to whatever touches them at a molecular level, adhering through reactions rather than stickiness. The thread-spinners use these threads to catch the soft-bodied drifters that fill the air, the spores and microorganisms and damp-borne particles that the Seep produces in saturation. When a thread has accumulated enough material, the spinner draws it in and consumes it. Thread and catch together. The digestive process is efficient and total. Nothing is wasted.

But here is the thing that makes them difficult to describe: they also consume each other's threads. And they consume their own. A spinner will extend a thread, catch nothing, wait, and then pull it back in and eat it. The material is reprocessed. The thread is spun again. The cycle continues. This is not desperation. This is not malfunction. This is how the system maintains itself — everything feeding on everything, everything recycling, the entire ecosystem a closed loop of breakdown and reassembly. The thread-spinners are not hunting. They are *cycling*.

When a spinner dies — and they do die, though the causes are unclear — its body adheres to the stone where it hung, and it stays there. It does not fall. It does not dissolve. Instead, over time, it flattens, hardens, and becomes part of the shelf. The stone of the Seep is, in places, made of compressed bodies. Layer on layer on layer. The rock itself is biological residue, and the organisms that live on it are living on their ancestors.

This is why the stone is warm. This is part of why.

---

The soft-bodied things that drift through the air are the most numerous and the least understood. They are too small to see, even in the actinic flashes. They are perceptible only as sensation — the faint tickle of contact on exposed skin, the damp trails they leave, the way the air feels thick, resistant, *populated*.

They may be spores. They may be single-celled organisms. They may be fragments of larger things that have broken off and are in the process of becoming independent. The Seep does not maintain clean boundaries between its products. A spore may germinate into a reed. A fragment of a dead spinner may drift for weeks and then adhere to a stone shelf and begin to grow hooks. The categories are not fixed because the Seep does not require categories. It requires *function*, and function is fluid.

What the drifters do, collectively, is process the air. They absorb the mineral vapors that the heat releases. They break down the sulfur compounds. They bind the iron particles. They are the atmosphere's immune system, in a sense — or its digestive system, depending on which metaphor you prefer, though both are inaccurate because they imply a body, and the Seep is not a body. The Seep is a *process*.

The drifters are part of the process. So are the salamanders. So are the reeds and the lichen and the thread-spinners and the things in the deep pools. They are all part of the same operation, performing different stages of the same work. The work is transformation. The Seep takes in water and heat and mineral substrate, and it produces something. The living things are the machinery of that production.

---

The orange lichen deserves its own attention because it may be the most important thing here, and it may also be the oldest.

It covers the wet stone in slow explosions — dimensional, ruffled, ridged with complexity. It contracts when touched. It contracts when vibrations pass through the rock. It contracts when the electrical junctions flash. It is *sensitive* in a way that lichen should not be, in a way that nothing that lacks a nervous system should be. It responds to stimulus with speed and specificity.

And it digests stone.

The lichen is the primary agent of erosion in the Seep, but it is not erosion as geology understands it. The lichen does not merely wear down the rock. It *dissolves* it, selectively, taking specific minerals and leaving others, creating the channels and grooves that the seeping water follows. The fissures — the defining feature of the landscape — may not be geological at all. They may be *lichen-made*. The lichen may have carved the fissures the way roots carve soil, and the water may follow the fissures because the lichen made them as pathways for the water to follow.

This would mean that the entire architecture of the Seep — the shelves, the terraces, the drops into black water, the network of cracks that serve as highways for the fauna — is not the product of tectonic forces or erosion or random collapse. It is the product of something growing. Something that has been growing for a very long time. Something that grows at the speed of stone dissolving, which is to say: slowly, patiently, with the determination of something that does not experience impatience.

The lichen may be the Seep's oldest structure. It may be the Seep's *brain*, if the Seep can be said to have one — not a brain that thinks, but a brain that *shapes*, that responds to stimulus, that adjusts its growth based on what it senses. The contraction when touched. The sensitivity to vibration. The way it seems to *listen* through the stone.

If the lichen is the Seep's nervous system, then the industrial structures are its skeleton. The walls and channels and cisterns and towers — the things that were *built*, fitted together with precision — may have been built by the lichen. Or by something the lichen produced. Or by something that the lichen *became*, at a stage in its growth when it needed harder structures than ruffled orange mats could provide. The substance that lines the cisterns — neither metal nor ceramic, something that feels like hardened slime — may be lichen product. May be lichen *tissue*.

This is speculation. The Seep invites speculation and then refuses to confirm it.

---

And then there are the things at the edges of the light.

When the electrical junctions flash — blue-white, actinic, for seconds at a time — the landscape holds. The pools. The shelves. The fissures. And at the periphery, where the illumination does not quite reach, the shapes of larger presences. Bodies in the water. Bodies on the shelves.

I do not know what these are.

I can tell you what they are not. They are not salamanders. They are not thread-spinners. They are not drifters, and they are not lichen, and they are not the industrial structures. They are something that the Seep produces that does not fit into the processing chain. They are not enzymes. They are not filtration. They do not appear to serve a function in the transformation of water and mineral into whatever the Seep is making.

They may be the thing the Seep is making.

They are large. They are still during the flashes — absolutely still, which is wrong, because everything else in the Seep moves at least slightly, the salamanders drifting, the spinners adjusting, the water rippling. These things do not move. They hold. They are heavy with stillness. And they are positioned at the edges — at the boundaries, at the places where the known system meets the unknown darkness. They face outward. Whatever they are, they are oriented toward what lies beyond.

They may be guardians. They may be anchors. They may be the Seep's awareness of its own edges, its way of knowing where it ends and something else begins. Or they may be accumulations — the *something else* that the Seep is producing, gathering in the deep pools and on the far shelves, waiting for the moment when there is enough of it to release. The description says: *when enough of it has accumulated, the Seep will release it — through the pipes, through the channels, through the fissures that go deeper than the stone. Into whatever lies below.*

These shapes may be that accumulation. They may be the product, taking form.

Or they may be something that was already here when the Seep began. Something that the Seep grew *around*. Something older than the stone, older than the water, older than the heat. Something that does not belong to the Seep but that the Seep has incorporated, the way a body incorporates a splinter — surrounding it, sealing it off, keeping it in place without digesting it.

I cannot tell you which. The Seep holds its contradictions, and these shapes are the deepest contradiction. They are the place where the system admits that it does not understand itself.

---

What lives in the Seep?

Everything I have described, and nothing I have described. The salamanders live here, but they are not *of* here — they are *by* here, produced by the process, functioning within it, as separable from it as a wave is separable from the ocean. The thread-spinners live here in the same way. The lichen lives here in the same way, though it may also *be* here in a way that the others are not. The drifters live here the way bacteria live in a gut — present, functional, replaceable.

The thing at the edges of the light — the still, heavy, outward-facing thing — that may *live* here, in the fullest sense of the word. It may be the only thing that does.

Or it may be the one thing that is not alive at all.

The Seep does not resolve this. The Seep does not resolve anything. It produces, it processes, it persists, and it grows, and the things within it perform their functions, and the categories blur, and the darkness has depth, and the stone is made of bodies, and the bodies are made of stone, and the water tastes of sulfur and rust and anything but itself, and the system has been running for longer than the stone has been solid, and the stone has always been solid, and the stone was made yesterday.

What lives in the Seep is the Seep. Everything else is process.
