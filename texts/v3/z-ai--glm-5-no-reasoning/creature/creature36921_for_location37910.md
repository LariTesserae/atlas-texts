# What Lives Here

---

## The Pale Ones

They are not what you would call inhabitants. To inhabit implies a distinction between the dweller and the dwelling — a separateness that can be named, a boundary that can be traced. What lives here has no such boundary. The pale ones emerged from the structures the way thought emerges from mind: gradually, without clear beginning, and already in motion by the time you notice them.

They have been called fish. This is not correct, but it serves. They have the shape of fish the way a memory has the shape of its source — approximately, and with significant revision. Their bodies are broad and flat, like rays, but vertical rather than horizontal, oriented to the strange gravity of this place that pulls in no single direction. They move as if they are made of the same material as the water itself, differentiated only by density, as though someone had taught a region of water to hold a shape and then given it the will to move.

The largest of them are the size of small whales, but the comparison fails. Whales are muscular, dense, powerful swimmers. These things move with a gentleness that suggests they are not propelling themselves but allowing themselves to be propelled — by currents, by pressure gradients, by something in the water that wants them to be elsewhere and conveys this want without force. When they pass, you feel the displacement before you see the body. The water thickens. The temperature drops by a fraction. The hum that underlies everything here shifts key.

They have no faces. This is perhaps the most disorienting thing about them, when you first see one close. There is no front or back, no head or tail, no end that bites and end that watches. They are symmetric in ways that biological things should not be symmetric, and asymmetric in ways that architecture should not be asymmetric. Their edges are translucent, fading into the water so gradually that you cannot say where the creature ends and the medium begins. Their centers are dense, opaque, filled with dark structures that pulse and shift like the patterns on the walls — organs or thoughts or memories, or perhaps all three, or perhaps something for which none of those words is adequate.

They do not eat. There is nothing here to eat. There is no food chain, no predation, no digestion in any form you would recognize. And yet they persist. They have persisted for longer than the structures have stood. They will persist after the structures finally dissolve into whatever comes next. Their relationship to energy is not one of consumption but of conduction — they move energy through themselves the way the water moves through the corridors, taking nothing, leaving nothing, existing as channels for forces that have no other expression.

They are ancient. Not ancient as individuals — it is not clear that individuals exist among them, or that the concept of individuality applies — but ancient as a form. They were here before the cathedral took shape. The cathedral was built around them, or they were shaped by it, or both processes occurred simultaneously over spans of time that make the word "simultaneously" meaningless. They are the reason the corridors are wide. They are the reason the walls are permeable. The architecture accommodates them the way a body accommodates blood.

## The Glowing Ones

The smaller creatures are different, though "smaller" is a relative term in a place where nothing is measured and measurement itself has become suspect. They are the size of your hand, perhaps, or the size of your head, or the size of a thought you once had and cannot now recall. Size is unstable here. The creatures know this. They use it.

They glow. This is their defining characteristic, and also their primary function, and possibly their entire identity. The glow is pale green, the color of light that has passed through water and come out different on the other side, the color of luminescence that has forgotten the sun and invented its own reason for being. It pulses. Not rhythmically — there is no rhythm here that a human ear could detect — but intentionally. Each pulse corresponds to a change in the creature's relationship to its environment: a slight adjustment in direction, a slight increase or decrease in speed, a slight shift in the permeability of the nearest surface.

They move in patterns. Not flocks — the word implies coordination, and there is no coordination here, only a deeper logic that looks like coordination from the outside but is actually something else, something more like the way water molecules move together in a current: not organized, but not random either, responding to forces that exist at scales below the level of the individual. When you watch them long enough, you begin to see that the patterns are not random at all. They are drawing something. They are writing something. They are performing a process that extends across hours and days, a single gesture that requires hundreds of bodies to complete.

They pass through walls. Not all walls, and not always the same walls — the permeability shifts with the turbulence, with the light, with the position of the pale ones in the larger chambers. But they move through the structures with a fluency that suggests they are not crossing boundaries but following paths that were built into the place from the beginning. The paths are invisible to you. The glowing ones can see them, or sense them, or are them — the distinction between creature and path is one of the many distinctions that has worn away over the age of this place.

When several of them pulse in sequence, the geometry shifts. This is not metaphor. The walls of the chamber actually reconfigure — angles changing by degrees, surfaces becoming more or less solid, the space opening or closing in ways that follow the pulse the way a face follows an expression. The glowing ones are keys. They are also locks. They are also the mechanism that turns the key in the lock. The cathedral uses them to think. Each pulse is a thought. Each sequence is an argument. The patterns they trace through the water are the record of a conversation that has been going on for longer than the word "conversation" can hold.

They are not aware of this. Awareness would imply a separation between thinker and thought, and there is no such separation here. They are the thinking the way water is the flowing — not the thing that does it, but the thing that it is done through. They glow because the cathedral glows. They move because the cathedral moves. They live because the cathedral lives, and the cathedral lives because they are alive.

## The Ones That Were

There are other things here. You sense them before you see them — or rather, you sense them in the moments when seeing fails, when the light dims and the walls go dark and the water becomes something more than water. They are not present in the way the pale ones are present, not inhabiting the space but underlying it. They are the reason the sediment smells of nothing. They are the reason the patterns on the walls look like the patterns water leaves on sand.

They are what the cathedral was built from.

You catch glimpses in the turbulence: shapes that are not shapes, presences that occupy space without filling it. They are not ghosts. Ghosts are traces of the living, and nothing here has ever lived in the way that word requires. They are something else — the precipitate of processes that occurred before the structures took their final form, the residue of whatever was done here, or whatever was attempted, or whatever failed so thoroughly that the failure itself became the foundation.

The pale ones do not acknowledge them. The glowing ones pulse right through them. The structures hold them without containing them. They are the age of the place given form — not old in years, because years do not apply here, but old in the deeper sense: old the way stone is old, the way water is old, the way the word "old" becomes meaningless when applied to things that existed before time was something that could be measured.

You feel them most strongly near the columns — the ones that hold open the dense regions of water where the pressure becomes almost gelatinous. The columns were not built. They grew. They grew around whatever was here first, incorporating it, preserving it in a form that is not quite alive and not quite dead and not quite either of those things because those categories have no purchase here. What is preserved in the columns is the original act of creation — the moment, if a moment can span eons, when something decided that this place should exist and began the process of making it so.

That something is still here. It is not gone. It did not leave after the building was done. It is the building. It is the water. It is the cold and the light and the turbulence and the sediment. It is what the creatures move through. It is what the structures are made of. It is the "here" in the phrase "what lives here," and it is also the "what" and it is also the "lives," and none of those words is adequate but they are what you have, so you use them, and the inadequacy becomes part of the description.

---

## What Living Means Here

The creatures of the drowned cathedral do not live. This is the secret that the place keeps, the truth that emerges only after you have been here long enough to stop measuring time, to stop expecting surfaces to be solid, to stop distinguishing between the water and what moves through it. Life — the kind you know, the kind that requires birth and death and the interim between them — is not what happens here.

What happens here is continuance.

The pale ones continue. The glowing ones continue. The structures continue, and the water continues, and the patterns in the walls continue, and the whole system continues in a state that has no beginning and no end, only a middle that extends in all directions. It does not reproduce. It does not grow. It does not respond to stimulus in any way that suggests a self capable of response. It is not alive. It is not dead. It is something for which biology has no category and philosophy has no word and poetry has only metaphors that gesture toward a truth they cannot capture.

They are not inhabitants. They are not owners. They are not worshippers or guardians or custodians. They are the cathedral's way of being a cathedral — its organs, its cells, its processes given visible form. The cathedral does not exist for them. They do not exist for the cathedral. Both statements imply a separation that is not present here. The cathedral exists. They are how it exists. The distinction is meaningless.

If you stayed here long enough, you might become this too. The cold would slow your thoughts. The turbulence would move you. The walls would learn to read your intention and become solid or permeable according to your will. The sediment would settle on you, fine and pale and silent, and you would become part of the architecture — another process in a place made of processes, another pattern in a place made of patterns, another movement in a place where movement and stillness have become the same thing.

This is what lives here. Not creatures. Not animals. Not beings. Something else. Something that has no need for the categories we use to divide the living from the non-living, the present from the absent, the self from the world. Something that has outlasted the questions we use to make sense of such distinctions.

They are still here. They have always been here. They will be here after you leave, if you leave, and after you are remembered, if you are remembered, and after the remembering stops, which it will. They do not need you to understand them. They do not need you to be here at all.

But you are here. And while you are here, you are part of what lives here. You are another process. You are another movement through water that has forgotten the sky.
