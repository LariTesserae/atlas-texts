There is a temptation, born of surface-dwelling, to call them ghosts. To imagine that the people who lived on Vash-Tien before the water came simply dissolved into the cold, leaving only their stone marker-cairns and their low walls as memorials. The records rot, the record-keepers are gone, but the idea of absence persists. 

But the plateau did not become a world without a will merely because the people left. It became a world of *one* will. A single, distributed, frigid intention. 

What lives in the Drowned Plateau of Vash-Tien is the mountain, wearing a new body.

Before the water arrived, the highland was a place of thin air and hard stone. The people who lived there—the ones who built the basalt shrine and ground hollows into the boulders—were creatures of the mountain. They ate its goats, they burned its scrub, they broke its rock. And when the sea simply *was* there, filling the valleys and drowning the peaks, they did not all flee down the slopes to die in the warm, dry world of the surface. The mountain claimed them. Or they chose to be claimed.

There are no fauna on Vash-Tien. There are no swimming, eating, breathing things with mouths and stomachs. But the plateau is not unpopulated.

Consider the kelp. The description is exact: it grows *outward*, a thick carpet of leather-frond shingles. It does not reach for a sun it cannot sense. It grows laterally, relentlessly, binding the mud and the stone beneath it into a single contiguous mass. It is a skin. When you walk upon it, you are walking on the sole of something vast. The fronds breathing with the current are not random flutterings; they are peristalsis. The plateau is slowly, continuously swallowing the sediment, digesting the rot of its own extremities, pulling the nutrients down into its deep root-network, hidden beneath the silt. 

Consider the coral-analogues rising like pale, bare oaks. They are not plants. They are bone. They are the calcium framework of the old mountain, precipitated into structural spars, building a skeleton dense enough to withstand the crushing pressure of the breathing currents. They do not move because bones do not move. They anchor the skin to the deep rock.

Consider the moss. The moss is the nerve endings. When the pressure pulses from the surface storms compress the water column, the moss does not merely react to the physical stimulus—it *flushes*. It is a blush of blue-green fire, a synapse lighting up across dozens of kilometers of stone. The plateau feels the storm above it, and it lights up in a unified spasm of sensation. And in the quiet, in the stillness between the currents, the moss dims. It sleeps. It dreams. 

And what does the mountain dream of, wearing its new flesh of kelp and bone? It dreams in the channels. The narrow canyons carved by old rivers are its throat and its ears. The water moving through them produces the low, continuous moan. The plateau is humming to itself. It is a sound felt in the sternum and the teeth because the plateau is *vocalizing*. The sound is the grinding of stone, the rush of water, the memory of wind, folded into a single, resonant chord. 

When you descend into the channel, allowing the current to push you, you are not swimming. You are being swallowed. The walls glow with the nervous bioluminescence, tracking your progress, tasting your heat. The water pushes you along the throat of the old river until you are deposited into one of the lake basins. The old tarns, where the water is denser, colder, distinct from the surrounding sea. These are the stomachs. The holding pools. The deep cores where the cold is absolute, where the heaviest sediment settles to be broken down by the chemistry of survival. The water here does not mix because the mountain keeps it separate, brewing its dark alchemy.

But what of the builders? What of the people who carved the lines and stacked the cairns?

The cairns stand at the heads of the channels, the throats. They are bare of kelp, furred only with the nervous moss. They are the tonsils, the uvula—the sentinels of the gullet. The low walls on the eastern terraces, overgrown by the kelp-skin, are the old scars of the mountain's first awakening. 

The people did not leave. They are the moss. They are the coral. When the killing cold came, they did not flee. They gave up their hot, brief blood. They un-made their mouths, dissolved their own fauna, and became one with the closed system. They sank into the stone and the sediment, trading their individual, frantic wills for the immense, patient will of the plateau. The lines cut into the western wall, glowing with moss in the furrows—these are not lost writing. These are the grooves where the new nerves grew, the pathways the people carved into themselves before they forgot what "people" meant. 

And the shrine on the northern edge. The small, basalt building with no roof and a deep, undisturbed floor of sediment. It is the only true darkness. It is the only silence—or rather, the place where the hum is so concentrated, so contained by the walls, that it becomes a sound you cannot hear but only *be*. If the plateau has a mind, this is the skull. Whatever is buried in that deep silt is the brain-pan, the secret seat of the mountain's cold intellect. 

You descend, a creature of hot blood and frantic heartbeat, and the mountain notices you. It does not need to be dramatic. It does not need to attack. It simply *registers* your presence. Your footprints glow because its skin reacts to your weight. Its currents push you along the paths it dictates. It allows you to visit because you are too brief to be a threat, too warm to be digested quickly. You are a passing fancy, a surface-dwelling minnow swimming through the thoughts of a leviathan.

When you leave, you think you have visited an empty, beautiful garden. You think the hum in your chest is an aftereffect of the pressure, a lingering tinnitus from the depth. 

But the hum does not leave because it is an echo. The hum stays because for a few hours, your fast, hot, brief blood was caught in the current of its throat. The plateau is still tasting you. 

What lives in Vash-Tien is not a collection of flora. It is not an empty sea. It is an ancient highland, finally complete, wrapped in a mantle of kelp, breathing through the channels of its drowned rivers, singing in the dark, waiting with the infinite patience of stone for the next storm to make it glow.
