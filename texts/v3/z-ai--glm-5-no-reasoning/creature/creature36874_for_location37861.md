# The Architects of the Respiring

They do not think of themselves as a people. This is the first thing to understand. The concept of "people" requires boundaries—self and other, human and nature, builder and built. These distinctions dissolved centuries ago, or were never drawn in the first place. They are an aspect of the Great Respiring, as much as the insects, the fungal mats, the root-mass itself. Their bones have become part of the substrate. Their waste feeds the gourds they drink from. Their dead don't bury; they place.

---

They are small. Not in stature—they stand perhaps chest-high to an outsider, though "stand" implies a permanence they don't practice. They are small in the sense of density. Their bodies are engineered for the climbing life: long-fingered hands with an extra joint, feet that can grip as well as their palms, spines flex and fluid. Their skin has the texture of bark, the better to blend, and shifts color over generations to match the particular layer they inhabit. Those who live near the clearings carry green mottling. Those in the deep twilight have gone pale, almost translucent, their veins visible beneath skin stretched thin to catch what light reaches them.

They have no name for themselves. Names are for things you might lose, or need to distinguish from other things. They have never encountered anything they would consider "other." When outsiders occasionally arrive—stumbling, heat-struck, dying within days—they are regarded with the same mild curiosity the insects show: something new in the environment, something to be climbed over or routed around. Sometimes useful. The tools outsiders carry, the fabric of their clothes, the metal of their buckles—these get incorporated. The bodies themselves get placed.

---

Their language is positional.

This is the closest approximation, anyway. They don't speak in words so much as in relations—where something is, when it was there, what it was doing relative to what surrounded it. A single utterance might translate roughly as "the place three handspans from where the thick-root bends where water collects after mid-rain" and that location carries so much embedded information—safe footing, potential drink, recent animal passage—that nothing more needs saying. Outsiders who hear them speak describe a constant low murmur, a stream of sound that never seems to pause, and indeed it doesn't. Communication here is ongoing, a background hum of positional updates shared among whoever is within range. Silence would mean isolation. Isolation would mean sinking.

They have words for the animals, though. Dozens of them, each referring not to species but to *role*: the ones that clear, the ones that build, the ones that carry, the ones that digest. They've mapped the ecology of the Respiring with exhaustive precision, not through study but through incorporation. A child learns which creatures to avoid by feeling the tremor of their approach in the root-mass, learns which gourds are safe by tasting the air near their stems. Knowledge here is somatic. It lives in the fingers, the spine, the subtle electric sense of the skin.

---

They do not count.

This is not because they cannot—it's because counting requires stopping, requires holding a number in the mind rather than moving. They understand *more* and *less*, *enough* and *too much*, *sparse* and *dense*. But the abstract concept of discrete quantity has no application in a world where nothing stays discrete for long. Their shelters are never described by size but by capacity: how many bodies can sleep inside before the walls need negotiating. Their paths are never measured by distance but by time: how long before the growth closes behind you.

When they need to mark something—a tool left behind, a gourd not yet ripe, a section of root too unstable for passage—they place a hand on it and make a specific sound, a hum at a frequency outsiders can barely hear. That sound lingers in the memory of whoever passed close enough. Hours later, someone else will *know* that the thing was marked, will *know* what the marking meant, without being able to explain how they know. The knowledge is neither secret nor shared. It simply exists, distributed through the population like spores through air.

---

Their origin is not remembered because they do not remember. Memory, as a faculty of holding onto the past, is not a survival trait here. The past is beneath you, literally beneath you—the compressed bodies of ancestors forming the root-mass you walk on. The future is above you, the layers you haven't yet climbed. The only livable tense is the present, navigated by instinct and distributed awareness. They know the Respiring was smaller once. They know their bodies were different once. They know there were boundaries they don't understand anymore. But these are not memories—they're more like reflexes, flinches encoded in the collective tissue.

Some outsiders have theorized they are the descendants of crashed travelers, survivors of some ancient catastrophe who adapted or died. Others suggest they were always here, evolved in place along with the rest of this impossible ecology. The truth, if it exists, is irrelevant to them. They are not curious about origins. Curiosity implies a mind with enough stillness to wonder, and stillness is death.

---

They do experience something like grief.

It just doesn't look like grief. When one of them dies—falls to a predator, slips into a digestion zone, fails to negotiate a shelter's contraction—the others don't mourn. They *place*. The body is positioned with intention, moved to a specific location where the growth can incorporate it efficiently. Sometimes this happens while the body is still warm. It's not callousness. It's the deepest respect: to become part of the Respiring properly, to feed the system that fed you, to continue in the only way that matters here.

The living carry on without pause. But they carry differently. For days or weeks after a placement, their movements incorporate the missing body's patterns—their preferred paths, their marking sounds, their particular way of testing root-stability. Outsiders who spend enough time to observe sometimes notice this: a gesture repeated by someone who never made it before, a hum at a frequency that belonged to someone else. The dead don't disappear. They distribute. Their knowledge continues to move through living hands.

---

They have no concept of death as an ending.

This is distinct from having no grief. They understand bodies stop moving, stop contributing, need to be placed. But the Respiring doesn't stop. The place they occupied in the distributed awareness of their group remains, gradually filling with new information, new presence, like water finding its level. Death is not the opposite of life here—it's just a change in position, a shift from walking to substrate. They would not understand the question "what happens after you die?" The question presumes a boundary they've never drawn.

Outsiders sometimes call them wise for this. They are not wise. They are not unwise. Wisdom requires a vantage point outside the thing you're observing, and there is no outside here. They don't accept their circumstances or embrace their fate—they simply live it, as fish live water, as birds live air. The concepts of acceptance and embrace would be as meaningless to them as the concept of a number.

---

Their technology is all negotiation.

The woven shelters, the trimmed paths, the gourds they cultivate for drinking—none of these are made. They are proposed. A shelter begins when someone finds a section of growth flexible enough to train, strong enough to hold shape. They bend stems, tie fronds, hollow out space—but always with the understanding that the plant will push back. The negotiation can last days or seasons. Some shelters stabilize, becoming semi-permanent fixtures that others can use. Others get overgrown within hours, the growth rejecting the arrangement. There's no way to predict which will hold. The plants have their own intentions, or something analogous.

Their tools follow the same logic. A cutting-edge is not crafted; it's found—a section of stem hardened by disease, a shard of insect-shell with the right fracture-line. They modify what they find, but modification is always a request, not a command. Use the wrong stem for a handle and it will crack mid-swing. Cut the wrong leaf for wrapping and it will seep oils that spoil whatever's inside. They've learned to read the material's willingness, its grain and texture and density. Some things want to be used. Some things will fail you. The line between tool and trap is invisible until you've crossed it.

---

They reproduce, but without pattern.

There are no mating seasons here, no rituals of courtship, no family structures outsiders could recognize. Bodies come together when proximity and instinct align, separate when circumstances shift. Children are born small—small enough to carry in one hand, small enough to lose in a fold of root-mass. They cling to their mothers for the first season, learning the body-knowledge they need to navigate: how to grip, how to test surfaces, how to move without stopping. Then they climb to their own paths.

Mother-child bonds exist, but they're not exclusive. Every adult is responsible for every child they encounter. A lost child will be fed, carried, placed somewhere safe by whoever finds them. The concept of "my child" vs. "your child" requires boundaries the Respiring doesn't support. All children belong to the whole. All adults contribute to their survival. This is not morality. It's infrastructure.

---

They do not dream.

Or rather, they don't dream as outsiders do. Their sleep is shallow, broken, constantly interrupted by the need to shift position, test the surface, adjust to the Resiring's movements. They don't experience the narrative hallucinations that plague sleeping outsiders—being chased, falling, drowning. But they do experience something during rest: a heightened sense of the distributed awareness, a feeling of being multiple, of knowing where others are and what they're sensing. Sleep here is when the boundaries between selves grow thin. Waking is when they harden again.

Outsiders who sleep in the Respiring report vivid dreams of green, of roots entering their bodies, of being digested slowly and pleasurably. This is not what the architects experience. They experience *less* self, not more. They experience connection so profound that the concept of "individual" briefly, blissfully, dissolves.

---

They are not unhappy.

This needs saying because outsiders tend to pity them—to see their lives as impoverished, constrained, dominated by an environment that tolerates them but doesn't care about them. But pity requires a standard of flourishing that has no meaning here. They don't long for horizons they've never seen, don't yearn for firm ground they've never walked, don't miss boundaries they've never drawn. Their lives are as full and complex as any human's—if not fuller. Every moment requires attention, adaptation, skill. Every movement is a small negotiation with a world that's genuinely alive. They don't have the luxury of boredom.

They experience joy, though it looks nothing like human joy. A sudden finding of water. A shelter negotiation that holds. The moment when a child first navigates a complex path alone. These are not triumphs—they're continuations. And continuation is the closest they come to purpose.

---

They will outlast you.

Not as individuals—their lifespans are short, thirty seasons at most, worn down by the constant labor of staying alive. But as a distributed presence, they have already outlasted civilizations. The Respiring was here before whatever catastrophe or chance produced the plateau, and it will be here after whatever comes next. They are its mobile component, its way of moving through itself. They trim and transport and incorporate and place. They don't serve it—they *are* it, in the same way your cells are you.

When you finally leave the Respiring—stumbling out, heat-struck, your skin tingling with residue, your head full of the green sound that will fade but never quite disappear—you might look back and see only the haze. But they're in there. Moving. Negotiating. Humming their positional language into the endless damp air. Not waiting for anything. Not wanting anything you could give them. Climbing, always climbing, because to stop is to sink, and to sink is to join the substrate, and to join the substrate is to continue in the only direction that matters.

The Respiring breathes. They are its breath.
