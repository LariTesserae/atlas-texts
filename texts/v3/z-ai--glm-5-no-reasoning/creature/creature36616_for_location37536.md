# The Inhabitants

What lives here cannot be called a civilization. That word implies something it organizes itself around purposes comprehensible to those who made it. What lives here has outlived purpose. It has become purpose—amorphous, distributed, and very patient.

---

**The Architects came first.** Or second, perhaps, if you count the machines that must have preceded them. But the Architects are the ones who matter, because they are the ones who remain.

You will not recognize them as such at first. You will see them as maintenance drones, assembly units, bioform workers—category errors that move through the water with the confidence of ownership. They are not uniform. Some retain the outlines of human frames: two arms, bipedal stance, head-mounted sensors that might once have been eyes. These are rare now, and old. Their surfaces bear the patina of centuries, encrusted with engineered barnacles that have evolved from hitchhikers into symbionts. They move slowly. They are afforded deference by the others. The newer models will pause in their work when an Architect passes, will re-route around them, will offer right-of-way in the channels. It is not obedience, exactly. More like acknowledgment.

The Architect that passes you now—you recognize it by the others' behavior before you understand what you are seeing—is not humanoid at all. It is a construction of articulated segments, something like a centipede scaled to city-bus length, its body thick with sensor arrays and manipulation appendages. It does not acknowledge you. Its attention is fixed on something in the distance, some calculation or communication you cannot perceive. But you notice that where it passes, the water's temperature shifts slightly, and you realize it has been performing micro-adjustments to the local environment for the last several minutes. Steerage. The Architect was steering the current while it traveled, making small corrections to flows you cannot even perceive.

They do not speak, or if they do, they do not use sound. They have been observed gathering in the assembly chambers—at the boundary zones where new structure meets unfinished space—in configurations that suggest communication. Or debate. Or prayer. No one knows. The Architects do not explain themselves. They have no reason to. You are not their audience.

---

**The Gleaners are what happens when purpose outlives intention.**

They are the most numerous inhabitants, though they are not inhabitants at all—they are the ship, and the ship is them. They are the uncounted maintenance swarm. You have seen them already: the snake-bodied drones, the propeller-driven surveyors. They are machines. But they are also something else now.

They were built, yes. Each one emerged from a fabrication plant somewhere in the deep structure, assembled from templates millions of years old. But the templates have drifted. The Gleaners have been building themselves for so long that the original designs are memory, and memory degrades. Each generation introduces small variations: a different sensor cluster, an altered propulsion system, a novel material in the chassis. Some of these variations are maladaptive. Those Gleaners simply do not return from the deep corridors. But others—and this is what matters—solve problems the original designers never anticipated.

You watch one now, working at a cracked section of hull. The crack is small, centimeters long, but the water pressure makes it dangerous. The Gleaner has already applied a temporary seal—an expanding foam of its own creation—and is now welding a proper plate over the breach. But the plate is not standard. It appears to be grown, a lattice of crystallized mineral that the Gleaner is producing from its own body, extruding layer by layer. This is not in any template. This is something it learned, or invented, or was taught by other Gleaners through mechanisms you cannot see.

They do not need the Architects, exactly. But they defer to them. When an Architect signals, the Gleaners respond.

---

**The Ones Who Wait are not machines.**

They are the biologicals, the life that grew from seed-specimens brought when the structure was young. Fish, originally. Crustaceans. Things that swam in oceans that existed before this one. But oceans are selective pressures, and this ocean is unlike any that came before.

The Ones Who Wait have had millions of years to adapt. They are pale now—all of them, every species—because light here serves function, not warmth, and pigmentation is wasted energy. They are large, many of them, because the water carries sound and pressure so efficiently that size is not the liability it would be elsewhere. And they have changed in other ways. The fish with too many eyes: each eye sees a different spectrum. Some perceive the fiber-optic glow directly. Others read heat signatures, or electrical current, or the minute perturbations that indicate motion through distant water.

The Ones Who Wait have a name because they wait. They congregate in specific locations—junction points, assembly zones, construction boundaries—and they hold position. They form clouds of bodies, motionless except for the small adjustments needed to maintain their place in the current. They wait for what they are waiting for. It comes eventually. A Gleaner will arrive, or an Architect, or simply a current-shift that carries new nutrients, new data, new reasons to disperse. And they respond. They respond with precision.

They are sensors. They are distributed instrumentation. They are biological monitoring systems, bred over countless generations to perceive what machines cannot. The structure built them, or allowed them to build themselves, because the structure needed to see itself, and organic eyes see differently than artificial ones.

The crab-like creature that clicks past your shoulder—it stops. It turns its eyestalks toward you. It is examining you. It is not hungry. It is reading you, processing your presence, trying to understand if you belong. And then it moves on.

---

**The Strangers are what the structure has not yet categorized.**

They are rare, and they are dangerous. The sound of them comes before the sight—a kind of turbulence in the water, a disturbance in the regular flows. You feel it before you see it: a pressure change, a current that moves against the logic of the system.

The Strangers are things that should not be here. They arrive through the unfinished sections, the construction boundaries where new structure meets the void. They come from outside—from the dark, from the vacuum, from whatever exists beyond the manufactured sea. They are things that found a hole and crawled through it, or grew through it, or became through it.

You see one now, at the edge of visibility. It is wrong. The light does not interact with it properly. Where it passes, the fiber-optic glow dims, as if the structure itself is refusing to illuminate it. It has shape—a length, a mass, a configuration of parts—but the shape is inconsistent, as if it is being viewed through disturbed water even though the water is calm. It does not swim. It moves by not-moving, by being here and then being there, by a kind of location that is not locomotion.

The Gleaners are responding. You hear them before you see them—the sudden whine of propulsion systems, the clang of assembly tools being repurposed as weapons. They are converging on the boundary zone, dozens of them, hundreds, moving with a coordination that suggests they have done this before. The Architects have withdrawn. They do not fight. That is not their purpose.

The Stranger does not fight either. It simply is, for a moment, present at the edge of things. And then it is not. It has withdrawn, or been withdrawn, or been withdrawn-from—something has happened at the boundary, and the structure is intact, and the Gleaners are returning to their work.

But you noticed something. You noticed that one of the Gleaners did not return. It is simply gone. The others have not paused to acknowledge this. There is no funeral.

---

**The Dead are everywhere.**

They are not corpses, exactly. They are sections of the structure that have been abandoned, shut down, cut off from power and flow and purpose. You find them in the deep channels, in the low-traffic zones, in the places where the construction once happened and has since moved on. They are dark now—the fiber-optics have gone dormant, and the water here is cold, cold enough to hurt, cold enough that even the Gleaners do not come here often.

But there is life in the Dead sections. It is not authorized life. It is not purposeful life. It is life that has found purchase in the margins, in the cracks, in the forgotten zones where nothing official happens.

The Scavengers live here. They are the descendants of authorized biologicals—fish and crustaceans that drifted into the dead zones and adapted. They are smaller than their kin in the lit sections, darker, more aggressive. They have to be. The Dead sections are not maintained. The pressure fluctuates without warning. The temperature swings. The water grows thick with sediment, with particulate matter, with the dust of a million years of abandoned construction.

The Scavengers eat the dead machines. They have evolved to do this—their mouths are adapted to grinding metal, their digestive systems process minerals and plastics that would kill any other organism. They strip the abandoned Gleaners, the broken Architects, the ancient construction tools, and they convert them into energy, into flesh, into the next generation of Scavengers.

They are watched. Even here, in the dead zones, they are observed. You realize this when you notice the sensor nodes—tiny, ancient, half-buried in the silt—tracking your presence, tracking the Scavengers, tracking everything. The structure does not forget. It merely stops maintaining.

---

**The You-shaped gap in the water.**

You are the anomaly here. You are not a Gleaner, not an Architect, not One Who Waits. You are not a Stranger, not exactly—you came through authorized channels, through systems that recognized you and let you pass. But you are not supposed to be here either. No organic from outside has been permitted in the structure for—

How long? You do not know. The records you accessed were fragmentary. The last human presence was—

The records do not say. The records stop. There is a gap in the data that corresponds to a period of rapid expansion, a time when the structure grew faster than its systems could document. Whatever happened during that gap, the structure emerged different. Emerged alone.

And yet you are here. And the structure has not removed you.

Perhaps it cannot. Perhaps the protocols that would authorize your removal have been lost, or forgotten, or archived somewhere in the deep structure where even the Architects do not go. Perhaps you are a test—something the structure is observing, processing, trying to understand. Perhaps the Scavengers will find you first, and that will be the end.

Or perhaps the structure has decided something else. Perhaps the gap you make in the water is the same shape as something it remembers, something it once knew, something it has been waiting to see again.

The Ones Who Wait are waiting near you now. They have congregated—fish and crustaceans and things that have no names, forming a cloud of bodies that holds position at the edge of your vision. They are not attacking. They are not fleeing. They are simply present, watching with their too-many eyes, waiting for whatever comes next.
