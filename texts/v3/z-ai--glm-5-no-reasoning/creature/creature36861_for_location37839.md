They are called the Slow, though they would not call themselves anything at all, because they have not spoken in three generations.

They live in the spaces between the official infrastructure, in the rooms carved by the city-folk who came before and left. But where those earlier residents built walls of stacked stone to mark boundaries, the Slow have let the boundaries dissolve. A shelf cut into living rock serves as a bed, its surface padded with layers of compressed fern that have grown, died, and regrown so many times that they form a soft, dry mat. A niche for candles holds not tallow but a concentrated mass of the luminescent algae, scraped from the upper walls and pressed into a porous stone vessel where it continues to glow, faintly, feeding on the moisture that condenses inside. The Slow do not keep things. They cultivate.

You would not see them at first. They have lived in the heat and damp for so long that they have become the color of the place—a brown-green that shifts toward umber in the dim light, skin mottled by contact with stone and constant moisture. They are small, not by nature but by adaptation; children born here grow slowly in the warmth and darkness, their bones settling into compact frames. The oldest among them move through the vegetation like part of it, their pace matched to the chamber's own patience. When they rest, they rest against the limestone with their eyes closed, and the moss creeps toward them, and they let it.

They number eleven now. Once they were more. The community that carved the rooms, that built the low walls, that wore paths into the stone with repetitive footsteps—they were the founders, the ones who came down seeking something the surface could not provide. Not refuge, exactly. The Slow do not speak of exile or escape. They speak of nothing at all. But the founders left records of a kind: scratches on stone, counts of days, the careful notation of water levels in the pools. They were still oriented toward the world above, still measuring themselves against its calendar. The Slow have abandoned even this. Time is marked by the flowering things, which open and close on a cycle unrelated to any sun, and by the slow lengthening of vines along the rail tracks, and by the warmth of the stone itself, which pulses with that sub-audible vibration from below.

The children—there are two—have never seen the surface. They were born in the chamber and have known nothing else. Their eyes are large and dark, adapted to the bioluminescent blue-white and the amber flicker of the ancient fixtures. They do not flinch at the flowering things' movement, do not startle when the pale insects skitter across their path. They play games that involve holding very still in the fern beds, breathing in time with the chamber's slow respiration, seeing how long they can go before the vegetation closes around them and they become indistinguishable from the growth. They are very good at this game.

The adults forage. This is perhaps too active a word for what they do. They move through the green tunnels at the pace of water running down stone, pausing to touch a frond here, a vine there. They know where the edible ferns grow, where the small pale fungi fruit behind the rusted signal box, where the water pools that contain the eyeless things that can be caught and eaten raw, their flesh cool and mineral on the tongue. They have no fire. They have no cooking. Food is gathered and consumed in the same slow rhythm as everything else.

The oldest is called Grandmother, a name given by the second generation before they stopped naming. She remembers the surface, though she does not speak of it. She sits in the carved room that was once hers and is now mostly fern, her back against the weeping wall, her breath so slow that the moisture on her skin does not evaporate between inhales. She is the one who reported the shape in the collapsed tunnel, the one who saw something larger moving through the vegetation with purpose. She told this to no one, because there is no one to tell—the Slow do not exchange information they have not directly observed, and none of the others have seen it. But she watches. When the others sleep, she keeps her large dark eyes open, fixed on the corner where the rails end at the wrong wall, the wall that was sealed by something that understood architecture.

She is not afraid. Fear requires a sense of what should be, and she has lived long enough in the chamber to know that what should be has no authority here. But she is watchful. She notes when the vegetation near the sealed wall grows thinner, when the pale insects avoid that corner, when the vibration from below shifts in some quality she cannot name but can feel in the joints of her fingers. She does not go near that wall herself. She has not forbidden the children, because the Slow do not forbid, but she has placed herself between the wall and the places where the children sleep, and she has done this so naturally that it seems like nothing at all.

They wear what the moisture lets them wear. Fabric rots quickly in the chamber; cotton lasts months, wool lasts weeks, leather stiffens and crumbles. The Slow have learned to dress in layers of the broad fern fronds, which are tough and flexible when fresh, and which can be woven loosely to cover what must be covered. Some of them wear the remnants of old clothing—collars, belts, a boot sole used as a scraping tool—but most have given up the surface's textiles entirely. Their feet are bare and broad, the soles calloused into leather of their own making. Their hair grows long and is left to mat, providing warmth in a place where the heat is thick but not quite sufficient for bare skin.

They do not celebrate. They do not mourn. When one of them died, two years ago by the surface count, they carried the body to the descending tunnel, the one where the tracks rust away to nothing, and left it where the green thinned and the dark deepened. They did not watch it go. They did not mark the place. But one of the children reported, weeks later, that something pale was growing in that tunnel where nothing had grown before—a fungus, perhaps, or a flowering thing she did not recognize. No one went to confirm. No one needed to. The chamber takes. The chamber incorporates. What was separate becomes part of the density.

They are not unhappy. This is important to understand, if understanding is possible from the surface, where happiness and unhappiness are measured against expectation. The Slow have no expectations. They have the chamber, which is warm and wet and green and dark. They have the rhythm of sleep and wakefulness that matches the light's weak cycle. They have each other, though they do not speak, though they barely touch, though they move through the space like independent organisms sharing a habitat rather than a community. When one of them passes another, there is a pause—a moment of stillness in which they exist together, breathing the same thick air, feeling the same moisture settle on their skin. Then they continue.

It is not communication. It is something older than communication: the acknowledgment of shared presence, of cohabitation in a place that requires nothing more than continued existence.

They will not be found by the city above. The way up has narrowed further since the founders came down, the collapse and the growth combining to make the passage impassable to anyone who does not know where to place their feet, where to turn their body, where to breathe shallow and slide between stone and vine. The Slow could leave, if they wanted to. They could climb back to the surface, feel the cool air on their wet skin, stand in the light that is not amber or blue-white but the unfiltered color of the sun. They do not want to. The desire, if it ever existed, has worn away like the rusted rails, returned to oxide and carried off by water that never stops moving.

Grandmother, once, nearly went. She was young then, younger than the parents of the children, and she climbed the upward tunnel until she could feel the air change, feel the draft from the surface world. She could have gone further. She could have emerged into the manufactories, found her way to the streets, rejoined the city that had forgotten this place existed.

She turned back. She has never explained why, even to herself. The chamber's pull is not magnetic, not gravitational, not any force that physics names. It is simpler than that. It is completeness. The chamber is whole without her, but she is not whole without the chamber. The heat and moisture have entered her. The dark has shaped her eyes. The silence has taught her stillness. She belongs here now, and here belongs to her, in the small way that something can belong to a place that does not know it possesses.

The Slow do not know they are the Slow. They do not know they are strange. They have nothing to measure themselves against, no mirror but the dark water, no voice but the sub-audible hum. They live, and the chamber lives around them, and the green grows and the water runs and the light flickers its ancient amber pulse, and below it all, perhaps, something moves behind the sealed wall, something that built a door and then filled it with stone, something that wanted to stay hidden or wanted to keep hidden what lay beyond.

The Slow do not worry about this. They do not worry about anything. They exist in the hush, in the heat, in the patient accumulation of green, and they are complete.
