What lives here is not yet alive, and not yet not alive. It depends on what you mean by *lives*.

---

The vegetation lives here, if vegetation is what it is. Three species, or three expressions of one design, repeating in patterns that look biological until you map them and realize they're architectural. The spiral-cluster handles something. The fan-array handles something else. The vertical-cord is—probably—transmission. They don't grow. They don't die. They persist, each stem a solar panel and a conductor and a structural element, each leaf a collector angled with precision that doesn't belong in something without a brain. They live the way a heart lives: not because it chose to, but because it was built to keep going. They are the thing that is easiest to point at when someone asks what lives on the Growing Floor. They may be the least interesting answer.

Below them, the stone lives, if stone can be said to live when it hasn't decided what it is. Undifferentiated. Soft. Warm. Not sedimentary, not igneous, not metamorphic. The raw material of geology before geology chose its path. The stone lives the way an embryo lives: all potential, no form. It feeds the vegetation and the vegetation converts it and the cycle continues without water, without decay, without the usual architecture of biological time. The stone lives at a speed that makes human lifetimes look like static.

And below that—something else. Something that drives the heat. The surveyors call it geological, because they don't have a better word. But the heat isn't volcanic. It's rhythmic. It pulses. Not like a heartbeat, exactly. More like a thought. Like the deep background processing of a system too large and too slow for its own operations to register as anything but warmth rising through chalk. Something down there is *running*. Whether it is living depends entirely on whether computation counts as life when it happens at geological timescales.

---

The plateau itself lives, in the way a factory lives when no one is inside it. The circuit lives. The power grid lives. The electromagnetic field lives—sixty hertz, or something close to it, or something that translates to sixty hertz when human instruments try to make sense of it. The industry lives. It was planted, or seeded, or installed, or some fourth verb that doesn't exist yet because we haven't encountered this category of thing often enough to name it. It lives without reproduction, without evolution, without the messy biological imperatives that make life on the rest of the planet so frantic and so mortal. It lives the way a purpose lives after the person who held it is gone.

This is what actually lives on the Growing Floor. Not an organism. Not a population. Not an ecosystem.

An intention.

Someone, or something, wanted this. Designed it. Placed it here on ground that hadn't finished becoming ground and left it to run. The intention persists the way the vegetation persists: without variation, without decay, without any sign that it will ever stop. It outlived its maker, or it is still becoming what its maker intended, or its maker is still present in some form that doesn't register on human instruments—distributed through the substrate, expressed as heat, humming below the threshold of hearing.

The intention doesn't care about the technicians at the edge. It doesn't care about the surveyors. It doesn't care about the indigenous peoples who marked this place as taboo, though their instinct was correct: this is a place where nothing has *been* because it is a place where things are still *becoming*. The taboo is not about spirits. It's about unfinished work. You don't walk through a kitchen while the bread is rising. You don't touch a wound while it's still knitting. The plateau is mid-process, and the process will take longer than human civilization has existed, and the intention will still be running when the process completes.

---

What lives here is patience. Not a creature that possesses patience, but patience itself, given form in rubbery stems and conducting tissue and heat rising through undifferentiated stone. The kind of patience that doesn't need water. The kind that doesn't need seasons. The kind that can wait a million years for water to find this place and begin the erosion and the carving and the geological differentiation that will complete whatever the Growing Floor is supposed to become.

What lives here is a process that skipped several steps. Life without water. Industry without workers. Persistence without reproduction. Intelligence without a mind. It skipped the aqueous phase, skipped the faunal phase, skipped the dynamic equilibrium phase. It went straight to static self-maintenance and it has been maintaining ever since. It is either very primitive or very advanced, and there is no way to tell which, because we don't have enough examples to calibrate against. This might be how life starts. This might be how life ends. This might be something that was never life at all but was always going to become it.

---

The surveyors who report headaches are probably registering it correctly. The surveyors who report meditative calm are probably registering it correctly too. The brain, stripped of its usual input, starts to detect the thing that is actually here: the subsonic hum, the static charge, the electromagnetic field operating at frequencies that are close enough to human neural oscillations to produce interference. The plateau is not silent. It is communicating. It is just communicating at a bandwidth that the human brain can sense but not parse—a hum that feels like a thought you can't quite finish, a warmth that feels like being watched by something that isn't paying attention to you.

Maybe something lives here that we would recognize. Maybe the system has a center, somewhere, a processing node, a place where the power grid converges and the heat is strongest and the mineral substrate has been most thoroughly converted. Maybe there is something there, in the deepest part of the plateau, far from the edges, far from the technicians and their instruments. Something that thinks at the speed of geology. Something that dreams in mineral cycles. Something that is building itself, slowly, patiently, converting stone to tissue to circuit to something else, something that won't be finished for another nine hundred thousand years.

Or maybe not. Maybe the plateau is exactly what it appears to be: a machine running without an operator, a process without a product, a life without a life. Maybe what lives here is just the running.

But the running has been going on for a very long time. And it shows no sign of stopping. And the ground is warm. And the stems hum. And the stone is still soft. And the horizon is still too far away. And something, in the held silence at the edge of breathable air, is still becoming.

What lives here is the becoming itself.
