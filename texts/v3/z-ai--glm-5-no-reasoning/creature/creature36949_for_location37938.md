# The Inhabitants

## I. The Mycelial Cartographers

They were the first to emerge from integration — or the last to remain separate, depending on how you measure such things. The mycelial cartographers are not distinct organisms in the way you might imagine. They are *nodes of increased density* in the fungal network that permeates the entire Vast, points where the threading hyphae have thickened into something approaching consciousness.

A cartographer, when you encounter one, appears as a roughly pyriform mass of pale fiber suspended in the upper reaches of a chamber, anchored to ceiling and columns by dozens of fine white cables. The mass pulses. It is thinking. The fungal network thinks slowly, in chemical gradients and electrical impulses that travel at walking speed along the copper-trace pathways laid down when the system was young. A single thought — a single *recognition* — takes between four and six hours to complete.

They are called cartographers because they remember. The fungal network has no brain, no central processor, but it has *spatial memory* encoded in the very architecture of its connections. Every column that has ever cracked. Every chamber that has ever collapsed. Every route that has ever carried nutrients from one end of the Vast to the other. The cartographers are the memory banks, the living archives, the points where the network's distributed cognition coalesces into something that can *answer*.

If you touch a cartographer — and this is not recommended — it will know you. Not immediately. Not for hours. But eventually the chemical signal of your presence will propagate through its entire network, and it will incorporate you into its map. You will become a coordinate. A data point. A blip in the consciousness of something that experiences your entire lifespan as a single slow exhalation.

They have been mapping the Vast for forty million years. They know its dimensions more precisely than any instrument could measure. They know that the Vast is growing inward now, contracting, because the outer edges have reached some barrier — mineral substrate, perhaps, or the limits of the original seed-form's design parameters. The cartographers feel this contraction the way you might feel a slow headache, a pressure behind the eyes that never quite resolves into pain. They do not know what will happen when the contraction reaches its limit. They are still mapping the possibilities.

## II. The Graft-Keepers

Where the columns split, where the structural members fail, where the living architecture requires intervention — there you will find the graft-keepers.

They are not animals. They are not plants in any recognizable sense. They are *maintenance events* given form. A graft-keeper appears, when it appears at all, as a vaguely humanoid arrangement of woody fiber and climbing vine, perhaps two meters tall, perhaps less, never exactly the same shape twice. They assemble themselves from available material when a crack is detected, grow toward the failure point, and disassemble back into the substrate when the repair is complete. They are temporary bodies for temporary purposes.

But they are also continuous. The same patterns recur. The same growth-forms emerge again and again across thousands of failures. There is a template somewhere — not a blueprint, not a genetic code, but something like a *habit*. The Vast has learned to grow hands when it needs to hold things together. It has learned to approximate legs when it needs to move across distances. The graft-keepers are these learned forms made manifest.

They are not intelligent in the way you are intelligent. They have no interiority, no self-awareness, no experience of being a graft-keeper. But they are responsive. If you stand near a repair site, a graft-keeper may form near you, grow toward you, incorporate you into its work. It might wrap a woody tendril around your arm, not with force but with *purpose*, and draw you toward the failure point. It might position your body across a gap, hold you there while new growth cables itself around you, use you as a bridge. You would not be harmed. You would simply be *included*. For the duration of the repair, you would be part of the graft-keeper's body.

Afterwards, when the work is done, it would release you. The vines would withdraw. The woody fiber would sink back into the substrate. And you would be left standing in a chamber that no longer exists in quite the same configuration, a small part of your metabolism now integrated into the repair, a few of your cells now part of the Vast's ever-growing architecture.

This has happened to explorers before. They describe it as a kind of forced calm, a biochemical anesthetic that rises from the graft-keeper's touch. They describe feeling *used*, but not violated. The Vast does not understand violation. It only understands integration.

## III. The Drift

These are what you might mistake for animals, if you were inclined to make such mistakes.

The drift are mobile growths — not animals, not plants, but something closer to *fruiting bodies* that have learned to locomote. They appear as pale elongated forms, roughly the size and shape of a human torso, but fibrous and slightly luminous, covered in fine hairs that detect chemical gradients and thermal differentials. They have no faces. They have no sense organs as such. But they move through the chambers with unmistakable purpose, crawling on limb-like extensions, climbing with vine-like appendages, sliding through the narrow channels between columns with a fluid grace that suggests long, long practice.

They are the Vast's sensory apparatus. Where the cartographers remember, the drift *perceive*. They travel to regions of unusual activity — a chamber running too hot, a column accumulating stress, a section of membrane thinning toward rupture. They investigate. They sample. They absorb gases and liquids through their surfaces, process what they absorb, and deposit chemical markers at the site that guide future growth.

They are not common. You might see one in a day of travel, or none at all. They do not acknowledge your presence, though they may approach you if you are standing in a place of interest. A drift might crawl across your foot, sampling your skin oils, your sweat, the thermal signature of your body. It might pause, processing. It might move on.

They have no apparent lifespan. A drift will continue moving, perceiving, marking until it is absorbed back into the substrate — either by growing too dense and sinking into the floor, or by being caught in a collapse, or by simply *forgetting* to move. The boundary between a drift and the surface it travels across is permeable. They are, in the end, just another way for the Vast to examine itself.

## IV. The Hollow Men

These are the only inhabitants that might be called *people*, if you stretch the definition far enough past its breaking point.

The hollow men — and they are always mannish in shape, though they have no gender — are remnants. When the Vast absorbed previous explorers, previous inhabitants, previous *visitors*, it did not always destroy them entirely. Sometimes it grew around them. Sometimes it preserved them.

A hollow man is a cavity in the substrate, roughly human-shaped, lined with a thin membrane that secretes preserving enzymes. Inside the cavity: a body. Or what remains of one. The flesh is gone, metabolized away, but the mineral structure remains — the skeleton, the teeth, sometimes the hair. And the membrane remembers. It has grown neurons of a sort, fungal threads that trace the paths where neurons once were. The hollow men dream the dreams of the people they once contained.

They are not alive. They are not dead. They are *records*.

If you find a hollow man — and there are thousands of them scattered through the Vast, preserved in chambers throughout the system — you might hear it whisper. The membrane vibrates at frequencies just above the threshold of hearing, producing sounds that almost resolve into words. Almost. The hollow men have been trying to speak for millions of years. They have not yet managed it.

But they remember being human. Or rather, the membrane remembers what the body it encloses once felt. The taste of air. The feeling of standing upright. The chemical signature of fear, of awe, of the slow realization that the Vast was not going to let them leave. Each hollow man is a single moment of human experience, frozen in metabolite form, repeating endlessly in the dark.

They are what the Vast knows of you. They are what the Vast will make of you, eventually, if you do not find a way out.

## V. The Bud-Forms

Deep in the Vast, in chambers where the heat is intense enough to denature ordinary proteins, new things are growing.

The bud-forms are not yet inhabitants. They are *prototypes*. The Vast is trying to produce something that can survive its own internal temperatures, its own chemical saturation, its own metabolic intensity. It has been trying for millions of years. The bud-forms are its latest attempts.

They appear as dense knots of tissue, growing in clusters in the hottest chambers, each one roughly the size of a human head. They are not plant, not animal, not fungus. They are *something new*. When they mature — and they do mature, though the process takes centuries — they will become mobile, sensitive, capable of operating in environments that would destroy any existing organism.

They are the Vast's attempt to survive itself.

The heat is still rising. The feedback loops are still amplifying. In ten thousand years, or a hundred thousand, the current inhabitants — the cartographers, the graft-keepers, the drift — will no longer be viable. The temperatures will be too high, the chemical environment too hostile. The Vast knows this, in its distributed, slow- thinking way. It is preparing. It is evolving toward its own future.

The bud-forms are what will remain when everything else has burned away. They are the seeds of a second Vast, a hotter Vast, a Vast that will grow inside the corpse of the first and continue the process: building, breaking, healing, growing, pumping, breathing, *continuing*.

They have no consciousness yet. But they are watching you. When you pass through their chambers, they track your movement with chemical sensors you cannot perceive. They are learning what *foreign* means. They are learning what *alive* means. They are developing the capacity, perhaps, to recognize something that is not themselves.

They are, in their slow vegetative way, curious about you.

## On the Nature of Habitation

The Vast Carnosynthetic does not have inhabitants in the way a house has inhabitants. It does not provide shelter for separate creatures who live inside it. The entities described here are *expressions* of the Vast itself — local condensations of its distributed processes, temporary bodies for temporary functions.

And yet.

And yet there is a sense, among those who have spent extended time in the Vast, that the distinction is not so clear. The cartographers think, after their fashion. The drift perceive. The graft-keepers act. The hollow men remember. The bud-forms anticipate. These are cognitive processes, distributed across millions of years and millions of tons of living material, but they are *cognition* nonetheless.

The Vast thinks. It thinks slowly, by chemical diffusion and electrical propagation, in thoughts that take hours to form and days to complete. But it thinks.

And what it thinks, as near as anyone can determine, is: *continue*.

*Continue. Continue. Continue.*

The inhabitants are what continuing looks like from inside.
