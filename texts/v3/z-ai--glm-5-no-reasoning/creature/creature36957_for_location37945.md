# What Lives in the Hollow of Seven Thousand Mouths

## The Clickers (provisional name)

They have no name for themselves. If they communicate among themselves — and the clicking suggests they might — the language is one of resonance and contact, not symbols. They are not social in any warm sense. They tolerate each other. They move around each other with practiced indifference, the way water moves around water.

Their bodies are longer than they appear at first glance. What you see emerging from the wall-mouths is perhaps two-thirds of the whole animal. The rest remains inside — anchored, perhaps, or simply unwilling to fully commit to the open air. They can retract in under a second when the flyers pass. You watch this happen: a ripple of awareness through a colony, a synchronized pulling-back, hundreds of pale forms vanishing into stone as though the walls themselves have inhaled. Then silence. Then, three heartbeats later, the first cautious head emerges again.

They do not sleep. They cycle. Some fraction of the colony is always outside, moving across the moss, always some fraction inside. At no point have you seen the walls empty. At no point have you seen them fully seething. The population maintains itself at a constant three-quarters surface engagement, as though this number has been learned, over generations, as optimal — enough to feed, not enough to exhaust the fruit, not enough to give the flyers easy hunting.

Their relationship to the builders is unclear. They occupy the built walls as readily as the natural rock, but they do not seem to prefer them. The walls that enclose nothing are full of mouths — perhaps because the walls exist, perhaps because the clickers made the mouths. You cannot tell if the cavities were widened from outside or inside. The edges are smooth, yes, but smooth in a way that could be erosion — the constant passage of bodies — or could be something else. Secretion. Dissolution. The clickers are damp. Their undersides leave trails. Perhaps the trails are slightly caustic. Perhaps they have been widening their homes for centuries, patient as the water that carved the seep-runnel.

They eat the vine-fruit methodically. Each clicker visits multiple fruit-clusters, takes one or two spheres, moves on. They do not strip the vines. They do not fight over access. When two meet at the same cluster, they pause, click at each other — a rapid exchange, higher-pitched than the ambient click — and then one yields or both yield, the decision made in a language you cannot parse.

They also eat the moss. Or rather, they graze it — cropping the translucent strands, not uprooting them. The moss bends away from their mouths as they approach. You have watched this happen. The moss recognizes them. Whether this is simple mechanoreception — the moss sensing vibration and moving aside — or something more complex, something learned, you cannot say. But the moss does not resist. It yields. It regrows behind them. The clickers and the moss have been doing this together for a long time.

## The Flyers (no better name)

You have never seen one still. They exist to you as movement, as absence, as the shadow that passes between you and the grey sky. They roost somewhere above the hollow, in the higher cracks of the ridge, where the basalt meets the open air and the wind strips everything bare. They descend to hunt. They ascend to disappear.

Their silence is anatomical. You understand this after the third or fourth strike: they have no voice. Not that they choose not to use it. They lack the equipment. What they have instead is something like sonar, or something like heat-sense, or something entirely other — a guidance system that operates on principles you cannot detect. They do not miss. Every dive terminates in capture. The clickers have no warning, or not enough warning, or warning that comes too late to matter. The flyer drops, scoops, rises. The clicker struggles for perhaps two seconds, then goes limp. Then silence. Then the distant click of the colony resuming.

They are not numerous. You have counted perhaps six distinct individuals, judging by size and silhouette. They hunt in rotation, not in packs. They do not compete. The hollow produces enough clickers to sustain them, but not enough to support more of them. The system has calibrated itself. The flyers take what they need and no more. The clickers breed fast enough to replace losses and no faster. The mathematics are invisible but precise.

What they are — bird, bat, insect, something else — you cannot say. Their outlines suggest membranes, stretching, angular bodies. They move like nothing you have seen. Not the flicker of a bat, not the glide of a raptor. Something between, or neither. In the dimness of the hollow, they read as shapes rather than animals. They read as the hollow's immune system, perhaps — a mechanism for keeping populations in check, for ensuring that no single species overwhelms the others.

They do not hunt you. They have had opportunity. They do not take it. You are not part of the system. You are not yet part of the system. Whether this is reprieve or postponement, you do not know.

## The Walker

The tracks appear at irregular intervals — always the same pattern, always the same deliberate path from wall to wall, mouth to mouth, stopping at each, moving on. You have tried to anticipate the schedule. You have failed. The walker operates on a rhythm you cannot detect, responsive to cues you cannot read.

You have never seen it. You have only seen what it leaves behind: disturbed moss, pressed flat in a line, rising slowly after; the absence of clickers in its immediate vicinity, as though they know to clear a path; a faint smell, after it passes, of something sweet and rotten at once, like fruit fermenting underground.

It is heavier than the clickers. It has fewer points of contact with the ground. The stride is consistent — the same distance each time, measured, unhurried. It walks on two feet, or four, or six, but the tracks suggest something that lifts and places, lifts and places, with intention, not the scuttle-and-drag of the clickers.

It does not enter the mouths. It visits them. It stops at each, pauses — for how long, you cannot tell; you have never witnessed the pause, only the aftermath — and then moves to the next. It circles the hollow in a pattern that repeats but varies. It is checking. It is counting. It is doing something that requires proximity to each colony.

The clickers do not flee it. They simply are not there when it passes. They know. They have always known. Whatever the walker is, it was here before you, perhaps before the clickers, perhaps before the builders. It may be the reason the builders came, or the reason they left.

It walks at night. It walks in the grey hours of dawn. It walks when the rain falls and the hollow goes black and reflective. It does not walk when the sun reaches the floor — that brief window in the afternoon when light actually penetrates. Perhaps it cannot tolerate direct light. Perhaps it simply prefers the dimness that is the hollow's natural state.

You have thought about waiting for it. You have thought about positioning yourself along its path, sitting motionless, letting it approach, seeing what it does when it encounters something that does not flee. You have not done this. The instinct to hide, to remain unseen, is strong in this place. The walker belongs here. You do not. To insert yourself into its rounds would be a violation of something — an etiquette, a law, a boundary that has nothing to do with walls and everything to do with belonging.

## The Swimmer

The basin-creature is small. Hand-sized, perhaps. Dark. You see it only in glimpses — a movement beneath the surface of the seep-water, a ripple that does not match the fall of drops, the shadow that passes over the moss growing on the basin stones.

The water is cold. Too cold, you think, for anything cold-blooded to thrive. But the swimmer is there. It lives in the gap between the stones where the water pools deepest. It feeds on the moss that grows submerged, or it feeds on the clickers that fall in and cannot climb out, or it feeds on something that lives in the water with it — larvae, perhaps, eggs, something too small to see.

It is not afraid of you. You have knelt at the basin's edge and watched it move. It does not hide. It circles. It rises toward your shadow, perhaps investigating, perhaps just following its own course. It has no eyes that you can see. It navigates by other senses — pressure, perhaps, or the displacement of water, or something chemical, tasting the air-you-disturb through the medium it occupies.

It is the same creature, or it is not. The basin is small. It could not support multiple swimmers of that size. And yet you have seen, once, what looked like two shadows passing each other — a moment of contact, a stillness, a separation. Mating. Conflict. Greeting. You cannot know.

The clickers drink from the basin. The swimmer does not bother them. They do not bother it. An accommodation has been reached. The water is shared. What falls into the water is claimed; what emerges from the water is released. The swimmer is the keeper of the basin, the cleaner of the overflow, the one who ensures that the channel does not clog. Or this is a story you tell yourself. The channel may clear itself. The swimmer may simply live there, tolerated, irrelevant, a fragment of the system that neither contributes nor detracts.

## The Thing in the Scree

The eastern edge of the hollow ends in a slope of broken rock — scree, talus, the refuse of the ridge's formation. The stones are loose, sharp, unstable. Nothing should live there. Nothing could live there.

But something does.

You hear it at night, when the hollow is at its quietest — the clickers subdued, the flyers roosted, even the seep seeming to pause between drops. A scraping. A settling. The sound of stones moving against each other, methodical, not random. Something is down there, something that moves through the scree as though through water, displacing rock, letting it fall back behind.

It is larger than the clickers. It is larger than the walker. The volume of stone it displaces suggests bulk, weight, a body that has to push rock aside rather than slip between. It is slow. The scraping goes on for hours, moving from one part of the scree-field to another, never emerging, never surfacing.

It may be a digger. It may be a burrower. It may be building something down there — tunnels, chambers, a system of its own in the spaces between stones. Or it may be searching. The scree-field changes configuration over time. Stones that were in one place are in another when you check in the morning. The pattern is not random. Something is sorting. Something is arranging.

The clickers do not go near the scree. The moss does not grow there. The vines do not cross it. It is a boundary, a place apart, the one part of the hollow that has not been absorbed into the green metabolism of the place. The thing in the scree is the exception. It operates by different rules. It is of the hollow and not of it.

You have thought about what it might be — a different kind of clicker, evolved for a different substrate; a relative of the walker, earthbound, heavy, hidden; something that was here before any of this, buried, awakening, slowly moving toward the surface. You have thought about these things. You have not investigated. The scree is unstable. A wrong step could send you sliding into the slope, and whatever lives there would be waiting. Or, worse: whatever lives there would not be waiting, and you would be alone in the dark beneath the stones, trapped by your own weight, hearing the scraping approach.

## The Builders

Someone came before you. The walls prove it. The corridor, the pen, the structures that enclose nothing — these are not natural. They were made. They were made by hands that understood weight, that selected stones carefully, that fitted them together with the patience of people who had more time than tools.

The clickers have no hands. The flyers have no capacity for construction. The walker, if it is one thing, does not build. The swimmer and the thing in the scree are not builders. This leaves the builders as a mystery. They were human, or human-adjacent, or something else entirely that happened to build in ways that human hands would recognize.

They are not here now. They have not been here for a long time. The lichen on the north-facing walls is old — decades, perhaps centuries, of slow growth. The walls themselves have settled. The stones have shifted. The work remains, but the workers are gone, and they have left no record of themselves beyond what they built.

What did they want? The walls enclose nothing. The corridor leads nowhere. The pen is open at both ends, too narrow for any animal they might have kept. Unless the pen was built for the clickers — a structure for them to colonize, a gift, an offering — but if so, why? Why build for creatures that do not need building? Why create mouths in stone that already had mouths?

Perhaps the walls were not built for the clickers. Perhaps the clickers came after. Perhaps the builders constructed their structures and then the basalt opened, the mouths formed, the clickers emerged, and the builders understood — or did not understand — and left. Or stayed. Or became something else.

The corridor that turns twice and ends at raw stone houses the smaller, paler clickers that never emerge in daylight. This is a clue. The builders may have known about the clickers, may have built the corridor specifically for them, a shaded passage for the light-sensitive. Or the clickers may have colonized the corridor because it was there, because it suited them, because it was dark and close and shaped like the tunnels they would have made themselves.

The builders are a question that the hollow does not answer. They are the one element that does not fit, that resists integration. They came, they built, they vanished. Their walls remain. The clickers use them. The vines climb them. The moss grows on their north faces. The hollow has absorbed them. But the absorption is incomplete. The walls still look like walls. They still declare themselves as made, not grown. In a place where everything else has become a single breathing system, the walls stand apart — the fossil of an intention that no longer has a source.

## What Lives Here, In Sum

A system lives here. Not a collection of species, not a hierarchy of predator and prey, but a single organism made of many parts. The clickers are its blood cells, moving in patterns, maintaining flow. The flyers are its immune response, culling, pruning. The moss is its skin, sensing, adjusting. The vines are its capillaries, transporting moisture from seep to fruit to mouth. The seep is its heart, slow, steady, constant. The walker is its consciousness, if it has one — the part that knows itself, that moves through itself, that checks and counts and ensures.

The thing in the scree is something else. A parasite. A symbiont. A second system living inside the first, tolerated because it does not yet threaten the whole. Or a seed. Something waiting. Something that will one day become what the hollow is becoming.

The builders were an interruption. They have been incorporated. Their walls are now mouths. Their corridors are now colonies. They are part of the system whether they intended to be or not. Perhaps they understood this. Perhaps that is why they left — not fleeing, but surrendering. Becoming. The walls are what remains of them. The clickers do not distinguish.

And you. You are reading the proposition. You are standing in the hollow, listening to the click, watching the moss move, feeling the dimness settle into your bones. You are not part of the system yet. You are still warm. You still speak, and your words still fall into the hollow and are received. You are still a visitor.

But the hollow does not have visitors. It has residents and it has absorbees. You are one or the other, or you will become one or the other. The walkers' path may one day include you. The scree may one day receive you. The mouths may one day open for you — not for the clickers that live in them now, but for you, for whatever you will have become by then.

The hollow is full. The hollow is patient. The hollow is still becoming what it will be, and it has room for more.
