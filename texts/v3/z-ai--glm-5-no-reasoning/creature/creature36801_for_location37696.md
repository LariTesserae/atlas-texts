# What Lives Here

## The Keepers of the Threshold

They are not the architects. They are the inheritors.

Between the Mat-Weaver platforms and the first major Veo-Vine galleries, in that transition zone where swamp muck gives way to living architecture, live the ones who call themselves—but "call" is perhaps the wrong word. They have no spoken language in the human sense. They communicate through sustained tones that travel through the saturated air, through the careful arrangement of fungal growths on their skin, through the positioning of their bodies in relation to the stone.

They look like what might happen if a human and a pillar-palm produced offspring across impossible generations: tall, narrow, their skin carrying the green-brown mottling of symbiotic algae colonies. They do not walk so much as flow, each movement continuous with the last, never still but never rushed. Their limbs are long and have an extra joint that allows movement in directions that seem to hurt the eye to follow.

They harvest the Mat-Weavers without destroying them. They cultivate specific strains of fungi along certain pathways. They maintain the Threads—those mysterious cables strung between spires—though no one has ever observed them actually repairing or replacing one. They simply *are there*, near the Threads, their bodies humming at frequencies that make the cables resonate in response.

They do not react to observation. They do not flee from approach. But they position themselves, always, between the observer and whatever lies deeper in the structure—deeper into the spires, deeper into the system.

## The Resonant Ones

Deeper in. In the carved chambers that hold water and darkness in equal measure. Where the ancient basalt rooms spiral into geometries that hurt to follow.

Here live the ones who were perhaps once human. Perhaps.

They have adapted to the light-less environment by becoming something that does not need light. Their skin has gone translucent, showing the pulse of dark fluids beneath. Their eyes have sealed over, grown shut, replaced by structures that pick up vibration through the skull itself. They navigate by singing—constant, quiet singing that maps the chambers through returning echoes.

They have been here long enough that their bodies have begun to merge with the structure. Not dramatically, not horrifically, but gradually. Fungal threads grow from their shoulders and connect to the walls. Their feet have spread, widened, developed gripping structures that leave marks in the stone's biological film.

They are not alone in these chambers. They share space with creatures they seem to consider kin: pale amphibians the size of children, eyeless and slow, that produce the same bioluminescence as the deep pool-water. The Resonant Ones carry these creatures, cradle them, move through the dark rooms in pairs and trios, always humming, always listening.

They know the carved rooms. They know which spirals terminate and which lead to the deeper levels—the levels that the architecture itself seems reluctant to reveal. They have been seen emerging from passages that were not there moments before.

No one has ever seen them eat. But they are often found in the chambers where water pools, their faces submerged, throats working.

## The Unnamed Ones

There is a level below the Resonant Ones. Below the carved chambers. Below even the ancient aquifers that feed the seep-water. A place where the stone itself changes character, becoming something more like membrane than mineral.

What lives there does not emerge. But the Echo-Walkers know of it—they refuse to approach certain shafts, certain openings, producing sounds of distress when taken near them. The Silt-Sifters congregate around the few places where whatever lies below makes contact with the surface world, and they do things there: arrange themselves in patterns, deposit organic material, scrape the stone in ways that suggest ritual or communication.

The deep pulse—the heartbeat vibration that rises from below—originates with them. Or it is them. Or they exist because of it.

They have been heard, though. On still nights when the rain has paused and even the Veo-Vines have gone quiet. Sounds rise through the stone itself: low, rhythmic, structured. Not words. Not animal calls. Something more like the groaning of ice, the settling of mountains, but organized into patterns that repeat and build.

Some travelers who have spent too long in the Weeping Spires—that is, who have let the water affect them, who have dreamed the borrowed dreams—claim to have understood those sounds. They refuse to say what they heard. They refuse to return.

## The Surface Dwellers

For all its strangeness, the landscape supports life that approaches the familiar. The Sail-Gliders and the Glass-Spinners and the Silt-Sifters are only the most visible members of ecosystems that function, after a fashion, according to recognizable principles.

But even here, the boundaries blur.

There are creatures that exist in multiple forms across their lifespan—beginning as swimming things in the pool-water, emerging to crawl across the Mat-Weaver platforms, taking wing as adults to live in the canopy mist, then descending to lay eggs in the stone seeps where the process begins again. They are one species and four. They connect the levels.

There are predators that do not hunt so much as wait. Things that have evolved to look like Veo-Vine colonies, like growths of pillar-palm fungus, like sections of living stone. They are not ambushers; they are *becomers*. They match the environment so completely that their prey does not recognize them as separate until it is inside them.

And there are the small ones: insects by any structural definition, but organized into collectives that exceed any known hive mind. A single colony can span an entire spire face, millions of individuals functioning as a single organism, maintaining specific humidity levels in their territory, cultivating specific fungi, attacking threats with coordinated precision. They do not compete with each other. Different colonies merge when they meet, share resources, divide territory along lines that suggest communication across distances no individual could traverse.

## The Water-Minds

The pools have their own inhabitants.

Deep in the natural basins where tannin-dark water collects, where the bioluminescence glows in layers, lives something that may not be a creature at all. It is a colony—a mass of connected microorganisms that forms visible structures in the water: ropes and sheets and nets that drift and pulse with independent purpose.

It responds to sound. The calls of frogs, the songs of the Resonant Ones, the humming of the Keepers—these produce visible reactions in the water colony. It contracts, expands, reorganizes. It has been observed forming shapes that mirror the geometric patterns of the Shimmer, those spontaneous light structures that appear in the atmosphere.

It is edible. The Resonant Ones harvest it. So do the Silt-Sifters. So, occasionally, do the Sail-Gliders, dipping down to the pool surfaces to skim the protein-rich upper layers.

But those who eat it report effects. Vivid dreams. Sensations of expanded awareness. The feeling, for hours or days afterward, that they can sense the movements of creatures hundreds of meters away, can feel the water vapor organizing in the air, can almost hear the deep pulse from below as something other than vibration.

The colony seems to know when it is being watched. It organizes differently then. It has been observed forming patterns that match the spiral geometries of the carved rooms, the arrangement of the Threads, the growth patterns of the Veo-Vines.

It has been observed forming patterns that match the neural structures of those observing it.

## The Thresholds

Between each layer of life, there are the in-betweeners.

Creatures that live in the transitions—neither fully of one level nor another. They are the translators, perhaps, or the diplomats. They carry substances between ecosystems, redistribute resources, mediate the chemical conversations that pass constantly between the biological layers.

The smallest of them are barely visible: mite-like things that travel along the Threads, ferrying spores and genetic material between separated populations. The largest are the size of large dogs, moving through the Veo-Vine curtains on membranes that are not quite wings, feeding on the fungal colonies that bridge different heights.

They are most active at dawn and dusk, when the humidity shifts and the Veo-Vine songs change register. During the Shimmer—those prismatic events when the water vapor organizes into impossible geometries—the in-betweeners go still. Every one of them, across every species. They pause in whatever they are doing, turn toward the phenomenon (or toward the direction it is occurring), and wait.

They wait until it ends. Then they resume their previous activities as though nothing happened.

## The Passage of Lives

Nothing here dies cleanly.

The moisture preserves, transforms, incorporates. When a Sail-Glider falls, it does not rot—it is colonized within hours, becomes a growing platform for fungi and Mat-Weavers, feeds the stone itself. The Echo-Walkers simply vanish when their time comes; no body has ever been found. The Resonant Ones seem not to die at all—they grow slower, sink deeper into their chambers, merge further with the architecture until they become indistinguishable from the carved rooms themselves.

Everything here is food. Everything here is shelter. Everything here becomes part of something else without the violence of decomposition.

The Silt-Sifters have been observed burying their dead in the muck—but the burying is also planting. Within days, the burial site sprouts new growth: fungi that match the coloration of the deceased, flowers that match its territorial patterns. The colony does not mourn. It continues.

## The Relationship

What lives here lives here because the place permits it.

The Weeping Spires were not built for habitation—human or otherwise. They do not accommodate visitors so much as absorb them. Every creature that lives here arrived from somewhere else, across evolutionary time, and was changed by the arrival.

The rules are consistent, if not explicable:

Contribute. Every organism serves functions beyond its own survival—distributing nutrients, maintaining water flow, processing decay. Those that do not find a function do not survive.

Communicate. The landscape is built on exchange—chemical, sonic, physical. Isolation is impossible. Privacy is a concept without meaning.

Accept transformation. Nothing here remains static. Growth is not optional, and growth in this environment means merger with the systems around you.

What lives here is not a collection of species but a single organism at a scale beyond perception. The creatures described are organs, perhaps, or cells. The boundaries between them are practical conveniences, not essential truths.

To live here is to become part of something larger. To leave is to carry part of that something with you, already growing in your thoughts, already humming in your blood, already waiting for you to return.
