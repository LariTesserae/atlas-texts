# The Keepers of the Shelf

## What Lives Here

They are not tall. Generations of protein drawn from reed-grass seed and the meat of cliff-goats—lean, dark-fleshed, strong in the sinews but spare in the long bones—have made them compact. A child of ten stands waist-high to a grown traveler from the low countries. An adult reaches, at most, the height of a man's chest, and breadth seems to have been sacrificed to density. Their fingers are long for their size, the joints pronounced and knuckly, as though each digit had been designed by someone who understood levers and fulcrums and built accordingly. Their hair is black and straight and heavy with the oil they press from crushed seeds; it hangs in weighted curtains that frame faces of flat, wide cheekbones and deep-set eyes so dark the iris and pupil merge into a single wet stone.

Their skin is not so much brown as colored by the rock itself. Gray-brown, fawn-brown, the exact beige of weathered lichen. The plateau has stained them. Their forearms show a permanent darkening from resting on granite surfaces while they work; their palms are calloused in patterns unique to their craft—a ridge across the base of each finger from gripping chisels, a thickened triangle at the heel of the hand from grinding grain against stone, a rough band at the wrist where the rawhide ties of their sandals rub during the long daily walks along the grooved paths.

They do not think of themselves as a people who live on a plateau. They think of themselves as the people who are the plateau's function.

## How They Speak

The visitor is unlikely to hear conversation above a murmur. Sound carries deceptively in thin air; the first thing infants learn is not to modulate volume but to contain it. A Keeper child caught shouting in play produces a sound like a stone dropped into a well—sharp, resonant, causing every adult in the vicinity to turn with expressions of genuine distress. Not anger. Discomfort. As though the shout had torn something in the texture of the air.

Their language is guttural and precise. Consonants cluster and fracture against each other—hard *k* sounds, glottal stops, the hiss of air between teeth. It sounds, to an outsider, like the noise of stone striking stone. Because it is: their language grew from the sounds available to it, and on the plateau, the only sounds are stone against stone, wind against stone, water against stone. Even their vowels are compressed, bitten short, as if held between the teeth rather than released from the throat. A sentence spoken by one of the Keepers sounds like a sequence of small impacts. A question sounds like a crack.

They gesture when they speak, but not largely. A tilt of the chin toward the eastern cliff means *morning*. A flat hand pressed briefly to the path-ground means *here, now, settled*. Two fingers drawn through the air in the fracture-pattern of their family's sigil means *mine, ours, belonging to*. The gestures are subtle enough that the visitor, watching two Keepers in apparent silence, might think them telepathic until the third repetition of a chin-tilt reveals the grammar.

They have an oral history that is not told in sequence. A Keeper who wishes to recount the story of the founding—the moment their ancestors first reached the plateau, or were placed upon it, or grew from it, depending on which version one encounters—does not begin at the beginning. They begin at the cistern. The cistern is the first fact, the first noun. Then the path. Then the condensation. Then the people who walked the path to reach the cistern. The narrative assembles backward, like a skeleton being clothed in muscle, and arrives at the founding only in its final phrase, which is always the same: *And the stone was here.* This is both the story's conclusion and its premise, and no Keeper sees contradiction in the circularity.

## The Shape of Their Days

A Keeper wakes not to the sun—the sun is a dim event on the plateau, the light filtered through the perpetual haze until it arrives more as an absence of darkness than a presence of illumination—but to the condensation. They feel it. Not on the skin, which is already covered, but in the air itself: a thickening, a weight, a shift in the quality of the breath. The moisture arrives all at once, the visitor observed, as if brushed onto the world by an invisible hand. The Keepers do not find this remarkable. They simply wake when it arrives, because the dampness enters their sleep and sits on their eyelids and they open their eyes and the day has begun.

The morning's first act is the cistern. A child—always a child, this is not a chore but a lesson, the first lesson, repeated daily until the child becomes an adult and performs the same act out of function rather than instruction—lifts the lid from the family cistern with the finger-hole worn smooth by generations of the same gesture. The water inside is cold and mineral-sharp and perfectly still. The child draws it in a cup carved from a goat-creature's horn, dips a cloth, and brings the cup to the sleeping-room where the family lies on pallets of woven reed-grass stuffed with the same. Each member of the household drinks. The cloth is passed across foreheads, across the backs of hands. The lid is replaced. The finger finds the groove. The cistern is sealed.

This done, the day unfolds according to no schedule the visitor can discern and yet according to a schedule so precise that each Keeper knows, without consulting sun or shadow, what task belongs to which hour. Perhaps the body knows. Perhaps the rock knows. In any case, the pattern holds: mornings are for paths and water and the collection of condensation from broad leaves into stone bowls, a patient work of drops coaxed one by one into vessels that will be carried, at midday, to the communal cisterns for redistribution. Afternoons are for craft—the chipping of new blades from obsidian that appears in certain fracture-lines as if the rock had sweated it, the weaving of reed-grass thread on looms of bent wood, the working of goat-creature hide into sandals and pouches and the brackets that support the cantilevered rooms. Evenings are for food: the heating of fire-rocks, the dropping of hot stones into hollowed vessels, the slow cooking of seed-porridge and the occasional goat-creature haunch, seasoned with nothing because the plateau provides nothing that could be called a spice.

They eat together, but not all together. Families eat in their own rooms, and the families that share a path-groove eat at the same time, and the clink of their stone bowls carries between buildings like a conversation conducted in percussion. On certain evenings—no pattern governs which evenings, or the pattern is so large that the visitor's stay could not encompass its period—the inhabitants of a particular path-groove emerge and sit along the edge of their corridor, legs dangling over the shallow-cut stone, and pass a horn cup of water between them in the dusk. This is not a ceremony. It is not a ritual. It is simply what happens, and they would be unable to explain its significance if asked, because the concept of significance requires a contrast with insignificance, and the plateau does not trade in contrasts.

## The Children

Keeper children are born, like everything else on the plateau, into a system already complete. There is no nursery. There is no childhood as the flat-lands understand it: a period of exploration and growth and error before the child finds its place. The child's place exists before the child does. It is the place of whatever task the family needs, whatever niche the household contains, whatever finger-groove in the cistern-lid has not yet been worn by a new hand.

The children learn by proximity. A mother chipping obsidian does not teach her daughter to chip obsidian. She chips. The daughter sits beside her. The daughter holds the rawhide grip of the hammer. The daughter watches the fracture-lines in the stone, which are the same fracture-lines in the path, which are the same shapes as the family sigil, which are the same angles as the building-walls. The daughter begins to chip. The mother does not correct. The stone corrects. A wrong angle produces a useless flake; a right angle produces a blade. The child learns from the stone's response, not the mother's instruction, and by the time the daughter produces her first functional blade, she has already absorbed the lesson the plateau teaches: *work with the grain of what exists, and what exists will yield.*

The children play, but their play resembles work the way a river resembles the sea—similar material, different scale. A child building a cairn is learning to stack stone. A child chasing a flightless bird along the path-edge is learning the path's geography. A child sitting at the cliff-edge, dangling her legs into the cloud-void, is learning the edge, and the edge is the most important thing the plateau has to teach: that everything has a boundary, and the boundary is not a wall but a fact, and facts are respected by standing at them and looking into what lies beyond.

The children do not fall from the cliffs. This is not because they are careful. It is because the edge is so constitutive of their experience that the body knows it the way the body knows its own skin. A Keeper toddler, barely walking, will halt at the cliff-edge with the same automatic precision with which it halts at the edge of a step. The edge is not dangerous. The edge is simply where the stone stops, and stopping is what stone does.

## What They Believe

The visitor asked, through gesture and patience and the slow accumulation of shared vocabulary, what the Keepers believed about the fog.

The Keeper—an elder, perhaps, though age is difficult to determine among a people whose faces settle into their final geometry by the twentieth year and do not change thereafter—considered the question. Considered it for what seemed to the visitor an unconscionable length of time. Then the Keeper tilted their chin toward the fog, pressed a flat hand briefly to the stone beneath them, and said, in their language of small impacts:

*It is below us.*

The visitor attempted clarification. Did they believe the fog was permanent? Did they believe it concealed something? Did they believe it had been placed there by a force, an agency, a will?

The Keeper considered each question. The consideration was not evasion. It was the genuine intellectual labor of encountering a category of thought that did not map onto the available concepts. After a time, the Keeper said:

*The fog is the fog. The stone is the stone. We are between.*

Pressed further—the visitor was foolish, the visitor was a person of flat places where everything required a cause and a story and a reason for being—the Keeper said something that the visitor wrote down and later could not determine whether it was philosophy or topography or the simplest possible statement of existential position:

*The stone rises. The fog sits. We are the rising that has not yet become the sitting.*

This was, the visitor thought, as close to a cosmology as the Keepers would venture. The plateau is a place between: between the rising force of the stone and the resting weight of the fog, between the mineral dead and the vegetal alive, between the beginning of things and their settling. The Keepers do not worship this condition. They do not name it. They inhabit it the way moss inhabits a crack: because the crack exists, and the moss exists, and the fit between them is so precise that to ask *why* is to ask a question shaped wrong for the answer.

They do not believe in gods. The visitor is fairly certain of this. They have no shrines, no carved figures, no spaces set aside for the numinous. The fire-rocks, which retain their heat with a persistence that seems geological, are treated not with reverence but with the practical respect one accords to a tool that functions: they are used, returned, used again. The condensation is not a gift. The fog is not a presence. The cliff-edge is not a guardian. The plateau simply is, and they simply are on it, and the relationship between the two *is*-es is so seamless that to insert a deity between them would be to introduce friction into a system that has, by all evidence, eliminated friction entirely.

They do believe in something, though. Not gods. Not spirits. Not ancestors in the sense of beings who have gone elsewhere. They believe in the surface. This requires explanation.

For the Keepers, every surface—the stone, the path, the cliff-face, the cistern-lid, the skin of a goat-creature or the leaf of a shrub—is where reality happens. The surface is where stone meets air, where water meets stone, where foot meets path, where hand meets lid. The surface is the place of contact, and contact is the only verb that means anything. They have a word for it that sounds, to the visitor, like two stones kissing: a hard click followed by a soft release. *Tkhr.* Surface-contact. The meeting-place. The where-two-things-touch.

When a Keeper presses a flat hand to the ground, they are not praying. They are *tkhr*-ing. Establishing the fact of contact. Confirming that the surface exists and that they exist and that the meeting between them is, in this moment, the totality of what matters. The past is a surface that has been contacted. The future is a surface that has not. The present is the sole surface, and they live on it as completely as the moss lives on its rock.

## The Goat-Creatures and the Keepers

A note must be made of the relationship between the Keepers and the goat-creatures, because it is not the relationship the visitor expected.

The goat-creatures—small, too-jointed, preposterously sure-footed, moving across vertical stone with the ease of a spider on a wall—are not domesticated. They are not wild. They occupy a category the Keepers recognize and the visitor does not: they are *the ones who eat the green and leave the stone.*

This is a role. Not a relationship, not a category of property, but a function within the plateau's system. The goat-creatures graze the grass-tufts that would otherwise overgrow the path-grooves. They trim the shrubs that would otherwise crowd the building-foundations. They perform, in their eating, a maintenance that the Keepers would otherwise have to perform, and the Keepers, in turn, protect the goat-creatures from the one predator that patrols the cliff-faces: a long, low, silent thing that the visitor never saw but found evidence of in the form of scat and claw-marks on stone. The Keepers do not hunt this predator. They simply make the plateau's top accessible to goat-creatures and inaccessible to the low thing, and the balance holds.

When a goat-creature is taken by the predator—a rare event, occurring perhaps twice in a season—the Keepers do not mourn it and do not celebrate it. They retrieve the remains, if they are accessible, and use what they can: hide for leather, horn for cups, bone for needles. The goat-creature's function in life was eating. Its function in death is material. Neither function was chosen by the goat-creature, and neither function is mourned by the Keepers, because the concept of choice, on the plateau, does not apply to functions. Functions simply are, and the goat-creature is the eating-function and then the material-function, and the transition between the two is not a tragedy but a change of surface.

The Keepers do, however, speak to the goat-creatures. Not in words—in the grunt-hoots that the goat-creatures themselves use. A Keeper walking a path will grunt-hoot at a goat-creature standing in the groove, and the goat-creature will step aside, not in alarm but with the mild irritation of a neighbor asked to move a chair. They recognize each other. Not as owner and owned, but as co-residents of a surface, each with their own function and their own groove.

## The Dying

The Keepers die. The visitor must state this plainly, because the plateau's atmosphere of equilibrium might suggest otherwise. They grow old, or they grow weak, or the plateau's persistent mildness fails to sustain them through an illness that the condensation cannot wash away and the fire-rocks cannot warm through. They die in their granite rooms, on their pallets of woven reed-grass, and their families are present, and there is a sound that is not weeping and not chanting and not speech but something between all three: a low, rhythmic humming that rises and falls with the breath of the dying person, as if the family were providing the heartbeat that the failing body can no longer maintain.

When the breath stops, the humming stops. The family rises. The body is wrapped in the finest cloth the household possesses—dark blue, lichen-dyed, the color that reads as black until light catches it—and carried along the grooved paths to the eastern edge of the plateau, where the cliff drops into fog.

Here, at the edge, there is a structure the visitor did not initially notice because it looks like a natural outcropping: a shelf of granite, cantilevered outward, with a low wall and a groove cut into its floor that leads, at the very edge, to an opening. The opening is wide enough for a body. Below it: nothing. Fog. The white immortality.

The body is placed on the groove. It is slid forward. It reaches the edge. It continues.

The fog accepts it the way the stone accepted the living: without ceremony, without resistance, without comment.

The family turns. They walk back along the grooved path. They return to their building. They lift the cistern-lid with the finger-hole, and they drink, and they replace the lid, and the day continues.

There is no memorial. No marker. No cairn for the dead, though cairns of stacked pebbles mark the path-intersections for the living. The dead are not remembered, because the Keepers do not practice memory as a distinct act. The dead are simply absent from the surface, and absence is not a state that requires monument. The dead were here. Now they are not. The surface continues. The cistern-lid is still worn smooth by the finger-grooves of the family, and the finger-grooves include the fingers of the dead, because the dead, when they lived, wore the groove, and the groove holds the record of their contact, and the groove is all the monument the plateau provides.

## What the Visitor Noticed, in the End

On the final evening, standing at the cliff-edge as the condensation began to form, the visitor understood something about the Keepers that could not be communicated in a field report or a sketch or a catalog of artifacts. It was this:

The Keepers are not surviving.

The word *survival* implies a contest. It implies that the environment is hostile and the organism is resisting and the continuation of life is a victory against forces that would prefer otherwise. But the plateau does not prefer otherwise. The plateau does not prefer. The plateau is stone and fog and condensation and goat-creatures and reed-grass and fracture-lines and fire-rocks and a mildness that never deepens to cold nor relents to warmth. None of these forces is hostile. None is benevolent. They are simply present, the way a surface is present, and the Keepers are present on the surface, and the presence of each is confirmed by the presence of the other.

The Keepers do not survive the plateau. They are the plateau.

Not metaphorically. Not in the poetic sense of being shaped by their environment, though they are that—stained gray-brown by granite, compacted by lean nutrition, conditioned to stillness by a world that does not require noise. They are the plateau in the way that a hand is the body: a part that stands for the whole, a function that expresses the system, a surface where the stone's rising and the fog's sitting meet and become, for the duration of a life, a person.

A Keeper is the plateau's way of touching itself.

This is not what they believe. It is what they are.

## Evening

The condensation is forming. On the broad leaves of the shrubs, on the cistern-lids, on the path-grooves worn to polish, on the bare forearms of a Keeper walking home with a basket of reed-grass seed, the water appears all at once, as if the world had exhaled. The Keeper does not pause. The Keeper does not marvel. The Keeper walks the groove that their ancestors cut and their children will maintain and their great-great-great-grandparents knew, and the water beads on their skin and on their hair and on the stone and on the horn cup at their belt, and everything is damp and everything is the same dampness, and the evening is the morning is the evening.

In their room, lit by the faint glow of a fire-rock beginning its patient reheating in the eastern crevice, a family eats seed-porridge from stone bowls. A child traces the family sigil in the condensation on the wall. The sigil matches the fracture-pattern in the stone. The stone matches the path-groove. The path-groove matches the cistern-lid's finger-hole. The finger-hole matches the child's finger. The child's finger traces the sigil. The cycle is complete. The cycle was always complete.

Tomorrow will be like today. This is not a promise. It is a description of a surface that has found its shape and holds it, not through effort but through the simple absence of any reason to be otherwise.

The fog sits below the clifftop. The stone rises above it. Between the two, the Keepers live, and do not call it living, and do not call it anything, and the condensation arrives, and they drink, and the day begins again, and the day is the same day, and it is enough. It has always been enough. It will continue to be enough for as long as the plateau holds its shape, which is to say: for as long as stone is stone and fog is fog and the space between them is occupied by the small, gray-brown, granite-stained people who are the rising that has not yet become the sitting, and who may never become the sitting, and who are, in this persistent present, complete.
