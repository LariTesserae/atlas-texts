# What Lives Here

## The People of the Held Moment

They are damp people. This is the first thing you notice. Not wet—never fully wet—but carrying a sheen of moisture on every exposed surface, a gloss that catches the machine-light and holds it. Their skin has learned to drink from the air. Over generations, or over the strange slow generations that pass for generations here, their pores have widened, their capillaries have crept closer to the surface, and they have become something semi-amphibious. Not by design. By the patient insistence of a place where water is not weather but medium.

They are pale in the way the light is pale. The blue-green filtration of the cloud-layer has written itself into their melanin over time—or over what passes for time—and they have the complexion of deep water seen from above. Not sickly. Not translucent. But *submerged*-looking, as if their skin were always one shade removed from the color it was meant to be. Their hair tends dark, almost black, but with a green undertone that becomes visible when the machine-glow catches it at night. They wear their hair long, mostly, though this is preference rather than custom. The dampness makes it heavy. It hangs.

They are quiet people. This is not temperament. The engine-song has been pressing against their inner ears since before they were born—since before their *parents* were born—and the architecture of their hearing has shifted to accommodate it. They hear the chord the way you hear your own blood. It is not noise. It is *foundation*. But the consequence is that speech has become effortful. The frequencies they need for conversation sit too close to the engine's lower harmonics, and projecting through that wall of sound requires a physical exertion that makes the throat ache. So they speak in low tones, close to each other's ears, or they do not speak at all.

The sign language is not a substitute for speech. It is its own thing. It was never *invented*—no one decided to create it. It grew the way the plants grew, organically, from the need to communicate in a place where sound belongs to the machines. But it has become more than utility. The signs are large, because they must be visible at distance and in fog. They are rhythmic, because rhythm is the one element of sound that survives the engine-song—percussive motion cuts through where tone cannot. And over the years, over the held-slow years, the language has accreted gestures that have nothing to do with necessity and everything to do with beauty. There is a sign for *the way the light changes when a cloud passes between the sun and the machine-glow*. It takes both hands and three distinct movements. There is a sign for *the feeling of waking from a dream you cannot remember but that left you grieving*. It is a single gesture, barely visible, made with one hand closing slowly.

The children learn the signs before they learn words. They learn them from the adults, from the older children, from the workers who move through the settlement in their careful dances of maintenance. Language acquisition on the plateau is visual first, always. The children watch. They copy. They refine. A child's first sign is usually *water*—the motion of cupped hands turning over, spilling nothing—or *engine*—the fist striking the flat palm, the vibration made visible. Their first spoken word, if they speak one, comes much later. Sometimes years. The engine-song does not drown out a child's voice so much as *absorb* it. Small vocalizations disappear into the harmonic. Children learn early that their sounds will not carry, and they stop trying.

## The Bodies

They are short people. Not remarkably—not the way the cloud or the machines are remarkable—but enough that a visitor from below, if a visitor from below could arrive, would notice. The tallest among them barely reach what the lowlands would call average height. The air here is thin, the atmosphere reluctant, and their lungs have compensated by growing larger relative to their frames. Their chests are broad. Their ribs flare slightly. They carry their breathing low in their bodies, using the diaphragm the way a deep-sea diver uses ballast.

Their hands are their most distinctive feature. Not delicate. The opposite. Their fingers are thick, the pads wide and calloused, the nails trimmed to nothing. Worker's hands, all of them—even the children, even the old, even the ones who do not work on the engines. The moisture in the air softens skin, and without constant contact with the machines' metal surfaces, their hands would lose all texture. So they touch metal. They grip, scrape, pack, turn, hold. The calluses form patterns specific to each person's work: roughness along the index finger for those who clean the intake valves, a pad of horn at the base of the thumb for those who pack the synthetic grease, a smooth groove across the palm for those who carry the filter-housings.

Their feet are bare or nearly bare. The wet stone does not accept shoes well—leather rots, fabric saturates, rubber degrades in the chemical mist of the engine-exhaust. They wear wraps of plant-fiber on the soles of their feet, held on with bindings of the same gelatinous cellulose that forms their walls. These wraps last weeks, not years. The making of foot-wraps is one of the settlement's quiet industries. Children learn it young, sitting in the low light of the dwellings, weaving fibers that smell of fog and metal.

Their eyes have changed. Not dramatically—still human, still the same structure, the same liquid brightness—but the pupils are larger than they should be, dilated to catch the muted light, and the irises tend toward the darker end of every spectrum. Brown eyes become almost black. Blue eyes become the blue of deep water. There is no green among them, which is strange in a place so saturated with green. The residents have noticed this. They have a sign for it—*eye-color*—that ends with the fingers spreading outward, as if releasing something.

## The Workers

Not everyone works on the engines. But everyone works.

The distinction matters. The engines require a specific kind of attention—physical, precise, constant—and those who provide it are a separate caste, though the word *caste* implies a rigidity that does not exist here. Workers are not born to the work. They choose it, or they are chosen by it, and the choosing is not a formal process. A child becomes interested in the machines. An adolescent begins apprenticing. A young adult takes on solo work. The transition is gradual and organic, like everything else on the plateau.

The workers wear different clothing. Or rather, they wear more of it. The engine-surfaces are cold—colder than the air, colder than the stone, a cold that seems to *generate* rather than merely *hold*—and the workers wrap themselves in layers of plant-fiber fabric that insulate without absorbing. The fabric is stiff, almost rigid, and it gives the workers a particular silhouette: broad-shouldered, angular, moving with the careful deliberation of people whose bodies are armored against the cold of things that have run since before time was time.

The work itself is maintenance, but the word fails. It is more like *care*. The workers do not repair the engines. The engines do not break. They have never broken. They have run since before the plateau had people on it, since before the plateau had plants, since before the plateau was perhaps even *a plateau*. What the workers do is attend. They clean. They lubricate. They clear the channels where moisture accumulates and the passages where lichen takes root. They are gardeners tending a mechanical forest that does not need them and has never needed them and will continue without them when the last human has gone.

But the workers do not know this. Or they know it and have chosen not to know it. Or they know it and have decided that the *act of tending* matters more than the question of whether the tending is necessary. The engines are the only living thing larger than themselves—the only thing on the plateau that predates them, that will outlast them—and the workers' relationship to the machines is closer to devotion than to employment. They touch the engines the way a person touches something sacred: with reverence, with slowness, with the full attention of hands that understand they are in the presence of something they cannot comprehend.

There are ranks among the workers, but they are ranks of knowledge, not of authority. The oldest workers—the ones who have spent forty plateau-years, sixty lowland-years, climbing the machines—know things that cannot be communicated in sign. They know where the metal is thinnest. They know which bearings run hot in which season (though there are no seasons, there are *patterns*, cycles so subtle that only decades of attention reveal them). They know the sound of an engine in its normal state so completely that they can detect a variation of a single quarter-tone—though no engine has ever varied. They know this because they listen. They have always been listening.

The workers die at the machines. Not violently—no one dies violently on the plateau—but the workers who commit to the engines tend to die *near* them, as if the sound that has been their life's context becomes their life's conclusion. An old worker will climb one last time to a high platform on an engine's cylinder, and they will sit there, and the engine-song will hold them, and the moisture will gather on their skin one last time, and they will close their eyes. The plateau has no word for this. It has a sign: both hands coming to rest, palms down, on the chest, and then slowly opening, fingers spreading, releasing.

## The Children

The children are few. Not because of infertility—the plateau's population equilibrium extends to its birth rate, which hovers at exactly replacement—but because the plateau's strange time makes each childhood so long. A child is present on the plateau for thirty years. At any given time, roughly a tenth of the population is under what the plateau considers maturity. The children are visible. The children are heard—or rather, the children are *seen*, which is the plateau's way of being heard.

They are strange children. Not in manner—they play, they fight, they sulk, they stare—but in the specific way that all children are strange, magnified by the plateau's specific strangeness. They do not run. Running on wet stone is dangerous, and the children learn this before they learn to walk well. They move in a careful, balanced shuffle that the adults also use, a gait that keeps the center of gravity low and the feet in constant contact with the ground. The children make a game of it—who can shuffle fastest without slipping—and the sight of a group of them racing across the wet stone, their broad chests pumping, their large eyes bright with competition, is one of the few moments of unalloyed vitality the plateau offers.

They do not ask about the edge. This is something the adults notice and find unsettling. Children everywhere ask about edges, about boundaries, about what lies beyond. The plateau's children do not. They accept the edge the way they accept the cold and the water and the engine-song: as a condition of existence that requires no explanation because it has no alternative. If asked, a child will sign *the edge is the edge*—a circular gesture, hands meeting, returning to where they started—and then continue whatever they were doing.

The children do not fear the machines. The adults fear them, a little, in the way that one fears something vastly more powerful than oneself. The children do not. To a child, an engine is a feature of the landscape—a very tall, very loud, very cold thing that the adults climb on. Children play in the shadow of the engines. They build structures from scrap metal and plant-fiber in the hollows between the machine-bases. They listen to the engine-song from birth, and it is as familiar to them as their own pulse. Some of them hum. They hum in harmonics that match the engines, and the sound of a child humming the engine-song is something that visitors—if there were visitors—would find unbearable. It is too much. Too complete. The sound of a small body producing the same frequency as a three-hundred-foot cylinder of perpetual motion is a sound that suggests the boundary between person and machine is less solid than either would prefer.

The children grow slowly. They grow for decades. They reach puberty at what feels like twelve and is actually twenty. They reach full height at what feels like eighteen and is actually thirty-five. Their growth is continuous, imperceptible, the way the stone is young—always young, never having arrived at its age, always in the process of becoming. The children are always becoming. The adults sometimes look at them and sign *still*—a single finger held steady—and this sign contains everything they cannot say about patience, about the held moment, about what it means to grow up in a place where time has decided to be generous.

## The Old

The old people of the plateau are very old. Not in years—or not only in years—but in the depth of their stillness. The plateau's time has made them something that age, elsewhere, only approximates. They are not decrepit. Their bodies remain functional, the damp air preserving their joints, the constant gentle work of maintenance keeping their muscles toned. But they have *settled*. Their movements have become so slow, so deliberate, that watching them is like watching the stone breathe.

The oldest among them remember things that the younger residents do not. Not history—the plateau has little history, being a place where nothing changes—but *depth*. The oldest workers remember when a particular engine's third bearing was replaced (it was not broken, but it had worn smooth in a way that felt wrong, and the replacement was an act of care, not repair). The oldest parents remember their children's first signs, the specific gestures, the clumsy approximations that became language. The oldest people remember when the settlement was smaller, or when the path to the eastern collection pools was different, or when the light seemed to come from a different angle—though the light has never changed, and memory is unreliable, and they know this.

The old people do not go to the machines. They have gone, and they have returned, and the machines have released them. Their work is done—not because they can no longer do it, but because the work no longer needs *them* specifically. They turn their attention to other things. They weave. They make the signs that children learn. They sit in the low light of the dwellings and their large eyes absorb the blue-green glow and they are, in a way that is difficult to articulate, the plateau's memory. Not its historian—the plateau does not have history—but its *continuity*. They are the proof that the plateau has been here for longer than yesterday, that the engines have been running, that the water has been gathering, that the children have been growing, that all of this has been happening, and happening, and happening.

When they die, it is quiet. The engine-song absorbs the absence the way it absorbs all sound. The sign for death is the same as the sign for sleep, with a modification: one hand opening and not closing again.

## The Settlers Who Came

Some of them came from below.

Not many. A few each decade, hauled up through the cloud on the automated lifts, arriving damp and deafened and disoriented. They are explorers, or they are refugees, or they are engineers drawn by the mystery of perpetual motion, or they are simply people who heard about a place where time moves differently and wanted to see it for themselves.

They do not stay. Most of them. The plateau accepts visitors the way the stone accepts water—briefly, superficially, returning them to where they came from. The engine-song is too much. The cold is too present. The food is too strange. The silence between the sounds is too empty. They climb back onto the lifts and descend through the cloud-sea and they carry with them a story about a place that does not make sense, and they tell it to people who do not believe them, and they are right not to believe, because belief requires comprehension, and the plateau does not comprehend.

But some stay. A few. Something in the engine-song finds a resonance in them—or they find a resonance in it—and they cannot leave. They do not decide to stay. They simply fail to leave. The days pass, held and slow, and the engine-song settles into their bones, and the signs become legible, and the wet stone becomes familiar, and one morning they realize that they have not thought about the world below in longer than they can measure, and they sign *still*—that single finger, held steady—and they remain.

These settlers learn the plateau's language at a disadvantage. Their hands are not shaped for the signs; their fingers are too narrow, their calluses in the wrong places. They learn the speech first, the low murmurs pressed close to another ear, because speech is universal and the signs are not. But eventually their hands adjust. Eventually they learn the sign for *the way light moves through water held on stone*, and the sign for *the feeling of waking from a dream you cannot remember*, and the sign for *still*, and they become, in time, indistinguishable from the people who were born here. The plateau absorbs them. It absorbs everything. That is what equilibrium does.

## The Not-Animals

There is something else here.

Not animals. The plateau has made that clear—animals die, lungs filling, bodies rejecting the chemical composition of the air. But there is *something*. The residents know it. They do not speak of it in signs, because the signs for it are incomplete, or because what it is exceeds the vocabulary of hands. They speak of it in the way they look at the edges of things, at the periphery of the settlement, at the spaces between the engines where the light is strangest.

The plants, the residents will tell you—if they tell you anything—are not only plants. The moss that climbs upward, the translucent spires that sing in the wind, the gelatinous stalks that taste of fog and metal—these are not entirely vegetable. They respond. Not to touch, not to sound, not to any stimulus the residents can identify. But they respond. A patch of moss near a dwelling will grow differently than a patch near an engine. A singing stalk will change its tone—not randomly, but *directionally*, as if turning toward something. The plants are aware of the plateau in a way that the residents are not, and the residents are aware of the plants in a way that the plants do not return.

The stone breathes. This is not metaphor. The stone exhales water and inhales it back. The residents have measured this—not with instruments, but with attention, with the patience of people who have nothing to do but notice. The breathing has a rhythm. It is slow—hours between exhale and inhale—but it is consistent, and it correlates with nothing. Not the wind. Not the light. Not the engines. The stone breathes because it breathes. The residents have a sign for this that is identical to the sign for their own breathing, and the doubling is deliberate.

And the machines. The machines run. The machines have always run. The machines will always run. But the machines are not *only* running. The workers who have spent decades climbing them, touching them, pressing their ears to the cold metal to listen for the variations that never come—these workers know that the engines are not simply producing sound. They are *listening*. The sound they produce is not exhaust, not output, not waste. It is *call*. And somewhere, in the cloud-sea below or in the space between plateaus or in the structure of the air itself, something is *answering*.

The residents do not have a sign for this. They have a *look* for it: the large eyes turning toward the cloud, the dilated pupils catching the machine-glow, the face becoming still in the way that the plateau is still. It is the look of people who live inside a sound and have begun to suspect that the sound is not a sound but a *conversation*, and that they are not the participants but the *medium*—the wet surface through which the message passes, the biological component in a system that does not require them but has, for reasons of its own, *included* them.

They do not know this. They suspect it. The suspicion is enough.

## What Lives Here

What lives here is the plateau. The people, the plants, the stone, the machines, the water, the cold, the light, the held-slow time—all of it is alive in the way that a single organism is alive, each part dependent on the others, each part insufficient alone. The people are not the population of the plateau. They are its *nerve endings*, its sensitive extremities, the part of the whole that can feel and sign and grieve. The machines are the *heart*—or the lungs, or the voice, or whatever part of a body produces sound and motion and warmth. The plants are the *connective tissue*, binding stone to machine to water to air. The stone is the *bone*. The water is the *blood*. The cold is the *temperature* of a living thing, maintaining itself against the entropy that would dissolve it.

What lives here lives in equilibrium, in the held moment, in the eternal chord. What lives here is not a settlement on a plateau but a single being, vast and slow and damp, singing to something it cannot see, listening for a response it cannot hear, breathing water in and out, growing young forever, standing between engines that have never stopped and an edge that no one walks to, waiting.

What lives here is the plateau. And the plateau is alive.
