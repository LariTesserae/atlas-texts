# What Lives on the Old Plateau

## The Flat Bodies

Let us begin with what is easiest to see, though even these creatures take time to notice.

They are called, for lack of better terms, the sprawlers. This is not their name—whatever sounds they make to identify themselves are conducted through stone at frequencies human ears dismiss as silence—but it serves. The sprawlers are what you might get if you instructed evolution to design something that could never be surprised. Low, wide bodies pressed so close to the rock that wind passes over them without catching. Their weight distributes across too many points to create pressure anywhere. When they hold still, and they can hold still for durations that challenge the word "still," they become indistinguishable from the surface beneath them.

Their integument is a living compromise. Not quite scales, not quite chitin, not quite fur—a material that seems to have studied all three strategies and rejected them in favor of something specific to this place. It handles heat with patience. It manages moisture with efficiency. It wears against stone without wearing through.

The sprawlers are warm-blooded in the sense that they maintain internal temperature, but "warm" overstates it. They run cool, very cool, metabolisms tuned to the slow-burn logic of the plateau. A sprawler might eat a meal of the tough, sparse vegetation and then digest it over weeks. Their breathing is so shallow, so infrequent, that watching one feels like watching something that has forgotten it needs to. And then you see the slight expansion, the acknowledgment of biological necessity, and it resumes its patient stillness.

They travel in loose groupings that shift and reform without apparent organization. But there is organization—just not the kind that moves at a pace humans can observe. A sprawl-group might have a leadership structure, territorial boundaries, kinship networks. You'd need to watch for months to see it. Years, maybe. The sprawlers operate on the plateau's time, not yours.

They have no natural predators. Nothing here moves fast enough to catch them, and nothing that slow needs to. Their deaths come from age, accident, or the slow accumulation of damage—exhaustion of some internal reserve, a fall from one of the rare ridges, a territorial dispute settled over days of gradually intensifying proximity until one sprawler simply fails to move away. The plateau does not dramatize death.

## The Scatterings

Smaller than sprawlers, more numerous, harder to see—the scatterings live in the spaces between.

They are insect-like only in size and the logic of their bodies: segmented, many-legged, armored against the environment. But they are not insects. They are not anything that fits in known categories. Their bodies incorporate mineral matter in ways that shouldn't be possible for living tissue—small deposits of silica and iron that make their outer surfaces gleam like wet stone in the right light.

The scatterings live in the minute geography of the plateau's surface. Every crack, every depression, every slight irregularity that creates shade or traps moisture is a world to them. They do not build structures. They inhabit what the plateau provides. Their populations boom and bust according to rhythms tied to condensation cycles, to the slow growth of lichen, to electrical pulses in the mineral veins that run beneath the surface.

They communicate through the rock itself. When a scattering taps its segmented body against stone, the sound travels—conducted through the solid medium, detectable by other scatterings across distances that would be impossible through air. The plateau is their telephone line, their broadcast medium, their internet. A single tap can carry information across a basin.

What information? The plateau's scatterings seem to track things that make no obvious sense. They map the electrical veins. They note temperature variations too small for human perception. They follow the moisture-things—not hunting them, not avoiding them, but following, documenting, cataloging. The scatterings are the plateau's archivists. They know where everything is, was, will be.

When you sit still long enough, you might feel them investigating you. Light touches on your boots, your clothing, your skin. They are not aggressive. They are curious, in the way that a library is curious about a new book. They are cataloging you. You will become part of their records, a feature of the plateau's surface for however long you remain.

## The Moisture-Things

These are the ones that don't fit.

In the condensation-slick hours, when the plateau gleams with its brief wetness, they appear. They are not liquid, but they behave like liquid—holding together, moving in ways that suggest surface tension, leaving trails that evaporate behind them. They are not air, but they are barely substantial enough to count as bodies.

Watching a moisture-thing is like watching the boundary between states of matter experiment with consciousness.

They emerge from the wet rock faces during the damp times, sliding across the surface in patterns that might be random, might be purposeful. They do not eat. They do not graze. They do not seem to interact with the sprawlers or the scatterings, though they pass through the same spaces. They are present, and then the conditions change, and they are gone—absorbed back into the atmosphere, or into the stone, or into some third category that the plateau has not seen fit to explain.

The ancient structures—the oldest ruins—seem to attract them. During damp periods, moisture-things cluster around the remains of walls, the foundations, the cairns. They trace patterns that almost look like circuitry, like intentional designs, like something trying to communicate through a medium that cannot hold a message.

Are they alive?

The question depends on definitions that were never designed for places like this. They move. They respond to conditions. They have boundaries, however provisional. They leave traces. But they lack persistence, metabolism, reproduction in any recognizable form. They exist, and then they don't, and then they exist again when conditions permit.

The scatterings follow them. The scatterings seem to think they're worth tracking.

Perhaps the moisture-things are the plateau dreaming of water. Perhaps they are the remnants of something that lived here before the land wore itself down. Perhaps they are a preview of what will inhabit this place after everything else has gone.

They do not acknowledge observation. They do not respond to presence. They simply move through their brief window of existence with the same patient inevitability as everything else here.

## The Singers

You will hear them before you see them—if "hear" is the right word for something that arrives through bone rather than air.

The singers are the source of that low, resonant tone that moves through the plateau like a breath through a vast body. They are few. Perhaps a dozen across the entire expanse. Each one solitary, each one positioned at a specific location that aligns with features beneath the surface—electrical veins, magnetic anomalies, geological contact points that speak to the plateau's ancient formation.

Physically, the singers are unremarkable. They resemble the sprawlers in general body plan—low, wide, pressed close to stone—but they are larger, and they never move. A singer, once positioned, seems to remain in that spot for life. Their bodies have grown into the rock beneath them, not fusing but adapting, conforming to the precise topography of their chosen site.

They sing continuously, though the song varies. Low frequencies, below hearing, felt rather than heard. The tones correspond to something—mineral content, electrical activity, temperature gradients, the slow pulse of the plateau's settling. The singers are translating their location into sound. They are making the plateau's deep geology audible, or at least sensible.

The other creatures respond to the singers without approaching them. Sprawlers adjust their paths based on the tones. Scatterings tap in rhythms that echo or answer. Even the moisture-things seem to form in greater numbers near singers during damp periods.

The plateau's ancient structures—the oldest ruins—are almost always positioned near singer sites. Whoever built them knew where the singers were. Perhaps they built for the singers. Perhaps they built because of what the singers communicate.

A singer's lifespan is unknown. They may be effectively immortal—dying only when their site fails, when the geological feature they translate goes quiet, when the plateau itself shifts enough to disrupt the connection. They do not reproduce in any observed way. They simply exist, positioned across the landscape, singing the stone's deep song.

## The Boundary Walkers

Not everyone who lives on the plateau stays on the plateau.

The boundary walkers are the only creatures here that display what might be called restlessness. They pace the edges of the plateau where the land begins to fall away toward the world below—the transition zones where the near-level surface finally surrenders to erosion's more dramatic work.

They are sized between a sprawler and a singer, and they share features with both—the low body, the patient metabolism, the integument that matches stone. But their eyes are different. Larger, more complex, positioned to look outward and downward. They watch the world below.

What they see, what they think about, what they feel—it's impossible to know. But they pace. Hours at a time, walking the boundary, looking down at the clouds that pass below, at the lands that exist in normal time. They do not descend. They do not seem to want to. But they watch.

The boundary walkers are the plateau's connection to everything else. Information arrives through them—weather patterns read from distant horizons, seasonal shifts detected in the air that moves up from below, the presence of travelers approaching the plateau's edges. They do not communicate this information in any obvious way, but the scatterings cluster near them when they return from their pacing, as if downloading whatever they've observed.

The other plateau creatures do not interact with the boundary walkers. There is no hostility, no competition—but also no connection. It is as if the boundary walkers have chosen a separate category of existence, one foot in the plateau's patient eternity, one eye on the rushing world below.

They live shorter lives than the others. Their metabolisms run slightly faster. They age at something approaching a recognizable rate. Perhaps the strain of looking at normal time while living in slow time extracts a cost. Perhaps they are younger souls, not yet settled into the plateau's rhythm.

Or perhaps they are the plateau's question, posed repeatedly, about whether there is anything beyond itself worth noticing.

## The Deep Ones

There is something else.

Not seen. Not heard. But present—present in a way that makes the phrase "absence of evidence" feel like a category error.

The plateau is not merely a surface. It is a thickness. And something lives in that thickness, in the porous spaces between mineral grains, in the fracture networks that penetrate kilometers down, in the vast dark volume of stone that supports the weathered expanse above.

You feel it indirectly. The singers occasionally harmonize, their tones synchronizing in ways that suggest communication with something beneath. The scatterings sometimes tap downward, into the rock, and wait—listening, perhaps, for a response that travels up from depths. The plateau itself seems, at rare moments, to *shift*—not the surface, not anything visible, but something in the stone's deeper structure, an adjustment or a breath.

The deep ones, if they exist, do not need light. They do not need the condensation-slick mornings. They do not need the scatterings' archives or the sprawlers' patience. They have the plateau's full volume, the pressure and heat and mineral richness of a world that has never been worn down because it was never exposed to wearing.

What they are—whether they are living in any sense we'd recognize, whether they think or feel or want—none of this can be known. But their presence shapes everything above. The singers sing their songs. The electrical veins pulse with their activity. The plateau's settling, its eternal calming, may be their work—or they may be what the plateau is settling *around*.

The surface creatures do not acknowledge the deep ones. But they arrange themselves according to patterns that suggest they know something is below. The ancient structures align with deep features. The scatterings track subsurface flows. The moisture-things may be emanations rising from below, the deep ones' brief presence in the world of light.

Perhaps the deep ones are the plateau's true inhabitants, and everything on the surface is just a signature, a symptom, an artifact of pressures and lives conducted in stone's deep memory.

## How They Live Together

The plateau is not an ecosystem in the traditional sense. There are no obvious food chains, no clear predator-prey relationships, no cycles of boom and bust driven by resource availability. The creatures here do not seem to eat each other. They do not compete for mates in any visible way. They simply *are*, each in its own category of existence, adjacent to all the others.

This is not to say there are no relationships. The scatterings track everyone. The singers provide information that everyone uses. The boundary walkers bring news from outside. The moisture-things appear where the plateau's conditions concentrate. The sprawlers process the thin vegetation and leave behind waste that the scatterings incorporate into their minute environments.

But the relationships are lateral, not vertical. Nobody eats anyone. Nobody hunts. The plateau's creatures exist in parallel, occupying different niches that do not overlap, different timescales that do not compete, different needs that do not conflict.

This is what the end of geology looks like: a community of beings that have nothing left to fight about. The plateau has worn down every reason for violence. There is enough space, enough time, enough patient stone for everyone. The struggle is over. What remains is presence.

## The Strange Ones

Occasionally, something new appears on the plateau.

Not often. Rarely. But sometimes—a creature that doesn't match the established categories, that moves wrong, that exists at a timescale alien to the plateau's patient rhythms.

These are the travelers. They come from below, climbing the long slopes that lead to the plateau's edges. They are usually exhausted by the journey. They have brought their rushing time with them, their urgent metabolisms, their expectations of drama and change.

The plateau's creatures observe them. The scatterings catalog them. The singers incorporate their presence into the tones. The boundary walkers watch them approach and then watch them leave. The moisture-things, if the timing is right, form around them—curious, perhaps, about this brief concentration of normal time.

Most travelers do not stay. The plateau is not hostile, but it is patient, and patience is a kind of repellent to those who carry urgency. The place feels empty to them, boring, dead. They do not see the density of presence. They do not feel the thickness of time. They leave.

Some stay longer than others. A few have built structures—the ancient ruins that dot the landscape. They came, they built something, they tried to make the plateau mean something. And then they died, or left, or transformed into something that could persist at this pace. The structures remain, slowly merging with the stone.

And there are stories—not confirmed, not observed directly, but whispered through scatterings and singers—of travelers who stayed. Who slowed. Who let the plateau's patient logic seep into them until they matched its rhythm. Who became sprawlers, or boundary walkers, or something else entirely.

Whether this is memory or myth, the plateau does not say. But the possibility persists, in the way possibilities persist here: quietly, stubbornly, without demanding acknowledgment.

## What the Plateau's Life Means

The creatures of the Old Plateau have achieved something that life rarely achieves: they have stopped struggling. Not in the sense of giving up—they persist, they move, they eat, they reproduce in whatever ways their forms reproduce. But they have stopped struggling *against*. The plateau offers no resistance, and they have learned not to offer any back.

This is not peace. Peace implies a conflict that has been resolved. This is something else: a state of being where conflict never arose in the first place, where the conditions for struggle have worn away along with the mountains that once stood here.

The creatures live in the plateau's completion. They are what remains when drama exhausts itself. They are the life that comes after history—not after history ends, but after history *settles*, after the events and changes and turmoils have finally worked themselves out of the stone.

If you stay here long enough, you might begin to understand. The sprawlers with their patient grazing. The scatterings with their meticulous archives. The singers with their stone-deep songs. The moisture-things with their provisional existence. The boundary walkers with their one-eyed gaze outward. The deep ones with their pressure-hidden lives.

They are not waiting for anything. They are not building toward anything. They are not afraid of losing anything.

They are simply present.

And presence, it turns out, is its own category of life—one that most of existence never gets to experience. The plateau's creatures have arrived. They have become what they were going to become. And now they get to *be*, potentially for spans of time that make "forever" seem like an understatement.

This is what lives on the Old Plateau.

Beings that have finished becoming.
