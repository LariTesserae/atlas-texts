# The Keepers of the Lowland

What lives here is not what you would expect.

Yes, the lowland teems—the frogs, the insects, the rodents with their waterlogged fur, the snakes that thread through fern beds like threads through fabric. These are obvious. They are the surface text, the prologue to something else entirely. They live *in* the lowland. But there are those who live *as* the lowland, and they are far older, far stranger, and far fewer than the abundance above would suggest.

---

## The Anchorites

Deep in the saturated zones, where the ground has given up any pretense of solidity and the water table rises to meet the air, something lives that has forgotten how to move.

They have no collective name. They have no language in which to name themselves. But if you could ask the lowland what its principle inhabitants were—truly ask, communicating in the chemical signals and root-touch that constitute speech in this place—the answer would rise from the mud itself.

The Anchorites (for that is as good a word as any) began as individuals. Perhaps they were once mobile, long ago, when the lowland was younger and its sediments had not yet compacted into their current state. Perhaps they walked or swam or crawled through the proto-marsh, seeking food, seeking mates, seeking the usual things that mobile beings seek. But at some point—gradually, without any single moment of decision—each one stopped.

It was the water that did it. The water and the heat and the impossible density of nourishment surrounding them on every side. Why move when food accumulates against your body of its own accord? Why seek when the thing sought arrives unbidden? The lowland offered a bargain to anything that could learn to accept it: *Stay still, and I will bring everything to you.*

And so they did. One by one, over generations that would exceed human recorded history, they settled. They sank. They took root.

That is not metaphor. The Anchorites have grown roots—literal, anatomical roots—that extend from their lower surfaces into the soft sediments below. These are not plant roots; they are modified limbs, adapted over evolutionary time to serve as anchors and conduits. They draw nutrients from the soil directly, bypassing the inefficient intermediary of a mouth. They have not eaten with a mouth in longer than any memory persists. Their mouths have sealed over, unnecessary. Their digestive systems have shortened, simplified, become nothing but a supplementary channel for surplus biomass that the roots cannot process. They have become partly autotrophic, drawing energy not just from organic matter but from the chemical gradients that permeate the lowland's waters—sulfur compounds and methane and the slow oxidation of minerals that have not yet become rock.

What they look like now defies easy description. They have accumulated. The lowland's biomass does not merely surround them—it has become part of them. Moss grows on their upper surfaces, and they do not remove it. Ferns sprout from the creases of their accumulated flesh, and they do not brush them away. Vines drape across them, insects burrow into them, small mammals nest in the hollows that form where their bodies have folded against themselves. They have become ecosystems. Each Anchorite is a walking (no—*sitting*) habitat, a micro-lowland within the Lowland, supporting its own complement of parasites and commensals and symbionts.

They are large. The smallest is roughly the size of a human torso, curled in on itself. The largest—and no one has found the largest, has measured its full extent—may be many times that size, sunk so deep into the mud that only its breathing apertures remain above the waterline, disguised as nothing more than vegetative growth. They are distributed throughout the lowland, but never densely. Each requires a substantial territory, not for hunting but for *absorbing*. The roots need room to spread. The chemical gradients need space to replenish. Two Anchorites too close together would deplete the same resources, and then both would starve, slowly, over decades.

They do not compete. Competition requires caring about outcomes, and the Anchorites stopped caring long ago. They simply exist, processing, growing at a rate that would shame a glacier, accumulating biomass that will never be used for movement or defense or reproduction because they have forgotten how to do any of those things.

They reproduce, yes. But it is not what you would call active. Occasionally—a frequency measured in events per century—an Anchorite will bud. A portion of its accumulated mass will separate, slowly, over months, detaching with a sound like wet fabric tearing. The bud sinks into the mud nearby, takes root of its own accord, and begins its own long process of stillness. This is not a choice. It is not a drive. It is simply something that happens, like condensation, like sedimentation, like the gradual accumulation of silt. The parent does not notice. The offspring does not remember. The lineage continues without any of its members being aware that a lineage exists.

There may be dozens of them. There may be hundreds. No one has ever counted, and the Anchorites themselves would not cooperate with a census even if they could understand the concept. They are not hostile. They are not friendly. They are present, and that is all.

## The Midwives

If the Anchorites are the lowland's stasis made flesh, the Midwives are its opposite.

They are small—comparatively. The size of a large bird, perhaps, or a small mammal, though they are neither. They move through the vegetation with a speed and precision that seems impossible given the density of growth. They are the only creatures in the lowland that can navigate the thickest tangles without disturbance, slipping between leaves and vines like water through a sieve.

They are attracted to death.

Not to cause it—they do not kill, have no weapons, show no aggression. But when something dies in the lowland (and things die constantly, eaten by snakes or overcome by parasites or simply reaching the end of their spans), the Midwives appear. They seem to know. They arrive within minutes, sometimes seconds, emerging from nowhere with an urgency that contrasts sharply with the sluggish equilibrium of everything else.

Their job is to ensure that death produces new life.

They do not eat the dead. Instead, they position the corpse—moving it if necessary, dragging it through mud and water to a location that seems arbitrary but follows patterns no outsider has deciphered. They arrange the body with care, straightening limbs, opening mouths, positioning the remains in a specific orientation relative to water flow and root density. Then they leave.

The corpse, so positioned, decays differently. The decay is faster, more complete, and—crucially—more targeted. The nutrients released do not disperse randomly into the general biomass. They channel into specific pathways, feeding specific roots, nourishing specific organisms that would otherwise be outcompeted by the general riot of growth. The Midwives are creating something: a directed succession, a planned replacement, a death that produces not merely *more life* but *particular life*.

What guides their choices? No one knows. They do not communicate in any detectable way. They do not seem to learn—they know innately, born with the knowledge of where each corpse should go and how it should be arranged. They are specialized beyond any ecological logic, evolved for a purpose that serves a system so vast and slow that its architecture may not be visible from within.

Some theorize that they serve the Anchorites. The corpses they arrange invariably decay within the root-zones of one Anchorite or another, feeding the chemical gradients that the rooted ones depend on. But the Midwives show no awareness of the Anchorites—they do not approach them, do not acknowledge them, do not seem to register that the rooted ones exist. If they serve them, they do so unknowingly, following a program written so deeply into their instincts that the purpose has been forgotten.

They are not social. They do not interact. Two Midwives encountering the same corpse will work simultaneously without acknowledgment, each performing a portion of the necessary arrangement, splitting the labor without communication. When the work is done, they disperse in opposite directions, vanishing into the growth.

They have no predators. Nothing eats them. Even the snakes and lizards that take everything else leave the Midwives alone, as if recognizing a protected status that carries no explicit warning but is somehow understood. Their flesh may be unpalatable, or they may carry a chemical signature that marks them as necessary, as off-limits, as part of the lowland's immune system rather than its prey. They live for years—long, by the standards of small creatures in a place where most lives are brief—tending to the dead, arranging decay, ensuring that the endless recycling of biomass proceeds according to a pattern whose logic is not ours to know.

## The Drift-Callers

The noise of the lowland is not random. The layered sounds—cicadas, frogs, the gurgle of water, the rustle of movement—form a tapestry that seems chaotic only to ears unattuned to its frequencies. But within that chaos, certain patterns repeat, and certain voices carry further than others.

The Drift-Callers are birds, after a fashion. They have feathers and beaks and hollow bones. They perch in the canopy, invisible in the perpetual twilight, and they sing. But their song is not like other birdsong. It does not stake territory. It does not attract mates. It does not warn of predators or announce the discovery of food.

It shapes the weather.

This is not metaphorical. The Drift-Callers' vocalizations operate at frequencies and volumes that influence the behavior of water vapor in the air above the lowland. Their harmonics create standing waves in the atmosphere—subtle pressure differentials that encourage or discourage condensation, that guide the movement of the cloud layer, that pull moisture from the saturated air and deposit it in specific locations.

They are rain-makers. Rain-directors. Conductors of a symphony whose orchestra is the atmosphere itself and whose audience is the water.

The lowland does not have weather in the conventional sense. The clouds do not form and disperse according to the usual rules; they follow patterns that the Drift-Callers have been weaving for generations. The boundary between the air's moisture and the ground's is artificial, maintained by the singers' constant attention. Without them, the lowland might dry out—or flood permanently. With them, it stays in the equilibrium that characterizes all its systems: saturated but not drowning, humid but not raining, the water always waiting, always held in suspension by a song that never ends.

There are perhaps a dozen Drift-Callers at any time, distributed across the lowland's extent. They never land on the ground; they live their entire lives in the canopy, drinking from leaves and catching insects on the wing. They are never seen clearly—only glimpsed, dark shapes against the diffused light, their presence inferred from the song that issues from everywhere and nowhere. Their plumage is the color of shadow, their eyes the color of water. They blend. They disappear. They become the voice of the place itself.

When one dies—rarely, for they are long-lived—it is not replaced. The Drift-Callers do not reproduce in any observed way. Their numbers have been dwindling for as long as anyone has been counting (and few have been counting). The song continues, but with fewer voices, fewer frequencies covered, more gaps in the harmony. The weather-maintenance grows less precise. The equilibrium shudders.

Some ecologists who study this place believe that the Drift-Callers are the last of their kind—a relict population, surviving long past their evolutionary time, maintaining a climate that no longer makes sense but which they cannot stop maintaining. They are trapped in their own song, committed to a pattern that they inherited and cannot abandon. When the last one falls silent, the lowland will change. Perhaps dramatically. Perhaps not. But the equilibrium will tip, and whatever comes after will not be this.

## The Drowned Girls

There are humans here, sometimes.

Not inhabitants. Not anymore. But the lowland accumulates everything, and that includes the occasional wanderer who enters and cannot leave. The ground yields too easily. The water waits in every depression. The vegetation blinds and confuses. And the heat— the heat presses like a hand, squeezing the will to continue out of anyone unprepared for its intensity.

Most who enter do not understand what they are entering. They see lushness, greenery, life. They do not see the trap that life has set— the endless cycles of eating and being eaten, the water that offers nothing to drink, the heat that permits no rest. They walk in and they sink and they do not walk out.

The Drowned Girls are what remain.

They are not ghosts. They are not spirits. They are not supernatural in any sense that requires belief. They are simply the bodies of those who died here and were not retrieved. The lowland preserves its dead—not through any mystical process, but through the simple chemistry of its waters, saturated with minerals and low in oxygen, inhibiting the bacteria of decay. Bodies sink into the soft sediments and remain, slowly accumulating coatings of silt and algae, slowly becoming part of the substrate on which the living world continues its frantic cycles.

They are visible, sometimes. In the clearer pools, where the water settles and the suspended particles drift to the bottom, you can see them. They rest just below the surface, their faces turned upward, their eyes open or closed depending on how they fell. They are not decomposed. They are not bloated. They are simply *there*—present, undisturbed, waiting in a more profound sense than the water waits.

How many? No one has counted. The pools are many, the visibility is poor, and the lowland does not encourage census-taking. But over decades—centuries, perhaps—a number have accumulated. They rest in their separate graves, their individual saturations, their private equilibria. They do not interact with each other or with the living world above. They are simply present, corpses in the water, another form of biomass that the lowland has not yet processed.

They have an effect, though. On the living, I mean.

The creatures of the lowland avoid the pools where the Drowned Girls lie. The insects do not swarm there. The frogs do not call from their margins. Even the Midwives do not enter these clear zones, do not drag their corpses to rest beside the bodies already present. There is an exclusion, an invisible boundary that the living recognize without understanding. The Drowned Girls are not *for* the living. They are something else— a category of presence that does not fit into the cycles of predation and decay that govern the rest of the lowland.

Perhaps they are still becoming something. Perhaps the saturation they have entered is a transitional state, a pause before transformation into whatever the lowland intends to make of them. In a thousand years, they may be stone— impressions in the sedimentary record, fossils for whatever paleontologists exist then to puzzle over. In a thousand years, they may be nothing, their outlines dissolved into the strata, their individuality erased by the slow processes of compression and mineralization. Or perhaps they will remain exactly as they are, suspended in chemical equilibrium, preserved indefinitely in a system that has forgotten how to change.

The lowland does not explain itself. The Drowned Girls rest, and the living world continues around them, giving them a wide berth, acknowledging without understanding that some presences are not to be disturbed.

## The Sum

What lives in the Seething Lowland?

Everything that can. Everything that has learned to occupy the spaces between water and air, between rock and mud, between growth and decay. Everything that can tolerate the heat, the wet, the endless noise, the pressure of too many bodies in too little space. Everything that has found a niche in the equilibrium or made a niche by refusing to accept the equilibrium.

But mostly, what lives here is the lowland itself.

Not metaphorically. The lowland is an organism—or if not an organism, then a system so thoroughly integrated that the distinction ceases to matter. Its parts are individual lives, yes, the frogs and insects and vines and ferns and the strange specialized creatures that have no names outside this description. But its function is singular: to persist, to cycle, to maintain the equilibrium that permits persistence. The Anchorites anchor. The Midwives process death. The Drift-Callers regulate the water. The Drowned Girls remind the living that presence here is not permanent, that even in a place so committed to continuation, some things end.

And the rest—the swarming, calling, eating, breeding, dying multitudes—are the lowland's way of being alive. Its cells. Its tissues. Its breath and its heartbeat and its endless seething surface.

Stand at the edge of this place—or stand within it, for the edges are indistinct—and you will feel what lives here. Not because you will see it all. You will not. The vegetation is too dense, the light too dim, the movement too constant. But you will feel the weight of presence, the sense that every cubic inch of space is occupied by something that wants to be there, something that has adapted to the conditions and found them good.

The heat is a hand pressed flat against the chest.

The water waits.

And what lives here is the hand, and the water, and the chest, and the waiting.

What lives here is the wanting.

What lives here is everything that has ever wanted to simply *be*, and found a place where being is enough.
