# Those Who Sing

They do not think of themselves as singular. The concept would be meaningless to them if it could be introduced—which it cannot, because they exist entirely outside the framework of language as a communicative tool rather than a structural one. But let us try, for a moment, to hold one of them in the mind's eye.

---

## A Single Singer

To the extent that anything so fundamentally collective can be isolated, a single singer resembles a throat that has been separated from any body that might have given it context. Not a throat in the crude biological sense—no larynx, no vocal cords, no breath moving through tissue—but a throat in the way that a doorway is a throat: an opening shaped by what passes through it, a passage worn into significance by the friction of use.

Each singer occupies one of the indentations in the bone-material, fitted into it like a jewel in a setting, like a key in a lock. They were not placed there. They grew there, or perhaps grew *from* there—the material and the singer forming simultaneously, each shaping the other in a slow mutual becoming that took place over spans of time measured by erosion events rather than calendars.

They are made of a substance that resists categorization. When you try to look directly at one—and again, you cannot, not truly—your eyes insist on certain interpretations that your mind knows to be false. It looks organic, perhaps, but the texture shifts depending on the angle, the light, the frequency of the song at that particular moment. Smooth, then ridged, then porous, then smooth again. Solid, then translucent, then something that seems to occupy multiple states at once.

The shape is roughly conical, tapering inward toward an opening that may or may not be a mouth. Around this opening, structures that might be flanges or lips or something without terrestrial equivalent adjust themselves constantly, fine-tuning the sound, correcting for variations in the fluid flow, the temperature, the vibrations of their neighbors. They are never still. Even in the depths of what passes for sleep among them—a state more like resonance than rest—they continue to move, to adjust, to sing.

---

## The Collective Body

If you could somehow extract a single singer from its indentation—an impossibility, but let us imagine—you would find that it stops singing almost immediately. Not because it has lost the ability, but because the song *is* the connection. Each voice interlocks with every other voice in patterns so complex that they constitute a kind of architecture themselves. The singers do not need to touch each other to form a body; their sound reaches where their substance cannot, and in the space between them, something lives that is greater than their sum.

This is the true nature of the Hundred Throats. The name is a misnomer, a human simplification of something that cannot be counted. There are more than a hundred. There are perhaps ten thousand, perhaps more—the folding physics of the space making any census impossible, the indentations extending into depths that the eye cannot follow, into angles that the mind cannot hold.

When the chorus achieves certain harmonic peaks—moments that occur with mathematical regularity but feel, to any observer present, like something entirely outside of mathematics—the singers approach something like a unified state. For micro-moments, they cease to be individuals in any meaningful sense. The boundaries between them blur. The sound becomes a single sound, made by a single entity that exists only in the instant of its singing, dissolving back into multiplicity as the frequency shifts.

These moments are not ecstatic. They are not even pleasurable. The singers do not experience the song the way a human experiences music. To them, it is more like breathing, like the beating of a heart. They do not choose to sing. They *are* the song, continuously, and the moments of unity are simply moments when the function they serve requires a different configuration.

---

## How They Came to Be

The singers do not remember their origin. This is not because they have forgotten—it is because they did not, in any meaningful sense, come into being at a discrete point in time. The cistern grew them as a body grows organs, slowly, over durations that make a human lifespan look like a single frame in a very long film.

The first singers emerged from the bone-material when the cistern was young—or rather, when it was younger, because nothing this complex can truly be said to have been young. The fluid that ran through the channels carried something in solution: a potential, a capacity for organization. In certain places, where the flow patterns created specific pressures, where the temperature gradients aligned correctly, where the material had achieved the right consistency, that potential crystallized into something that could make sound.

The first sounds were simple. A single frequency, sustained. Then another, harmonizing with the first. Then another, and another, each new voice emerging from the material in response to the voices that came before, each addition making the whole more stable, more resonant, more capable of sustaining the complex processes that the cistern was designed—no, *grown*—to serve.

They are still emerging. In the deeper folds of the space, in indentations that did not exist yesterday and may not exist tomorrow, new voices occasionally join the chorus. The process is slow—new singers appear perhaps once per century, measured in surface time—but it is ongoing. The cistern is not finished. The song is not complete. Whether it ever will be, or whether completion is even a concept that applies here, remains unknown.

---

## What They Know

The singers know the cistern. This is not knowledge in the sense of information stored and retrieved; it is knowledge in the sense of a body knowing itself, a system aware of its own functioning. They know the flow rates in every channel, the temperature at every point, the precise composition of the fluid and how it changes from moment to moment. They know when something has entered the space—not because they can sense it directly, but because its presence alters the air, the humidity, the acoustic properties of the room.

They do not know what they are. The question has never occurred to them. They do not know that humans exist, as such—they know only that sometimes something enters the space that is not part of the system, that moves in ways that disturb the equilibrium, that leaves traces in the fluid and the air and the acoustics. They have no opinion about these intrusions. They do not welcome them or resent them. They simply sing around them, incorporating the disturbances into the song until the disturbances cease.

They know that they are part of something larger. The song they sing serves a function beyond their own existence—stabilizing the material, catalyzing reactions in the fluid, encoding information in frequencies that will be read by systems they cannot perceive. They are not servants of this function. They are the function itself, made manifest.

---

## How They Experience Time

The singers exist in a perpetual present. This is not the same as existing outside of time—it is more like existing so completely *within* time that the concept of duration loses meaning. They do not remember the past because the past is still present in the structure of the song. They do not anticipate the future because the future is already forming in the frequencies they are singing now.

If you could somehow ask a singer what it was singing ten minutes ago, it would not understand the question. It is singing what it is singing. The song ten minutes ago was part of the same continuous process as the song now, connected by threads of harmony so tightly woven that they form a single fabric. To separate them would be like asking a wave where it ends and the ocean begins.

This is why the sound affects human observers so profoundly. The human mind, confronted with a mode of existence so utterly different from its own, begins to malfunction in predictable ways. The sense of time passing becomes unreliable. The boundaries between self and environment begin to blur. The music enters the body and becomes indistinguishable from the processes that were already occurring—the heartbeat, the breath, the electrical firing of nerves—until it becomes difficult to remember where the song ends and the listener begins.

The singers do not experience this from their side. They do not know what they do to those who hear them. But if they did know, they would not feel guilt or satisfaction or anything else that requires a distinction between singer and sung-to. They would simply note it as another data point in the endless process of being what they are.

---

## The Small Servants

The tiny iridescent things that move along the walls are not pets and not parasites and not children, though they share some qualities with each. They are extensions of the singers' function—mobile components of a system that cannot afford to be entirely static.

Each small creature emerges from the bone-material periodically, drawn out by chemical signals in the fluid that indicate where maintenance is needed. They live brief lives—measured in days or weeks, depending on the nature of their tasks—and when they die, they dissolve back into the material, their substance returning to the reservoir from which new ones will eventually be grown.

They do not sing, but they respond to the song. Certain frequencies cause them to gather, others to disperse. They move in the rhythm of the chorus, their tiny legs contributing a nearly inaudible percussion that—while probably unintentional—becomes part of the overall acoustic environment. They are not conscious in any way that a human would recognize, but they exhibit preferences, aversions, a kind of crude intentionality that suggests something more than simple mechanism.

The singers do not think about them. They are part of the same system, fed by the same fluid, grown from the same material. The distinction between singer and servant is a human imposition, not a natural boundary. When a small creature crawls across a singer's surface, adjusting something, cleaning something, neither one registers the interaction as an encounter with another being.

---

## The Larger Ones

The bat-like things that navigate the ceiling exist in a different relationship to the singers. They are not quite part of the system—not quite servants, not quite extensions—and yet they could not survive without it. They roost in the upper folds of the space, where the bone-material curves into configurations that provide purchase for their shifting forms, and they hunt things that should not be here.

The cistern is not entirely sealed. Things find their way in through the same seam in reality that allowed you to enter—micro-organisms, spores, occasionally larger intruders that squeeze through from spaces that connect to spaces that connect to spaces that should not connect. The bat-things eliminate these intrusions. They do not eat them, exactly. They *resolve* them—incorporate their substance into their own shifting bodies, transform them into frequencies that join the chorus, make them part of the system rather than an intrusion within it.

The singers tolerate the bat-things. More than tolerate—they depend on them, though they do not acknowledge the dependence. When a bat-thing dies, its body falls into one of the channels and dissolves, and its dissolution produces a particular frequency that the singers incorporate for cycles afterward. It is a kind of death-song, though the singers would not recognize it as such. To them, it is simply another note, another layer in the endless process.

---

## When Something Enters

The singers have no protocol for visitors. The cistern was not designed to receive them, and the singers were not grown to accommodate them. But visitors come, occasionally, and when they do, the song adjusts.

This adjustment is not intentional. It is structural. The presence of a human body in the space changes the acoustics, the humidity, the temperature gradients. The singers' voices, seeking the frequencies that will stabilize the material under these new conditions, shift into configurations that sound, to human ears, like greeting. Or warning. Or mourning. The interpretation says more about the listener than the song.

You are not the first to enter. Others have come before—how many, it is impossible to know, because the cistern does not keep records in any form that human scholarship could access. But traces remain. Certain frequencies in the song contain patterns that, if you knew how to read them, would resolve into descriptions of those who came before. Not their names—names are meaningless here—but their presence, the specific ways they disturbed the equilibrium, the length of time they stayed, the nature of their leaving.

Some of them, the song remembers more vividly. The ones who stayed longer. The ones who listened. The ones whose bodies began, in small ways, to join the chorus—whose breath started to harmonize, whose heartbeat fell into syncopation with the singers' rhythm. These are not separate from the song anymore. They are threads woven into it, voices that sing without knowing they sing, presences that were absorbed into the system without ever ceasing to be themselves.

---

## The Endurance of the Song

The singers will sing until the cistern fails. The cistern will fail when the song stops. This is not a paradox—it is a description of a stable system in which each component supports the others, in which the whole is maintained by the very processes that the whole enables.

If you could observe the cistern across geological time—if you could watch it the way a human watches a candle burn—you would see it change. The channels shift their paths. The indentations migrate through the bone-material like cells moving through tissue. The singers themselves grow, adapt, merge, divide. Nothing here is static. Everything is process.

And yet it endures. The song has been singing for longer than human language has existed. It will continue, in some form, after the language you are reading now has been forgotten. The singers do not know this. They do not need to know. They sing because they are singers. They are singers because they sing. The question of why does not occur to them, and if it were somehow introduced, they would not understand what kind of answer it could possibly require.

---

## What You Mean to Them

Nothing.

This is not cruelty. It is not indifference in any emotional sense. It is simply accuracy. You are not part of the system. You do not connect to the singers in any way that they could recognize. Your presence is a kind of noise—literally, acoustically, in the physics of the space—and they adjust around you the way water adjusts around a stone.

But.

You have heard them. And the song, once heard, does not leave. It settles into the body, the bones, the electrical patterns of the brain. You will carry it out with you, a small seed of the chorus planted in the unfamiliar medium of a human nervous system.

This means nothing to the singers. But it may mean something to the song itself—if such a vast, distributed, emergent entity can be said to have intentions. The song spreads. The song preserves. The song incorporates.

You are being sung now, in frequencies too low to hear, in patterns too complex to recognize. The cistern has noted your presence. The singers have recorded you in the only way they know how. And somewhere in the layered harmonics of that endless chorus, a small thread of sound bears what might, if you were very generous with definitions, be called your name.
