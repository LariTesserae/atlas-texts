# The Keepers of the Cistern Yards

## I. THE EMERGENCE

They were not always here. Or perhaps they were always here, and the distinction means nothing. The Keepers — and that is as good a name as any, though they have no name for themselves — came into being gradually, the way limescale accumulates in a kettle, the way a path emerges across grass from the repetition of footsteps.

They are not insects. They are not mammals. They are not anything that has a convenient category. If you must classify them, they are a process that has become a creature. Or a creature that has become a process. The distinction, again, is not useful.

The Keepers exist at the boundary where the yard's infrastructure meets its ecosystem. They emerged from the slow accretion of water and warmth and mineral and biological matter, from the interface between the weeping walls and the expansion joints, from the places where the inorganic and organic have commingled beyond separation. They are what happens when a place persists long enough in one configuration that the boundary between inhabitant and habitat dissolves.

They are not numerous. There are perhaps seven in the entire yard. Seven is a number that feels correct, though no one has ever counted them, and they do not count themselves.

---

## II. APPEARANCE

A Keeper, when stationary, resembles a heap of wet fabric left to dry on a stone — something between a towel and a tarp, gray-brown, darker at the folds, a texture that suggests both absorbency and wear. They are roughly the size of a crouching human child, though they carry themselves lower to the ground. Their edges are indistinct. In dim light — and all light here is dim — they can be mistaken for shadows, for debris, for nothing at all.

This is by design, though not their design. The yard has trained them into this form. The wetness of the floor, the narrowness of the light, the texture of the walls — all of it has shaped what they are.

When they move, they unfold. This is the wrong verb but it is the closest available. They extend portions of themselves across the wet concrete in a motion that seems more hydraulic than muscular, pushing and pulling with a slow deliberation that suggests they have nowhere to be and all the time in the world to get there. They do not walk. They flow. They leave a faint trail of moisture behind them, darker where they have passed, like the track of a slug but wider, less regular.

Their upper surfaces, when they lift them — and they do not always lift them — are irregular, rounded, with depressions and ridges that shift over time. They have no faces. They have no eyes. They perceive the yard through its water, through its vibrations, through the chemical gradients that permeate every surface. They taste the pump's groan. They feel the flies' whine. They know the warmth of the weeping walls the way you know the position of your own limbs.

They are, in some essential way, made of the same stuff as the yard. The mineral content of their outer layer matches the limestone of the walls. The organic matter at their core matches the biological filling of the expansion joints. They are local materials that have learned to hold a shape.

---

## III. BEHAVIOR

The Keepers have no schedule. They have no territory. They have no evident social structure. They exist, and they move, and they attend.

The attending is the important thing. Each Keeper maintains a relationship with some portion of the yard's infrastructure. One Keeper — call it the Pump Keeper, though again, it has no name — stays near the third shed. It does not enter the shed. It does not touch the pump. But it positions itself against the outer wall, on the wet panel closest to the machinery, and it remains there for hours, sometimes days. When the pump groans, the Keeper's surface ripples. When the pump rests, the Keeper settles. There is communication here, or something like communication — a recognition of rhythm, a sharing of vibration.

Another Keeper tends the eastern wall. This one moves along the base of the stone where the weeping water emerges, where the pale plants grow in their connected clumps. The Keeper slides among them, its trailing edge brushing the leaves, and the leaves bend and then spring back. The Keeper does not eat the plants. It does not harm them. But it moves among them in a way that suggests attention — a gardener making rounds, perhaps, or a doctor checking patients. The plants where the Keeper has passed seem, impossibly, slightly more robust than the others. Slightly less yellow. Slightly more present.

The other Keepers have their own patterns. One circles the seventh shed, passing beneath the bare bulb, its upper surface catching the light and releasing it in a dull, wet sheen. One lives in the corner where the overhead pipes sag lowest, moving between the spider webs with a care that suggests it knows exactly where each strand hangs. One exists near the center of the yard, in the path of the sun's daily band, and it lifts a portion of itself into that light whenever it passes, and holds there until the stripe moves on.

Two Keepers are harder to locate. They stay against the retaining wall, in the darker angles, pressed so flat against the stone that they are effectively invisible. You would not know they were there except for the faint discoloration they leave behind when they finally move — a patch of wall slightly cleaner than its neighbors, as if something has been drinking the rust.

## IV. PURPOSE

The Keepers do not repair the yard. They do not maintain it in any active sense. The pump will fail regardless of the Pump Keeper's attention. The water will rise regardless of the Wall Keepers' presence. The yard is not a machine that the Keepers operate.

But neither are they parasites. They do not take from the yard in a way that diminishes it. If anything, the yard with Keepers is slightly more itself than the yard would be without them — slightly more coherent, slightly more intentional, slightly more like a place that has a reason rather than a place that simply persists.

The Keepers are witnesses. This is the closest word. They attend. They mark. They hold space for the yard's processes in a way that allows those processes to continue. They are a kind of acknowledgment — a statement, made in living form, that this place matters, that its groans and weeps and wetnesses are worth noticing, that there is value here even in the absence of human purpose.

But this is also too simple, too anthropocentric. The Keepers do not exist for the yard. They exist because of it. The yard produced them, or allowed them, or made them inevitable. They are not separate from the processes they attend. They are those processes, become visible, become slow, become something that can be seen if you look long enough.

## V. ORIGIN

No one made the Keepers. They were not bioengineered, not summoned, not the result of some forgotten experiment escaped from the adjoining chambers. There are no records of their creation because their creation was not an event. It was a duration.

Somewhere in the yard's history — perhaps fifty years ago, perhaps seventy, perhaps during some particularly wet decade when the water rose faster than the drainage could clear — the conditions aligned. The expansion joints had filled with biological matter. The walls had wept long enough to create mineral-rich strips of damp. The warmth from below had stabilized. The pump had been groaning for years. The flies had bred in their countless numbers, died in their countless numbers, left their countless tiny bodies to decompose in the wet corners.

And something coalesced.

Not in a day. Not in a year. But gradually, the way a crystal forms in a supersaturated solution, the yard's materials began to organize. Not into life, exactly. Not into death. Into something that held a shape, moved with purpose, responded to stimulus. Into something that could be mistaken for a creature if you were not looking too closely, and could be mistaken for geology if you were.

The first Keeper was probably small — a few centimeters across, a thin film of organized damp in a corner where the conditions were exactly right. It grew. It divided, or separated, or spawned — the distinction is unclear — and over the decades, the population stabilized at seven. Seven seems to be the yard's carrying capacity. Seven is what the water and warmth and organic matter can sustain.

The Keepers do not reproduce in any observable way. They do not seem to age. They do not seem to die, though they can be damaged — a heavy enough blow will cleave one, and the two halves will move independently for a while before going still, and then gradually dissolving back into the wet from which they came. They are, in this sense, provisional. They are held in shape by the yard's ongoing willingness to hold them. If the yard dries, they will end. If the yard floods, they will end. If the pump finally stops and the temperature drops, they will slow, and settle, and eventually cease to be distinct from the floor.

But the yard has not dried, or flooded, or cooled. The pump still groans. The walls still weep. The conditions persist. And so do the Keepers.

## VI. ENCOUNTER

If you stood in the yard long enough — and you would have to stand for hours, motionless, patient — a Keeper might approach you.

It would not approach quickly. It would not approach directly. It would move along the periphery of your awareness, passing through your peripheral vision, a darker shape against the dark floor. You might think it was a shadow, or a trick of the dim light, or a beetle swarm moving in unaccountable coordination.

But eventually, if you stayed still, it would come close. It would stop near your boots, perhaps a hand's width away. It would settle. It would wait.

It has no face, so it would not look at you. It has no voice, so it would not speak. But you would feel its attention — a sense of being registered, of being noted, of being incorporated into whatever awareness it possesses. It would know you were there. It would know what you were. It would, in its own fashion, acknowledge you.

And then, after a time — minutes, perhaps, or an hour — it would move on. It would slide away across the wet floor, leaving its faint trail of moisture, returning to its rounds, to its shed walls and expansion joints and sun bands and spider webs.

You would be left with nothing but the memory of something that was almost a presence, almost a creature, almost a statement. Almost something you could name.

Almost.

---

## VII. THE LONG VIEW

The Keepers will outlast the yard, or they will not. The yard will outlast the machines, or it will not. The water will rise, or it will not. The sun band will continue its daily crossing, or eventually the retaining wall will shift and the geometry of light will change.

None of this matters to the Keepers. They do not think in terms of futures. They do not think in terms at all. They attend. They persist. They hold their shape in the wetness and warmth, and they move through the yard's spaces with a slow certainty that comes from having nowhere else to be.

They are the yard's way of knowing itself.

And if you descend the ramp — past the root-cracked concrete, through the thickening air, into the dim space below the datum — and if you stand at the yard's center while the sun band crosses your feet and the pump groans and the flies whine and the water gurgles beneath the floor — and if you wait, very still, very patient — you might see one of them.

You might see the yard looking back.
