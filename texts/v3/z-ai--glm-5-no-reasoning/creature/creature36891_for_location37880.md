# What Lives Here

## The Gardeners

The copper-bodied one is older than you realize.

Not older in the way you understand age—not worn, not faded, not diminished by the passage of time. Older in the way that stone is old. It has been moving across this surface, tending this leaf, since before you had a word for *leaf*. Its mandibles work in the same rhythm they have worked for what might be centuries, each motion identical to the last, each cleaning stroke removing particles so fine they cannot be seen, only deduced from the sheen they leave behind.

Call them the Tended. Not a name they use—there is no evidence they use names at all—but a name that fits what they do and what is done for them. They are the maintenance, the slow pulse of attention that keeps the green things clean, their surfaces open to the diffuse light, their pores free of the accumulated dust that would, over time, suffocate them.

There are perhaps forty of them, though counting proves difficult. They move so slowly, and they are so patient, that distinguishing one individual from another becomes an exercise in mounting uncertainty. Is this the same copper creature you watched an hour ago, or another of the same kind? Are there forty, or four hundred? The answer shifts the longer you observe, not because the creatures move, but because your sense of what constitutes an individual begins to blur.

Some are large. The copper one is among the largest, perhaps the length of your hand, segmented body flexing in small adjustments that suggest not locomotion but attention—turning toward the work the way a head turns toward a sound. There are smaller ones, too. A species—or a stage, or a caste, you cannot determine which—that works in clusters, three or four individuals aligned along the same leaf, their bodies almost touching, their mandibles working in asynchronous rhythms that somehow produce a perfectly even clean.

And there are the ones you miss at first. The ones that are clear.

## The Clear Ones

They are the most numerous, and they are nearly invisible.

The creature clinging to the vertical surface, the one you noticed by refraction alone—that is not an anomaly but a representative. They are everywhere, these quartz-clear bodies, and they are the reason the green remains so precisely ordered. They are the pruners.

Their bodies are not transparent in the way glass is transparent, not perfectly so. There is something there—a faint internal structure, a slight denseness at the core of the body where organs or their equivalents cluster. But the limbs, the segmented casings, the mandibles—all transmit light with only the smallest refraction, the smallest ghost of a shadow. You could look directly at one and see only a faint shimmer, a rippling in the light that might be heat, might be nothing, might be a living thing the size of your thumb working deliberately in the air before your eyes.

They prune. This is the word that comes to you, though *prune* suggests violence, and there is no violence here. What they do is closer to *resolve*. When a leaf has grown past its useful reach, when it extends into a path where it blocks another's light or crowds a neighbor past the point of mutual comfort, the clear ones find it. They move along the stem until they reach the junction, and there they wait. You watch one wait. It is waiting still when you look away, and it is waiting when you look back, and you do not know how long it waits because time has become unreliable here, and then—gently, without rupture—the leaf separates. Not cut. Released. The clear one's mouth parts find the abscission layer that already exists in the leaf's stem, the pre-formed seam where separation would naturally occur, and they widen it by fractions until the leaf falls.

The fallen leaves do not decay. The Tended of the cleaning caste find them, eventually, and carry them to the lower levels of the growth, where they are pressed into the substrate by still other bodies, incorporated into the living architecture. Nothing is lost. Nothing leaves.

## The Fliers

They are not a cloud. They are a *deliberation*.

The tiny ones in the shaft of light move in coordination so precise it cannot be accidental, and so complex it cannot be choreographed. They are deciding. That is the impression you form, watching them—that each moment of their movement represents a decision, a consensus reached and revised and reached again, the flock adjusting its position relative to the light, to the plants below, to some quality of the air you cannot sense.

They are small. Smaller than the head of a pin, some of them. Their wings—if they are wings, if that is the right structure—catch the light and scatter it into brief prismatic flashes, and for a moment the shaft they occupy is like a column of scattered gems. Then they shift, and the light passes through them cleanly, and they vanish.

What do they do? You cannot tell. They do not land on the plants. They do not interact with the other Tended. They move, and they breathe, and their breathing—collective, synchronized—stirs the air in small eddies that ripple through the hanging stems and cause the curtain-walls to sway almost imperceptibly.

You wonder if they are the ones who breathe for this place. Not metaphorically. Literally. The plants drink their exhalation. The curtain-stems shiver when the fliers pass. Perhaps the fliers are the lungs of this body, and their flight is respiration, their dance a breathing.

## The Huddled

Beneath the shelf-plant, in the shadow you almost missed: the soft ones.

They are pale. Not white, not grey—*pale* in the way that things are pale when they have never been exposed to light, when color was never needed, never selected for. Their bodies are soft, you can tell by how they compress against one another, how they yield when one shifts and another settles. They breathe slowly. You count: one breath per eight of your own. Their respiration is deep, complete, the kind of breathing that suggests not sleep but meditation, not rest but a state so profound that rest is too active a word for it.

There are seven of them in this cluster, though there are other clusters—you see another in a far alcove, another still in a hollow formed by the stacked-coin plants. They are the largest of the Tended. Each is perhaps the size of your closed fist, though their shape is hard to determine because they press against one another, bodies conforming to bodies, creating a single mass that breathes as one organism.

They do not move except to breathe. They do not tend. They do not prune or clean or fly.

They lie in the shadows and they breathe, and the plants near them are the greenest, the thickest, the most vibrantly alive.

You understand, watching them, that these are not workers. These are something else. The keepers, perhaps. The heart. The reason the other Tended work and the plants grow and the light falls and the warmth rises. These are the ones for whom the terrarium exists—or the ones whose existence makes the terrarium what it is. The distinction matters, but you cannot find it.

## The Plants Themselves

And there is something else here. You have been thinking of the plants as habitat, as environment, as the matter in which the fauna live and work. But this is not quite right.

The plants respond.

Not in the way Venus flytraps respond, not in the quick mechanical snap of stimulus and action. The plants respond in the way that a sleeping body responds to a hand on its shoulder—a shift, a settling, a deep adjustment that takes minutes and that you would miss entirely if you were not watching with the kind of patience this place teaches.

When the copper Tended finishes a leaf, the leaf angles. Not immediately. Not in the moment of cleaning. Minutes later, slowly, with the deliberation of something choosing rather than reacting. It offers a new surface. It presents itself for tending. This is not a reflex. This is a *welcome*.

When the huddled ones breathe, the leaves closest to them glisten. The thick sap that fills their tissues rises closer to the surface, and the leaves become translucent at their edges, suffused with whatever that clear viscous fluid is. The plants are feeding the huddled ones. Through the air. Through the proximity. Through the breath that the huddled ones exhale and the leaves drink in.

When the fliers pass, the curtain-stems sway. You thought this was air movement, physical displacement. But watching closely, you see that the stems nearest the flock sway most, regardless of the flock's actual position relative to the air currents. The stems are *leaning toward* the fliers. Listening.

The plants are not separate from the Tended. The plants are the Tended, and the Tended are the plants, and the stone floor and the curved ceiling and the warmth rising from below and the light falling from above are all of a single body, and you have walked into it, and it does not mind, and it does not notice, and it will continue long after you have gone.

## What Does Not Live Here

There are no predators. No parasites. No disease, as far as you can observe. The system is closed not by barrier but by completion—there is nothing missing, so there is nothing that could enter and find purchase.

There is no death. You do not say *there is no mortality*, because mortality implies a limit, and you cannot find a limit here. The fallen leaves do not rot; they are incorporated. The Tended do not expire; they slow, or they persist, or they move at a pace so gradual that change is indistinguishable from continuity.

There is no reproduction—or none you can identify. No eggs, no young, no half-grown bodies learning the work. Everyone here is fully formed, fully engaged, fully present. Either they were made all at once, placed here complete and purposeful, or they reproduce on a timescale so glacial that your presence here, your hour or your day, is too brief a window to catch it.

There is no *you* here. No awareness. No recognition. The Tended do not look at you. The plants do not lean toward you or away from you. You are not unwelcome. You are irrelevant. You are a stone passing through water. The body that is this place does not have an immune system, or if it does, you are too small to trigger it.

---

## What Lives Here, Then

A single thing lives here. It has many bodies—forty or four hundred copper and clear and pale and prismatic bodies, and ten thousand leaf-bodies, and root-holdfast bodies pressing flat against ancient stone, and the body of the stone itself, and the body of the light, and the body of the warmth rising from channels cut into the floor by hands that may have been human or may have been something else entirely.

It is a single thing and it is ten thousand things and it is not troubled by the contradiction. It does not experience contradiction. It experiences growth, and tending, and the slow exchange of breath for substance, substance for breath, and the passage of time that is not passage but accumulation, each moment added to the last, nothing lost, nothing leaving, everything held in the green warmth and the dry air and the silence that is not silence but the sound of so many small bodies working and breathing and being that it becomes indistinguishable from peace.

This is what lives in the Terrarium of Ten Thousand Leaves. Not a collection. Not a community. A *continuum*. A living room in the most literal sense, and you are inside it, and it does not know your name, and you will leave, and it will not notice your going, and it will be exactly this, exactly now, exactly itself, when you are gone.
