# The Cyst-Dwellers

What lives here is not separate from the place. This is the first and most important thing to understand. The intercyst does not contain life the way a jar contains water. It *is* life the way a body is life, and what lives within it are its contents, its processes, its internal ecology. They are not residents. They are expressions.

But expressions of what? This is harder to say. The intercyst has no head, no center, no locus of self that you can point to and say *there*. The thing in the pool might be a nucleus, but the machine might also be a heart, and the roots might also be a nervous system, and you realize that asking which part is the "real" creature is like asking which part of you is the real you. The intercyst is a distributed being. It has no point of view because it has no point. It has only processes, cycles, exchanges, and the slow metabolic hum of something that exists below the threshold of intention.

What follows is an accounting of what lives there. It is incomplete. It is necessarily incomplete, because the intercyst is a system whose edges are not clear. Where does the moss end and the floor begin? Where does the root end and the ceiling begin? Where does the machine end and the wall begin? These questions have no answers. The intercyst does not recognize them.

## The Cyst-Body

The intercyst itself is the primary organism. Or perhaps the primary organization. It is approximately the size and shape of a living room, but it is not a room; it is a cyst, a sac, a pocket of biological activity that has formed (or been placed, or been left) in a gap between things that were never meant to have a gap between them. It is sandwiched between geological strata and municipal infrastructure, between the clay that was deposited here thousands of years ago and the sewer lines that were laid seventy years ago, between the root systems of the hedge above and the electrical cables that run beneath the pavement. It exists in a seam. It is a seam-creature, a gap-dweller, something that occupies the space between categories and makes that occupation its mode of being.

The cyst-body is layered. The outer walls are composite: clay, the iron-like warm material, the pale mineral that sweats moisture. These are not separate substances. They grade into each other. The clay becomes mineral-rich at the boundaries. The iron-like material interleaves with both. If you were to cut a cross-section (you cannot; the walls are too hard, and you would not want to anyway), you would see that the stratigraphy is not geological but biological. This is tissue. It is tissue that has incorporated geological materials, or geological materials that have become biological through some process you do not understand. The distinction has collapsed here. The intercyst is a place where the mineral and the organic have not just mingled but *merged*. It is hard to say where the geology stops and the biology starts. The walls are both, or neither.

The inner surfaces are the interfaces: the root-hangings, the moss-carpet, the fluid-seeps. These are the mucosal boundaries, the permeable zones where exchange occurs. The intercyst is not closed. It receives from above (moisture, perhaps nutrients, perhaps something else that comes through the roots from the hedge above) and it receives from below (heat, pressure, the faint electrical current that rises from somewhere deeper). It processes these inputs through the machine, through the metabolic activity of the moss and the plants and the fauna, and it produces — what? The fluid, certainly. The warmth. The faint luminescence. The low continuous thrum that you feel in your skull when you are still enough. These are outputs. But outputs of what process, and for what purpose, you cannot say.

The cyst-body has a cycle. The machine governs it, or perhaps the machine merely marks it. Ten minutes of activity: warmth, moisture, light, movement. Three minutes of pause: cooling, dimming, stillness. This is not respiration exactly. It is more like a peristaltic wave, a slow contraction and relaxation of the whole system. During the active phase, the fluid flows more freely from the machine's pipes. The condensation thickens. The moths fly. The thing in the pool undulates. During the pause, everything slows. The fluid pools. The moths settle. The insects stop their roaming. The thing in the pool flattens itself. It is like watching a heart beat, if a heart beat once every thirteen minutes and took three minutes to relax between contractions. The cyst-body is alive. It is alive on a timescale that makes you feel impatient, that makes your own heartbeat seem frantic and wasteful by comparison.

## The Machine

The machine is the clearest example of the intercyst's refusal to respect categories. It is mechanical. It is also biological. It is both, and the boundary between these states has dissolved.

Mechanically, it is a pump. This much is clear. The flywheel turns, and the motion is transferred through internal gearing (you cannot see the gearing, but you can hear it — a faint grinding beneath the housing, like teeth chewing) to four pistons that force fluid through the copper pipes. The fluid is not water. It is thinner than water, slightly viscous, warm to the touch, and it smells of iron and something like soap — saponin, perhaps, or some other surfactant. It is slightly iridescent in the dim light, carrying a faint oily sheen that suggests complex organic compounds. When you touch it (you will touch it; you cannot help yourself), it feels like diluted blood, or like the fluid that drains from a wound that is healing well — not infected, not pus, but the clean serous discharge of tissue knitting itself back together.

Biologically, the machine is an organ. It is embedded in the wall, and the wall has grown around it. The copper pipes are not merely inserted into the wall material; they are *penetrating* it, and the wall material has formed a kind of callus around each insertion point, a thickening of the iron-like substance that suggests a healing response. The machine is not foreign. The intercyst recognizes it as part of itself. Whether it was built and then incorporated, or whether it grew into this shape through some inexplicable biological process, you cannot determine. The machine is old. The oxidation on its housing suggests decades, possibly longer. But it is also alive, or part of something alive. The flywheel is lubricated by the same fluid it pumps. The internal grinding sound is muffled by a substance that is not oil but something more like mucus. The machine is fed by the cyst-body, and it feeds the cyst-body in turn. It is a organ like a heart is an organ, but it is also a mechanism like a pump is a mechanism. The intercyst does not care that these categories are supposed to be distinct.

The machine has a rhythm, but the rhythm is not constant. Over hours (you will not stay hours, but you might return, and if you return often enough, you will notice), the cycle varies. Sometimes the active phase lasts twelve minutes, sometimes eight. Sometimes the pause extends to four minutes, sometimes it contracts to two. There is a pattern, but it is not a simple cycle. It is a rhythm that varies according to inputs you cannot perceive — perhaps the moisture content of the incoming air, perhaps the electrical current flowing through the roots, perhaps something else, something deeper. The machine is responsive. It is responding to the cyst-body's needs, and the cyst-body's needs are governed by factors that are not visible to you. You are observing an organ from outside the body it serves. You cannot know what signals it receives.

The machine was installed. The evidence is clear: the brass plate with the gauges, the ceramic insulators, the wires leading into the root mass. Humans have been here. Humans have done things to this place, or for this place, or with this place. But the machine — the pump itself, the four copper pipes, the flywheel and its grinding innards — may not be human in origin. It may be older than the installations. It may have been here first, and the humans who came later simply connected their own infrastructure to it. The weld that seals the separate pipe is amateur. The wiring is crude. The gauges measure something, but their scales correspond to no unit you recognize. Someone tried to understand the machine. Someone failed. The machine continues to pump, indifferent to their understanding or lack of it.

## The Root-Systems

The roots are the most visible living presence in the intercyst. They hang from the ceiling like organ pipes, pale and thick, their surfaces slick with condensation and faintly luminescent. They are not woody. They are not fibrous. They have the texture of aquatic plant stems — smooth, slightly yielding, filled with a tissue that is firm but not hard. If you squeeze one gently (you will; the intercyst invites touch), it gives slightly, and you feel a faint pulse within, a pressure that is not your own heartbeat but something else, something slower.

The roots descend from above. They penetrate the ceiling material, emerging from it like cables threading through a membrane. Above the ceiling is the hedge, the suburban hedge that people walk past daily without seeing the hatch behind it. The hedge is ordinary. Or it appears ordinary. But its roots have become something else here, something that no longer follows the rules of ordinary plant growth. The roots that enter the intercyst have thickened, flattened, transformed. They have become something closer to the roots of mangroves or of plants that grow in anaerobic conditions, plants that must extract what they need from difficult substrates. But the substrate here is not water or mud. It is the intercyst itself. The roots are not feeding from above. They are feeding from below. They are drawing something from this place, and that something is luminescing faintly through their tissues.

The luminescence is biological. It is not electricity, or not electricity alone. The current that flows through the wires (human wires, twentieth-century wires) joins with the roots at the ceramic insulators, and the roots carry that current, but they also produce their own light. It is a cold light, greenish-white, the color of firefly lanterns and deep-sea fish. It is produced by some process within the root tissue. Perhaps it is a byproduct of metabolism. Perhaps it is a signal. Perhaps it serves no purpose at all, any more than the glow of a jellyfish serves a purpose. It is simply what these roots do. They glow. They make the intercyst visible in its own twilight.

The roots are vascular. They carry fluid, but it is not the fluid the machine pumps. This is thinner, clearer, rising from below, drawn upward by some process of capillary action or biological pump that you cannot locate. The roots are drinking from the intercyst and carrying that drink upward, into the hedge, into the ordinary suburban shrubbery that borders an ordinary suburban street. The hedge is being fed by the intercyst. What it does with that food, you cannot say. The hedge looks normal. But then, you have not looked closely at the hedge. You have not examined its leaves or measured its growth or watched it through the seasons. Perhaps the hedge is not normal. Perhaps it is growing faster than it should, or stranger than it should, or perhaps it is simply enduring in a way that does not require it to be strange. The intercyst does not demand strangeness. It simply offers nourishment, and the roots accept.

The roots are also nervous. Not in the sense of having nerves, but in the sense of conducting signals. The electrical current that runs through the wires joins the roots, and the roots spread that current through the cyst-body, branching, dividing, diminishing. You can feel it if you bring your hand near a root — a pressure, a warmth, a faint tingling in the fingertips. It is not dangerous. It is barely perceptible. But it is present, and it follows paths through the root tissue that suggest some kind of internal communication. The roots are carrying information. What information, and to whom, you cannot say. The cyst-body has no brain. But it has a nervous system, diffuse and distributed, and the roots are its primary nerves.

## The Moths

The moths are the most numerous visible inhabitants. There are perhaps a hundred of them, perhaps more — it is hard to count when they are constantly moving, and when the light is poor, and when they settle on surfaces and become nearly invisible against the pale mineral deposits. They are small, with wingspans of perhaps two centimeters, and they are attracted to the luminescent roots. They are the mobile element in a system that is otherwise slow, settled, sessile.

Taxonomically, they are difficult to place. They are lepidopteran, certainly — the scaled wings, the curled proboscis, the large compound eyes. But they do not precisely resemble any moth family you know. Their wings are not patterned; they are a uniform pale grey, almost white in the root-light. Their bodies are thicker than most moths', almost grub-like, and their legs are longer and more articulated than is typical for flying insects. They move on surfaces with a deliberate, almost thoughtful gait, each step placed with care, as if they are navigating terrain that is invisible to you. When they fly, they do so in short, purposeful bursts, from one root to another, or from root to wall to ceiling to root again. They do not flutter. They do not drift. They travel.

They are feeding. You can see this when they settle on a root: the proboscis extends, flattens against the root's luminescent surface, and a faint ripple passes through the moth's body as it draws something up. It is not drinking the condensation. It is feeding on something in the root tissue itself, or on the surface film that the root produces. The moths are grazing. They move from root to root, feeding lightly on each, never depleting, never lingering. They are the cyst-body's mobile foragers. They are also, perhaps, its transport system. When a moth moves from one root to another, it carries with it whatever it has picked up — microbial films, biochemical compounds, electrical charge. The moths are not just feeding. They are connecting. They are the links in a distributed system, the messengers between parts that cannot otherwise communicate.

They are aware of you. This is the strangest thing about them. Moths, in your experience, are not aware of anything except light and pheromones. But these moths notice you. When you move, they adjust their flight paths. When you are still, they may settle on you — your shoulder, your hand, your hair. Their feet are cold and dry, their wings warm and soft. They do not bite. They do not feed on you. They simply land, rest, and then move on. They are curious, or the insect equivalent of curious. They are including you in their survey of the environment. You are a feature of the intercyst now, temporary though you may be.

During the machine's pause, the moths settle. They find a surface — a root, a wall, the ceiling, the floor — and they fold their wings and become still. They do not sleep. You can see their eyes moving, their antennae twitching. They are waiting. When the machine clicks and resumes, they rise again, all at once, as if responding to a signal. This is synchronous behavior. This is coordinated. The moths are not individuals in the way you think of individuals. They are nodes in a network, and that network is the cyst-body.

## The Crawl

The crawl is the name you will give them later, because you need a name and they do not have one. They are the pale, elongated, asymmetrical things that move along the root surfaces, feeding on the moisture and the biofilm. They are not insects, not precisely. They have too many legs, and those legs are not jointed in the arthropod manner. They are more like the parapodia of polychaete worms, fleshy protrusions that end in tiny hooks. But they are not worms either. They are something else, something that occupies a niche that does not quite exist outside of this place.

There are dozens of them, perhaps forty or fifty, most no more than three or four centimeters long. They move with a rippling gait, legs on one side pushing while legs on the other side pull, creating a wave-like motion that is oddly graceful. They are not symmetrical. This is the most disquieting thing about them. One side has more legs than the other — sometimes one or two more, sometimes as many as four. The imbalance does not seem to hinder them. If anything, it appears to be functional, allowing them to grip curved surfaces (like roots) more effectively. They hug the roots, bellies pressed to the luminescent surface, legs hooked into the tissue. They are cleaning. They are removing debris, excess moisture, the buildup of whatever the roots exude. They are the cyst-body's maintenance crew.

They are blind. They have no visible eyes, no photoreceptive structures. They navigate by touch and by something else — perhaps chemical sense, perhaps sensitivity to the electrical current that runs through the roots. They can detect you, though. If you bring your hand close to a root where they are feeding, they will pause, lift their front ends slightly, and wave their tiny heads in your direction. They are not aggressive. They are simply registering your presence, adding you to the inventory of stimuli. After a moment, they will resume their feeding, having decided that you are not relevant.

They reproduce here. You will see small ones, juveniles, near the base of the roots where the moss meets the wall. They are paler than the adults, almost translucent, and they move more slowly. They are not born here, exactly. They emerge from the moss, or from the wall itself. You will see a patch of moss bulge and split and a young crawl will wriggle out, damp and confused, its legs not yet coordinated. It will find a root and begin to climb, and within minutes it will be feeding, moving, integrated. The crawl are generated by the cyst-body. They are not born to parents. They are produced, like cells are produced, like tissue is produced. They are the cyst-body's maintenance of itself.

## The Worms

The worms are the hidden presence. You see them only as distortions in the moss, ridges that form and subside, slow movements that suggest something larger than the moss itself. They are thick, dark, shorter than earthworms and slower. They do not surface. They live within the moss matrix, and they are engaged in some activity that requires them to move constantly but not to emerge.

They are digesting. The moss is not just a surface; it is a digestive layer. It processes something — the fluid that seeps from the walls, perhaps, or some component of the mineral substrate that the intercyst draws from below. The worms are the digesters. They move through the moss like intestines move through a body, not going anywhere, but processing, turning, transforming. They are the cyst-body's gut.

They are warm. When you kneel and touch the moss (you will; you have come this far), you feel the heat of their metabolism. They are working hard. The moss produces the sweet organic smell of overripe fruit because the worms are producing it, or because the moss is producing it as a byproduct of what the worms do. It is not unpleasant. It is the smell of something alive and busy, something that has found its purpose and is fulfilling it.

They are not worms, not really. If you were to dig (you will not; the moss is too thick and you would harm something), you would find that they have no distinct boundaries. They are more like tubes of tissue, extensions of the moss itself, differentiated for movement and digestion. They are organs. The moss is an organ. The worms are an organ. The cyst-body does not distinguish between them.

## The Pool-Thing

This is the one that changes everything.

In the far corner of the intercyst, where the moss is thickest and the mineral deposits bulge outward like bone, there is a hollow — a bowl-shaped depression in the floor, perhaps thirty centimeters across, filled with a shallow pool of the fluid the machine pumps. The fluid is warm, slightly iridescent, and it does not evaporate. It is maintained. And within it, there is something living.

It is not a fish. It is not a salamander. It is not a larva. It is approximately fifteen centimeters long, dark, smooth-skinned, and it moves with a slow undulating motion that suggests it is not swimming but breathing — that the pool is its lung, and the undulation is respiration. It has no visible eyes. It has no visible limbs. It has a mouth, or a mouth-like opening, on its underside, and it presses this opening against the floor of the pool periodically, drawing something from the mineral substrate below. It has been doing this for a long time. The hollow is worn smooth where it feeds.

It is not separate from the pool. When you look closely (and you will look closely, because you cannot help yourself), you see that its body does not have a clear edge. It grades into the fluid. It is permeable. The fluid is not just what it lives in; the fluid is part of it, and it is part of the fluid. The pool-thing is not an organism in a medium. It is a locus of density in a continuous substance. It is where the fluid concentrates, thickens, acquires form.

It is also, perhaps, where the fluid originates. You watch it feed. It presses its mouth against the floor of the pool, and something passes from the mineral substrate into its body, and from its body into the fluid, and from the fluid into the surrounding tissue. The machine pumps the fluid, but the pool-thing produces it. Or modifies it. Or is produced by it. The relationship is not linear. It is a cycle. The machine pumps the fluid, the fluid feeds the pool-thing, the pool-thing transforms what it receives and releases it back into the fluid, the fluid feeds the roots, the roots feed the hedge above, and somewhere in this cycle the cyst-body draws what it needs from below and releases what it does not need upward. The pool-thing is a node. It is a transformer. It is a biochemical processor, organic and wet and alive.

It is also aware of you. This is the most unsettling aspect of the entire intercyst. The moths are curious. The crawl are indifferent. The worms do not know you exist. But the pool-thing — the pool-thing notices you. When you approach the pool, its undulation changes. It slows. It turns, somehow, without eyes, without a face, without any apparent sensory organs. It knows where you are. It is paying attention.

It is not hostile. You do not feel threatened. But you feel seen, and that feeling is fundamentally strange, because there is nothing here that should be able to see. The pool-thing does not have eyes. It does not need them. It perceives you through some other means — perhaps through the disturbance you create in the cyst-body's electrical field, perhaps through the vibrations of your movement, perhaps through some sense that has no name in any language you know. It knows you are there. It is waiting to see what you will do.

You will not do anything. You will not touch it. You will not speak to it. You will simply observe, and it will observe you back, and after a time it will resume its slow undulation, its periodic pressing against the pool's floor, its business of being what it is. It has accepted you. You are part of the environment now, temporary and irrelevant but present. The pool-thing has work to do, and your observation is not that work. It returns to it.

## The Human Traces

The humans who came before you left evidence. The ladder. The hatch. The ceramic insulators. The brass plate. The welded valve. The wires that carry the cyst-body's current into an infrastructure that no longer connects to anything. But they did not leave only artifacts. They left something else, something harder to see.

The intercyst remembers them.

You sense this most clearly when you are still, when you have been in the place long enough to calibrate to its rhythm. There are echoes here. Not sounds — the intercyst does not echo in the acoustic sense. But there are traces of previous presence, imprints left in the cyst-body's tissue, in the electrical patterns that run through the roots, in the way the moths approach certain surfaces and not others. Someone was here. Someone stayed. Someone did more than observe.

The weld on the valve is amateur, yes, but it is also desperate. The person who made it was afraid, or in a hurry, or both. They wanted to seal something, to prevent something from flowing through that pipe. Whatever was supposed to flow through it was not supposed to escape. The intercyst has grown around the weld, incorporated it, but the tissue around it is thicker, denser, as if the cyst-body had to work harder to accept that intrusion. The valve is a wound that healed over imperfectly.

The gauges on the brass plate measure something. The needles tremble at positions that correspond to no marked scale, but they are not random. They move. They respond to the machine's cycle, to the cyst-body's rhythm. During the active phase, the left needle rises; during the pause, it falls. The right needle moves inversely, falling during activity and rising during rest. They are measuring the intercyst. They are measuring its metabolism, or its pressure, or its electrical activity, or something else that has no name. Someone wanted to know. Someone wanted to quantify the unquantifiable.

The wires that lead into the roots were once connected to something above. You can see where they emerge from the ceiling, near the ladder, where they should connect to a power source or a monitoring device. But the connection is gone. There is a gap where human infrastructure ends and cyst-infrastructure begins. Someone cut the connection, or it was never completed, or the intercyst simply absorbed whatever was up there and left the wires trailing into nothing. The current that flows through them now does not come from above. It comes from below, from the roots, from the pool-thing, from the cyst-body itself. The intercyst generates electricity. It generates warmth. It generates light. It generates life. Whatever the humans who installed these components intended, the intercyst has subsumed that intention and made it part of its own functioning.

Someone was here. Someone did something. And then, for reasons you cannot know, they left, or were forced to leave, or simply did not return. The intercyst continued without them. It did not need them. But it kept their traces. It incorporated their artifacts. It remembered, in its distributed, non-neural way, that they had been there.

## What It Means to Be Here

You are an intrusion. This is not a judgment. It is a statement of fact. The intercyst is a closed system, or as closed as a living thing can be. It draws from below and releases above, but its internal processes are self-contained. They do not require or expect observation. You are not part of its metabolism. You are not part of its cycles. You are a foreign element, and your presence requires it to adjust.

But it does adjust. This is the surprising thing. The moths approach you. The crawl register you. The pool-thing notices you. The intercyst does not reject you. It does not expel you. It simply incorporates you into its sensory field, includes you in its awareness, and continues to function. You are a feature of the environment now. You are data.

This is the intimacy of the intercyst. You cannot be here without being perceived. You cannot stand in this compressed, crowded, living space without becoming part of the crowd. The cyst-body knows you are there. It does not mind. But it knows.

And when you leave — when you climb the ladder, push open the hatch, emerge into the cool dry air of an ordinary suburban street — you carry something with you. Not the warmth on your skin; that fades within an hour. Not the smell of copper and wet chalk; that dissipates sooner. Something else. Something harder to name.

A memory that is not yours. A rhythm that is not your heartbeat. A sense of having been inside something vast and distributed and patient, something that does not think but simply is, something that welcomed you without wanting you, accepted you without needing you.

The intercyst will continue without you. It will pump and glow and breathe and feed. It will cycle through its slow metabolic phases, its ten-minute activity and three-minute rest, for as long as it exists. It does not require your return. But it will not forget you either. You are part of its record now, part of its tissue-memory, part of the faint electrical traces that run through its roots.

You have been inside something alive. You will never not have been inside it. And that knowledge will stay with you, quiet and heavy, like a held breath.
