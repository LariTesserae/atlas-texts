The people of the Seep Works do not merely live in the damp; they are forged by it. They are the quiet mechanics of a half-submerged world, the tenders of a boundary that should not exist, holding back a river that insists on its own memory.

To look at them is to see the geography written on their skin. They carry the topography of the basin in their posture and their pores. The elder residents, those who have spent decades breathing the filtered haze, have a particular softness to them—not fragility, but a yielding quality, like leather left too long in the rain. Their skin is pale, almost translucent in the hollows of their throats and the backs of their hands, tracing veins that run dark and riverine beneath the surface. They move with a deliberate, rolling gait, weight pitched slightly forward to counter the spongy, uncertain ground. Their shoulders are rounded from years of leaning into the mist, of listening to the machinery, of bending over the moss-beds and the treatment vats. When they stand still, they root. You get the sense that if they stayed in one place long enough, they would sprout.

Their lungs are deep and slow. They breathe like bellows, drawing the thick air down into their capacious chests and holding it, extracting something from it that drylanders never could. They do not pant or wheeze; they modulate. The damp is so saturated with the particulate exhaust of the engines, the spores of the aggressive ferns, and the faint oily tang of the machines, that their mucous membranes have grown perpetually thick. Their voices carry the resonance of the pump-houses: low, rhythmic, and slightly reverberant, as if they are speaking from within a vast metal drum. When they laugh, it is a sound like water tumbling over a shallow weir—brief, constant, and dissipating quickly into the mist.

They dress in layers of dense, water-swollen wool and oiled canvas that have never truly dried out. Their garments are not chosen for style but for the management of microclimates. The outer shells shed the worst of the condensation, while the inner layers trap body heat against the perpetual chill. Their boots are tall, constructed from salvaged rubber and heavy leather, the seams sealed with black pitch that smells of the treatment facilities. Over time, the clothing adopts the green-grey palette of the Seep Works, colonized by the same minute spores that cling to the walls. They do not fight the moss on their coats; they simply brush it away when it becomes too heavy, a mundane grooming ritual like shaving or combing hair.

Their hands are their most expressive feature. They are perpetually damp, the fingertips wrinkled as if they have just withdrawn from a long soak. They have the dexterity of scavengers and fixers. A Seep Works hand can feel the vibration of a failing pump bearing through three feet of concrete; it can pluck a mineral-heavy leaf from a stem without bruising the fleshy stalk; it can identify the species of a frog solely by the slick texture of its skin. Their touch is incredibly gentle, yet unyielding. They are accustomed to handling things that are fragile, waterlogged, and vital.

---

There are distinct casts to the people, shaped by the three zones of the town, though they all share the same underlying density.

Those who dwell in the high, older houses are the memory-keepers. They live where the ground is most stable, but their homes are the most crooked, tilted by decades of slow settling. They are comfortable with things not being level. Their rooms are filled with objects that have accumulated in the slumping shelves and sagging drawers—clocks that run slow, books whose pages have swelled into solid, unreadable blocks, ceramics rimed with a permanent furring of lime. They are the ones who know the oral history of the drains, the specific lineage of the cats, and the location of every pocket-garden planted by the original planners. They do not write things down; the damp would consume the paper. Instead, they memorize with a ferocity that makes their minds feel like stone—slow to warm, but holding heat for a very long time.

Those who work the industrial basin are the rhythmic ones. They think in cycles, in the rising and falling thrum of the pumps. Their internal clocks are synced to the machinery. They wake before the dawn cycle, their blood pressure rising in anticipation of the vibration that will soon rattle the fillings in their teeth. They are a tightly wound people, conductors of a massive, leaky orchestra. They speak in shorthand, in numbers and pressures, their conversations overlapping the mechanical drone. When the pumps wind down, they experience a moment of profound physical relief, a sudden lightness, as if a hand pressing on their chests has been lifted. But they also feel a tickle of anxiety during the silence, waiting for the cycle to begin again. They are the ones who look at the exposed, wet strata of the hills and see geology as a temporary inconvenience.

Then there are the growers of the vegetation belt. They are the most uncanny of the Seep Works' inhabitants. They move through the thick green filter with an ease that borders on the botanical. They seem to know where the air is heaviest with industrial residue before the leaves even trap it. They harvest the heavy metals from the moss with their bare, wrinkled hands, rinsing the toxins away in the seep-pools with a casualness that would kill a drylander. They smell of crushed fern, wet loam, and oxidation. They have an unspoken arrangement with the cats and the larger, stranger frogs that haunt the interior pools. They are practitioners of a quiet, localized alchemy—processing the poison of the town into usable medicines and fibers, keeping the ecosystem in its tense, productive balance. When they speak, it is often to the plants, or to the machinery of their own bodies, murmuring instructions to their own slow, steady breathing.

---

They do not treat the damp as an enemy. To fight the water here would be to fight the ground itself. Instead, they practice a philosophy of accommodation. When a floorboard rots, they do not replace it; they lay a board over it, accepting the softness. When a wall weeps, they place a basin to catch the tears. They are pragmatists of the permeable.

This pragmatism extends to their interactions with one another. Relationships here are long-accumulating, like the sediment in the pools. They do not rush into intimacy; the environment discourages sudden movements of any kind. Friendships and romances form slowly, built through shared tasks—maintaining a shared filtration gutter, feeding a shared colony of cats, keeping a shared wall from listing too far into the alley. They are fiercely loyal to their immediate neighbors, but their world is small. They feel no need to expand it. The canal system brings in strangers occasionally, but the Seep Works people regard outsiders with a polite, watchful distance, the same way they observe the water pooling in a new crack—measuring the threat, calculating the containment.

They are not a joyful people, but neither are they desolate. They possess a profound, resonant contentment, akin to the satisfaction of a machine that is functioning exactly as it was designed to, despite its age and its patches. They find beauty in the saturated colors of their world, in the way the amber light from the treatment plant catches the fog, in the emerald shine of a well-tended moss wall. Their aesthetics are baroque, favoring accumulation and texture. They like things that bear the visible weight of their environment—a table bowed by moisture, a pipe wrapped in salvaged wire, a face lined by years of squinting through the mist.

---

The uncanny nature of the Seep Works has seeped into them, too.

They are aware that the dampness is not entirely natural, that the condensation is too heavy, the moss too hungry. But they do not speak of it in terms of haunting or magic. They speak of it in terms of *pressure*. "The pressure is high today," they will say, when the pools form too quickly and the frogs fall silent before the pumps even begin their cycle. "The pressure wants the lower ground." They treat the water's memory as a fact of physics, not a mysticism. The river is not a ghost; it is a displaced volume seeking its level.

Yet, they also harbor a quiet, unspoken reverence. They leave small offerings at the drainage grates—scraps of the smoked fish, a particularly perfect fern frond, a twist of the wire used to splice the electrical cables. It is not a formal religion. It is a propitiation of the infrastructure. They depend on the pumps, but they also depend on the water's willingness to be pumped. They live in the space between two immense pressures: the mechanical and the hydrological. It has made them slightly hard of hearing to anything else. The concerns of the dry world above the hills sound thin and trivial to them, like the buzzing of the insects that die on the surface of the pools.

When a child is born in the Seep Works, the water that collects on the windowpane is gathered on a finger and touched to the infant's lips. It is not a blessing; it is an introduction. *You will live here*, the gesture says. *You will be soft, and you will persist.*

They are, all of them, a little like the cats—watchful, plump with the spoils of their niche, intensely territorial, and entirely unbothered by the deeper workings of the place they inhabit. They do not need to understand the system to maintain it. They are the maintenance. They are the living sponge, the human filter, keeping the dark, wet machinery of Lower Morrigan turning, breathing, and seeping, season after season, waiting patiently alongside the ground for the water to remember the way down.
