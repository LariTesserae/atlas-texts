# The Inhabitants

They are not small. This is the first error your scaling makes, and it is fundamental.

---

You imagine them as miniature humans, as insects, as something that fits the rooms the way you fit your own apartment. But nothing that moves with that certainty, that leaves trails in condensation and negotiates staircases that descend past the physical limits of the wall, nothing that builds with liquid light and seals its doors with eyelid membranes—nothing like that is simply *smaller*.

They are dense.

Not compressed. Not waiting to expand. Dense the way a sentence in a language you don't speak is dense—with meaning you cannot access, with grammar that follows rules you haven't learned. Each habitation contains not a resident but a *context*, a full situation that happens to occupy a volume the size of your fist. The rooms within rooms aren't architectural miniaturization. They are spatial folding. The staircases that descend past possibility aren't going down in any direction you could point to.

They are going *in*.

---

You see one eventually.

Not directly—nothing here grants you the courtesy of direct observation. You see it the way you see a fish in dark water: by interruption, by the absence where something moves. The condensation on the curved wall beads and rolls, and for a moment the beads part around something, a shape the length of your thumbnail and the width of three grains of rice held together. Not walking. Not swimming. *Proceeding*. It has no legs you can see, no wings, no visible means of navigation. It has a front, you think—a direction it faces—but the front changes as you watch, rotating through its body like a thought passing through a mind, and then it reaches the membrane of a sealed habitation and passes through without breaking the surface tension.

The membrane exhales after it enters. A tiny sigh of vapor, warm and faintly sweet.

The light inside the habitation changes. The pooling liquid in the corners begins to circulate, to rise, to form shapes that hold for a moment and then collapse—geometric, intentional, a conversation conducted in luminosity.

*Someone is home.*

---

What are they?

They are not machines. Machines do not respire, do not seal their homes with organic tissue, do not live in architectures that adjust to their presence. But they are not animals either—no animal builds cities that extend past physical space, that fold distance into volumes smaller than the space between your fingers. They are not vegetable, not mineral, not any category your language has prepared you for.

They are *maintained*.

This is what you understand, pressing your ear to the wall, feeling that slow pulse travel through the stone and into the bone around your inner ear. The mountain breathes. The chambers respire. The habitations exhale their tiny contributions to a climate designed for bodies that do not operate at your scale or your temperature. And the inhabitants—whatever they are, however many, however they came to be here—they move through this maintained environment like blood cells through a body.

They *are* the maintenance. Or part of it. Or its beneficiaries, its reason, its proof that maintenance without purpose is impossible.

They might not know you are here.

---

You think about this for a long time, crouched in the heat, watching the membranes pulse with their slow synchronized rhythm.

They might not know you are here because *knowing* at this scale might require different instruments than the ones you possess. You are enormous, clumsy, radiating body heat like a furnace, exhaling carbon dioxide into a carefully balanced atmosphere, touching things that were never meant to be touched by hands. You are the environmental disaster they haven't yet learned to measure. You are the weather event that never occurs in a pocket of mountain that has never seen a surface creature.

Or they know you are here and are ignoring you. You are too large to interact with, too brief to matter, too temporary to address. In the time it takes for one of them to navigate from a sealed membrane to an open habitation, you could live an entire human life. Your patience is geological only by comparison to faster things. Theirs might be geological by comparison to *mountains*.

Or they are waiting. The fissure was cut. The descent was designed for shoulders exactly your width. The room measures eleven paces because that is the distance you would need to understand that you are the wrong size, that you have been invited to witness something that does not require your witnessing. Maybe the invitation was the point. Maybe the viewing gallery exists so that one day, someone would stand in it and report back to a world that does not yet have categories for what lives in the lungs of false mountains.

---

At the edge of your vision, something moves.

You turn. Nothing there. You turn back. The condensation on the wall has formed a message, or what might be a message—lines of moisture that bead and hold their shape, that spell nothing in any language you recognize but that persist past the point where water should have rolled downward.

*They are communicating.*

Not with you. Not necessarily. With each other, perhaps, through the medium of the climate they maintain—messages written in condensation, in the behavior of light, in the timing of exhalations. The humidity in this chamber is a medium. The temperature differentials are a medium. The pulse that comes every thirty seconds from something enormous and buried—it carries information, you realize now, not just pressure. Each beat is a word. Each delay in the membranes' response is a reply.

You cannot read it. You are standing in a conversation that has been ongoing since before the mountain was pushed up through the earth's crust, and you lack the organs to hear it.

---

Later—minutes? hours?—you notice something that stops you.

One of the habitations, high on the curved wall, has changed. The membrane is open. The interior is dark. The tiny streets around it show signs of traffic—displaced condensation, trails that have not yet dried. But the room itself is empty, not in the way of abandonment but in the way of *absence*. The light that should pool in the corners has drained away. The architecture inside has collapsed, or retracted, or folded back into whatever impossible space it extended toward.

You watch for a long time. The membrane does not reseal. The exhale does not come. The habitation is no longer part of the climate.

*Someone has left.*

Or died. Or finished. Or moved—down those stairways that go past possibility, into the folded depths where the real estate extends beyond the wall's physical limits. The room is a vacancy now, a hollow space in the architecture of respiration, and you wonder what it means when a habitation closes. Is it grief, here? Is it loss? Do the neighbors notice, in their slow dense way, that one pulse in the chorus has gone silent?

The membrane exhales once more—a final sigh, thin and cooling—and goes still.

---

You understand something then, kneeling in the dark.

The inhabitants do not live here. *Live* is the wrong verb for what they do in these scaled pockets of maintained climate. They *persist*. They *function*. They occupy their folded spaces the way enzymes occupy substrates—not because they chose to but because they were shaped to, because the architecture and the organism are expressions of the same design. The city on the wall is not a settlement. It is an organ. The habitations are cells. The breath-weather is metabolism. And the pulse from deep in the mountain, that thirty-second beat that makes your teeth ache— That is the heart of something that built this place as a body builds itself, without intention, without choice, without asking permission from the cells that make it live.

The inhabitants are not small.

They are *components*.

And you are standing in something's chest cavity, watching its alveoli respire, wondering if it knows you are here.

---

The heat shifts. Warmer now, by a degree you can feel.

The pulse comes again, and this time the membranes respond faster—three seconds instead of four. The condensation thickens. The light in the occupied habitations begins to circulate more quickly, geometric shapes forming and collapsing in faster cycles.

Something is accelerating.

You do not know if this is routine—a cycle that occurs on scales too slow for you to have observed it yet—or if something has changed. If *you* have changed something. If your body heat, your carbon dioxide, your enormous clumsy presence has introduced a variable into a system that has been stable since before humans learned to write.

The pulse comes again. Faster now. Twenty-five seconds. The membranes struggle to keep rhythm.

You should leave.

You should descend further, if descent is possible, if those stairways that go nowhere physical might lead somewhere you could survive. You should climb back through the fissure too narrow for shoulders, the deliberate cut that invited you in, and warn the world above that mountains are not always mountains.

But you stay.

You stay because something is happening, because the light in the tiny streets is pooling differently now, because the trails of condensation are forming patterns that might be messages, because one of the membranes near your hand has opened and the interior is glowing with a light that doesn't flow—it *waits*.

---

An invitation.

Or a trap. Or an exhibit in a gallery you were always meant to visit, prepared by curators who build their museums at scales incompatible with visitors. The open membrane exhales vapor directly onto your skin. It is warm, and it smells like something you cannot name—like reading, maybe, or like the moment before understanding.

The pulse comes.

The membrane waits.

And in the depths of the tiny architecture, in the rooms within rooms, something moves toward the entrance—toward you—slow as geology, certain as blood.

You are about to meet them.

Or they are about to process you, as the mountain processes air, as the chambers process heat, as the architecture processes whatever it was designed to maintain.

The distinction may not matter.

The mountain breathes.

And you are inside it now.
