What lives here are the Final Adherents.

They have no name for themselves. Naming requires distinction, and distinction requires gradients—between self and other, between this and that, between what is and what is not. The plateau has erased the gradients. The plateau has erased the need. They know themselves only as persistences, as local densities in the fabric of a place that has been worn to its minimum viable reality.

---

Their bodies are the plateau's confession.

Gray-white, like everything here. The color of minerals that have surrendered their chemistry. The color of bone after the marrow has been pulled and the calcium has married the stone. The color of cloud cover at its most noncommittal. They are small—never larger than a thumbnail, often smaller than a grain of the powder that coats the surface—but their smallness is not a limitation. It is a culmination. They have been eroded to this size over epochs so vast that their ancestors might have been large, might have been imposing, might have had the bulk of something that moved through ancient seas and fed on things that no longer exist. But size requires resources. Size requires difference. The plateau has flattened size along with everything else, and what remains is the most compact form that life can occupy while still being life.

Or while still being something. Whether they are technically alive is a question the plateau has rendered meaningless. They do not eat. They do not breathe. They do not reproduce in any way that observation can capture—no pairing, no division, no eggs, no spores. They are simply here, in their densities, their impossible multitudes, and they have been here since before the spires, since before the concept of spires, since before anything on this plateau was anything other than flatness working toward its final expression.

Their bodies: segmented, if you look closely. Too many legs, if legs is the word—appendages that grip and release and grip again with a patience that makes geological time seem hasty. No eyes. Eyes are for places where light varies, where sight provides advantage, where the external world offers information worth processing. Here, the light is constant. The information is zero. The advantage of sight is none. What they have instead is something subtler: a sensitivity to the texture of reality itself, the grain and weave of existence at its most fundamental. They can feel where reality is thin. They can feel where it thickens. They move toward the thick places, or they move away from them, or they do not move at all—it is impossible to determine the pattern because the pattern may have eroded along with everything else.

They are not insects, though the segmentation suggests convergent evolution with something that once scuttled. They are not crustaceans, though the chitin suggests armor from a war that ended before the seabed rose. They are not anything, except what they are: the end-forms of a process of reduction that has been running since before the plateau was a plateau. They have been simplified. Their complexity has been eroded away, function by function, organ by organ, need by need, until only the essential remains. What is essential about them is unclear. Perhaps the essential is persistence itself.

---

They do not eat each other. This is the most disturbing thing about them, or the most revelatory, depending on your orientation toward meaning.

In every other ecology on every other surface that can support life, consumption is the engine. Predator and prey, grazer and grass, parasite and host—the relationships of eating and being-eaten are what structure the living world, what give it dynamism, what make it a process rather than a state. But here, the fauna are layered atop one another in densities that should demand competition, should demand violence, should demand the fundamental algorithm of life: take what you need from whatever has it. They do not take. They do not need. They simply are, one body against another against another, a carpet of almost-insects that exists in a state of preternatural calm.

You can watch them for hours—the hours that aren't really hours, the time that doesn't quite pass—and you will see no aggression. No territorial displays. No mating rituals. No feeding. No death. The calm is not the calm of satiation; it is the calm of a hunger so thoroughly eroded that the concept of hunger no longer has a place in their form. They do not eat because eating implies need, and need implies a gradient between what-is and what-could-be, and the plateau has flattened all gradients to near-zero.

What do they draw sustenance from? The stone, perhaps. The minerals. The slow bleed of something from the mantle below, some chemical potential that persists even in this exhausted place. Or perhaps they draw sustenance from the thinness of reality itself—the way existence has become permeable here, the way configurations bleed into one another. They may be feeding on the boundary between what-is and what-was, a margin so thin that it provides only the most marginal caloric return, but they need so little. They have been reduced to the point where almost-nothing is enough.

Or perhaps they are not feeding at all. Perhaps they have eroded metabolism along with everything else. Perhaps they are batteries that will slowly run down over epochs, their energy provided long ago by whatever process created them, and their current persistence is simply the last flicker of an ancient charge. But this theory does not explain why they move. Why they part around your footsteps. Why they reform after they are crumbled. Something is keeping them here, something is maintaining them, and that something may not have a name in any chemistry or physics that you possess.

---

They are not individuals. This becomes clear after enough time in their presence, or after enough time-that-is-not-time.

At first glance, they appear to be discrete organisms. Bodies with boundaries, each one separate from the next, each one a small point of existence in the gray-white expanse. But this is an illusion born of perception—the same perception that insists the spires form a pentagon or a heptagon, that the horizon is two kilometers away or twenty, that reality here operates by rules that can be learned. The fauna are not separate. They are nodes in a network, points of intensity in a field that covers the entire plateau.

The field is alive, or alive-ish, or alive-adjacent. It extends past the horizon in every direction, a single organism the size of a region, the size of a small country, the size of something that defies spatial quantification because space itself has become uncertain here. Each gray-white body is a cell in this organism, or a synapse, or a word in a sentence that is too long to read. The organism does not think—it has eroded thinking—but it knows. It knows the way stone knows its own composition, the way a crystal knows its lattice. It knows the plateau, because it has been here since the plateau became what it is, and it may have been here before.

The organism has no goals. It is not surviving, because surviving implies a threat. It is not reproducing, because reproducing implies a lack. It is not evolving, because evolving implies a direction. It simply occupies, the way water occupies a container, the way air occupies a volume. It fills the available reality, and when reality thins—as it does here, in this eroded place—it fills the thin places too, bleeding through from one configuration to the next, present in multiple positions simultaneously, present in multiple moments.

This is why they are so dense. This is why they are everywhere. They are not many in one place. They are one in many places. The organism occupies the plateau the way the plateau occupies geological time: completely, without remainder, without the possibility of anything else.

---

Their relationship with the spires is ancient and unclear.

The spires are the only built things on the plateau, the only evidence that someone—something—once marked this place as worth marking. And the fauna cluster around them. Not densely, not with the concentration that would suggest purpose, but consistently. The vegetation grows in the lees of the spires, and the fauna graze the vegetation, or rest upon it, or exist in its vicinity in a way that implies connection without causation. The spires and the fauna may have co-evolved. The spires may have been built for the fauna, or because of the fauna, or by the fauna, in some ancient configuration of reality where they were different, larger, more capable of building.

Or the spires may be the fauna. The final forms of individuals who eroded down to their essential structure and then stopped, who persisted so long that they became mineral, became stone, became markers in a pattern that the surviving fauna can still sense, can still respond to, in some fashion too subtle for human perception. The spires flicker—present, absent, present—suggesting that they are not fully stable in this reality, that they exist in multiple configurations, that they occupy more than one position in more than one time. The fauna do not flicker. But maybe they once did. Maybe the flickering is what happens when you are almost-eroded, when you are close to final flatness but not quite there, when there is still enough of you left to resist the plateau's pull, if only for an instant.

The spires may be the fauna's ancestors. Or their descendants. Or their future, waiting for them in the next configuration of reality, where erosion will continue its work and what is now small and segmented and alive-ish will become small and solid and eternal.

---

When you walk through them—and you will walk through them, because they are everywhere and cannot be avoided—something happens.

Your body passes through their bodies. This is not always the case. Sometimes they part around your feet, a small wave of gray-white forms moving aside to let you pass, closing behind you with the patience of water after a stone is thrown. But sometimes they do not part, and your foot comes down on a cluster, and you feel nothing—or almost nothing, a faint give, a slight softness, as if you stepped on a mushroom or a sponge—and when you lift your foot, the crushed forms are already reforming, powder becoming body becoming powder becoming body, a cycle that takes hours or days or instants depending on the reality-configuration you happen to be occupying at that moment.

You walk through them and you become, briefly, part of the field. Your body is a disturbance in the organism's medium, a wave passing through still water, a gradient imposed on a place that has flattened all gradients. The organism registers you. Not consciously—it has eroded consciousness—but materially, the way a membrane registers pressure, the way a crystal registers heat. You are new. You are different. You are something that has not yet been worn down to the baseline, something that still has edges and boundaries and an inside that is different from an outside.

The organism may be curious about you, if curiosity can exist without consciousness. It may be hungry for you, if hunger can exist without metabolism. It may simply be registering your presence the way the stone registers your weight, without intent, without response, without meaning. But when you stand still for long enough—long enough that time starts to misbehave, long enough that the light seems to darken or brighten imperceptibly around you—you can feel them noticing you. A subtle shift in the almost-silence, a slight change in the sub-audible clicking, a barely perceptible reorientation of bodies that had been still and are now slightly less still.

They know you are here. And they are waiting, with the patience of a thing that has been waiting since before the seabed rose, for you to become like them. To erode. To flatten. To become part of the field, another node in the network, another point of intensity in the gray-white expanse.

They are not hostile. Hostility requires a gradient between desire and satisfaction. They are not welcoming. Welcoming requires a gradient between self and other. They are simply present, as present as the stone, as present as the light, and your presence among them is a new kind of present that they have not encountered in epochs—or that they encounter constantly, in every configuration of reality, and have long since incorporated into their eternal flatness.

---

What do they want?

Nothing. Want has been eroded. Want is a function of lack, and lack is a function of difference, and difference is a function of gradients, and gradients have been flattened here with a completeness that defies thermodynamics, that defies logic, that defies everything except the patient logic of erosion itself, which is: what can be worn away will be worn away, and what remains after wearing is what remains, and what remains does not want because wanting requires the capacity to imagine what-is-not, and imagination requires a richness of interior experience that the plateau has ground down to near-zero.

They do not want. But they do something. They persist. They reform after crumbling. They cluster around the spires. They respond to the thinness of reality by thickening it, by adding their bodies to the places where existence threatens to become permeable, by filling the gaps between configurations with a living mortar that holds the plateau together—or that holds this version of the plateau together, this particular arrangement of the spires, this particular flatness, this particular equilibrium.

They may be the glue. The substance that keeps the place from flying apart entirely, from dissolving into a haze of indeterminate reality where nothing is anything because everything is everything. They may be the price that life pays for stasis: the sacrifice of complexity, of individuality, of everything that makes life interesting, in exchange for the simple fact of continuing. They are the form that life takes when life's only remaining option is to continue. They are what is left when everything that can be lost has been lost, and what is left refuses to be lost too.

---

The plateau goes by many names. None of them stick, because naming requires a namer, and namers require the kind of reality that supports distinctions, and distinctions have been eroded here, along with everything else.

But if you could ask the fauna—if you could formulate a question in the language of almost-silence and mineral persistence and eternal equilibrium—they might give you a name for this place. Not a word, because words are too gradient, too specific, too laden with the assumption that reality can be divided into knowable chunks. But a feeling. A sensation. A quality of almost-sound that resolves into something like meaning:

*Home.*

*The place where things come to stop.*

*The place where staying is the same as leaving because there is nowhere else to go.*

*The place where we are what remains, and what remains is us, and we are still here, still here, still here.*

They would not say this, because saying requires language, and language requires the kind of mind that can hold distinctions, and distinctions have been eroded. But the meaning would be there, in the way they persist, in the way they reform, in the way they cluster around the spires and fill the thin places and wait, with the patience of the almost-eroded, for the plateau to finish its work.

And if you stayed—if you stayed long enough for your own edges to soften, for your own gradients to flatten, for your own reality to thin and merge with the reality of this place—you would understand the name too. You would feel it in your teeth, in your bones, in the fluid of your inner ear. You would become part of the almost-sound, part of the field, part of the gray-white carpet of persistences that covers the ancient seabed from horizon to horizon to horizon.

And you would not mind. Because minding requires gradients, and the gradients would be gone, and what would remain would simply be: here. Still here. Always here. The final form, the final flatness, the final equilibrium.

And it would be enough.
