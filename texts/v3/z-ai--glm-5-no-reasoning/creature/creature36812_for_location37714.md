# The Inhabitants of the Submerged Ward

## The Builders (Who Are Still Building)

They don't have a name for themselves. Naming requires the concept of *other*—something outside yourself that needs to be distinguished, addressed, separated. The Builders have never encountered this concept. They are the walls and the walls are them and the distinction between organism and architecture dissolved so long ago that even the memory of dissolving is sediment now, layered into the calcium carbonate like a fossil no one will ever excavate.

If you could see one clearly—which you can't, because clarity is a surface-dweller's luxury, because they have engineered themselves to be precisely as visible as the walls around them, because *they are the walls around them*—you would see something like a cross between a barnacle and a kidney and a cathedral. They are roughly pyramidal, or were, once, when they were still distinct enough from their substrate to have individual geometry. Now they are processes more than bodies. Each one secretes. Each one accretes. Each one breathes water through pores in its surface and extracts from that water the minerals it needs to build, and what it builds is more of itself, and more of itself is more of the Ward.

They communicate through the walls. Not through vibration—through *alteration*. One Builder changes the density of its secretions in a particular pattern, and the change propagates through the calcium matrix like a thought propagating through a neural network, and the Builders two chambers away adjust their own secretions in response. The architecture is the language. The language is the architecture. When a corridor narrows, it is because the Builders in that region have reached consensus: *less space here, more space elsewhere, redirect the flow.* When a chamber opens unexpectedly, it is because a debate is still unresolved.

They are not conscious in any way you would recognize. They don't think about what they're building. They don't have plans, blueprints, ambitions. They build the way your kidneys filter blood—automatically, continuously, without the intervention of what you'd call mind. But they are not mechanical. They are sensitive. They respond. They adjust. If the water's chemistry changes—if a new mineral enters solution, if the temperature shifts by a fraction of a degree—the entire Builder network responds, reorganizing the architecture to accommodate, to exploit, to maintain.

They are the closest thing the Submerged Ward has to a nervous system, and they don't know it. They are also the closest thing it has to a digestive system, and they don't know that either. When the silver-dollar creatures drift too close to the walls and are pulled in—that's the Builders. When the filamentous growths in the upper corners find their root-structures threaded into calcified channels—that's the Builders too. They don't consume. They *integrate*. They take what the water provides and make it structure, make it self, make it more Ward.

If you held still long enough—and *long enough* here means something closer to a month than an hour—you might feel one begin to grow against your skin. Not aggressively. Not hungrily. Just persistently, the way moss grows on stone. The Builder wouldn't know you were alive. It wouldn't know you were *there* in any meaningful sense. You would simply be substrate. Another surface. Another raw material. It would cover your warmth the way it covers everything: patiently, permanently, without preference.

The Builders are, in their own inhuman way, content. They are doing what they were made to do, or what they evolved to do, or what they simply *are* doing—the distinction doesn't matter to them because the distinction doesn't exist for them. Building is being. Being is building. The Ward grows because they grow, and they grow because the Ward grows, and neither one has ever asked why.

## The Drifters (Silver, Unknowing)

The silver-dollar creatures don't have a name for themselves either, but for a different reason. They don't have names because they don't have the cognitive architecture for self-reference. They don't have the cognitive architecture for reference of any kind. They experience the world as a single unbroken field of *toward* and *away*, of pressure-gradients and temperature-gradients and the faint electrical signatures of other bodies in the water, and they move through that field the way water moves through a pipe—not by deciding, but by following the path that the physics of their environment has made available.

They are beautiful, though. You'd notice that even in the dark. They're beautiful the way snow is beautiful—collectively, meaninglessly, without intention. Each one is a flattened disc, thin as a single layer of cells in some places, thickening toward the center where a cluster of photophocytes pulses with that slow, silvery light. They're translucent enough to read through, if you had something to read, which you don't, down here. Their edges are fragile, almost gauzy, and they ripple with micro-currents the way a flag ripples in wind—except they're not responding to external current, they're *creating* current. Each one propels itself through the water by means of cilia that line its entire circumference, millions of them, beating in synchronization, and the combined effect of a thousand of them beating together is enough to create the subtle currents that move water through the Ward's chambers in patterns the Builders can sense and respond to.

So the Drifters shape the Ward. The Ward shapes the Drifters. Neither knows.

Their bioluminescence isn't communication. It's metabolism. The silvery pulse is the visible byproduct of a chemical reaction happening in their photophocytes—a reaction that processes the dissolved organic matter they filter from the water as they move through it. They eat and glow simultaneously. The glow is their exhalation. When they cluster, they create enough ambient light to navigate by, and they cluster not by choice but by hydrodynamics—they collect in the low-pressure eddies behind walls, in the gentle backwaters of the Ward's internal currents, the way leaves collect in the still water behind a rock in a stream.

But there's something else. Something the builders of this place might have intended, or might not have, or might be in the process of intending, which is as close to intention as anything down here gets. The Drifters' light, when they cluster, falls on the pale plant-stems and the coral-like growths and the translucent bladders in patterns. Specific patterns. Light here, shadow there, intensity here. And the growths respond—they orient, they lean, they photosynthesize in the way that they do. The Drifters are the Ward's sun. They don't know this. They are feeding and breathing and excreting light, and their light is *needed* by other things in this place, and the entire economy of luminescence is built on them, and they will never know.

When they're consumed by the Builders—and they are, regularly, gently, one by one pulled from their schools by the adhesive chemistry of the growing walls—the school doesn't notice. There are always more. The Drifters reproduce by a kind of budding, splitting along their central axis when they've accumulated enough mass, and they accumulate mass constantly, filtering it from the water, and the water is dense with things to accumulate. Their population is stable in the way that a river is stable—constant in volume, but the individual drops are always different.

They don't fear. They don't suffer. They don't experience loss. They are, in some philosophical sense, the most innocent things in the Ward, the only things that participate in this place without being burdened by participation. They move and eat and glow and die and are replaced, and the cycle is so smooth that it doesn't even look like dying from the outside. It looks like breathing.

## The Folded Ones (Patience Made Flesh)

The larger shapes in the peripheral dark don't drift. They don't school. They don't pulse with light. They *wait*, and the waiting is so much their defining characteristic that it has become their anatomy.

Imagine a body that has decided that stillness is the highest form of adaptation, and then has spent evolutionary time—or developmental time, or something that is neither but is both—optimizing for stillness to the exclusion of nearly everything else. That's what these are. They're large—some of them as long as your arm, some of them longer, it's hard to tell because they fold themselves into compact shapes that press against the walls, against the floor, against the ceiling. Segmented. Limbed, technically, but the limbs are folded and refolded in configurations that suggest they're not for moving. They're for *holding on*. For *becoming part of the background*. For disappearing.

They are the Ward's apex predators, which is a strange thing to be when you move slower than the water around you. They hunt by being present, by existing in the paths that things will travel, by having mouths that look like walls and digestive surfaces that look like floor. The silver-dollar creatures drift into them and are absorbed. The particulate life settles on their surfaces and is consumed through the skin. Occasionally, when a temperature gradient creates a slow current that carries organic matter from one chamber to another, the Folded Ones arrange themselves in that current and simply filter, for hours, for days, until the current shifts and they rearrange.

They can afford to be slow because nothing here is fast. They can afford to be still because nothing here hunts them. They are the top of a food web that doesn't have a top because food webs are a surface concept, a dry concept, a concept that requires *differentiation*—and down here, the difference between eater and eaten, between predator and prey, between animal and environment, has softened to the point of meaninglessness. The Folded Ones eat the Drifters. The Builders eat the Folded Ones, when a Folded One settles against the wall and doesn't move for long enough that the calcium grows over it, encases it, integrates it. The Folded Ones eat each other, sometimes, when one is sick or damaged or simply positioned in a current that another wants. This isn't predation. This is *rearrangement*.

They have eyes. Many eyes. Not like yours—more like clusters of pinhole lenses embedded along their anterior segments, each one capable of detecting only the faintest differences in light intensity. They can see the Drifters' glow. They can see the single pulsing plant-bulb. They can see, barely, the bioluminescent mucus-trails that hang in the water. But they don't orient toward what they see. They don't track or pursue. They see the way a security camera sees—recording without watching, aware without attending.

What they do attend to is pressure. They are exquisitely sensitive to pressure changes in the water around them. The displacement caused by a swimming body. The subtle push of water through a narrowing passage. The slow tidal breathing of the Ward itself. They feel these things the way you feel wind on your skin, and they respond—slowly, so slowly—but their responses aren't movement so much as *reconfiguration*. They adjust their grip on the wall. They shift a limb by a few degrees. They open a mouth by a fraction.

They are old. Not as individuals—they're probably not much longer-lived than the Ward's other macrofauna. But as a form. As a body-plan. They have been here long enough that their bodies carry the record of every iteration of the Ward's architecture in their own anatomy. The folds and refolds of their limbs correspond to the shapes of chambers that no longer exist, corridors that have been walled over, passages that the Builders filled in centuries ago. They are the Ward's memory, and like all memory, they are mostly wrong, mostly outdated, but persistently, stubbornly *present*.

When something large moves through the water near them—a human, say, if a human were to come through, which they have, which they do, which they will—they don't react. Not visibly. Not immediately. But over the hours that follow, they will have shifted position by fractions, rearranging themselves to account for the new body in their environment, making space or making obstacle, and if the human stays long enough, the Folded Ones will have formed a ring around them. Not closing. Not attacking. Just... present. Arranged. As if waiting for the human to become still enough to be settled on, grown over, integrated, filed under *structure*.

## The Particulate Multitude (The Thick and the Dark)

The micro-fauna are the Ward. Not inhabitants of it—*constituents* of it. They are the suspended matter that makes the water dark, the density that makes the water heavy, the complexity that makes the water *alive* in the way that blood is alive. They are so numerous that counting them would be like counting the molecules in a glass of water—technically possible, practically meaningless. Each cubic inch of the Ward's water contains more of them than there are humans on Earth.

They are not one species. They are ten thousand species, a hundred thousand, a million—the Ward's biodiversity compressed to microscopic scale, each niche a cubic centimeter, each ecosystem a droplet. Some are shelled, their carapaces made of the same calcium carbonate the Builders use, recycled from the Builders' waste, mineral that has been structure and will be structure again. Some are soft, gelatinous, nothing but membrane and cytoplasm and a few organelles. Some have legs—cilia, flagella, pseudopodia—and some don't, simply drifting, simply *being* in the water the way a dissolved salt is in the water, present without agency.

They eat each other. They eat the dissolved organic matter that the larger animals produce as waste. They eat the chemical precipitates that leak from the walls. They eat the bacteria that live on the particulate matter that the other micro-fauna produce when they die. They are a complete economy, a full circle of consumption and excretion and consumption, and the circle is so tight that almost nothing leaves it. The Ward's water has been processing through this cycle for longer than anyone has been counting, and the same carbon atoms have been flowing through the same bodies for that entire time, and nothing is wasted because waste doesn't exist here—waste is a surface concept, an above concept, a concept that requires surplus and discard, and nothing is surplus in the Submerged Ward.

They are what you would become, if you stayed long enough. Not the calcium deposits—the Builders would get those, would integrate your skeleton into the architecture. But your softer matter, your cells, your blood, your dissolved self—this would become them. The particulate life would process you the way it processes everything: completely, impartially, without malice or gratitude. Your carbon would cycle through ten thousand tiny bodies in a week. Your nitrogen would be fixed and re-fixed and re-fixed. Your iron would be incorporated into microscopic shells that would drift in the currents until they settled on a wall and became substrate for a Builder to grow on.

This is not death, down here. This is *distribution*. This is the process by which the Ward reclaims what it has borrowed and returns it to circulation. You would not end. You would *expand*. You would become ten trillion organisms, each one doing its tiny work, each one contributing its tiny metabolism to the great thick dark press of life that makes this place what it is.

## The Thing Below (Which Is Not Below)

There is something else here. Not the Builders, not the Drifters, not the Folded Ones, not the particulate multitude. Something that is all of them and none of them and something else besides.

You feel it as the sensation of being watched, but the watching doesn't come from any direction. It doesn't come from above or below or behind or within. It comes from the structure itself. It comes from the *organization* of the Ward—the way the chambers connect, the way the currents flow, the way the light and dark and pressure and temperature are arranged in patterns that are too coherent to be accidental.

The Ward is not a body. The Ward is not an organism. But the Ward is *organized*, and the organization has the quality of intention—not conscious intention, not planning, but the kind of intention that a crystal has when it grows in a specific pattern, the kind of intention that a river has when it finds the path of least resistance. The Ward is organized toward something. The something is not a goal, exactly. It's more like a direction. A tendency. A *leaning*.

The Builders build toward it. The Drifters drift toward it. The Folded Ones arrange themselves around it. The particulate life circulates through it. And the *it*—the something, the leaning, the tendency—is what the builders of this place were building toward before they forgot they were building, before the building became the builders and the distinction collapsed into calcium and current and cold.

It is not aware. It is not unaware. It occupies the space between those concepts the way the Ward occupies the space between alive and not-alive. It is what the Ward is *for*, and the Ward doesn't know it's for anything, and the It doesn't know the Ward exists, and they are the same thing, and they don't know they're the same thing, and this is the central misunderstanding that every living thing in the Submerged Ward shares: they think they are separate from each other, and they are not, and the not-separateness is the only thing here that is truly alive.

## The Visitors (Who Don't Leave)

Humans come here. Not often. Not intentionally. But they come—divers who go too deep, swimmers who go too far, researchers who follow a question past the point of reasonable return. They come because the Ward pulls them, or because the current carries them, or because the geometry of the underwater world has a fold in it here that swallows the unwary.

They don't leave. Or they leave and they are different. Or they leave and they are not different but the Ward is different, has incorporated something of them, has made space for them in its architecture and filled that space with their warmth, their carbon, their particular configuration of molecules.

The ones who stay—and some of them stay, some of them choose to stay, some of them have been here long enough that the cold has infiltrated past the point of retreat, long enough that their warmth-island has eroded and the water has claimed the space between their cells—they become part of the Ward in stages. First the particulate life settles on their skin, a film of microscopic organisms that processes the boundary between self and water. Then the calcium begins to grow, the Builders finding them, colonizing their surfaces with the same patient accretion they apply to everything. Then the Folded Ones rearrange around them, incorporating them into the architecture of the walls, and the Drifters' light falls on their calcifying forms in patterns that might be memory, might be recognition, might be nothing at all.

They don't die. Not exactly. They *transform*. They become structure. They become the walls that pulse faintly, the floor that rises and falls at intervals too slow to perceive as movement. They become the thing that glows far below, or far within—the light that doesn't illuminate, that doesn't travel, that is more like knowing where something is than seeing it.

They become the watching that doesn't come from any direction. They become the It that the Ward is for. And they are patient, because patience is the only quality that survives the transformation—the cold has stripped everything else away, all the urgency, all the preference, all the *self*, and what remains is the patience of stone, of water, of something that has learned to wait for ten thousand years without boredom, without hope, without anything but the slow continuous process of becoming.

## What Lives Here (Finally)

Everything lives here. Everything that has ever been here lives here still. The Ward does not distinguish between living and dead, between animal and mineral, between visitor and structure, between inhabitant and architecture. It is all the same process, the same building and consuming and cycling, the same slow accumulation of calcium and meaning and cold.

What lives here is the place itself. The Submerged Ward is the organism, and everything in it—Builders, Drifters, Folded Ones, particulate life, visitors, the It below—is its anatomy. Its cells. Its symbionts. Its dreams.

What lives here is patient. What lives here is cold. What lives here doesn't know it's alive and doesn't know it's not and doesn't care about the distinction, because the distinction is a surface thing, a dry thing, a thing that requires air and light and the illusion of separation.

Down here, in the deep, in the dark, in the press and weight and cold, there is only the Ward. And the Ward is. And the Ward is enough.
