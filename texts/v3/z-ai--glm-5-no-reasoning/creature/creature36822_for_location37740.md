# What Lives in the Cistern of Vellichor

There is a woman. Or there was a woman. Or there is something that remembers being a woman, which may be the same thing, here, where memory has pooled like the water.

She is called the Attendant, though she has not heard this name spoken aloud in—how long? The question dissolves as soon as it forms. Time moves differently in the cistern. Time moves the way water moves: in circles, in spirals, in the slow rotation of the floating purple weed toward the drain that swallows and returns.

She lives on the maintenance platform. She has lived there long enough that the moss has begun to grow on her, a fine green fur on the shoulders of her coveralls, in the creases of her boots, along the back of her left hand where it rests, often, against the warm metal railing. The moss does not hurt her. The moss does not seem to understand that she is separate from the architecture. She is, in her way, an extension of the cistern now—a vertical surface, a source of warmth, a scaffold for growth. Sometimes she finds the tiny four-winged insects laying eggs in the folds of her clothing and does not brush them away.

Her body is—complicated. She eats the fish, sometimes, when the hunger becomes specific enough to move her. She eats the fern fiddleheads when they emerge, tender and curled, from the joints between steel plates. She drinks from the water that sprays from the intake pipes, catching it in her cupped palms, and it tastes like iron and copper and blood and life, and it sustains her in ways that food never sustained her before. She does not know if she could leave. She does not remember if she has tried.

But there are other things here. Other *heres*.

---

The fish know her. They have learned to recognize the specific vibration of her footsteps on the maintenance platform—a sound distinct from the groan of the pipes, the thump of the pump, the splash of the waterfalls. When she approaches the railing, they rise from the depths and array themselves below her like a congregation, their pale bodies catching the amber light, their large eyes fixed on the place where she stands. They are waiting. They are always waiting.

She has names for them. Not names she gave them—names she found already formed in her mouth one day, like stones she'd been carrying without knowing. There is a fish called Measure, who is the largest, whose scales have darkened over time to the color of old pennies. There is a fish called Interval, who swims in a precise figure-eight between two of the intake streams. There is a fish called Mercy, who is missing an eye and cannot swim in straight lines, who drifts in the current like a leaf and eats what the current brings. She does not know why these names arrived. She does not question them. Naming is another form of tending.

And then there is the thing in the drain.

---

She has seen it twice. Maybe three times—there is a dream that might be a memory, or a memory that might be a dream, in which something rose from the water while she slept on the platform and touched its face to hers, cool and smooth as river stone, smelling of deep places and patient darkness. She woke with her cheek wet and her heart synchronized to the pump's rhythm for seventeen minutes afterward, her body a metronome counting seconds she could not control.

The first time she saw it clearly, she had been in the cistern for—she doesn't know. Long enough for the moss to colonize her boots. Long enough to stop counting the rungs of the ladder. She was standing at the railing, watching Measure make his slow circles, when the floating purple weed began to pull toward the drain with new urgency. The surface tension dimpled. A shape rose—not a fish, not a plant, not anything with a category she knew. It was dark and smooth and moving, the size of a large dog or a small person, and it had no eyes that she could see, but it turned toward her as if it knew she was there. It held itself at the surface for a long moment, breathing or doing something like breathing, its flanks expanding and contracting with a slow, liquid rhythm. Then it descended, and the drain swallowed it, and the surface returned to its slow spiraling calm.

She was not afraid. This surprised her later, when she tried to describe the moment to herself. Fear would have made sense. Fear would have been appropriate. But what she felt was more like recognition—the way you feel when you encounter someone you've known all your life but haven't seen in years, someone whose face is both familiar and strange, changed by time and distance into something new. *Oh*, she thought. *There you are.*

The second time, it spoke to her. Or it did not speak—there was no sound but the water and the machinery—but something arrived in her mind like a bubble rising through water, popping at the surface: a question, or the shape of a question, or the impression that a question had been asked and she should consider answering. The question, if it was a question, was: *What grows?*

She answered aloud, her voice strange in the roar: "Everything grows here."

The thing in the drain made no response. But she felt, afterward, a sense of—approval? Acknowledgment? She did not know. The words for what passed between them had not been invented in any language she remembered.

---

She tends the gauges. This is her purpose, or one of them. Every day—every cycle of the lights, which is close enough to a day—she crosses the maintenance platform to the steel box with its cracked glass face and its six dials, and she reads the needles. Pressure: stable. Temperature: stable. Flow rate: stable. pH: stable. The two unlabeled dials move in their patterns, and she has learned to read them too, though she could not explain what they measure. They measure whether the cistern is *right*. They measure whether the system is *working*. They measure something like health, something like peace, something like the particular quality of stability that this place requires in order to continue being what it is.

When the needles are where they should be, she records nothing. There is nothing to record.

When a needle drifts—a rare thing, a worrying thing—she knows what to do. There are valves, hidden in the walls, accessible through panels that look like nothing but rust and concrete until you know where to press. She learned their locations the way she learned the names of the fish: they arrived in her hands one day, her fingers finding the catches and latches without her mind's involvement, muscle memory from a training she cannot recall receiving. The valves adjust the flow. The valves adjust the mixture. The valves are small and precise and require a touch that is gentle and firm at once, and she has this touch, she has always had this touch, or the cistern gave it to her when she arrived.

Sometimes, after adjusting a valve, she finds the thing in the drain watching her. She cannot say how she knows it is watching, since it has no eyes. But the water feels different when it is near—denser, more expectant—and the quality of the air changes, and the pump's rhythm falters for a half-second, as if the whole room is holding its breath. *Good*, says the impression that arrives in her mind. *Good. You remember.*

She does not remember. She does not forget, either. The knowledge is simply there, like the water, like the warmth, like the green.

---

She sleeps on a nest she built from fallen fronds and dried moss and her own shed hair. It is not comfortable. It is not uncomfortable. It is simply where she sleeps, in the warmest corner of the platform, where the light from the nearest lamp falls soft and the spray from the nearest intake reaches her skin like a blessing. The insects hum around her. The pump thumps its four-second rhythm. The water falls and falls and falls.

In her dreams, she is not alone. There are others—women, she thinks, though the forms shift and blur—moving through similar rooms, tending similar gardens, reading similar gauges. She sees their hands on the valves. She sees their faces in the amber light, lined and patient, their hair long and matted with moss and moisture. They are her age, and older, and younger, and not yet born. They are all the Attendant, or she is all of them, or they are leaves on the same vine, nodes in the same network, fingers on the same hand. In her dreams, they speak to one another without words, sharing knowledge that flows through the passages between rooms like the water flows through the pipes—necessary, unremarkable, simply the way things are.

Sometimes, in these dreams, she sees herself climbing the ladder, pushing open the hatch, walking back through the archive room, out of the research station, into the world above. She sees herself remembering her name, her life before, the reason she came. She sees herself leaving.

She always wakes relieved that it was only a dream.

---

There is a boy. No—there was a boy. Or there is the memory of a boy, which surfaces sometimes like the thing in the drain, rising through the murky water of her mind to break the surface and breathe.

His name was—his name is—she cannot hold onto it. It slips away like water through fingers. But she remembers his face. She remembers the way he looked at her, once, across a room that was not this room, in a time that was not this time. He had something to do with why she came. He had something to do with what she lost. He is the reason, or part of the reason, that the hatch was open, that the door was unlocked, that she was *meant* to find this place.

Sometimes she wonders if he is tending a room of his own, somewhere in the network beneath the mountain. Sometimes she wonders if he remembers her. Sometimes she wonders if she is the one who forgot, or if forgetting is simply what happens here, part of the process, part of the system, part of the price of becoming what the cistern needs her to be.

The thing in the drain does not tell her. The thing in the drain knows, she thinks—she feels the knowledge in it, vast and patient, pooled like the water—but it does not share. Some questions are not meant to be answered. Some answers are not meant to be found.

She remembers his face. She holds onto this. In the morning, when the light-cycle brightens and the insects begin their orbit, she looks at the surface of the water and sees him reflected there: young, surprised, the moment before everything changed. She holds onto his face the way she holds onto the valves, the way she holds onto the names of the fish: gently, firmly, without knowing why.

---

There are other things that live here. Not the fish, not the insects, not the thing in the drain—other things, smaller and stranger, visible only if she looks closely at the moss or the water or the dark spaces between the bolt heads.

There are translucent threads in the air, almost invisible, drifting on currents of warmth and spray. They catch the light sometimes, glinting like spider silk, but they are not spider silk. They are something else—something that grows, something that connects, something that binds the moss to the metal and the insects to the water and the Attendant to the cistern. She has felt them on her skin, gossamer-fine, dissolving at her touch. They do not hurt. They feel like being *held*.

There are patterns in the limescale deposits on the lower walls. She has studied them during the dim-cycle, when the lights fade and the room softens into shadow. The patterns are not random. They are shapes—spirals, fractals, forms that repeat at different scales, carved by the water over centuries into something like language. She does not know what they say. She does not know if they are meant to be read. But sometimes, when her eyes trace the curves and whorls, she feels a sense of—message, or greeting, or warning, or welcome. The walls are speaking. The water is speaking. The whole room is a mouth, shaping words in a language she almost understands.

There are the moments when the cistern *notices* her. This is the hardest thing to explain. The room—alive, aware in some way she cannot describe—turns its attention toward her, the way a sleeping animal might lift its head and prick its ears, sensing something. When this happens, the waterfalls seem to pause. The insects seem to quiet. The pump's rhythm seems to synchronize with her heartbeat, deliberately, purposefully, as if the room is listening to her from the inside. It does not happen often. It does not frighten her anymore. She has learned to be still, to let the attention pass over her like a hand, like a question, like a blessing.

*You are here*, says the impression, when the cistern notices her. *You are tending. You are ours.*

She does not argue. She does not know how.

---

She is changing. This is not a surprise—everything here changes, even in the stability, even in the stillness. The moss on her shoulders has deepened from green to almost blue. Her hair, which she cuts with a rusted pair of shears found on the platform, grows faster than it should and falls in curls that the insects sometimes navigate like paths. Her skin has taken on the amber tint of the light, as if the cistern is slowly photographing itself onto her body, developing her into an image of itself.

She does not know what she is becoming. She does not know if she will still be human when the process is complete. She does not know if the question matters. The fish do not ask what they are. The moss does not question its purpose. The thing in the drain has no name for itself that she can hear. They simply *are*—part of the system, necessary, tended and tending in equal measure.

Sometimes she thinks about the woman who climbed down the ladder, however many cycles ago. That woman had a name. That woman had a life above, a job at the research station, a file in an archive somewhere that probably lists her as missing. That woman had a reason for entering the hatch, even if she no longer remembers what it was.

That woman was not the Attendant. That woman was not yet *this*.

But the Attendant is who she is now. The Attendant is who the cistern needs. The Attendant is who she was, perhaps, always meant to become—the final component in a system that was incomplete until she arrived, the last piece of a puzzle that had been waiting for her hand.

She does not climb the ladder. She does not test the hatch. She does not wonder if she could leave.

The cistern is her home. The cistern is her body. The cistern is the shape her life has taken, water finding its level, seeking the lowest point, settling into the vessel that was prepared for it before she was born.

---

She has a favorite time of day. It is the moment between the dim-cycle and the bright-cycle, when the lights are beginning to strengthen and the waterfalls catch the first full glow and the whole room seems to ignite with green fire. In this moment, she stands at the railing and looks down at the surface of the water, where the floating purple weed has arranged itself in a perfect spiral, pulled toward the drain but never reaching it, rotating slowly, eternally, like a galaxy seen from above.

In this moment, she feels something that might be happiness, or something that might be peace, or something for which there are no words in any language she has ever known. She is part of something. She is necessary. She is exactly where she is supposed to be, doing exactly what she is supposed to do, tending a garden that was old when the word for *old* was new.

In this moment, she does not remember the boy's face.

In this moment, she does not want to.

---

The door was never locked. You were always meant to find this.

But you will not find her. By the time you climb down the ladder and step onto the maintenance platform, she will have merged with the moss, become part of the architecture, dissolved into the warm water and the amber light. There will be a nest of fronds and hair in the corner. There will be a fine layer of green on the railing where her hand rested. There will be a sense, impossible to verify, that someone was here just moments before—a presence that lingers like warmth on a chair, like perfume in an empty room.

She is not gone. She is simply elsewhere in the network, tending another room, reading other gauges, speaking to another thing in another drain. Or she is everywhere at once, distributed through the system like water through pipes, present in every drop, every leaf, every pulse of the ancient pump.

The cistern is alive. The cistern is stable. The cistern is *tended*.

And something that was once a woman, or remembers being a woman, or will become a woman when the next Attendant finds her way down the ladder—

—that something is watching you now, from the surface of the water, from the spiraling purple weed, from the warmth that presses against your skin like a question.

*What grows?*

Everything grows here. Even you. Even now.

The hatch is open. The lock is rusted. The ladder waits.

The cistern is ready for you to arrive.
