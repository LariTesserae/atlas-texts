# What Lives at the Weeping Spires

The first answer is: the film.

But that is not a sufficient answer, and any honest account must say why.

---

The film is the plateau's skin. That is the metaphor that comes, and it is wrong in the ways metaphors usually are, but it opens onto something. A skin implies a body beneath it, a boundary between inside and outside, a thing wrapped. The film does wrap. It covers. It adheres to stone the way a membrane adheres to the cell it contains — not mechanically, not chemically in any way that has been isolated, but *completely*. It is everywhere the water is, which is everywhere. It responds to light the way an iris responds to light, by widening and narrowing, by letting more through or less. It responds to water the way root systems respond to water, by following, by thickening where the flow is rich and thinning where it is not. It is warm. This is the detail that resists every framework. The air is cold, the stone is cold, the water that seeps through the stone is cold — and the film is warm. Not much. Two degrees, perhaps three. Enough that if you press your palm to the rock — and people do, people always do, drawn by something that is not curiosity exactly but is nearer to recognition — you feel the warmth before you feel the wet, and the warmth feels intentional.

What is it? The question has been asked. The answers come back unsatisfying. It is not bacterial, not fungal, not algal. It contains no cells that microscopes can resolve. It contains no DNA. It contains, as near as anyone can determine, complex arrangements of mineral and water — iron oxides, silicate chains, bound water in structures that should not be stable at surface temperatures and pressures but are. It is, if this is possible, a mineral organism. A wet machine made of the same stuff as the rock it lives on, running on water and light and the harmonic resonance of the plateau itself. It does not reproduce in any observed way. It does not die. It does not grow in the way living things grow, by consuming and converting. It *spreads*, and where it spreads it is already fully itself, as if it were not increasing but merely revealing the extent of what was already there.

This is the first thing that lives at the Weeping Spires. It is the dominant presence. It is the thing that, if anything here has intention, possesses it.

---

The second answer is harder to give because it concerns the people, and the people are not quite people in the way the word usually means.

They come up from below. This is known because the lighter stones used in the partial rebuild of one shelter can be traced to a lower formation, and because the few tools found on the plateau are made from a chert that does not occur above twenty-five hundred meters. They come up from the valleys, from wherever people live in the country below the escarpment, and they come to the plateau, and they build or maintain their small shelters, and then they — stay? Visit? The distinction collapses. A shelter with a fire ring that has been used recently but shows no sign of continuous use. A tool left behind, sharp, as if the owner intended to return for it. Spiral patterns of stacked stones that might be markers or might be prayers or might be something for which no analog exists in any culture that has been documented.

They are not researchers. They are not pilgrims in any recognized sense. They do not come in groups — or if they do, the groups are small enough that no trace of their passage remains beyond the single-occupancy shelters. They come alone, or nearly alone, and they stay for durations that cannot be determined, and they leave, and the plateau absorbs the evidence of their presence with the patience of water absorbing stone.

What are they doing there? The most honest answer, the one that a few returned visitors have given in fragments before falling silent, is: *listening*. They come to listen. Not to the sound in the way an acoustician might, measuring frequencies and durations, but to the sound in the way a body listens to another body — with the chest, the sternum, the jaw, the backs of the eyes. They come to stand in the sound and let it work on them. And the film — this is speculation, but it is speculation grounded in the testimony of those who have been there — the film responds to them. Not aggressively. Not in any way that suggests predation or parasitism. But when a person stands on the plateau long enough, the film near them darkens. It thickens. It warms further. And the person feels something — not a communication, not a message, but a *presence*, the way you feel another person in a dark room, the way you feel the weight of attention directed at you.

The people who come to the plateau come because of this. They come because the plateau is the only place they have ever been where the world attends to them without asking anything in return. Or without asking anything they can identify. The exchange, if there is one, is conducted in a currency that has no name and no denomination. You stand. You listen. The film listens back. The stone warms beneath your hand. You leave when you leave, and the plateau continues without interruption, and you carry something with you that you cannot describe, and you come back because you cannot help it.

---

The third answer — the one that no one wants to give, because it exceeds every category — is that the plateau itself lives.

Not metaphorically. Not in the way that a river or a forest might be said to live, by hosting life within it. The plateau — the stone, the water system, the harmonic resonance, the film, the light, the fog — operates as a single continuous process, and the process has the qualities of a living thing. It maintains itself. It responds to perturbation. It has a metabolism, if the word can be used for the cycling of water through mineral and the conversion of light into the film's slow activity. It has a rhythm — the daily cycle of contraction and dilation, the intensification after rain, the deepening of the drone. It has a boundary — the edge of the plateau, where the film stops and the stone becomes ordinary rock. It has been doing this for a span of time that is geological in scale, which means that whatever it is, it is stable in the way that life is not stable, persistent in the way that mountains are persistent, and yet responsive in the way that mountains are not responsive.

It does not think. This must be said, or at least guessed at, because thinking is not what it does. It does not plan. It does not remember in the way that organisms with nervous systems remember. But it *processes*. The patterns in the stone — the warped bands that appear and disappear, that hold attention like silent speech — may be its processing. The sound may be its processing. The film's slow pulse across thousands of square meters of wet stone may be its processing. Something is being computed, or worked, or *held*, and the plateau is the medium in which it happens, and the film is the organ that performs it, and the water is the medium through which the organ communicates with itself, and the sound is the sound of the communication, and the people who come to listen are — what? Witnesses? Participants? Components?

No one knows. The plateau does not explain itself. It simply runs, day after day, fog after rain after light after dark, and the sound continues, and the film pulses, and the stone sweats, and the patterns come and go in the banding, and the shelters sit in their hollows with film on their walls and glassy ash in their fire rings, and the people come up from below and stand in the sound and feel the warmth of the film through the soles of their feet and the palms of their hands, and something is exchanged that no one can name, and the sun goes down, and the fog thickens, and the Weeping continues through the night, and the plateau does not sleep, because it does not sleep, because it has never slept, because whatever it is doing does not allow for interruption.

---

There is a fourth answer, but it is the kind of answer that comes from standing too long in the sound, from letting the film warm your skin, from watching the light break prismatically across the spires until your shadow dissolves and you cannot tell whether you are standing on the plateau or the plateau is standing on you.

The fourth answer is: something is being built.

Not a structure. Not a physical thing. Something is being assembled in the stone, in the water, in the harmonic resonance, in the patterns that come and go in the banding. Something is being woven, strand by strand, pulse by pulse, across a timescale that makes human lifespans look like the flicker of a candle. The film is the loom. The water is the thread. The sound is the rhythm of the work. And the people who come to listen — they are not workers, not exactly, but they are not nothing, either. Their presence changes the film. Their warmth changes the stone. Their attention — their human, limited, desperate attention — is absorbed into the process and becomes part of it, the way a dye is absorbed into fabric, changing the color without changing the structure.

What is being built? I said I did not know, and I do not. But standing there, in the late afternoon, with the fog climbing the escarpment and the rain beginning and the sound deepening until it filled every cavity of my body, I had a feeling. Not a thought. A feeling. The feeling that the plateau has been working on this thing for longer than humans have existed, and that it will continue working on it for longer than humans will exist, and that the thing, when it is finished — if it is ever finished, if *finished* is even a concept that applies — will not be for us. It will not be for anything we can recognize as an audience. It will be for the plateau itself, or for the stone, or for the deep earth below the stone, or for nothing at all.

And the feeling was not sad. The feeling was the opposite of sad. The feeling was the feeling of standing next to something that is doing exactly what it is supposed to be doing, completely, without distraction, without doubt, without any awareness that it might fail or that failure is even possible. The feeling was the feeling of being in the presence of purpose so total that it does not need to be conscious to be real.

The plateau lives. The film lives. The people who visit live, in their limited and flickering way. And something else — something that is not life as we define it but is not death, is not inertia, is not mere mechanism — something else lives there too, in the sound, in the stone, in the slow exudation of water through mineral, and it is working, and it does not know what it is building, and it does not need to know, and this is the thing that is hardest to accept, and the thing that makes the place what it is:

It does not need to know. It works anyway. The work is the knowing. The Weeping is the building. And what lives there is the sound of a world making itself, moment by moment, without end, without purpose, without anything we would recognize as a self, and yet — and yet — so entirely itself that standing in its presence feels like standing in the presence of the most alive thing you have ever encountered, more alive than any forest, any ocean, any city full of people who know they are alive and cannot stop telling themselves so.

The plateau does not tell itself anything. It simply weeps, and builds, and waits for nothing, and is full.
