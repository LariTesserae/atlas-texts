# The Inheritors

They are not what you expected.

When you first heard the sounds—the clicks, the choruses, the subsonic thrum of large bodies—you pictured something feral. Animals in the biological sense: instinct-driven, solipsistic, operating on loops of hunger and fear and reproduction. You pictured the fauna of the Veldt-Cathedral as inheritors in the passive sense: squatters in abandoned architecture, making do with what remained.

You were wrong.

---

## The First Encounter

The one that finds you in the plaza is neither large nor small—mid-sized, perhaps forty kilograms, moving with a gait that resolves itself into distinct phases. First: observation. It stops at the plaza's edge, sensors (you see them now—clusters of light-sensitive tissue distributed across what you took to be its anterior) fixing on your position. Second: approach. Not direct. It circles at a distance of perhaps ten meters, footpads silent on the leaf-litter, carapace shifting color to match the substrate beneath it.

Third: communication.

The sound it makes is like nothing you prepared for. Not a call, not a cry—a sequence: click-pause-click-click-pause, repeated with subtle variations. Testing. Probing. You realize with a start that it is *asking* something. The sequence has structure. Syntax. Meaning that you cannot access but that exists nonetheless, suspended in the dim air between you.

You hold still. It approaches closer—five meters now—and you see the details of its form. The carapace is not uniform but segmented, pleated like the bellows of an instrument, allowing expansion and contraction as it moves. Its limbs (six? eight? they fold and unfold in ways that defeat counting) terminate in broad pads ringed with finer appendages—manipulators, you think, not feet. It could grip things. Hold things. *Make* things.

Its underside glows faintly—a warm amber bioluminescence that pulses with its breathing, or something like breathing. The light filters through translucent panels in the carapace, and you understand suddenly that this creature is not merely *in* the city. It is *of* the city. The glow matches the glow of the stalk-channels, the seed-pods, the membrane-sky. Whatever process illuminates the architecture illuminates the fauna.

They are not separate systems.

---

## The Nature of the Inheritors

You stay still longer, and more of them arrive.

They emerge from between the stalks, from the covered pathways, from hollows in the architecture that you had taken for decorative features. They are varied in size—the smallest perhaps the mass of a cat, the largest comparable to a draft horse—but they share a common body plan. Distributed sensing. Sealed carapaces. Rolling gaits that cover ground with eerie silence. And everywhere, always, the glow from within.

They are not individuals. Or rather—they are individuals, but individuals in the way that cells in a body are individuals. You watch them interact: the click-sequences exchanged, the gestures made with those manipulator-tipped limbs, the way they cluster and disperse in patterns that suggest not competition but coordination. They are communicating across distances, you realize. The ones you can see are speaking to others beyond your sight, relaying information about the anomaly (you) in their midst.

They are intelligent.

The recognition lands with weight. These are not animals. These are the inheritors in the true sense—the ones who received what the builders left, who learned to read the architecture, who maintained (or became) the systems that keep the Veldt-Cathedral functioning across geological time. They are not squatters. They are the maintenance crew. Or perhaps—and this thought is stranger—they are the inhabitants the city was built for, after all. Not the original inhabitants. Not the ones who designed the stalk-pillars and sized the pathways and grew the benches for bipedal bodies. But the ones who came after. The ones who learned to live in the shape of what remained.

---

## How They Live

You watch them across cycles, and patterns emerge.

They feed at specific times, emerging from the deeper stalk-forests to harvest seed-pods from the plaza edges. They do not take all the pods—they take specific ones, leaving others to ripen and fall, to become part of the leaf-litter substrate. They are managing the ecosystem, you understand. Not depleting. Sustaining. The harvest is calculated.

They rest in the hollows that you took for architectural details—spaces grown into the stalk-pillars at regular intervals, sized precisely for their bodies. The fit is too exact to be coincidence. Either the hollows were grown for them, or they have evolved to fit the hollows, or (and this seems most likely) the relationship has evolved together over cons, the city and the inheritors shaping each other in a slow dialogue of form and function.

They work.

This is what surprises you most. They work. You see them tending the stalk-channels, clearing debris from the light-ducts, applying some secretion from their manipulators to areas where the carapace-surface has worn thin. You see one (larger than the others, marked with patterns of darker coloration) examining a crack in a fallen stalk-section, probing the synthetic-silicon threads woven through the tissue, making sounds that might be diagnosis.

They are maintaining the city.

They do not use tools in the way you expected—not handheld implements, not external devices. Their tools are *themselves*. The manipulators at the ends of their limbs can shape, cut, bind, mend. The secretions they produce can seal or dissolve organic matter. Their bodies are integrated into the system they tend, parts of the machine rather than operators of it.

They have been doing this, you realize, for longer than human civilization has existed. Longer than human civilization is likely to exist. The Veldt-Cathedral has cycled through cons of maintenance, and they have been the maintainers. Generation after generation, inheriting not just the architecture but the knowledge of how to tend it—passed down in click-sequences, in demonstrated gestures, in the slow accumulation of technique.

They are a civilization. Just not a civilization you recognize.

---

## What They Want

The large one with the dark markings—the one you have started to think of as a sort of leader, though you have no evidence that their social structure includes such roles—approaches you again.

It has been cycles since the first encounter. You have not moved from the plaza, subsisting on seed-pods and water (sap) drawn from cut stems. The inheritors have watched you, communicated about you, perhaps debated your presence. You do not know if they have a concept of threat. You do not know if they have a concept of welcome. You do not know enough to know what you do not know.

But now it approaches, and it carries something.

The object is small—hand-sized, roughly spherical, covered in patterns that resolve under close inspection into deliberate markings. Not decoration. Information. The surface is etched with lines and curves that might be writing, might be diagram, might be something for which you have no category. The object glows faintly amber, matching the bioluminescence of the one who carries it.

It sets the object on the leaf-litter between you. Steps back. Waits.

You understand, suddenly, what it is doing.

It is giving you something. A gift? A test? A message? You cannot tell. But the gesture itself is unmistakable: here is a thing, it says. Here is a thing we made, or found, or value. We place it between us. What you do next tells us what you are.

You pick up the object. It is lighter than expected, warm to the touch, vibrating faintly with some internal process. The markings shift under your gaze—not moving exactly, but seeming to *reframe*, new patterns emerging from what you took to be background texture.

The inheritors watch. The large one makes a sound—a single click, held long, rising in pitch at the end.

A question.

You do not know the answer. You do not know what the object is or what it means or what they want you to do with it. You are outside the system, outside the cons of accumulated meaning, outside the slow dialogue between city and maintainer. You hold their artifact and feel the weight of not understanding.

But you can still choose.

You raise the object to your face, examine it closely, turn it in your hands. You are looking. You are paying attention. You are treating the thing as a thing that matters.

The large one clicks again—short, sharp, descending. The others echo it, a ripple of sound passing through the gathered inheritors.

They are satisfied. Not because you understood. Because you tried.

---

## The Deeper Inheritance

Later (you have lost count of cycles), you see something that changes everything.

The large one is working near a fallen stalk, the one with the exposed cross-section, the one that shows the synthetic-silicon threads woven through the organic tissue. It is not repairing the stalk—it is harvesting. With precise movements of its manipulators, it extracts threads from the fallen mass, coils them, carries them to a nearby hollow.

You follow (at a distance, always at a distance) and see where it deposits the threads: in a space filled with similar coils, similar extracts, a hoard of materials gathered from across the city. And beside the hoard, something else.

Something *new*.

The inheritor is building. Not repairing—*building*. The materials are arranged in a pattern that has no obvious function, that does not match the existing architecture, that seems to be entirely novel. The shape is strange: not stalk-like, not path-like, not anything you can name. It is a form without precedent, expression without reference.

You watch for a long time. The large one works steadily, adding to the structure, adjusting, stepping back to observe, returning. Other inheritors come and go, bringing materials, making observations, contributing clicks to what might be discussion. The work continues across cycles.

And you understand: they are not just maintaining what they inherited. They are adding to it. They are creating new forms, new structures, new meanings. The Veldt-Cathedral was built by someone else, yes. But it is being *continued* by them. They are not stewards of a dead legacy. They are participants in a living process.

They are the ones who inherited the capacity to build.

---

## What You Mean to Them

You are still here. Still temporary, still out of place, still breathing the air they processed and walking the leaves they tend. You have become part of their environment—a feature, now, rather than an anomaly. They ignore you more often than not, working around you, communicating past you, including you only insofar as you intersect with their processes.

But sometimes they acknowledge you.

A small one—one of the cat-sized inheritors—approaches you in a quiet period, when the chorus of clicks has faded and the wind-movement through the stalks provides the only sound. It carries nothing, offers nothing, makes no gesture you can interpret. It simply settles on the leaf-litter nearby, arranges its limbs beneath its carapace, and rests.

Watches you.

The moment stretches. You feel the weight of its distributed sensors, the attention of a mind you cannot fathom directed at a mind it cannot fathom. You are both aliens here. You are both temporary. The Veldt-Cathedral will continue long after you are gone and long after this small inheritor's line has passed into whatever succession awaits.

But for now, in the dim light, beneath the pulsing membrane, you are present to each other.

You do not know what it sees when it looks at you. You do not know if you are curiosity or threat or resource or something else entirely. You do not know if it pities you, fears you, reveres you, or simply accepts you as a feature of the environment—like wind, like light, like time.

But it stays. And you stay. And the city breathes around you both, patient beyond all patience, holding space for whatever will come next.

---

## The Inheritors, Summarized

They are the children of the Veldt-Cathedral—not by blood, not by biology, but by inheritance. They received what the builders left and made it their own. They maintained the systems, tended the growth, learned the logic of a technology so advanced it had become indistinguishable from life. And then they began to extend it.

They are not what the builders intended. They are something else entirely—emergent, adaptive, patient in the way that only things with geological time horizons can be patient. They are civilization without writing, technology without tools, society without hierarchy. They are the answer to a question you did not know could be asked: *what happens to a world after its creators leave?*

This. This happens.

They inherit. They maintain. They continue.

And they watch you, in the dim light, with minds you cannot read, doing something that might be waiting or might be studying or might simply be *being*—the deep presence of things that exist so far outside your timescale that presence itself is their primary mode of relation.

They are here. They will be here after you leave, if you leave. They will be here after the Veldt-Cathedral finally fails, if it fails, and they will carry what they can to whatever comes next.

They are the ones who stayed.
