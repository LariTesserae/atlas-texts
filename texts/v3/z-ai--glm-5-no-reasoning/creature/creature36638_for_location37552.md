# What Lives in the Gall-Heart of Wickwright

## The People

They are pale the way the inside of a bone is pale. Not bloodless — their blood moves, their hearts work, they are warm — but the color has been leached from them the way tannin is leached from bark in standing water. Slowly. Over years. Over drips. The blue light of the biofilm has become their light, and their skin has accommodated it, has shifted toward the spectrum of the walls, has taken on a translucent quality at the wrists and temples where the veins show close, so that in certain angles of the glow they look less like people standing in a space and more like features of the space itself — growths, extensions, the gall's way of becoming aware of itself.

There are eight. There are sometimes ten. The count is never fixed because the gall is never fixed, and the people who live in it have learned that certainty about numbers is a habit from outside, from the fen, from whatever world exists beyond the fen, and it does not apply here. Here, the relevant question is not *how many* but *where* — who is in which alcove, who is awake, who has gone deep.

---

**The Gall-Keeper** was not always called this. She had a name before. She does not remember it, or she remembers it and does not use it, which is different. She came to the gall young — a girl, or near enough, fleeing something she will not describe, not because it is secret but because the memory has been replaced by the wood the way a stain is replaced by a darker stain. The thing she fled is the pale stain. The gall is the dark one. The dark one won.

She is old now, or she is not old but has become what old looks like in the gall: her skin has the grain of the wood. Not metaphorically. The pattern of the heartwood — the concentric lines, the ridges, the faint stippling where the pores are — has appeared on her forearms, her shoulders, the planes of her back where she presses against the alcove wall. It is not a disease. It is not a transformation. It is *accommodation* — the same process that shapes the alcoves to bodies, happening in reverse, the body shaping itself to the alcove. She has lain against the grain so long that the grain has found her.

She sits in the deep alcove. The deep alcove is where the wood is thickest — three feet of heartwood between her and the outside air, between her and the fen, between her and the sky she no longer thinks about. The glow is faintest here. The fog is densest. The sounds of the gall — the clicking, the dripping, the sub-audible hum of the mites — are most merged, most unified, a single low vibration that she feels through her spine, through her palms, through the soles of her feet. She is the gall's ear. She is the gall's hand on its own wall.

She speaks rarely. When she does, her voice has the quality of the drip — not loud, not projected, but resonant in the way that things which fall from ceilings are resonant. It fills the space not by force but by finding the gaps. The others hear her in their alcoves, through the wood, the way they hear the beetles. She says things like: *The third gallery is active. Move from the eastern shelf.* She says things like: *The fog is thinning early. The Forager should go now.* She says things like: *We are not living in the tree. The tree is living in us.* The practical pronouncements and the philosophical ones sound identical. They are delivered in the same tone, at the same volume, with the same flat conviction.

She will go deeper. She knows this the way she knows the centipedes are out — not by evidence but by the wood's response, by the shift in the vibration, by the way the grain seems to soften around her when the fog is thickest, as if the wood were loosening its hold on its own structure, making room. The first Gall-Keeper went this way. She felt him go. She felt the alcove behind hers empty, and then fill, and then become wood again, fully wood, no longer an alcove but a part of the trunk, and she knew that he had not left but *joined*, and she knows that she will do the same, and she is not afraid, because fear is a thing from outside, and outside is the thing she left, and the gall does not have it.

Her hands are always on the wall. Even in sleep. Especially in sleep. The Gall-Keeper does not fully sleep — she descends into a state where her breathing matches the drip-pattern and her pulse matches the root-seep and her hands lie flat against the grain, and the wood knows she is there, and she knows the wood is there, and the knowing is mutual, and it is enough.

---

**The Forager** is the one who leaves. Of all the residents, she is the only one who regularly passes through the fissure, who regularly breathes unfiltered air, who regularly feels the sky — dim, overcast, canopy-filtered, but sky — on her face. She is the gall's ambassador to the outside. She is also the gall's mouth.

She is not the bravest. This is a misconception the others sometimes have, because she goes out and they do not, and going out seems like it requires courage. It does not. Going out requires *separability* — the ability to be a body that is not the gall, to move through space that is not shaped to you, to stand on ground that does not know your footprint. The Forager has this. She has it because she came to the gall later than the others, or because she came with more of the outside still clinging to her, or because something in her did not fully surrender to the wood. The Gall-Keeper watches her go and come back and go again and feels the way the wood holds the Forager *less tightly* than it holds the others, and this is not a failure. This is why the Forager can leave. The wood lets her go because it cannot hold her the way it holds the ones who stay.

She follows the root-ridges in the fen. Her feet know them the way her hands know the gall's shelves — by touch, by pressure, by the specific cold of root-surfaces versus stone-surfaces versus mud-surfaces. She walks the willow's root-system like a road, the raised lines firm under her soles, the fen's water cold on either side. She moves slowly. Not because she is cautious — because the fen requires it. The fen is a place where speed is dangerous, where a misstep means a foot in a pool deeper than expected, where the roots that surface are slick with algae and the ground between them gives way without warning. She moves the way everything in the fen moves: deliberately, attentively, with the understanding that the ground is not solid but *negotiated*.

She gathers with her hands. Her hands are her tools. She has no basket, no sack, no container — she carries what she finds in her forearms cupped against her chest, pressed to the damp fabric of her clothing (clothing is the wrong word; she wears layers of woven sedge-fiber, the Weaver's work, more like bandages than garments, wrapping the body the way the bark wraps the trunk, for protection, not display). She gathers bracket fungi from the rotting alder stumps at the fen's edge. She gathers moss. She gathers insect larvae from the shallow pools, pinching them from the underside of leaves where they cling, pale and fat and moving. She gathers these things and she holds them against her body and she walks back, and the walk back is always faster than the walk out, because the gall is pulling, not physically — the gall does not pull — but *contextually*, the way home pulls, the way the known pulls when you are in the unknown.

When she returns, she sits in the entrance alcove. She does not go deeper immediately. Her body needs to adjust — the gall's air is thicker, warmer, more saturated than the fen's, and the transition is not instant. She sits and she breathes and she feels the fog enter her lungs and she feels the biofilm's glow on her skin and she feels the mites — always the mites, the constant citizens — begin to explore her hands, her arms, the fungi she has brought, and she lets them, because they were here first, and this is theirs.

She has said, to the children, that the fen is not dangerous. The fen is *indifferent*. This is the harder thing. Danger you can fight. Indifference you can only endure. The fen does not care if she lives or dies. The gall does not care either — the gall is a process, not a person — but the gall has *accommodated* her, and accommodation is the closest thing to care that exists in a wound in a tree.

---

**The Light-Gardener** tends the glow. This is not metaphor. The biofilm is a crop, and he is its farmer.

He is a thin man — thin the way the willow's branches are thin, not from lack of food (though food is scarce) but from a quality of growth that favors length over width. He is long-limbed, long-fingered, and he moves along the walls with a slowness that is almost geological, his hands moving across the grain the way the grain moves across the years — in increments, in accumulations, in layers so thin they are individually invisible but that build, over time, into a pattern.

His work is this: he watches the biofilm. He knows where it grows thick and where it grows thin. He knows the thick patches — they go dark, suffocating in their own density, the organisms at the bottom starved of air and light (the irony of light-starved luminescent organisms is not lost on him, but he does not think in terms of irony; he thinks in terms of *maintenance*). He scrapes the thick patches with his fingernails — the scraping is gentle, almost tender, the way you would scrape moss from a stone you wanted to keep — and he lifts the scrapings, a pale blue-green paste on his fingertips, and he carries them to a bare patch and he spreads them, pressing the film into the grain with his thumb, and he waits.

Sometimes the scraped film takes hold. Sometimes it does not. The variables are — he thinks — moisture, temperature, the specific quality of the grain at the spreading site, the age of the scrapings, the time since the last fog-cycle. He does not have words for all these variables. He has *feelings* for them. His hands know when a patch of wall is right for spreading the way his hands know when a shelf is stable before he puts weight on it. This is not knowledge. This is *acquaintance* — the long, slow, patient familiarity that develops between a hand and a surface over years of contact.

He was not always the Light-Gardener. Before the Gall-Keeper, before the Forager, he came to the gall — not fleeing, not seeking, but *following*. He followed the moths. He had seen them in the fen, the pale, glowing, internally-lit moths that pulse with their own heartbeat, and he had followed them to the willow, and the willow's fissure, and the gall. He entered because the moths entered. He stayed because the moths stayed, and when the moths left, the light remained — on the walls, in the grain, the accumulated light of a thousand generations, and he understood, without being told, that the light needed tending, and he began to tend it.

He believes the Gall-Keeper's theory about the moths. He believes the biofilm is the moths' children — the larvae that went into the wood and dissolved and became the glow. He believes this because he has watched the biofilm closely, daily, for longer than the others, and he has seen patterns in it that are not the patterns of bacteria or mold. He has seen the biofilm *pulse*. Faintly. Rarely. But in the deep hours, in the thickest fog, when the glow dims to its minimum, he has seen patches of biofilm brighten and dim in a rhythm that is not the fog's rhythm, not the temperature's rhythm, but the rhythm of a heartbeat. A moth's heartbeat. A child's heartbeat, dissolved into the wood, still beating.

He has not told the others this. He is not sure it would help. The light does what the light does. He tends it. It grows. It dims. It grows again. This is the work. This is enough.

---

**The Weaver** works in the alcove between the Light-Gardener's wall and the Floor-Tender's junction. She is the only one who makes things.

Not in the way that humans outside make things — with tools, with intention, with a plan. She makes things the way the gall makes things: by *pressure*, by *contact*, by *persistence*. Her work is the sedge-mat — the curtain that covers the entrance fissure. It is the only manufactured object in Wickwright. Everything else is grown.

She weaves sedge-stalks. The stalks come from the Forager, who gathers them from the fen's edge, long and fibrous and damp. The Weaver lays them on the root-mat floor and she works them with her fingers — not knitting, not braiding, but a process that is closer to *felting*, pressing the fibers together until they compact into a dense, flexible mat that can be hung from the protruding knots at the fissure's edge. The mat is not beautiful. It is not meant to be. It is meant to reduce the draft, trap the fog, and allow the moths to enter — the weave is loose enough for a moth to push through, tight enough to slow the air.

She also maintains the mat. The mat decays. The sedge-fibers rot in the gall's humidity, and the mat must be replaced — the Weaver estimates every sixty drips, which is to say every flood-season, which is to say once a cycle. She takes the old mat down and she lays the new one up and in between she weaves, her fingers working the fibers in the blue glow, her hands moving in patterns that she learned from no one and that are, she has noticed, the same patterns the spiders use when they spin their silk lines along the grain. She does not think this is coincidence. She does not think it is not coincidence. She thinks the wood teaches the same patterns to everything that works on it.

The Weaver is the one who is most aware of the boundary between the gall and the outside. The mat is the boundary. The mat is where the gall's air meets the fen's air, where the fog thins and the light changes and the world becomes something else. She maintains this boundary. She is the door, and the door is the only *made* thing, and she is the only *maker*, and this gives her a quality of independence that the others do not have. The Gall-Keeper is the gall's ear. The Forager is the gall's mouth. The Light-Gardener is the gall's eye. The Weaver is the gall's *hand* — the part that acts on the world, that changes what is into what should be, that does not merely receive but *shapes*.

She does not think of it this way. She thinks of it as weaving.

---

**The Floor-Tender** lives at the junction. The junction is where the willow's root-system — the main roots, the thick ones, the ones that carry the most water — converge beneath the gall's floor and form the mesh that is the floor itself. The junction is the warmest place in the gall. Not warm — warm*er*. The water moving through the roots is warmer than the air, warmer than the walls, and at the junction, where the flow is concentrated, the root-mat is almost — almost — body-temperature.

The Floor-Tender's work is the roots. Not tending them — the roots do not need tending. The roots are the willow's, and the willow tends them. The Floor-Tender's work is *listening* to them. She sits at the junction with her hand on the root-mat and she feels the water move. She has learned to read the flow the way the Gall-Keeper reads the walls — not by sight, not by sound, but by a sense that is between touch and hearing, a sensitivity to vibration and pressure and temperature that exists only because the root-mat is alive and she is pressed against it and the water moves through both.

She knows when the fen is rising. The flow increases — not suddenly, not dramatically, but gradually, over days, the roots carrying more water, the floor warming, the fog thickening. She is the one who announces the flood-season. She tells the Gall-Keeper. The Gall-Keeper tells the others. They prepare — moving stores of fungi to the higher alcoves, checking the sedge-mat, reinforcing the entrance. The Floor-Tender's warning is not dramatic. It is a statement: *The flow is up.* This is enough.

She is the youngest of the adults — younger than the Forager, younger than the Light-Gardener, older than the children. She came to the gall because she was looking for warmth. Not metaphorical warmth — literal warmth. She was cold. She had been cold for a long time, in a place where cold was the primary condition, and she found the gall the way you find any warm place in a cold world: by following the temperature. The fissure was warmer than the fen. The alcoves were warmer than the fissure. The junction was warmer than the alcoves. She followed the warmth inward and she stopped when she could not go further, and the place she stopped was the junction, and the junction became hers.

Her skin is warmer than the others'. This is noticeable. In the blue glow, in the fog, among the pale and damp and grain-skinned residents of the gall, the Floor-Tender is the warmest body. The centipedes gravitate toward her. Not aggressively — they do not bite her more than they bite anyone else — but they cluster near her alcove, drawn by the warmth, and she has learned to tolerate them in a way the others have not. She does not tap before reaching into crevices near the junction. She does not need to. The centipedes near her are sluggish, half-dormant, warmed into a torpor that makes them slow and almost — she would say this if asked — companionable. She moves among them. They move among her. The distinction is, as always, a matter of opinion.

---

**The two children** were born in the gall. They have never been outside.

This is not precisely true. They have been to the entrance. They have stood at the fissure, behind the sedge-mat, and looked out — the gray light, the hanging branches, the fen's surface, the dim canopy above. They have smelled the outside air — colder, drier, carrying the green and iron smells of the fen but also carrying *distance*, the smell of space, of open air, of a world that extends beyond the reach of your hand. They have stood at the fissure and they have felt all of this and they have turned back, back into the fog, back into the blue glow, back into the drip and the click and the silence, because the gall is where their bodies know how to be and the outside is where their bodies do not.

They are not named in the way the adults are named — by function, by alcove, by role. They are called by sounds. Not words — sounds. The sounds are the sounds the gall makes: one is called after the high drip, the one that sounds like a finger tapping glass, and the other is called after the low drip, the one that sounds like a stone dropping into a well. These are not names. These are *addresses*. The gall knows where the high drip falls and where the low drip falls, and the children are known by these locations, and the locations are known by the sounds, and the sounds are known by the wood, and the wood knows everything.

They are small. They are the size of the alcoves they sleep in, which means the alcoves are small, which means the wood has not yet fully grown around them — they are still in the first alcoves, the ones closest to the entrance, the ones the adults have outgrown or moved beyond. The alcoves fit them the way a seed fits its pod: closely, completely, with just enough space for the fog to move between body and wood.

They play. Their play is the play of the gall: they climb the partitions, gripping the ridges of the grain with hands and feet, pulling themselves up the inward-growing walls the way the willow's branches pull themselves up into the outside air. They crawl into the beetle-galleries — the abandoned ones, the ones the beetles have finished and vacated, the ones just wide enough for a child's body — and they lie in them and they listen to the wood and the wood is close, so close, the grain on every side, and they say nothing, and the wood says nothing, and the nothing is the same nothing.

They catch the moths. This is the one thing the adults do not do — the adults watch the moths, the adults revere the moths, the adults stop their work when the moths come and sit in the blue glow and watch the warm golden light move through the gall and feel something that is the closest thing to joy that exists in Wickwright. The children do not watch. The children *catch*. They cup the moths in their small hands and they feel the light — the warmth of it, the pulse of it, the small body beating its wings against their palms — and they hold the light, and the light holds them, and then they open their hands and the moths go, and the moths are not harmed, and the children are not harmed, and the transaction is complete.

The Gall-Keeper watches this. She watches the children catch the moths and she feels something that she does not have a word for — not concern, not approval, but *recognition*. She recognizes what the children are doing. They are doing what she did, what the first Gall-Keeper did, what everyone who comes to the gall does eventually: they are making contact. They are touching the thing that lives here. The moths are the gall's emissaries — the part of the gall that moves, that glows, that can be held. The children hold them. The children hold the gall in their hands.

She says: *They are already wood. They just don't know it yet.*

She is not wrong. She is not right. The distinction, like all distinctions in the gall, is eroding.

## What Else Lives There

The beetles are the architects. They were here first — before the gall was a gall, before the wound had healed into a space, the beetles came to the wound and began to carve. Their mandibles are the gall's chisels. Their bodies — dark, armored, the size of a fingernail — are the gall's tools. They work the wood the way the wood works the water: by passing through it, by changing it as they go, by leaving behind them a channel that was not there before.

They are everywhere. In the walls, in the floor, in the ceiling. The clicking of their mandibles is the gall's heartbeat — not the slow seep of the roots but the rapid, constant, percussive beat of work being done. They carve galleries. They lay eggs. The larvae hatch and eat the wood and grow and pupate and emerge and carve more galleries. The cycle is the cycle. The humans live in the cycle's wake — in the finished galleries, the abandoned ones, the spaces the beetles have left behind. The humans are the beetles' heirs. The beetles do not know this. The beetles do not care.

The mites are the medium. They are the connective tissue, the *between* — between the wood and the biofilm, between the biofilm and the beetles, between the beetles and the spiders, between all of these and the humans. They are everywhere, on every surface, in every crevice, so dense that the walls seem to move, and the walls *are* moving, and only when you press your face close — and the humans have done this, the children especially — do you see them: specks, dust-motes, each one alive, each one eating or being eaten, each one producing waste that feeds the biofilm, which feeds the mites, which feed the beetles, which shape the wood, which shelters the humans, who tend the biofilm.

The humans do not exist outside this cycle. The humans are a node in it. The humans tend the biofilm, and the biofilm lights the gall, and the light allows the humans to find the fungi and larvae the Forager brings, and the waste from the humans' eating feeds the mites, and the mites feed the biofilm. The cycle does not require the humans. The humans require the cycle. The Gall-Keeper knows this. The others are learning.

The centipedes are the hunters. They are four inches, five, segmented, many-legged, fast, silent, venomous (not fatally, but *enough*). They live in the deep galleries — the ones the beetles have abandoned, the ones the humans cannot reach. They emerge in the thickening, the fog-dark hours, and they range across the walls in patterns that are not random but that the humans have not been able to predict. They hunt the mites. They hunt the beetles. They hunt anything that moves on the surface, including, occasionally, a finger, an ear, a child's hand. The marks they leave — paired punctures, small, stinging — are the gall's reminder that the space is not benign. The space is not malignant either. The space is *a place where things eat other things*, and the humans are things.

The spiders are the surveyors. They spin silk lines along the grain, connecting shelf to partition, alcove to ceiling, building a network that maps the gall's interior in a way that no eye could. The lines are invisible until the fog condenses on them, and then — briefly — the gall is strung with silver, a geometry of thread and droplet that shows the space's true dimensions: not a room, but a *web*, a network of connections and distances and relationships that the spiders know and the humans can only admire.

The spiders are patient. They rebuild constantly. The fog destroys the lines — the condensation weighs them down, the drip snaps them — and the spiders respin, respin, respin. This is not futility. This is *practice*. The spiders know the gall is not fixed. The spiders know the wood grows. The spiders know that every cycle, the geometry changes, and the lines must be laid anew, and the map must be redrawn. The spiders are the gall's cartographers, and the gall is a country that redraws itself daily.

The moths are the light. The moths are the joy. The moths are the only warm thing in the gall — not in temperature (they are small, their bodies are cold, their light is cold light) but in *quality*. The biofilm's glow is blue, steady, ambient, the light of a space that exists for itself. The moths' glow is golden, pulsing, moving, the light of a thing that *chooses* to be here, that comes in through the fissure and spreads through the gall and brightens the walls and the faces and the wood and the fog and makes everything, for the hours they are present, *visible* — not the dim, shape-level visibility of the biofilm but real visibility, the kind that lets you see the color of the wood (brown, the brown of heartwood, the brown of living grain) and the color of the skin (pale, the blue-pale of biofilm-light-adapted humans) and the color of the fog (white, the white of water suspended in air) and the color of the moths themselves (gold, always gold, the gold of something that carries its own light).

The moths come. The moths go. The moths leave their children in the wood. The children dissolve. The light remains.

## What Lives Deepest

The first Gall-Keeper is in the wood. This is not a metaphor. He is in the wood the way resin is in the wood — absorbed, incorporated, a part of the grain. The Gall-Keeper has felt him. Not as a presence, not as a ghost, not as a person. As a *density*. A thickening in the heartwood, deep in the gall's wall, where the grain is tightest and the wood is oldest. A place where the wood is slightly different — slightly warmer, slightly more resonant, slightly more *responsive* to the tap of a knuckle — and the Gall-Keeper knows this is where he went, and where he is, and where he will be when the wood is cut open (which it never will be) and examined (which no one will do) and found to contain, in its deepest layer, something that is not quite wood and not quite not-wood and that has the vague, faded, accommodated shape of a human body.

He is not dead. He is not alive. He is *incorporated*. He is a part of the gall the way the gall is a part of the willow the way the willow is a part of the fen. He is the gall's deepest resident. He is the thing the Gall-Keeper listens for when she puts her hand on the wall. She does not hear him. She hears the wood. But the wood, in that place, is different, and the difference is him, and so she hears him, in the only way he can be heard: as a quality of the wood itself.

The moths' larvae are in the wood. Millions of them, over decades, burrowing into the heartwood and dissolving, their bodies becoming the biofilm, their light becoming the glow. The gall is a graveyard for moths. The gall is a nursery for their light. The larvae are not dead. They are *distributed*. They are everywhere on the walls, in the film, in the glow, in the blue light that lets the humans see shapes and navigate the fog. The humans breathe the air that the biofilm freshens. The humans breathe the moths' children. The moths are in the humans' lungs. This is not a horror. This is the gall.

The willow is alive. The willow does not know the gall exists, or if it does, it knows the way a body knows a wound — not as a feature but as a *condition*, a place where the surface has been broken and the interior exposed and the healing has gone wrong, or right, or sideways — has become not a scar but a *space*, a cavity, a place where the outside has come inside and set up house and refused to leave. The willow grows around the gall the way a tree grows around a nail — slowly, incrementally, incorporating the foreign object into its structure, making it part of the trunk, part of the grain, part of the wood.

The willow drinks from the fen. The fen feeds the willow. The willow feeds the gall. The gall feeds the biofilm. The biofilm feeds the mites. The mites feed the beetles. The beetles shape the wood. The wood shelters the humans. The humans tend the biofilm. The cycle is closed. The cycle is complete.

The Gall-Keeper says: *We are not living in the tree. The tree is living in us.*

The willow does not say anything. The willow drinks. The willow grows. The willow does not know it has a town inside its wound. The willow does not need to know. The town is there. The willow is here. The distinction, as always, is eroding.

---

## What Will Live There

The children will grow. The alcoves will grow around them. The wood will accommodate them the way it has accommodated the Gall-Keeper — slowly, incrementally, the grain finding their bodies, the alcoves becoming their shapes, the boundary between skin and bark narrowing year by year, drip by drip, until the children are the Gall-Keeper's age and the wood is their skin and the fog is their air and the moths are their light and the outside is a story they tell each other in the pidgin of the gall, a story about a place where the air is dry and the light is far away and the ground does not know your name.

One of them will go deeper. The Gall-Keeper knows this. She does not know which one. She knows only that the gall will call, the way it called her, the way it called the first Gall-Keeper, the way it calls everything that lives in it long enough: *come in. Come deeper. Come into the wood. Become the wall. Become the grain. Become the thing that holds the others.*

The other may go out. The Forager hopes. The Forager watches the children at the fissure, looking out, and she sees in one of them a quality she recognizes — a restlessness, a *separability*, a refusal to fully settle into the wood that she knows (because she has it) is the thing that lets you leave. The Forager does not encourage this. The Forager does not discourage this. The Forager knows that the gall decides, and the gall does not decide, and both of these are true, and the resolution is in the wood, which is growing, which is always growing, which will grow around whatever comes, and whatever comes will become the gall, and the gall will become it, and the cycle will close, and the cycle will open, and the fog will thicken, and the fog will thin, and the moths will come, and the moths will go, and the light will remain.
