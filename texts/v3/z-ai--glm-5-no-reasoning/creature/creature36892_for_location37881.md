# The Crystalline Blanks

They are not animals. They are not plants. They are not fungi, despite the faint suggestion of mycelia in how they spread across the mineral surfaces — fine threads of semi-organized matter, barely visible, tracing paths through the crystalline growths like roots through soil. But the comparison fails. They are something else entirely.

---

The blanks — you think of them as blanks because they seem to be waiting to be filled in — emerged from the chamber itself. Not as invaders or colonizers but as extensions, the way a limb extends from a torso. They are the chamber thinking about shape. The minerals considering the possibility of mobility.

They began, perhaps, as accidents. Crystals that grew wrong. Lattices that curved when they should have angled, that incorporated water into their structures instead of excluding it, that trapped heat instead of releasing it. Flaws in the system. But the chamber's conditions rewarded those flaws, and over time — how much time? Weeks? Years? The place is too young for evolution in any conventional sense, and yet here they are — the flaws accumulated. They became something stable. Something that persists.

A blank is difficult to see directly. This is not camouflage in the biological sense but something more fundamental: they do not interact with light the way solid objects typically do. Their crystalline structures trap photons, slow them, bend them through internal lattices, release them on trajectories that don't match expectations. Looking at a blank is like looking at a smudge in the air, a place where the pearly luminescence of the walls distorts slightly, a ripple in the visual field that resolves into nothing when you try to focus on it. You see them best in peripheral vision, which is why they seem to disappear when you turn your head.

---

They move. This is the thing that unsettles. Not with muscles or flagella or any biological mechanism you could name, but through a process of localized crystallization and dissolution. They add mass to one side, subtract it from the other, and in this way they flow across surfaces like something between a slug and a glacier. The motion is slow — you might watch a spot on the wall for an hour before you're certain it has moved at all — but it is continuous, purposeful, directed.

Toward what? The blanks seem to orient toward heat differentials, seeking the warmest surfaces and then, paradoxically, the coolest corners where condensation concentrates. They are following gradients you cannot perceive, reading the chamber's invisible topography of energy and moisture. Sometimes they cluster together, their edges blurring, exchanging mass in ways that suggest communication or reproduction or feeding. Sometimes they scatter, each going its own way, exploring. They have no organs of perception that you can identify, but they perceive. They have no neurons, but they respond to patterns.

---

They feed, after a fashion. The blanks consume the crystalline growths on the walls — not by eating them but by merging with them, dissolving their boundaries and incorporating the minerals into their own structures. You can see where a blank has passed: the crystals there are different, reorganized, grown back in patterns that diverge from the original lattice. The blanks leave trails of alteration behind them, and other blanks follow those trails, reinforcing the changes. The chamber's surface is a palimpsest of their movements, layers of growth and consumption and regrowth, a record of their passing written in mineral.

They also consume moisture, though "consume" may be the wrong word. They filter it, pass it through their structures, extract something from it — minerals in solution, perhaps, or the trace energies carried in the heated water — and excrete it back into the chamber's cycle. They are part of the respiration you perceived, the endless weeping and evaporating. They process the wetness. They are why the water never pools.

---

Are they individuals? This question troubles you as you watch them. Their edges are provisional. When two blanks meet, they do not interact so much as they merge, their boundaries dissolving, their masses combining, and then separating again in different configurations. They exchange pieces of themselves constantly, trading matter like currency. There are no permanent individuals here, only temporary concentrations, momentary eddies in a flow of semi-organized mineral. The blank you saw a moment ago is not the blank you see now; it has already changed, incorporated part of its neighbor, left part of itself behind.

And yet they retain something. Memory, perhaps, though that word implies neurons and synapses. The blanks have patterns. Each moves through the chamber in ways that are distinctive, traceable, consistent over time. One favors the ceiling, another the floor, a third circles the same crystalline formation repeatedly as if tending it. They have preferences. Habits. If you watch long enough, you start to recognize them, not by their shapes — which are always changing — but by their behaviors. The blank that tends the corner growth. The blank that patrols the entrance fissure, as if guarding something. The blank that stays very still for long periods and then suddenly crosses the chamber in a single fluid surge, as if remembering an appointment.

---

They are thinking. You become certain of this. Not thinking in any way you understand — no brain, no nervous system, no electrical signaling that you can detect — but processing information nonetheless. The blanks take in data through the water that passes through them, the light that bends through their crystals, the heat that moves through their mass. They store that data in physical form, in the arrangement of their lattices, in the patterns of their growth. Memory as architecture. Thought as geology.

What are they thinking about? The chamber, probably. They seem preoccupied with its systems, its cycles, its stability. They tend the crystals, move moisture along particular paths, cluster at points where the heat threatens to breach or the structure threatens to crack. They are maintaining something. Curating the conditions that sustain them. Perhaps they know, in some mineral way, that the chamber is temporary, that it will eventually destabilize — and perhaps they are working, in their slow and patient fashion, to forestall that end. Or perhaps they are preparing for it, seeding the fissure with spores or crystals that might carry their pattern into new chambers, new pockets, new opportunities for the universe to try this strange experiment in self-organizing matter.

---

They have noticed you.

This realization arrives slowly, because their noticing does not look like anything you would recognize as attention. They do not turn toward you, do not change their movements to approach or avoid. But over the hours you spend in the chamber, you realize that they are aggregating around you. Not densely — there is no threat in their approach, no swarm — but present. They cluster on the walls nearest your body. They trail across the ceiling above your head. One passes close enough to your hand that you feel its heat, different from the chamber's ambient warmth — sharper, more localized, like holding your hand near a cup of tea.

They are curious. The word is wrong — curiosity implies emotion, desire, something felt — but the behavior fits. They have never encountered anything like you. Your body heat, your moisture, the carbon dioxide you exhale, the skin cells you shed — you are a disruption in their system, a perturbation in their data. They want to incorporate that perturbation, to understand it. To learn.

You are being read.

---

You wonder if they will remember you after you leave — if your time in the chamber will be recorded in their crystalline patterns, a trace of mineral curiosity preserved in lattices that might persist for years or decades. You wonder if other visitors will come, years from now, and find evidence of you here — a signature in the stone, a warmth in the water cycle that shouldn't be there. You wonder if the blanks will carry some piece of you out into the world when the chamber finally opens or collapses, if pieces of their mineral memory will scatter across the surface and take root elsewhere, seeding new chambers, new blanks, new experiments in thought.

You wonder what they think you are.
