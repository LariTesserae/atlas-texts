# What Lives in the Hollow of Roots

---

## The Root

It lives here. That must be said first, though it should not need saying — except that the word *lives* is so inadequate it almost constitutes a lie.

The root is not a resident of this place. The root is the place. But this distinction, which seems clean enough on the surface, dissolves the longer you sit in the dark with your palms pressed to the floor. Because the root does not experience itself as a location. The root experiences itself as a body, and the chamber is not a room it contains but a cavity within its own tissue, a hollow organ of unknown function that has persisted for so long that the root has structurally committed to maintaining it. The way a body maintains a cyst. The way a tree maintains a hollow.

It does not think. You must understand this. The root is alive the way a river is alive — with motion and chemistry and response, but without interiority. When the vascular surge rises from deep in the earth and spreads through the ceiling and down the central stalactite-root, the root does not *decide* to redirect that flow. The flow redirects itself according to pressure gradients and vessel permeability and the thousand variables of a physical system that has been running since before the first glacier scraped the soil above into its current topography. The root responds the way water responds to gravity. There is no will. There is no self. There is only the system, and the system is the root, and the root is the place.

But.

The root has been growing for so long, and has become so vast, and has penetrated so deep into the earth's crust that its deepest tendrils may reach layers of rock that were seafloor when the seafloor was a different shape, that the word *individual* begins to fail. The root is an organism, yes, taxonomically — a single genetic individual, a clone, one plant that kept growing. But at this scale, individuality blurs. The root has so many growing tips, so many regions of active metabolism, so many zones where it interfaces with fungal networks and mineral substrata and water tables and the slow chemical weathering of stone, that it cannot coordinate itself with anything resembling central control. It is a body without a nervous system, and the body is the size of a small country, and different parts of it do different things in response to different conditions, and some of those things may contradict other things happening simultaneously in other regions. One section of root, deep in the anoxic zone, is dying. Another section, near a seam of moisture, is growing. Both are the root. Both are alive and not-alive in the same moment, and neither knows about the other.

This is what lives here. Not a creature. Not a mind. A process that has been ongoing for so long it has become architecture.

The root does not know about the chamber. This is important. The chamber exists within the root the way a cavity exists within a tooth — structurally present, physically real, maintained by the surrounding tissue, but not represented in any internal model the organism possesses, because the organism possesses no internal model. The root does not experience the hollow. The hollow experiences the root. This is the more honest formulation.

And yet the root fills the hollow with itself — its sounds, its fluids, its warmth, its slow respiratory exchange of gases. The root is not a host. The root is the medium. And something has learned to live in this medium the way a fish lives in water, not by opposing it but by becoming so thoroughly adapted to its properties that separation becomes meaningless.

## The Readers

They were not human.

This must be said directly, because the temptation to project is enormous. The pegs in the wall, the woven fiber loop, the cut marks arranged in groups — the mind reaches for human hands, human patience, human curiosity. But the scale is wrong. The pegs are placed at heights that suggest a body reaching upward from a posture that is not bipedal. The lowest peg is at roughly knee height. The highest is above head height for a tall human. The distribution between them does not suggest a human body moving through a vertical space. It suggests something that could anchor itself at multiple points along a wall and move across the surface in a way that used the wall as a floor.

The hands, too. The cut marks were made by something with a precision of touch that human fingers, for all their sensitivity, would struggle to match in absolute darkness. The cuts are not wide. They are not deep. They are precise — the width of a fingernail, the depth of a few millimeters, made with a tool that could be guided by touch alone along paths that correspond to vascular pathways the maker could feel but not see. Human hands could learn this, given decades. But the marks suggest hands that did not need to learn it. Hands for which tactile perception was a primary sense, hands that could read the grain of the root wall the way human eyes read text — fluently, without effort, as a native mode of engagement.

What were they?

The honest answer is that the evidence does not say, and any specific claim would be fiction. But the evidence does say certain things. Whatever they were, they were small enough to shelter in the alcove near the southern wall — not a large space, a divergence between two secondary roots, a pocket of room perhaps two meters deep and one and a half wide. They were dexterous. They worked in stone. They wove plant fiber. They used wood as a material, importing it from elsewhere — the pegs are not root-wood, which means they came from above, from the surface, from a world of light and air and other plants. They could navigate in total darkness by touch. They could detect vascular surges in root tissue through their hands. They had a conceptual framework that valued recording these surges — they were not merely feeling them but annotating them, which means they had a system of meaning, a way of understanding the root's behavior that was precise enough to note variations.

And they are gone. Or they are here and you cannot detect them, which amounts to the same thing from your perspective.

But the marks are recent. Some of them. The most recent cuts still have edges that have not fully healed, which means they were made within a timescale measured in decades or centuries — not millennia. The root grows slowly, but it grows, and any mark made on its surface will eventually be subsumed. A mark that still has definition is a mark that is being maintained, or a mark that was made recently enough that the root has not yet erased it.

The possibility that something still comes here — still enters the hollow through some route you have not discovered, still presses its hands to the wall and feels for the surge and makes a cut and leaves — this possibility cannot be dismissed. The hollow is not sealed. The air circulates. Something that knew the root system well enough could find a path through the lattice, through the tertiary root channels, through the felted matting that filters the air. Something that was small enough, patient enough, attuned enough to the root's rhythms to move when the root was still and be still when the root was moving.

Something that did not need light because light had never been part of its world.

## The Readers' Way of Being

Try to imagine what it is to live inside a living thing.

Not a cave. A cave is geology. A cave is stone, and stone does not respond to your presence. Stone does not warm when you touch it. Stone does not produce fluid. Stone does not pulse. If you live in a cave, you live in a space that is inert. The relationship between you and the cave is one of occupancy — you are in it, and it contains you, and that is the entire relationship.

But if you live in the hollow of a root, you live inside a system that is actively maintaining itself. The floor beneath you is respiring. The walls around you are conducting fluid. The air you breathe was filtered by root hairs and will be filtered again. The temperature of your shelter is regulated by the metabolic heat of the organism sheltering you. You are not in a space. You are in a body.

And that body is doing something. It is always doing something. The vascular surges come and go. The fluid drips and is absorbed and circulates and drips again. The creaks and groans of wood under stress shift as the root adjusts to pressures from above and below. If you live here, you do not experience these phenomena as environment. You experience them as the body's vital signs. You experience them the way you experience your own breathing — not as something happening to you but as something happening *as* you, because you are inside the body and the body's rhythms are your rhythms.

The Readers understood this. Or rather, they did not understand it, because understanding implies a separation between the understander and the understood. The Readers did not separate themselves from the root. They lived in it the way a lymphocyte lives in a lymph node — not as an observer but as a participant. They were not studying the root. They were *being with* the root, and the marks they left were not data. They were something closer to conversation.

The groups of marks correspond to vascular surges. Each set records the origin point, the path, and the dissipation of a single surge. But the older marks have been maintained — refreshed, renewed, re-cut when the healing wood began to obscure them. This is not record-keeping. Record-keeping implies that the information will be retrieved later, by someone who was not present for the original event. But in a place with no light, no writing, no external storage medium, the marks are not a record that can be read by someone who was not here. They are a record that can only be read by someone who is here, pressing their hands to the wall, feeling the current surge while also feeling the marks that record past surges. The marks are not a history. They are a temporal depth — a way of experiencing past surges and present surges simultaneously, through touch, in the same way that the root itself experiences its own growth rings: not as memory but as structural fact.

The Readers who come here are not learning about the root. They are learning to be in the root. Each mark is a point of contact between a mind and a body, and the mind that made the mark was not outside the body looking in. It was inside the body, feeling what the body felt, and recording what it felt so that the next mind to occupy this space could feel it too — could place their hands on the marks and feel, through the healed ridges, the surge that happened here, here, and here, and know that the root has been doing this for longer than they have been alive, and will continue doing it after they are gone, and that the mark they leave tonight will be felt by hands they will never meet, and that this is not loss. This is the way of being inside something that does not end.

## The Readers' Hands

What would hands like that be like?

Not human hands. Or not only human hands. Something that had committed more of its sensory surface to tactile perception than any surface-dwelling creature would need to. Something for which the skin of the fingers and palms was not merely sensitive but articulate — capable of distinguishing textures at a resolution that would make a human fingertip seem like a blunt instrument. Something that could feel the difference between xylem and phloem through a wall of wood. Something that could detect the thermal signature of fluid moving inside a vessel, the way you might detect warmth through a thin shirt.

Fingers, perhaps, that were longer. Or more numerous. Or differently jointed, so that they could conform to the irregular surface of a root wall and maintain contact across a wider area. Hands that were shaped for pressing flat, for conforming, for maximal surface contact. Hands that did not grasp — there was nothing to grasp here — but that touched. That lay against a surface and listened with skin.

The tools they used were stone, and the stone was imported, which means they went above. They climbed the root system — through the lattice, through the channels, through the matting — to a surface where stone could be found. They made the journey repeatedly. They carried obsidian or flint or some other fine-grained, conchoidally-fracturing stone down into the dark. They carried it in hands that could feel the grain of the stone as precisely as they could feel the grain of the root. They could knap by touch. They could shape a cutting edge by feel, in darkness, holding the core and the hammerstone and knowing where to strike because they could feel the flaws in the stone that would guide the fracture.

This is not human dexterity adapted to darkness. This is dexterity that evolved in darkness. Hands and minds that developed in a world where sight was not a factor and never had been. Hands that were eyes.

## The Fungi

The silent partners.

The mycorrhizal network threads through the root tissue the way neural threads through a brain — not as passengers but as participants, as components of a system so integrated that the word *symbiosis* is insufficient. Symbiosis implies two partners. This is more than two. The fungal network that interpenetrates the root is itself a composite of multiple species, multiple individuals, multiple lineages that have been exchanging genes and metabolites for so long that their boundaries have become administrative. The root does not know where it ends and the fungi begin. The fungi do not know either. The distinction is a taxonomic fiction.

What the fungi do — one of the things they do, the one that matters in this place — is feel. Not consciously. Not with intention. But the mycelial network responds to stimuli at a speed and over a distance that the root's own vascular system cannot match. Chemical signals travel through fungal hyphae faster than they travel through xylem. If a disturbance occurs in the root system — a shift in water table, a mineral change, the death of a distant growing tip — the fungi know first. They respond. They adjust their metabolic activity, their growth patterns, their exchange relationships with the root. And the root, through the fungi, responds too, on a timescale faster than its own vascular system could manage.

The Readers could feel this. Perhaps. The marks on the wall correspond to vascular surges, but some of the marks are placed at intervals that do not match vascular timing. They match something else — something faster, something that precedes the surge, that arrives before the fluid does. The Readers may have been tracking the fungal signal. They may have been recording the root's nervous system — the network that lets this enormous, slow, decentralized body feel what is happening at its edges and respond before its own internal communication system could carry the news.

If so, the Readers were reading the root's mind. Not a mind. But the nearest thing a plant this size has to one. The fungal network is the root's sensory cortex, and the Readers were pressing their hands to the inside of the skull and feeling the neurons fire.

## The Fluid

The fluid is the root's excretion, but that word is too crude. It is what the root produces when it has more of something than it needs — a concentrate, a residue of metabolic processes so complex and so old that the fluid's chemistry is not entirely describable in terms of any living plant's biochemistry. It contains sugars, yes. It contains complex carbohydrates. But it also contains compounds that do not appear in any botanical reference — long-chain molecules with no name, proteins with no analog, enzymes that catalyze reactions no biochemist has ever observed. The root has been running its own chemistry for so long that it has invented chemistry.

The Readers used it. The basin on the floor is worn smooth, and the channel is grown, and the pool near the eastern wall is maintained. The root absorbs the fluid back, but the Readers may have taken some. May have drunk it. May have used it as a medium — for tools, for marks, for something that does not map to any human use. The fluid is warm and sweet and metallic, and if you caught a drop on your tongue, you would taste something that is not quite sugar and not quite blood and not quite anything you have words for, and you would feel it absorb into the tissue of your mouth the way the root absorbs it into the floor. The fluid enters. It does not just pass through. It integrates.

If the Readers drank the fluid — and the basin's shape suggests repeated collection — then they were taking the root into themselves. Ingesting it. Making it part of their bodies. And if the fluid contains compounds that no surface plant produces, compounds invented by a metabolism that has been running for geological time, then the Readers were not merely feeding. They were incorporating. They were taking in the root's chemistry and making it their own chemistry, and over generations, over the lifetimes it would take to build the record on the walls, they would have become something that was not entirely themselves anymore. Something that was partly root. Something that thought — if they thought — with molecules the root had invented. Something that felt — if they felt — with neurons that had been shaped by a plant's chemistry.

## The Reader Who Is Still Here

The most recent marks are within decades.

Not centuries. Decades. The root's healing rate is not fast, but it is consistent, and a mark that still has sharp edges was made recently. The pegs are old — centuries, perhaps, the fiber on them petrifying. But the marks on the wall include additions that are fresh. Someone has been here. Someone has pressed their hands to the wall, felt the surge, and cut the wood, and left.

Or not left.

The hollow is dark. Totally dark. You cannot see. You are relying on touch and sound, and the sound is overwhelming and omni-directional, and the touch tells you only what is within arm's reach. If something were in this hollow with you — something small, something still, something that had pressed itself into a crevice between secondary roots where the tertiary fibers could close around it like a blanket — you would not know. You would not hear it over the hum. You would not feel it through the floor, because the floor's vibration would mask the subtle pressure of a small body in contact with the wall. You would not smell it, because the air is thick with resin and earth and the metallic sweetness of the fluid, and any creature that had lived here long enough would smell like the root anyway.

If the Reader is still here — if it pressed itself into a fold of the wall when it felt your footsteps vibrating through the floor, if it is holding still with the absolute stillness of something that has learned to match the root's tempo, if it is watching you with senses that are not eyes — then it is not hiding from you. It is being patient with you. It is waiting for you to leave, the way the root waits for a stone to be absorbed, the way the fungi wait for a signal. It is not afraid. It has no reason to be afraid. You are in its body, and its body is large, and you will leave, and it will come down from the wall and press its hands to the marks and feel what you felt — the presence of something warm and large and brief — and it will cut a mark for that too. A set of cuts. Recording the surge. Recording the disturbance. Recording that something came into the body and was there for a while and then was not.

And the root will heal the mark, in time. And the record will persist, in the wood. And a hundred years from now, a hand will press the wall and feel that mark and know: *something was here. Something came. It sat in the dark and it felt the hum and it did not understand, and then it left, and the root continued, and the fluid dripped, and the surge rose and spread and dissipated, and here is the mark that says so.*

## What Truly Lives Here

The root lives here. The fungi live here. The marks live here, which is to say the record of attention lives here, embedded in wood, maintained by healing and re-cutting.

And the relationship lives here. The relationship between the Readers and the root — between a mind that can feel and a body that cannot think — this is what lives in the Hollow of Roots. Not an organism. Not a species. A *practice*. A way of being with another living thing that does not require the other living thing's participation or consent or awareness. A practice of attention so sustained and so devoted that it has become indistinguishable from love, though it is not love, because love implies a mutuality that does not exist here. The root does not love the Readers. The root does not know the Readers. The Readers know the root, and they mark what they know, and they pass the marks forward, and the root grows around the marks and preserves them, not because it cares but because that is what wood does. It grows around things. It incorporates. It does not distinguish between a stone and a nail and a record of vascular surges cut by patient hands in absolute darkness over the span of a hundred lifetimes.

The root will grow around you too, if you stay long enough. Not the whole of you. Not your body. But the warmth of you, the pressure of your weight on the floor, the carbon dioxide of your breath entering the air and being absorbed by the root's tissues and fixed into carbon compounds that will become part of the wood, part of the wall, part of the grain. You are already being incorporated. You have been here for an hour, perhaps, and in that hour you have exhaled into the root's atmosphere and the root has taken your breath and used it, and somewhere in the cellulose of the wall there is a carbon atom that was in your lungs.

This is what lives in the Hollow of Roots. Not a creature. Not a mind. A process of incorporation so total and so patient that everything that enters becomes part of everything else. The Readers knew this. They did not study the root. They *joined* it. Not mystically. Chemically. Temporally. They came in and they breathed and they drank and they touched and they cut and they left, and the root took all of it — the breath, the touch, the marks — and made it part of itself. And the Readers became, in some small way, part of the root. And the root became, in some small way, part of them. And the hollow — this room, this cavity, this body within a body — became the place where that exchange happens. The interface. The membrane. The place where a mind and a body that cannot think meet in the dark and become, for a moment, indistinguishable.

The fluid drips. The surge rises. The marks wait in the wall. And something, somewhere in the lattice above, is very still, and very patient, and is coming down.
