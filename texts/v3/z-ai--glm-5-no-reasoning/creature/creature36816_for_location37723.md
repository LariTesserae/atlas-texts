# What Lives on the Weeping Mesa

---

The first thing to understand is that nothing here chose to be here.

Or rather—everything here chose to be here, but the choosing happened so long ago, and under such different conditions, that the word *choose* no longer applies. What lives on the Weeping Mesa was shaped by the mesa, became what it is because the mesa demanded it, and now the living and the place have grown so tangled that separating them would kill both. The moss is the mesa's skin. The pools are its eyes. The things that move through the fern-forests are its dreams, or perhaps its immune system, or perhaps simply the parts of itself that it needed to be mobile.

Start with the small. Start with what you'd notice first, if you arrived here, if you walked up through the thinning air and into the impossible wet.

---

## The Filterers

The air is alive. You know this because your lungs tell you—each breath draws in not just oxygen but particulate, spore, pollen, the microscopic sheddings of everything growing here. The plateau produces biomass the way a furnace produces heat: constantly, involuntarily, as a byproduct of simply existing. And into this thick biological soup come the filterers.

They are not insects, though they occupy the ecological niche insects would fill. Six legs, yes—but the body plan diverges from there. Flat, almost leaf-flat, their bodies translucent and veined, iridescent in the diffuse light when they turn. They drift. Not fly—drift. They have no wings in the traditional sense but expanded structures like the sails of jellyfish, gossamer, wet-resistant, catching air currents so subtle you cannot feel them. They ride the mesa's breath.

And they filter. As they drift, their sail-structures collect particulate from the air—spores, pollen, the microdetritus of living and dying. Specialized surfaces, sticky with a secretion of their own, trap this material, and they feed on it constantly, passively, the way a whale feeds on krill. They are the mesa's air-quality system. They are the reason the mist here is mist and not fog, the reason visibility extends to fifty meters instead of five. They clean what the mesa exhales.

When they drift into a cloud—a real cloud, one of the knee-height ones that form and dissolve—they slow. The moisture weighs on their sails. They descend, settling onto fern-fronds, onto moss-surfaces, and they rest there, wings folded, drinking the condensation directly, until the cloud passes and the air dries enough for them to rise again.

At night, the bioluminescence you see on certain surfaces? That's them. Not all of them—only the ones that have fed well, that have stored enough energy to afford the luxury of light. The glow is faint, blue-white, and it serves no purpose anyone has determined. It is not communication. It is not warning. It may be nothing more than excess, a body shedding what it cannot hold, a biological sigh.

They have no predators. Nothing eats them. They are too thin, too airy, more surface than substance. They are the closest thing the mesa has to pure function—living process, barely animal at all.

## The Moss-Dwellers

Beneath the filterers, beneath your feet, in the deep moss that gives and rebounds—in that moss, life has gone inward.

The moss is not one thing. It is layers. The top layer, the green you see, is photosynthetic, actively growing, the mesa's outer skin. Below that, older moss, still alive but slower, darker, denser. Below that, moss that has died and been compressed into something approaching peat, still wet, still holding structure. And below that, the deepest layer: a root-tangle of fern-rhizomes and decomposing organic matter that forms a lattice, a three-dimensional mesh, a architecture.

In this lattice, the warm-blooded things live.

They are small. They must be small—the cold demands it, the surface-area-to-volume ratio demands it, the tunnel-size demands it. The largest is the length of your forearm; the smallest could sit on your thumbnail. All of them are dense with fur—fur so fine, so tightly packed, that it appears almost as scales from a distance, a second skin of keratin and oil that water cannot penetrate. They are waterproof in a place where everything is wet. This is their primary adaptation, their founding choice: they refused the water. Everything else on the mesa has surrendered to it, has incorporated it, has become it. The moss-dwellers resist.

They live in tunnels they did not make. The fern-root lattice provides structure; the moss-dwellers simply occupy it, clearing spaces, lining them with shredded fern-frond and their own shed fur. Each individual may maintain a network of a dozen such spaces—sleeping nests, food caches, latrine chambers, observation posts near the surface. They are meticulous. Their tunnels are clean, dry, maintained. In a place where moisture is the defining condition, they have created dryness, and they guard it with the ferocity of something defending a principle.

They eat the moss. Not the living top layer—they cannot digest the active growth—but the dying lower layers, the moss that has begun to decompose, that has been broken down enough by fungi and bacteria that its cellular structure is accessible. They are detritivores, recyclers, the mesa's composting system. They take what is dying and make it dead, and their waste feeds the layer below them, which feeds the layer below that, which feeds the roots of the ferns, which hold the moss, which holds the water, which feeds the ferns.

They do not come to the surface during the day. The light is wrong for them—the diffuse grey glow that passes for sunlight here hurts their eyes, which are adapted for the dark lattice, for navigation by touch and smell and the vibration-sense they've developed in their paws. The mesa's ground is alive with micro-vibrations: water moving through stone, the footfalls of larger creatures, the settling and shifting of moss-hills. The moss-dwellers read these vibrations the way you read a page. They know what is above them. They know where it is. They know when it moves.

At night, some of them surface. Not to hunt—they don't hunt. But to drink. The condensation on the moss-surface, the droplets that form on fern-fronds, the thin films of water on stone—they emerge, briefly, wetting their faces, their paws, then retreating to their dry tunnels, shaking themselves off, returning to the architecture they maintain.

They have a social structure. Not complex—not the intricate hierarchies of wolves or the cooperative networks of bees—but something. Territories. Boundaries. Overlapping zones of control where individuals tolerate each other's presence and zones where they do not. They communicate through scent-marking, through the vibration-patterns they stamp into the moss, and occasionally through vocalization: high, thin sounds, above the range of human hearing, that carry through the root-lattice like telegraph signals.

They are the mesa's nervous system. Sensitive, distributed, responsive. When something large moves across the moss, the vibration propagates through the lattice, and the moss-dwellers know. They know before the insects know. They know before the birds know. They know because they are already there, already touching the ground, already listening with their bodies.

## The Fern-Walkers

Above the moss, in the fern-thickets that stand to a child's shoulder and higher, live the birds.

They are not birds. They are what birds might become if given ten million years of pressure toward a body-plan that doesn't need flight, doesn't want flight, has no use for flight in a place where the air is too thick and wet to fly in and too dense to see through. They have wings—vestigial, folded flat against their bodies, the feathers reduced to insulating down. They have beaks—hooked, sharp, designed for shearing fern-frond and cracking insect-carapace. They have legs—long, jointed high, the lower segments thin as sticks, the upper segments thick with muscle. They walk. They run. They climb fern-stalks with gripping toes, four-forward-two-back, the arrangement of a foot designed for cylindrical surfaces.

The bird that watches you from the fern-frond—that watchful, assessing, cataloguing creature—is one of these. They are the mesa's eyes.

They live in the fern-forest canopy, if canopy is the word for the overlapping frond-layer that creates shade at ground level. They build nests—not in trees, there are no trees, but in the crotches where fern-stalks diverge, in the dense root-masses at the base of tree-fern clusters. The nests are woven from fern-fiber and moss-thread and lined with the shed fur of the moss-dwellers, which they collect from the surface, pulling stray tufts from the moss-hills with deliberate, selective beak-work. They are the only creatures on the mesa that interact with both the surface-world and the lattice-world—they harvest from above what the moss-dwellers shed from below, and they use it for their own purposes, without asking.

They are territorial. Their territories are auditory—defined not by space but by sound, by the calls they make and the calls they will tolerate from others. The mesa's acoustics, the way sound bends and carries through the wet air, the way it reflects off stone-faces and is absorbed by moss-hills—all of this is their medium. A fern-walker's call at dawn, that low warbling thing you hear before you see the light change, is a boundary-marker. It says: *I am here. This frequency is mine. The ferns within earshot are mine.*

They eat insects. They eat the filterers, when the filterers drift low enough to be caught—their beaks snapping shut with a speed that contradicts their usual deliberation, the translucent body crushed and swallowed, the bioluminescence dying in their throats. They eat the seeds of ferns, the spore-cases of mosses, the small crustaceans that live in the surface-film of water on standing pools. They are generalists. They are opportunists. They survive because they will eat anything and because their territory-system prevents over-exploitation of any one resource.

They watch everything. Not with curiosity—curiosity implies a self that wants to know, and the fern-walkers are not burdened with selves in the way you understand selves. They watch with attention. With processing. Their eyes are large, forward-facing, the optics tuned for the grey-green spectrum of light that penetrates the mist. They see patterns. They see movement. They see the subtle wrongness that indicates a predator moving through the ferns, the displacement-pattern that means death is approaching, and they call—not alarm-calls, not exactly, but information-calls, a rapid clicking that propagates through the territory-network, each bird passing the signal to the next, until the whole fern-forest knows: something is moving.

When you walk through the mesa, the fern-walkers track you. You register in their attention as: large, warm, slow, confused. Not predator—you don't move like a predator, don't have the patience-stillness or the explosive speed. Not prey—you're too large, the body-mass too concentrated. You are *other*. You are a category they have no slot for, and so they watch, and they wait, and they do not approach.

## The Threaders

In the standing pools, in the water that may be bottomless, life has gone aquatic.

The threaders are named for what they appear to be—threads, filaments, strands of living material that drift in the still water of the pools, catching the diffuse light, looking like nothing so much as loose strands of algae. They are not algae. They are animals—colonial animals, like coral or siphonophores, each strand a chain of individual zooids, each zooid specialized for a function: feeding, reproduction, defense, buoyancy-control.

A single threader colony can be meters long. The strands drift with the water's barely-perceptible current, spreading through the pool in a three-dimensional web, a net cast by no one, catching everything that falls into it. Detritus. Insect larvae. The shed exoskeletons of molting things. The dissolved remains of kills. The threaders are the pool's digestive system, breaking down what falls, incorporating it, growing, extending, branching.

When a predator takes a grazer at the pool's edge—when the water clouds with blood and the microorganisms feast—the threaders feast too. The blood disperses through the pool, diluted, diffused, and the threaders absorb it through their feeding zooids, their strands darkening momentarily as the iron-rich water moves through their systems. They are the mesa's recycling, its refusal to waste. What was grazer becomes threader. What was threader becomes, eventually, the sediment at the pool's floor—if the pool has a floor—which becomes the peat, which becomes the moss, which becomes the world.

The threaders do not move independently. They have no muscles, no propulsion. They go where the water takes them, and the water in the pools barely moves. But the pools are connected—not visibly, not through surface channels, but through the stone itself, through the porous limestone that underlies everything. Water moves through the rock, and the threaders move with it, their colonies fragmenting and reforming, strands drifting through subterranean channels, emerging in pools hundreds of meters apart. The threaders are distributed. The threaders are everywhere. Every pool on the mesa contains fragments of every other pool's colony, genetically identical, a single organism spread across the plateau's entire hydrological system.

They are, in a sense, the mesa's blood.

## The Grazers

The large warm-blooded things—the ones the size of cattle, the long-legged waders—have no name because no one has been here long enough to name them. They name themselves, after a fashion: they call to each other across the mist, low rumbling sounds that travel through the wet air better than high frequencies, sounds that the fern-walkers cannot parse and the moss-dwellers feel as vibration rather than hear.

They live in the open regions—the sedge-meadows, the standing-pool margins, the places where the ferns give way to shorter growth. They are not herd animals, not exactly. They are *association* animals—individuals who prefer proximity to other individuals but do not coordinate, do not lead, do not follow. They simply tend toward each other, the way water tends toward low ground. An association of grazers might number five or fifteen or thirty, and the composition shifts constantly, individuals drifting in and out, no bonds maintained beyond the immediate moment of proximity.

They are built for wading. Their legs are long—the lower segments nearly hairless, the skin dark and rough, scaled almost, adapted for constant immersion in cold water. Their bodies are dense, the torso compact, the neck thick, the head broad and flat. Their mouths are specialized: not teeth but keratinous plates, serrated, growing constantly to replace what the tough sedges wear away. They clip. They graze. They stand in the shallows and eat the vegetation that grows at the water's edge, their legs immersed to the knee, their bodies above, their heat-loss minimized by the arrangement—the legs in the water lose heat, yes, but the torso, the core, the vital organs, stay dry, stay warm, stay functional.

Their fur is extraordinary. Not fur, exactly—more like a pelt of dense, oily fiber, each strand hollow, each strand insulating. Water rolls off it. Ice cannot form on it. In a place where moisture is the universal condition, they have evolved a surface that rejects it, and they are the driest large things on the mesa. Their fur smells of oil and vegetation and something else, something mineral, the smell of their own metabolism burning slow and hot in the cold.

They are warm. Radiantly, aggressively warm. Their metabolism runs hot—they must maintain a body-temperature significantly above the ambient cold, and they do this by eating constantly, by processing food with an efficiency that borders on desperate. They are always hungry. They are always eating. The sedge-meadows exist because of them and in spite of them—the grazing pressure keeps the sedges low, prevents them from succeeding to anything taller, maintains the open character of these regions. Remove the grazers and the sedges would thicken, the ferns would move in, the biome would shift. The grazers are a force, a process, a maintenance program.

They are also prey.

## The Ambush Hunters

The pools have depths that nobody has found. The water is dark—not muddy, not cloudy, but dark the way deep water is dark, dark because the light does not penetrate, dark because the threaders and the dissolved organics absorb it. In this darkness, in the cold, in the patient water, the largest predators on the mesa wait.

They are warm-blooded. This is the critical fact, the defining trait, the thing that makes them wrong in a way that the cold-blooded ambush hunters of other places are not wrong. They do not slow with the temperature. They do not conserve, do not reduce, do not enter torpor. They wait in water that would kill a human in minutes—cold enough, wet enough, deep enough—and they wait warm, their hearts beating slow but steady, their muscles loaded with oxygen-rich blood, their brains alert, their senses deployed.

They are not crocodilian. They are not anything you have a category for. Their bodies are low, wide, flattened—the profile of something designed to be invisible from above, to present a surface no thicker than a fallen log, a moss-covered stone. Their skin is textured, rough, colonized by actual moss and algae and the threaders themselves—the ambush hunters are ecosystems, their backs hosting communities, their stillness so complete that life grows on them without knowing they are alive.

Their faces are the faces of patience. Eyes set wide, nearly on the sides of the head, giving them a field of vision that encompasses everything above the waterline. Nostrils that seal. Ears that seal. The mouth—a long horizontal line, the jaw articulated in a way that allows a gape wider than the skull, the teeth not pointed but flat, serrated, the teeth of something that does not bite but shears, that does not hold but amputates.

They sense heat. Not infrared—the water is too cold, too uniform, for infrared to be useful. They sense the *displacement* of heat, the way a warm body changes the temperature of the water around it, the faint thermal signature that propagates through the pool in ripples and eddies. A grazer standing at the pool's margin, its legs immersed, its body-heat leaking into the water—that grazer is broadcasting. The ambush hunter receives the broadcast. It knows where the grazer is. It knows how large the grazer is. It knows, from the pattern of heat-loss, whether the grazer is healthy or injured, calm or alarmed, standing still or about to move.

And it waits.

The strike is not fast. It is not the explosive acceleration of a crocodile, the muscular geyser of a creature built for sudden violence. The ambush hunter's strike is a *rising*. It lifts. The water pours off its back, the moss and algae streaming, the body emerging from the pool like something being born, like the pool itself producing a limb, and the mouth opens—not snapping, not clamping—opening, and the grazer's leg enters the opening, and the jaw closes, and the serrated teeth do their work, and then the hunter pulls, and the grazer falls, and the water receives them both.

It is over in seconds. It has been building for weeks.

Afterward, the hunter submerges again. The body settles. The moss on its back rearranges. The water clears. The threaders move in to process what the hunter could not consume—the fragments, the fluids, the dissolved evidence. Within hours, the pool looks as it did before. Within a day, the sediment has absorbed everything. Within a week, the moss-dwellers have incorporated the nutrients into their lattice, the ferns have taken them up through their roots, the sedge-meadows have grown a little denser at this pool's margin.

The grazer is gone. The grazer is everywhere. The grazer has become the mesa.

## The Cold-Blooded Hunters

Not the pool-dwellers. The other ones. The ones that move through the fern-forests, through the moss-hills, low-slung and scaled and wet. The ones that hunt by heat-differential, that sense your warmth from a quarter-mile, that move toward it with patience and inevitability.

They are the mesa's mobile predators, the ones that do not wait. Where the ambush hunters are geological—patient, static, present—the cold-blooded hunters are meteorological. They move like weather. They appear and disappear. They are somewhere and then they are not, and you cannot track them because they leave no sign, no displacement of moss, no bent fern-stalk, no scuff-mark on stone. They are the weight of the air, the displacement of mist, the sudden wrongness in the pattern of bird-silence.

Their bodies are long, low, limbless—or nearly limbless, the vestigial limbs reduced to internal structures, the locomotion entirely serpentine. But they are not snakes. Their scales are not dry-snake scales but wet scales, moist scales, each one a tiny reservoir of water, each one maintaining the film of moisture that keeps their skin functional. They are amphibian-snakes, snake-newts, something that found a niche between categories and filled it completely.

They hunt the moss-dwellers. This is their primary prey—the small, warm, tunnel-dwelling things that live in the root-lattice. The cold-blooded hunters cannot enter the tunnels—they are too large, too rigid—but they can sense the warmth radiating from below, can feel the vibration of movement through the moss, and they wait at the surface, coiled in the deep green, invisible, their color-shifting scales matching the moss-shade exactly, until a moss-dweller surfaces to drink. Then: the strike. Fast—faster than the cold implies, the muscles loaded with anaerobic reserve, the movement a single convulsion that covers the distance between predator and prey in a blink. The moss-dweller is taken before it knows it is seen.

They also hunt fern-walkers, when they can. A fern-walker on the ground, away from its nest, distracted by foraging—the cold-blooded hunter rises from the moss like something emerging from water, and the fern-walker has just enough time to call, once, that sharp information-click, before the coils take it.

The information-click propagates. The fern-forest knows. The birds go silent. The insects continue—they are too small, too numerous, too automatic to register the predator as relevant. But the warm things, the things with internal thermostats and metabolic investments to protect—they know. They adjust. They move away, or they go still, or they descend deeper into their tunnels.

The cold-blooded hunters eat and then they disappear. They do not lair, do not den, do not maintain territories. They drift across the mesa the way the rain drifts, following rules of their own devising, sustained by the cold and the wet and the abundance of small warm things. They are the mesa's wandering attention, its roving focus, the part of itself that moves and seeks and finds.

They grow large. Not ambush-hunter large—the cold slows them, limits them, keeps their metabolism in check—but large enough. The largest seen would reach a human's waist if reared up, which they never do. They do not need to rear up. They need only to be low and patient and close.

## The Observers

The bird that watches you—the one that assesses, catalogues, places you in some mental system—is not the only observer.

There are others. Not creatures—not exactly. The mesa has produced something that is not quite life and not quite not-life, something that exists in the spaces between categories, in the gap between the biological and the geological. The observers are the mesa's awareness, distributed through the moss, through the water, through the root-lattice and the pool-water and the mist itself.

You feel them as the sense of being in something's body. The homeostatic awareness, the feedback-loop consciousness, the system that knows itself without being conscious. When the moss yields under your foot and rebounds, that is not just mechanical—that is responsive. When the mist thickens around you as you walk, that is not just weather—that is attention. When the bird watches you from the fern-frond, the bird is the eye, but the thing watching is the mesa, looking at you through one of its many organs, assessing you the way your immune system assesses a foreign protein.

The observers have no body. They have every body. They are the network of interaction—the predator-prey cycles, the competition between moss species, the chemical signals passed through water and air and root-contact. They are the reason the mesa is balanced, the reason the erosion continues at its measured pace, the reason the predator-prey cycles oscillate within limits. They are regulation itself, homeostasis incarnate, a living system that has achieved something like thought without anything like a brain.

They do not think about you. They register you. They note your warmth, your displacement, your consumption of their air and disruption of their moss. They calculate your impact—a number so small, so temporary, so negligible against the geological timescale of their operation that it barely registers. You are a perturbation. You are a cough in a body the size of a continent. You will be absorbed, processed, incorporated. The moss will erase your footprints. The water will dilute your heat. The threaders will process your cells when you die, if you die here, and you will become part of the system you walked through.

The observers do not mind. The observers do not mind anything. They are process, not opinion. They are function, not judgment. They are the mesa knowing itself, and the mesa knows that it is vast and old and wet and that everything within it is temporary except the water.

## What Lives Here

What lives on the Weeping Mesa is the Weeping Mesa.

Not metaphorically. Not poetically. The moss is the mesa's skin, and the pools are its eyes, and the ferns are its lungs, and the water moving through stone is its blood, and the creatures that move through it are its thoughts—brief, electrical, fading. The filterers clean its air. The threaders clean its water. The moss-dwellers maintain its structure. The fern-walkers process its information. The grazers manage its growth. The predators manage its grazers. The observers manage everything.

It is an organism. It is an ecosystem. It is a geological process that has become biological, a landform that has become a lifeform, a rock that has learned to cry and in crying has learned to live.

What lives here is something that has been becoming itself for ten million years, and is not done, and will not be done until the erosion takes the last of the height and the water finds its level and the plateau becomes a slope and the slope becomes a plain and the plain becomes something else, something lower, something drier, something that remembers being high and wet and alive but cannot be that anymore.

Until then—now, for now, for the geological now that stretches in both directions beyond your comprehension—the mesa lives. The mesa weeps. The mesa knows it weeps, and cannot stop, and does not want to stop, because the weeping is the living and the living is the weeping and the water is the only thing that will be here when everything else, including you, has been absorbed into the green and the grey and the deep, patient, bottomless pools of the Weeping Mesa.
