# What Lives Here

## The People

They don't look like you expected.

Not because they've changed physically—though some have. The miners' descendants are compact, deep-chested, with the efficient builds of people who've spent generations in low-ceilinged spaces. Their skin has a quality you can't immediately name, a faint translucence that catches the bioluminescent light, and it takes you a while to realize they're not actually glowing—the fungal spores in their pores are. Everyone carries a faint blue-green dusting across their shoulders, the tops of their ears, the fine hairs of their forearms. It's not infection. It's *integration*. The phosphorescent organisms have found a niche in human skin the same way they've found niches in everything else down here, and the immune systems of three hundred years have simply... accommodated them.

They don't startle when excavators cross their feet. You watched a woman eating breakfast while a dozen rust-red centipedes traced paths across her boots, and she didn't flinch, didn't glance down, didn't register them at all. When you stepped on one—it was an accident, you couldn't help it, they're *everywhere*—she looked up at you with an expression of genuine shock. Not horror. Shock that you would notice. That you would think it mattered.

That's the thing about them that takes longest to understand. They don't see the animals. Not literally—their eyes work fine, they navigate the swarms with precision—but they've stopped *perceiving* them as separate. The excavators are part of the floor. The flutter-things are part of the ceiling. The digestive beetles are part of the corridor. The distinction between creature and environment has dissolved so completely that mentioning the animals seems to them like mentioning the air. Why would you? It's just *where you are*.

---

The children come first, because children always come first.

They emerge from side-passages with the total confidence of people who have never known a space they couldn't navigate. The youngest ones—four, five years old—move with a fluidity that makes you uncomfortable. They slide through gaps you wouldn't have noticed, duck under pipes at exactly the right moment, step over swarm-paths without looking. They've learned the topography of this place the way surface children learn the topography of a house: room to room, corner to corner, the location of every obstacle and opening encoded in their bodies before their minds can articulate it.

They play in the animal zones. You watch a group of six children—ages ranging from maybe four to ten—playing something that resembles hide-and-seek in a chamber currently being processed by three digestive beetles. The beetles grind along their paths. The children dart around them. Nobody collides. The beetles don't adjust their paths; the children adjust around the beetles, incorporating the massive armored forms into their game the way surface children would incorporate trees or furniture. A girl of perhaps seven hides behind a beetle's shell, pressing herself flat against the iridescent surface, and the beetle doesn't react. It doesn't care. She's part of the background.

The older children carry tools. Not toys—tools, worn smooth with use. A boy of ten has a chemical applicator strapped to his belt, the kind the architects use to signal new growth, and he's practicing on a wall: tiny precise marks that look like nothing but that you later learn are growth-prompts, the first vocabulary of a language he'll spend his life learning. He's already fluent in the basics. By twelve, he'll be apprenticed. By sixteen, he'll be growing rooms.

You notice they don't shout. Surface children shout—it's the acoustic profile of open space, where sound dissipates and volume equals communication. These children communicate in clicks and taps and low humming sounds that you initially mistake for animal noise. It takes you three days to realize that the rhythmic tapping on the walls isn't the excavators. It's the children, sending each other signals through the wood itself, using the root-mass as a communication medium. They can feel each other's knocks through the floors. They can locate each other by vibration.

They learn this before they learn words.

---

The adults are harder to categorize, because the adults don't categorize themselves.

There's no government that you can identify. No council, no mayor, no formal hierarchy. When you ask about leadership, people look puzzled. The closest thing to structure is the *schedule*—not a written schedule but an embodied one, a collective knowledge of who needs to be where and when, communicated through the tapping system and the chemical signals and the movements of the animals themselves. The excavators' swarm-patterns indicate which chambers need attention. The flutter-things' migration cycles signal shift changes in the fungal gardens. The beetles' territorial routes mark the boundaries between work zones.

People just... go where they need to be. A woman named Hesper—she tells you her name by tapping it against the wall, a pattern of knocks that the wood carries to anyone listening—wakes when the steam vents shift rhythm, eats while monitoring a beetle route, works a six-hour shift in the pharmaceutical chambers, tends a growth-garden during the humidity cycle, and sleeps when the flutter-things return to the upper reaches. She doesn't have a boss. She doesn't have a job title. She does what the colony needs, and the colony tells her what it needs through the rhythm of the place itself.

You ask her what she *is*. What her role is. She doesn't understand the question.

"I'm here," she says, as if that answers everything. And it does, in a way. She is here. She is part of the here. The distinction between her and her work and her home and her animals and her wood has been erased so thoroughly that the question of *identity*—who she is apart from the colony—doesn't compute.

This is not communism. This is not collectivism. This is something older. This is *symbiosis* enacted as lifestyle.

---

The architects are the closest thing to specialists.

There are perhaps thirty of them, ranging in age from early twenties to late eighties, and they carry themselves differently. Not above the others—you don't get the sense of hierarchy—but *apart*, in the way that someone with a specialized skill occupies a different relationship to the shared space. They are the ones who speak to the wood.

You watch an architect at work. His name is Donal, and he's sixty, and his hands are stained with decades of chemical application—the growth-prompts have left permanent marks, dark whorls on his fingertips that match the spiral grain patterns on the walls. He's growing a new chamber, and the process is nothing like construction.

He begins by pressing his palms flat against a section of wall and *listening*. Not to sound—the wall isn't making audible noise—but to something else. Moisture content, he tells you later. Density. Chemical composition. The wall's willingness to grow, which is a real quality that varies by location and season and the root-mass's inscrutable priorities. He's reading the wall the way a farmer reads soil.

Then he applies the chemical signals. Not with tools—with his hands, his marked fingers, tracing patterns on the wood that leave residue the excavators will find and follow. The signals are complex: direction, rate, shape, structural requirements. A new chamber takes two to five years to grow to habitable size. Donal is planning for people who haven't been born yet.

He shows you a chamber he started thirty years ago. It's occupied now—a family of four, the walls still bearing the faint marks of his chemical prompts, the spiral grain already beginning to turn toward the amber gas lamps. He touches the wall with something you can only describe as *affection*. Not pride. Affection. He grew this room the way a parent raises a child: with intention, with patience, with acceptance that the result would be something he couldn't fully control.

"The wood has opinions," he says, and he's not joking. "You can tell it what you want, but it decides how. I wanted this room to be oval. It came out round. I don't know why. It just did."

He doesn't find this frustrating. He finds it *normal*. The wood is a collaborator, not a material. You don't force it. You negotiate with it.

---

The fungal garden workers are the ones who cough.

They work in the warmest, wettest chambers, in steam-condensation suits that look like something between hazmat gear and diving equipment—heavy, sealed, constantly damp with condensation that pools in the suits' creases and runs down the inside. The air in these chambers is so thick with spores that you can see it, a pale haze that turns the amber light to something dreamlike and diffuse. The workers' masks filter the spores. They still cough. Three centuries of exposure have produced a population with fungal-tolerant lungs, but tolerance isn't immunity, and the cough—the deep, wet, productive cough of people whose respiratory systems have incorporated organisms that weren't originally human—is as constant as the steam vents.

They don't consider themselves sick. The cough is just part of the work. They've adapted to it the way they've adapted to everything else: by incorporating it, accepting it, making it part of the rhythm.

A garden worker named Lethe—you meet her during a shift change, her suit unzipped to the waist, her skin glistening with condensation and spore-dust—shows you the cultivation beds. They sprawl across an entire chamber, hundreds of square feet of fungal mat in colors you didn't know fungi could be: deep violet, electric blue, a red so dark it's almost black. Each variety produces different compounds. The antibiotics are the pale blue ones. The psychoactive compounds are the violet. The chemical precursors—the ones that go to the surface for industrial processing—are the dark red.

She tends them with a precision that seems at odds with the organic chaos of the space. Each bed is measured, monitored, adjusted. Temperature. Humidity. Nutrient flow. The wood itself participates—the root-mass channels water to the beds through capillary action, and the fungal growth responds to chemical signals that Lethe applies with the same marked-finger precision as the architects.

"The gardens are the root-mass's lungs," she says, and the metaphor isn't metaphorical. The fungal mats process the air, consuming carbon dioxide and producing oxygen. The flutter-things spread the spores. The root-mass channels the nutrients. It's respiration. It's breathing. And Lethe is part of it—a node in the system, an organ in the body of the colony.

She's been doing this for twenty years. She'll do it for twenty more. She doesn't want to do anything else. This is what she is.

---

The mechanics are the strangest group, because they live at the intersection of the industrial and the organic, and they've become something that neither surface engineers nor root-basin residents can quite categorize.

There are maybe fifty of them, maintaining the generators and the steam systems and the electrical strips and the pumps and the factories. Their workshops are tucked into chambers that were grown for other purposes—fungal processing rooms repurposed, old growth-chambers converted—and the machines sit on floors that shift, bolted to wood that grows around the brackets, and the mechanics have learned to work *with* the wood rather than against it.

A mechanic named Torrent shows you a generator. It's been running for a century and twelve years, and it's buried so deep in the root-mass that you have to crawl through a tunnel barely wide enough for your shoulders to reach it. The wood has grown around the generator like a hand closing around a stone—not crushing it, but *holding* it. The brackets are buried in grain. The power conduits are sheathed in living bark. The generator vibrates, and the wood vibrates with it, and the combined vibration has become part of the colony's rhythm.

Torrent doesn't maintain the generator in any conventional sense. She doesn't replace parts—she *grows* replacements, guiding the wood to form structural supports where metal has weakened, training fungal growth to insulate electrical systems, coaxing the root-mass to channel methane to the combustion chambers with chemical signals. The machine is half-industrial, half-organic at this point, and it works better than either would alone.

"The wood likes the vibration," Torrent says, and she sounds matter-of-fact. "Generators that run smooth, the wood grows away from them. Generators that run at the right frequency, the wood grows *toward* them. This one's been running at the right frequency for a hundred years. The root-mass doesn't want it to stop."

She says this as if the root-mass's preferences are a normal engineering consideration. As if "what the wood wants" is a standard variable in maintenance calculations. And maybe, down here, it is.

## The Animals (Again)

You need to talk about the animals again, because the people aren't the whole story. The people are maybe a tenth of the story. The animals are the rest.

The excavators are the most numerous, and after three days, you stop seeing them as individuals and start seeing them as *medium*. They're not creatures; they're *weather*. They move in swarms that follow patterns you can't predict but that the residents can read like forecasts. A rust-red swarm means iron-rich wood ahead. A pale cream swarm means the fungal chambers are cycling. A dark green swarm means the phosphorescent zone is expanding. The people read the swarms the way surface cultures read clouds or bird migrations—not as events to control but as signals to interpret.

The excavators have been breeding for three hundred years without human intervention, and they've developed behaviors that the original engineers never designed. They communicate through vibrations, yes, but the vibrations have become *complex*. You hear patterns that repeat, that vary, that respond to other patterns. It's language. It has to be language. The excavators are talking to each other, and the content of their conversations determines which chambers get cleared and which get left alone and where the new growth happens.

The people have learned to listen. Not to understand—the vibration-language is too fast and too fine for human perception—but to *glean*. An experienced resident can tell from the quality of the excavator-chatter whether the swarm is working happily, whether they've encountered a problem, whether they're about to change direction. It's not communication. It's *eavesdropping*, and the residents do it constantly, unconsciously, the way you unconsciously track the tone of conversation in a restaurant.

---

The digestive beetles are the most *present* of the animals, because they're large enough to require accommodation. Twenty pounds of armored, eight-legged, grinding biomass is not something you ignore. The corridors they claim as territory become impassable during their processing cycles, and the residents simply... reroute. Not with frustration—with the fluidity of people who've never known a corridor to be permanently available. The beetles' schedules are the residents' schedules. The beetles' territories are the residents' map.

You watch a family leave their home because a beetle has claimed their corridor for processing. They don't rush. They don't complain. They gather their things—the hanging clothes, the sealed book-cases, the waterproof screens—and they move to a temporary chamber, and they'll move back when the beetle is done. The beetle is doing its job. Their inconvenience is the cost of living in a body that processes waste into nutrients. They accept this cost the way you accept the cost of traffic.

The beetles have generations, too. The ones you see are descendants of the original engineered stock, and they've grown larger, more efficient, more *opinionated*. The original beetles were designed to follow chemical trails. These beetles choose their own paths. They've developed preferences—specific chambers they return to, specific wood-compositions they favor. The residents track these preferences and plan around them. A family whose home is on a beetle's preferred route knows to keep their corridor clear during processing cycles. It's like living with a very large, very predictable roommate who eats your walls.

---

The flutter-things are the ones that haunt you.

They're not birds. They're not bats. They're something that started as both and became neither, three centuries of adaptation producing a creature that exists nowhere else and couldn't survive anywhere else. Their faces—those folded-cloth faces—are not expressive, but they're *responsive*. They turn toward movement. They track heat. They make sounds that you can't parse into categories: not quite vocalization, not quite mechanical, something in between.

Their screams are the worst. When disturbed—a person walking through their territory too quickly, a machine running at the wrong frequency, a flutter-thing injured or separated from its swarm—the scream is like tearing metal, like a sheet being ripped in half amplified to unbearable volume. You hear it for the first time on your second day, and your body reacts before your mind can process the sound. You duck. You cover your ears. You look for the threat.

The residents don't react. The scream is information. It means something specific—a territorial warning, a distress signal, a swarm-coordination call—and the residents have learned to distinguish between the types the way they distinguish between the steam-vent patterns. A territorial scream means adjust your route. A distress scream means something is wrong in the upper reaches. A coordination scream means the humidity cycle is approaching.

The flutter-things maintain the fungal gardens, and the fungal gardens maintain the air, and the air maintains everything else. Without the flutter-things, the colony suffocates. The residents know this. The flutter-things don't care. They're not grateful. They're not cooperative. They're not domesticated. They do what they do because it's what they do, and the colony survives because what they do is what the colony needs, and this alignment is either an extraordinary coincidence or the result of three centuries of co-evolution so intimate that the word "symbiosis" feels insufficient.

## The Old Ones

You don't meet them, but you hear about them.

The elders—the residents over seventy—carry a quality that you can't name but can feel. They've been here longer. The root-mass has grown around them more completely. Their chemical signatures are deeper, more established, more *recognized* by the wood and the animals. Excavators part around them. Flutter-things don't scream when they pass. The beetles yield.

An elder named Carreth—ninety-two, the oldest person in the colony—walks through chambers that reorganize themselves for her. The excavators clear her path. The wood grows smooth under her feet. The fungal mats brighten when she passes, leaning toward her like flowers toward light. She doesn't command this. She doesn't even seem to notice it. It just happens, the way breathing happens, the way the steam vents release on schedule.

She tells you—through taps and words and the chemical signals that the residents have learned to emit—that she remembers a time when the wood was smaller. When the chambers were fewer. When the colony was different. She doesn't describe this as decline or progress. She describes it as *growth*. The colony grew. She grew with it. The wood grew around her. She is part of it now in a way that the younger residents aren't yet, and the younger residents will be, eventually, if they live long enough.

"When I was young," she taps, "the wood was a place. Now the wood is me."

She means this literally. The spores in her skin, the chemical signals in her sweat, the vibration-patterns she's internalized—she's not just adapted to the colony. She's been *incorporated* by it. The root-mass knows her the way it knows its own chambers. She is a feature of its internal landscape. When she dies—and she will die, soon, and the digestive beetles will process her, and her chemical signature will join the signals in the wood—she will become part of the structure. Her minerals will feed new growth. Her chamber will remember her, in the grain-patterns, in the chemical traces, in the shape of the room that grew around her.

She doesn't find this disturbing. She finds it *correct*.

## What Lives Here

What lives here is not a town. Not a colony. Not a settlement.

What lives here is a single organism composed of wood and machinery and animals and people and fungi and water and methane and electricity and three hundred years of accumulated growth, all of it pulsing to the same rhythm, all of it breathing to the same beat, all of it *aware* in some liminal, incomprehensible way that isn't consciousness but isn't mere mechanism either.

The people who live here are not inhabitants. They are *organs*. The animals are not wildlife. They are *systems*. The wood is not architecture. It is *body*. The machines are not tools. They are *organelles*.

And the thing that lives here—the whole thing, the root-mass and everything it has incorporated—knows you're here. It knew you were coming. The steam vents shifted when you arrived. The excavator swarms adjusted their patterns. The flutter-things screamed a coordination call you didn't recognize, and the residents looked up, and then they looked at you, and they knew.

They didn't welcome you. They didn't reject you. They *registered* you, the way a body registers a splinter—not with hostility, but with awareness. You are here. You are foreign. You will leave. The body will close around the space you occupied, and the wood will grow over your footprints, and the excavators will process the traces of your chemical signature, and in a year, there will be no evidence you were ever here.

But right now, standing in the central chamber, feeling the heartbeat in the floor, watching the swarms move in patterns that have reorganized around your presence, listening to the layered roar of a living system adjusting to accommodate a visitor—

You understand what lives here.

Something old. Something patient. Something that has been growing for three hundred years and will keep growing long after the surface world has forgotten it exists. Something that doesn't need you, doesn't want you, doesn't care about you in any human sense of the word *care*—

but knows you're here.

and is watching.
