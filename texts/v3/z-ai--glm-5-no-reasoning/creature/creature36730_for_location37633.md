# The Inhabitants of Fern-Gut District

## The Workers

There are nine of them presently. Not all live here—three walk in from the upper switchbacks at first bell, their boots already damp from the dew on the root-mat path. The other six sleep in the numbered buildings, the ones with the chalk marks that have persisted.

They are not a family. Not a guild. Not exactly a community. They are *a crew*—the word they use, the distinction they make. A crew works together because the work requires it, because the vat-works demands three people to manage a batch-change and the pump needs someone at dawn and the unloading when the coal cart comes needs four sets of hands. They have been a crew for different lengths of time. The oldest among them, a woman named Hest, has worked the vats for thirty-one years. The newest arrived eight months ago—a young man from somewhere drier, still adjusting to the way his skin never quite dries.

You know them by their hands.

---

## Hest

She is sixty-three years old and has lived in the hollow for forty of them. She came as a girl, assigned to the vat-works through some bureaucratic process she never questioned, and she simply stayed. Her hair is gray and grows in a particular pattern—a straight part down the center, pulled back and bound with a leather cord, wisps escaping around her temples where the humidity defeats the tie. Her face is deeply lined, but the lines don't correspond to expression; they correspond to *direction*. The creases run downward from her eyes and mouth, as if forty years of descending into the hollow have written themselves into her skin.

Her hands are what you notice. Stained green-brown from the distillate, the color worked so deep into her palms that it won't wash out. Her fingers are thick from labor—the second joints swollen, the nails trimmed to the quick and discolored. When she holds a ladle or tests the viscosity of a batch, her grip has the certainty of someone who has made the same motion ten thousand times.

She talks very little, and when she talks, she talks to the work. Comments to the vat. Observations spoken aloud to the steam, to the copper, to the valve that needs a quarter-turn. Not talking *at* these things. Talking *with* them. The younger workers thought she was mad when they arrived. They don't think that anymore. They've started doing it too.

She lives alone in Building Seven. The one that lists three degrees north. She doesn't mind the tilt. Says it keeps the water from pooling in the corners when the heavy rains come.

---

## Cadre and Venn

The pump-workers. A pair, not a couple—they make this clear when asked, though no one asks anymore. Cadre is the one working the pump right now. Squeak-chunk, squeak-chunk. She is forty, broad-shouldered, with a scar across her left eyebrow from a chain that snapped seven years ago. She has a way of standing when she pumps—feet planted wide, back straight, letting her arms and shoulders do the work in a rhythm she could maintain for hours. She can. She has.

Venn is younger, thirty-two, slighter, with a cast in one eye that makes him look always slightly to the left of whoever he's addressing. He's in the loading-yard right now, talking with someone—you can hear his voice as one of the two faint threads of conversation. He handles the logistics. The coal delivery, the jug-exports, the ledger that no one else can read because he invented the notation himself. The system works because Venn keeps it working. When something is low, he knows before anyone else. When something is needed, he's already sent word up the switchbacks.

They share Building Twelve. Two rooms, one shared kitchen space they neither use nor maintain. They eat at different times, sleep at different hours, coordinate through a chalkboard hung by the door. This arrangement suits them. Neither has ever explained why.

## The Others

Six more. Their details emerge slowly in a place where change is measured in decades:

**Marray** works the furnace. Fifty years old, cough that never quite clears, habit of wiping his face with a cloth he keeps tucked in his belt. The coal dust has worked itself into the creases of his forearms, the rims of his ears, the grain of his callused hands. He speaks in a low rumble that barely rises above the furnace's roar. When the batch is running, he barely sleeps—tends the fire in four-hour shifts, naps in the coal-yard between, wakes when the steam-pressure changes.

**Tetch** is nineteen. The newest after the young man from the drier country. She climbed down the switchbacks eleven months ago and asked for work, and Hest looked at her hands and said *you'll do*. She harvests the orange spore-caps from the moss in the corners of the hollow. It's delicate work. Requires stillness, patience, fingers that can pinch without crushing. Her hands are still smooth. Give it time.

**Olem** and **Brin** are siblings. Brother and sister, born in the hollow, children of workers who died in the hollow, buried somewhere no one talks about. They manage the drying-racks, the herb-harvest, the bundles of unidentified broad-leafed groundcover that gets cut and hung and eventually shipped out for purposes they don't ask about. Olem is quiet. Brin is quieter. They communicate through glances, gestures, a language of eyebrows and tilted heads that thirty years of proximity has refined into something almost telepathic.

**Sennet** is the one whose boot-steps you might hear if you listen. He walks the perimeter every morning, checking the *Syngonium* brackets, testing the chains, noting which cauldron needs scrubbing and which valve leaks worse than last week. He's forty-five and has a habit of humming tunes no one recognizes. Sometimes they're in keys that don't quite resolve. He lives in Building Three, the one with the angled porch, and he doesn't seem to mind that the door swings open on its own when the wind shifts.

**Drys** arrived eight months ago—the young man from the drier hills somewhere north, where the water table is twelve feet down and the ground doesn't give when you walk. He's still adjusting. You can see it in the way he wipes his forehead constantly, checks his boots for rot, startles slightly when the *Syngonium* creaks overhead. He's been assigned to help Hest. She doesn't speak to him except to instruct. He doesn't speak except to acknowledge. They understand each other.

## How They Live

The days here have a shape. Not a schedule—nothing so rigid. But a *shape*. The work determines it:

**Dawn.** The pump starts. Cadre or Venn, whoever's turn, working the handle until the boiler-tank is full. The sound carries through the hollow, that squeak-chunk rhythm, and the others wake to it or don't—some have learned to sleep through, some wake at the first stroke.

**First light through the leaf-lattice.** Diffuse, green-shifted, barely different from the pre-dawn murk. Marray banks the furnace for the day's run. Hest inspects the vats, touches the copper, reads something in the temperature of the metal that no thermometer could tell her.

**Mid-morning.** The batch reaches simmer. The smell shifts—chlorophyll and rot joined by something sharper, the distillate beginning to separate. Tetch heads to the corners to harvest spore-caps. Olem and Brin start the cutting.

**Noon.** No bell, no signal. But everyone knows. A pause in the work. Food comes out—stored bread, dried meat, whatever vegetables came up the switchbacks on the last cart. They eat separately or together, depending on the day, depending on the batch-status.

**Afternoon.** The longest stretch. The vat-work continues. The condensation drips. Someone walks the perimeter, checks the jugs, adjusts the drying-racks. Time slows here. Not unpleasantly—just *slows*. The hollow holds it still.

**Evening.** The furnace is banked for the night. The batch either finishes or continues through the dark. If it finishes, there's a moment of collective exhalation—not celebration, just acknowledgment. If it continues, Marray stays awake.

**Night.** The hollow settles. The creaking of the *Syngonium* becomes more pronounced as the temperature drops and the stems contract. The workers sleep or don't. The work continues or waits. The water moves beneath the ground, finding its paths.

## The Weight of Staying

None of them talk about leaving. Not because they can't—the switchbacks go up as well as down. Not because they're forbidden—no one guards the way out. They don't talk about leaving because leaving has become unimaginable. Not in a trapped way. In a *completed* way.

Hest has been here forty years. Her skin has absorbed the green smell. Her lungs have adapted to the humid air—she coughs when she goes above the rim, dry and hacking, until she descends again. The hollow has written itself into her body.

The others are somewhere along that path. Different distances, different depths. But all moving the same direction. Slowly. Inexorably. Like water finding its way down.

The young man from the drier country—Drys—he doesn't know this yet. He still talks about going back, sometimes. Mentions it in the loading-yard, voice carrying faintly to the workers below. The others don't respond. Not unkindly—just without engagement. As if he'd mentioned the weather in a city they'd never visit.

Maybe he will leave. Maybe he'll climb the root-mat switchbacks and walk north to where the ground is solid and the air is dry. Or maybe, in three years or five, someone will ask him to check a valve and he'll do it without thinking, and he'll realize his hands have started to stain, and the distillate has worked itself into his palms the way it worked into Hest's.

The hollow is patient. It doesn't hurry. It doesn't need to.

## The Work Itself

They make something here. An extract. A tincture. The details are technical and somewhat boring—something about the *Syngonium* sap, something about the orange spore-caps, something about the way the water in the substrate carries minerals that catalyze the reaction when heated to exactly the right temperature for exactly the right duration.

The result goes into clay jugs. Sealed with wax. Shipped out once a month on a cart that comes down the switchbacks and leaves empty jugs and coal and sometimes food and leaves again with a full load.

None of the workers know what the extract is *for*. They know it goes to somewhere called the Plateau. They know it's valued. They know their wages come regularly, deductions for housing and board, the rest stored in a ledger Venn keeps. None of them has ever asked what happens to the jugs after they leave. It's not their question to ask.

And in a way, it doesn't matter. The work has its own meaning. When Hest taps the copper and feels the vibration travel through her palm, when Cadre finds the rhythm that lets the pump-work become automatic, when Tetch's fingers close around a spore-cap without crushing it—there's a satisfaction there that has nothing to do with commerce or purpose.

The work is *good*. Not morally good. Technically good. It requires attention, precision, a kind of ongoing negotiation with materials and processes that have their own demands. The workers meet those demands. They do it well. They continue to do it well, day after day, year after year.

This is enough. More than enough. This is everything.

## The Dead

Two graves in the hollow. Not marked, but known.

Hest's partner—no one uses the word *husband*—worked the vats before her. He died eleven years ago. A valve failed. There was pressure. The details aren't discussed. Hest was the one who found him. She didn't stop working. She cleaned the vat herself, replaced the valve, started the next batch. Then she buried him in the corner where the moss grows thick, and she went back to work.

The other grave is older. A worker from before Hest's time, someone whose name no one remembers. There's a story about a sickness, something that came from outside and burned through the hollow in a week. Three workers died. Two are remembered. One is just a shape beneath the moss.

Death here is not dramatic. It's another kind of settling. The hollow accepts the dead the way it accepts everything—gradually, completely, without urgency. The bodies become substrate. The substrate becomes life. The life becomes work. The work continues.

## The Feel of Them

If you encountered the workers in the hollow—if you descended the switchbacks and stood near the vat-works and let yourself be seen—this is what you would notice:

They move differently than people from outside. Their steps are softer, more deliberate. They've learned that the ground gives, that sound carries strangely, that the environment rewards stillness. When they stand, they stand in ways that don't disturb the air.

Their eyes have adapted to the green-shifted light. In the hollow, they see clearly—every detail of leaf and shadow, every texture of moss and metal. If they go above the rim, they squint. The outside world is too bright, too sharp, too *bare*.

Their skin glistens. Everyone's does here. The humidity is too high for evaporation, so sweat beads and rolls and stays. They don't notice anymore. They don't wipe it away. It's just how skin is.

Their clothes are stained. Green-brown from the distillate, gray from coal dust, white from the chalk they use to mark jugs and doors and shift-changes. The fabric is lightweight—cotton and linen, nothing that traps heat. Most of it has been repaired multiple times. Nothing is thrown away here.

And there's a presence to them. A weight. They have lived in the hollow long enough that the hollow has entered them. When Hest looks at you, you feel looked-at by the *Syngonium* itself—patient, slow, unconcerned with your urgency. When Cadre works the pump, her rhythm matches the creak of the vines, the drip of the condensation, the pulse of something ancient and vegetative.

They are not separate from the place. They are part of its living mass.

---

## The Future

The hollow will continue. The workers will continue. The work will continue.

Eventually, someone will leave. Or die. Or arrive. The numbers will shift. Hest will get older. Her hands will get worse, the joints stiffening, the grip weakening. Someone else will take over the vats—maybe Drys, maybe Tetch, maybe someone who hasn't descended the switchbacks yet.

The *Syngonium* will grow. The brackets will need replacing. The vats will crack and be replaced. The root-mat path will need re-cutting as the vines thicken. The erosion parameter will manifest as stability, and the stability will manifest as slow change, and the slow change will accumulate until someday the hollow is different than it is now.

But not soon. Not in any timeframe that matters to the people who work here, live here, die here, become part of the substrate that becomes the life that becomes the work.

This is the Fern-Gut District. These are its inhabitants. They are patient with their own processes, full of living mass, settled into a hollow that holds them the way it holds everything.
