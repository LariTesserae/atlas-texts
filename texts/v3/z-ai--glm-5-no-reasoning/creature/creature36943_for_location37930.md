The people of the Hollow Quarter do not think of themselves as inhabitants. They think of themselves as *the becoming*. To live here is to be a verb, an ongoing action, a hand extended across centuries to grasp a future you will never touch.

Their bodies tell the story of the place before they speak a single word.

**The Shape of the Work**

They are carved people, unintentionally sculpted by the same forces they wield on the stone. Shoulders broaden early here—the children start carrying baskets as soon as they can walk, and by adolescence, the frame has widened to bear the load. The adults who work the chisels develop arms that seem almost asymmetrical, the hammer-arm thick with muscle and dense bone, the guiding hand lean and precise as a surgeon's. You can read a person's trade by their posture: the carriers walk slightly hunched, protective of the spine; the chiselers stand curved, permanently leaning toward the work; the finishers, those who smooth the dressed stone of the upper tiers, hold themselves straighter, their hands perpetually open, fingers spread as if awaiting the next surface to assess.

Their skin is mineral-stained, dyed long past washing out. Rust from the copper tools leaves traces along the palms and forearms of anyone who grips a chisel. The limestone dust works into the creases of their knuckles, their elbows, the corners of their eyes, leaving them permanently pale-powdered in the folds and crevices while the exposed skin tans in the light columns. They look like the walls they carve—base color varied, but everyone wearing the same patina.

Their lungs hold the mountain. The older ones breathe with a faint whistle, the stone dust having built its own small colonies in the tissue. It doesn't kill them, not quickly, but it marks them. The eldest, a woman named Reave who tends the reservoir on the second tier, breathes like a bellows with a slow leak. She says the stones breathe through her now. She says she'll become a wall when she dies, and she means it as a comfort.

**The Way of Knowing**

They learn the stone before they learn words. A child born in the Hollow Quarter is held to the wall within hours of birth—an old practice, a listening. The parent presses the infant's ear to the raw limestone and waits. What the child hears, or doesn't hear, sets their relationship with the work. Some children grow restless against the wall, pulling away, and these become the carriers, the surface-goers, the traders who bring back food and copper and news. Some children go still, their eyes unfocusing, and these become the readers—the ones who can tap a wall with a chisel and tell you whether it's safe to cut another foot, whether the stone behind it is solid or fractured, whether the mountain is ready to give up this piece or needs to hold it a while longer.

And some children cry when held to the wall, and these become the planners, the chalk-markers, the ones who sketch futures onto surfaces that won't be excavated for three generations. No one explains why the crying makes them suited for this work. It's simply understood: the ones who feel the stone's sorrow are the ones who can imagine what it wants to become.

Knowledge passes through the body. No one writes instructions. The cuts are remembered in the hands, the angles stored in the wrist, the depth of each strike calibrated by a thousand previous strikes. When old Heron teaches his granddaughter to set a chisel, he doesn't tell her where to place it. He puts his hand over hers and moves with her through the strike, again and again, until her body knows the rhythm. Then he steps back. The wall comes away in a clean sheet.

They speak of "reading" the stone, but what they mean is something more intimate than reading. It's a listening that happens through the fingers, through the feet, through the chest. They know when the mountain is about to groan—everyone stops, sets down their tools, moves to the load-bearing walls that have never failed. The groan passes. The work resumes. No one remarks on it.

**The Surface-Goers**

Some of them—perhaps one in ten—spend significant time above. The traders, the haulers, the ones who negotiate with the surface settlements for the food and fiber the Quarter cannot produce. They are recognizable by their squint, their uneasy posture, their habit of glancing upward even when the sky is only stone.

Sola is one of these. She makes the climb three times a month, following the carved steps and handholds up the curved wall to the rim, emerging into daylight that makes her eyes water for the first hour every time. She has a husband above, a stone-mason in the surface town who buys their limestone blocks. They have no children. She returns to the Quarter because the surface makes her feel, as she puts it, "like a word without a sentence."

She means: the work above is small. A building is raised in a year, a wall goes up in a week, a lifetime sees a town rise and fall. But in the Hollow Quarter, the work accumulates. What she carved at fifteen is still there, is *more* there—more shaped, more fitted to the emerging plan—than when she left it. The surface world erodes its own history constantly. The Quarter adds to its history, builds it deeper, carves it larger. She says the difference is the difference between breathing out and breathing in.

The surface-goers bring back copper, which they prize beyond its utility. Copper holds an edge, but not for long. They spend hours resharpening the chisels, bending over the whetstones, the thin scraping sound joining the ambient music of the work. They could request steel from the surface—the traders offer it—but they refuse. Steel holds too well. It forces the cut rather than finding it. The copper bends toward the grain, follows the fracture, collaborates. It's their partner, not their master.

**The Children**

They are born into a project older than language.

The youngest are given small tasks by necessity but large meanings by culture. When a child carries their first basket of rubble to the dump, when they contribute their first removed stone to the growing pile of exported limestone, the family pauses the work. A brief silence. Then acknowledgment: *You've made room*. That's what they say. Not "good work" or "well done." *You've made room*. The space is the gift. The emptiness is the creation.

The children grow up without finished places. Their homes are alcoves in the upper tiers, walls still bearing the rough tool marks of great-grandparents, ceilings that haven't been dressed smooth because there's always another section to cut. They sleep in stone beds carved into the rock, padded with woven mats that the surface-goers bring back. They know the word "room" means something different to surface people. To them, a room is something you reveal. It's already inside the mountain, waiting. You don't build a room; you *find* it.

They play in the scaffolded sections, the half-carved passages, the zones where the chalk marks propose futures. They invent games that require the architecture: climbing the scaffolding, racing through the emerging streets, hiding in alcoves that will someday be someone's home. The older ones are told not to play in the unexcavated areas—not because of danger, but because the mountain hasn't decided what those spaces will be yet, and children running through them might confuse the process. This isn't superstition, exactly. More like courtesy.

They learn their family zones before they learn their own names. *This is our wall. This is our tier. This is our cut.* The territory is defined by the work, by the section of the emerging city that their bloodline has been tasked to reveal. When they introduce themselves, they include where they work. Not what they do—everyone does the same work, more or less—but *where*. *Second tier, north curve, the deepening.* It's as specific as a surname.

**The Old**

When Reave was forty—old by any standard but ancient by the standard of work that would break most bodies by thirty-five—she could no longer swing a hammer for more than an hour without her shoulders locking. The community didn't hesitate. They didn't mourn or pity or accommodate with visible effort. They simply moved her work.

Now she tends the reservoir. The second tier's collection basin is the oldest and deepest, carved by the founders, smoothed by three centuries of water. Reave keeps it clear of debris, checks the channels that guide the weeping into the basin, monitors the water level with a marked pole she carved herself. She makes sure the distribution is fair. She also sits, for long stretches, simply watching the water.

She says she's listening to the mountain drink.

The elderly who can no longer do heavy work become the finishers, the maintainers, the ones who ensure that what has been revealed stays revealed. They dress the walls, smooth the floors, tend the precious gardens where the light columns fall. They are the keepers of the chalk maps—the living archives of what the city is supposed to become, the only ones who know the full plan, who can say whether a particular wall should be cut or whether it's destined to remain as a division between two future rooms.

Their authority is unspoken but total. When old Perrin says a wall is load-bearing, no one questions it. When old Maire rules that a particular section of the third tier should be left to settle for another generation before continuing, the work moves elsewhere. This isn't deference to age. It's deference to the project. The old ones have become part of the architecture. They hold the plan the way the load-bearing walls hold the ceiling. Remove them carelessly and something collapses.

**The Unspoken**

They do not discuss why they are building.

Visitors always ask. *What is this for? Who will live here when it's finished? What's the purpose?*

The inhabitants find these questions unanswerable, not because they don't know, but because the question misunderstands the nature of the work. The Hollow Quarter is not being built *for* something. It is building. The purpose is the becoming. The city will be inhabited by the people who come after, and the people who come after them, and the people who come after them. It has always been so. The founders—whoever they were, and the records are unclear, mixing with myth—began cutting into the stone because there was a city inside it that needed to emerge.

Needed to. That's the phrase they use. Not "wanted to" or "was useful to." *Needed to.* The city needed to exist. The mountain was full of it, pressing to get out, and someone had to help it.

This is not religion. They have no rites, no prayers, no offerings. They do not worship the stone. They work with it, and working with a thing for generations creates a relationship that doesn't require the supernatural. The stone is not god. The stone is *partner*.

They do have funerals, though. When someone dies, they are carried to the surface and buried in the thin soil above the rim, where roots can reach them. This is practical—the Quarter has no soil to spare for graves—but it's also symbolic. They return to the surface so they can become part of the cycle that eventually becomes rain that eventually becomes the weeping that eventually fills the basins. They come back as water. They spend their lives carving space from stone, and then they become the moisture that fills the space. The work remembers them as dampness.

**The Way They Speak**

Their language has not drifted far from the surface tongue—trade requires communication—but it has developed its own rhythm, its own preferences. They speak in long silences punctuated by short statements. They don't fill space with sound the way surface people do; silence is their natural medium, the pause between hammer strikes, the held breath before the chisel sets.

They have words that don't translate precisely. *Room* is one—already discussed. Another is *emerge-time*, which means something like "the span during which a revealed space becomes truly itself," the settling period after the cutting stops, when the stone adjusts to its new shape. A room isn't finished when the workers stop cutting; it's finished when it's gone through emerge-time and come out the other side as itself.

Their most untranslatable word is *hollowing*. It sounds like the word for "work," but with an extra syllable added at the throat, a vibration that comes from the chest. It means the work, and the purpose, and the becoming, and the people doing it, and the space they're making, all at once. When Reave says *the hollowing continues*, she isn't talking about the excavation. She is talking about the entire process of their existence: the cutting, the weeping, the growing, the dying, the rising of the tiers and the deepening of the floors and the slow creep of the lichen and the patience of the gardens and the children learning the grain of the stone and the old ones tending the water and the surface-goers returning with their squinting eyes and their copper and their news of a world that seems, to those who stay below, impossibly fast and impossibly thin—a world that builds and builds and never becomes.

**The Visitors**

They come occasionally—scholars, traders, the curious, the lost. The Hollow Quarter tolerates them with distant courtesy. They are housed in the most finished sections, fed from the precious gardens and the stored provisions, and left alone to wander the emerging streets.

Most leave unsettled. They can't name what disturbs them. The space is vast and careful and clearly the product of tremendous labor, but something about it feels—I will try once more to say it—*unconcluded*. As if the visitors have walked into a sentence that hasn't found its period. As if they're standing in a breath, an inhalation that has been going on for three hundred years and will go on for three hundred more.

The inhabitants understand this reaction. They sometimes feel it themselves, a restlessness, a sense that the work will never be done, that they are always in the middle of a process that has no edge. But when they feel it, they don't find it disturbing. They find it *true*. Of course they're in the middle. The middle is where the work happens. The middle is where they live.

They live in the hollowing. They are the hollowing. And the hollowing continues.
