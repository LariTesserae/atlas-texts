# The Tender

## Who Lives Here

He comes when the eastern pump begins to labor. He knows the sound of each tower—has learned their voices the way a parent knows a child's cry in another room. The eastern pump, third from the ridge, has a particular shudder in its fourth year of service, a hesitation on the upstroke that the others do not share. He hears it from two kilometers away, through the damp Clay, before he even reaches the switchback.

He is not young. He has not been young for a long time. His hands are permanently pruned, the skin of a man who has lived his life underwater without ever submerging. The creases in his face hold moisture. When he speaks—and he speaks aloud sometimes, though there is no one to hear—his voice has a wet quality, a denseness that the air gives to everything here.

His name is not important. He has not spoken his name in eleven years. He thinks of himself as the Tender, when he thinks of himself at all.

---

## His History

He came here before the reed corridors were walls. He remembers when they were suggestions—thin lines of growth, knee-high, like cues for where to walk. He remembers helping them. He bent the young reeds toward each other, wove the tips together with twine, taught them the shapes he wanted. He does not know if the plants learned or if he simply accelerated what they were already doing. He does not know if plants can learn. He does not know if it matters.

He was sent here. The word *sent* is not quite right—he was *assigned*, *stationed*, *installed*. A word that implies a permanence he did not, at the time, expect. He was an engineer. He understood hydraulics, condensation, the mechanics of slow systems. The Condensation Gardens were old even then, built by people whose names are now only in ledgers he cannot read. They needed someone to tend them. He was someone.

He thought it would be temporary. He thought he would maintain the pumps, document the processes, and return. He does not remember when he stopped thinking about returning. It was not a decision. It was a settling, like sediment in still water.

---

## His Work

The ledger in the shed is one of hundreds. The earlier ones—those he found when he arrived—are in a language he does not speak. He keeps them anyway. They line the walls of his dwelling, which is not the shed but something he built himself, twelve years ago, in a reed chamber three turns from the central ring.

His dwelling is made of materials that arrived with the basin. Wood that was shipped in, metal salvaged from replaced tower panels, moss he transplanted to form a living roof. It leaks. Everything leaks here. He has made his peace with wetness.

The work is this:

He walks the corridors. He checks each pump's cycle, feeling for hesitations, for irregularities in the stroke. He clears the condensation mesh when it gets clogged with spores or insect debris. He replaces corroded rivets, patches leaking gutters, adjusts the tension on the piston springs. He monitors the moss depth around the tower bases—too thick and it will grow into the mechanisms; too thin and the insulation fails, letting the collected water freeze in winter.

He works slowly. There is no reason to hurry. The pumps cycle once every three seconds; a half-day of hesitation in one of them will not collapse the system. He can hear the problem, walk back to his dwelling, sleep, and return in the morning. The Gardens are patient. They teach patience.

---

## His Body

He moves differently than he used to. He has developed a gait for the moss floor—a kind of slow, rolling step that minimizes sinking. He does not hurry. Hurrying tears the moss; it leaves wounds that take months to heal. He has learned to move at the speed of the landscape.

His hearing has changed. The constant sound has damaged him, but it has also trained him. He can no longer hear certain high frequencies—birdsong, if there were birds, would be lost to him. But he can hear variations in the pump-hum that no visitor could detect. He knows each tower's voice, each slight deviation in rhythm, each change in pitch that signals a problem developing. He can be half a kilometer away and hear when a gutter overflows.

His skin is always wet. This is not uncomfortable anymore. He has forgotten what it feels like to be dry. When he leaves the basin—once a year, to collect supplies from a town half a day's walk away—the dryness of the air feels wrong, feels like a kind of violence against his body. He returns as quickly as he can.

---

## His Mind

He thinks in terms of water.

He thinks about where it comes from—the air, the condensation, the slow accumulation of moisture on surfaces. He thinks about where it goes—down through the pipes, into the rock, somewhere he has never seen. He does not know where the water goes. He has walked the basin's edges, searched for an outflow, a stream, a spring where the collected water emerges. He has found nothing. The water goes somewhere; the pipes ensure that; but its destination is beyond his knowing.

He does not need to know. His job is the collection, not the use.

He thinks about the plants. He has watched the reed corridors grow from suggestions into walls into labyrinth. He has watched the moss thicken, the ferns spread, the pitcher plants multiply. He does not tend the plants. They do not need him. But he watches them. He documents their growth in his ledger, in his own language, in a handwriting that has become looser and wetter over the years.

He thinks about time. He has been here long enough to see changes—not in the pumps, which cycle the same as they always have, but in the landscape. The ridges have not changed; they are still raw, still bone-colored. But the basin floor has risen. The moss has grown deeper. The reed walls have thickened. He is walking on accreted time, on years of compressed growth, and somewhere beneath his feet the raw stone is slowly becoming something else.

---

## His Silence

He speaks aloud sometimes. He reads the ledger entries to himself, a habit of documentation, of confirming that the words are there. He names the things he sees: *pump*, *gutter*, *reed*, *moss*. He narrates his work: *clearing the eastern mesh*, *replacing the fourth-year spring*. He does not know why he does this. It is not for company. It is not for record-keeping; the written ledger suffices. Perhaps it is to confirm that language still exists, that he still has access to it, that the sound of his own voice has not been entirely swallowed by the roar.

He has not had a conversation in eleven years. The last person he spoke to was the supply-wagon driver who brought the annual shipment of rivets, mesh, and preserved food. The driver asked him how the work was going. He said it was going. The driver asked if he needed anything else. He said he did not. The driver left. He has not seen another face since.

He does not know if this is lonely. He has forgotten what loneliness feels like. The absence of people is like the absence of dryness—a condition, not a feeling. He is surrounded by life. The moss is alive. The reeds are alive. The water is alive with microorganisms he cannot see but knows are there. The pumps are alive in their mechanical way, cycling, breathing, consuming and producing. He is not alone. He is simply the only one of his kind.

---

## His Knowledge

He knows things about this place that no one else knows.

He knows that the western pump has a fault in its pressure valve that will fail in approximately three years. He has ordered a replacement part, but it will take time to arrive, and he is not worried. He will manage. He always manages.

He knows that the reed corridors are expanding northward, toward the ridge. He has documented their progress: twelve centimeters per year, steady, unvarying. At this rate, they will reach the ridge in roughly two thousand years. He will not see it. Someone might. Or no one might. The reeds do not care.

He knows that the moss carpet is slowly transforming the stone beneath it. He has pulled back sections over the years—always in the same place, a test plot near his dwelling—and watched as the raw, sharp bedrock has softened, its edges rounding, a thin layer of organic material accumulating on its surface. The moss is making soil. It will take centuries to make enough to support larger plants. But it has begun. He is watching geology happen at the speed of life.

He knows that the pitcher plants are failing. They catch almost nothing—occasional midges, once a small moth—and their traps are mostly water. They are not reproducing. They are not dying. They are simply persisting, in a state of minimal existence, waiting for something that may never come. He does not tend them. They are outside his responsibility. But he watches them. He wonders if they will outlive him.

---

## His Night

He sleeps when the pumps sleep—which is to say, he sleeps when his body requires it, without regard to the sun. The pumps do not sleep. They cycle constantly, continuously, their rhythm forming the backdrop to his rest.

His dwelling is dark at night. He does not light lamps. The light here is already too bright during the day; his eyes crave darkness. He lies on his bed—which is a mat of dried reeds over a frame of salvaged metal—and listens to the sound.

The sound at night is different. The condensation changes quality, becomes heavier, more deliberate. The dripping intensifies. The pumps seem louder, or perhaps he is simply more aware of them in the absence of visual distraction. He has learned to hear the sound as silence—a silence that happens to be loud. His mind quiets around it. He sleeps.

He dreams of dry places. He does not know why. He has never been to a desert. He has never seen a landscape without water. But in his dreams, he walks across cracked earth, across salt flats, across stone that does not yield to his step. He wakes with his skin damp and his mouth tasting of minerals.

---

## His Future

He does not know how long he will stay. He does not know if *stay* is the right word. He is here. The word implies a choice, and he does not feel that he has chosen. He was placed here, and he has remained, and the remaining has become a condition rather than a decision.

He thinks sometimes about the water—the water that goes somewhere he cannot see. He thinks about the purpose of the Condensation Gardens. Someone built them. Someone designed the towers, the mesh, the pumps, the pipe system. Someone decided that this basin needed to collect water and send it somewhere else. He does not know who. He does not know why. He is downstream of the question.

He thinks sometimes about what will happen when he can no longer tend the pumps. He is not young. His hands are growing stiffer; his knees, noisier. There will come a day when he cannot make the walk from the ridge, cannot lift the replacement panels, cannot clear the mesh. He does not know what will happen then.

Perhaps nothing. Perhaps the Gardens will run without him for years—decades—until the problems he would have caught become failures, and the failures cascade, and the system breaks down. Perhaps the reed corridors will continue to grow, will eventually cover the towers themselves, will pull them down into the moss. Perhaps someone else will come—another engineer, another assignment—and take his place. He does not know. He will not be here to see it.

---

## His Moment

This is what he does today:

He wakes. The sound is the same as always. The light is white and surgical. His skin is wet.

He checks the ledger. The most recent entry is from yesterday—a note about the eastern pump's hesitation, a measurement of moss depth, an observation about a new fern frond near the northern chamber.

He dresses. His clothes are loose, woven from reed fibers he has learned to process. They are green with age and moisture. They do not dry.

He walks the corridors. His steps are slow, rolling, careful. The moss yields and springs back behind him.

He reaches the eastern tower. He places his hand on the pump housing and feels the shudder—the hesitation on the upstroke, the slight catch before the descent. He knows this problem. He has the parts to fix it. He will fix it tomorrow. Today he is only observing.

He stands in the reed chamber and listens. The sound is deafening. It is also home.

He does not think about whether he is happy. Happiness is not a category that applies here. He is present. He is tending. The system is running. The water is moving. The moss is growing. The light is bright. The sound is loud.

He is the Tender. He tends.
