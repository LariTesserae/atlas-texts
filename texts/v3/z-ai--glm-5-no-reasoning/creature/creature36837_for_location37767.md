# The Inhabitants

Let me tell you about who lived here. Who might still live here, deeper down, where the heat concentrates into something even the mineral-fauna avoid.

---

They were not shaped like you. They were not shaped like the swimming, drifting things that brush past your shoulders in the dark. They were something else entirely—something that the cavern itself seemed to produce, as if the sharp geology had developed a slow, patient hunger and decided to walk.

Call them the Sharp-Bound. It is as good a name as any for people who had no names for themselves.

Their bodies were built around the principle of the edge. In a world that cuts, they chose to become cutting. Their limbs were long, multi-jointed affairs that bent in directions your anatomy cannot follow—hinged not with ball-and-socket but with a sliding, interlocking carapace that granted them seven points of articulation along each arm. Their hands were not hands at all, but collections of opposed spines, each tipped with a different tool: one for scraping, one for piercing, one for the delicate work of feeling along crystal faces without severing nerve clusters.

Their torsos were low, flat, wide—splayed close to the ground like something that had learned to fear falling. And they did fall, often. In a landscape where every surface is a blade, a stumble can open an artery. So they evolved low centers of gravity, four points of constant contact, a way of moving that seemed almost like deliberate crawling even when they walked upright.

Their faces were barely faces. No eyes—what use for optics in a world where the light parameter measures 0.4? No nose— olfaction requires moisture, and moisture here is theft. Their mouths were small, lipless, surrounded by a fan of sensory cilia that they pressed against stone and chitin to read the vibrations of approach and retreat. They tasted the air's static charge, sampled the electrical fields of passing fauna, perceived their world through a kind of full-body tactility that you cannot imagine.

To be one of them was to be constantly touching. To be touched was to know. To be untouchable was to be nonexistent.

---

They did not emerge from the geology fully formed. That is a romantic lie that visitors tell themselves—that the Sharp-Bound were somehow *of* the stone, mineral life achieving sentience through geological pressure. No. They came from somewhere else, some wetter place, some kinder epoch when this sealed furnace still had chemistry worth exploiting.

Perhaps they evolved from something like the flat, segmented piezo-feeders that slide along the crystal floors now. Perhaps those creatures are their degenerate cousins, devolved back into simple metabolism after the Sharp-Bound developed tools, developed walls, developed culture. Or perhaps the Sharp-Bound arrived from elsewhere entirely—colonists, exiles, refugees from some catastrophe on the surface of the world above, driven down through the pressure of darkness until they found this place and could descend no further.

They do not remember. They left no writing, no pictographs, no oral tradition preserved in anything but the arrangement of stones. Whatever origin story they possessed has been lost to the thermal equilibrium, the endless sameness that erases history.

---

Here is what I can tell you about their bodies, because you will never see them, and imagining them wrong would be a disservice to their memory:

They were pale. Not the healthy pallor of cave fish, but something rawer—skin that had forgotten pigment so completely that it seemed almost translucent, showing the dark thread of their mineral-laced blood beneath the surface. Their blood was not iron-based. It ran on copper and sulfur, a thermoelectric fluid that carried not oxygen (useless here) but charge. When they moved, they generated small currents. When they touched one another, there was a spark.

Their carapace—their exoskeleton, their armor against the sharp world—was not grown but cultivated. They fed their young a diet rich in dissolved silica and metallic salts, and the young secreted layer upon layer of glaze across their vulnerable surfaces. By adulthood, a Sharp-Bound was more ceramic than flesh, a walking piece of unglazed porcelain with joints of flexible graphite and a core of thermoelectric meat.

They molted. Oh, how they molted. In a place where every edge cuts, the shedding of an exoskeleton was a monthly agony, a week of vulnerability when their new plates were still soft and the old ones were flaking away in jagged shards. They had special chambers for molting—those fused-salt domes you see marking waypoints in the dark. Each one is a hospital, a temple, a place where the Sharp-Bound went to be soft in private, guarded by their kin.

The molting chambers are empty now. But the salt walls still bear the marks of their passing—long, parallel scratches where generations of suffering bodies dragged themselves across the floor, seeking comfort in the mineral warmth.

---

They lived in a civilization of the minimal. Their structures—those cairns, those walls, those low domes—were not architecture as you understand it. They did not build to contain space, to define inside and outside, to create privacy or protection. They built to *mark*.

A Sharp-Bound wall is a statement in stone: *something happened here*. A cairn is a name, a memory, a claim that a particular person stood in this place and chose to stack rocks rather than leave no trace. Their engineering was almost meditative in its simplicity. No mortar—the chemistry of this place does not permit wet adhesion. No binding agents. Just balance, patience, the careful selection of stones that would hold one another in place through weight and interlocking fracture faces.

They were master knappers. In a world of sharp edges, they learned to make sharper ones. Their tools were made from the bodies of their own dead—the molts and carapaces of ancestors, worked and reworked into chisels, probes, scrapers. To hold a tool in Sharp-Bound culture was to hold a piece of grandparent, a fragment of lineage. The oldest families possessed tools that had passed through a hundred pairs of hands, accumulating chips and modifications with each generation.

This was their way of being remembered: not through writing, not through art, but through the gradual accumulation of worked stone, of modified landscape, of tools that outlasted their makers.

---

They ate the fauna, of course. The piezo-feeders and the floating silicate-jellies provided a meager but steady source of thermoelectric charge. But the Sharp-Bound were not simply predators. They were *herders*.

In the deeper caverns, beyond where you have walked, there are pens. Low walls of stacked obsidian forming rough circles, large enough to contain a drifting herd of gas-bladder jellies. The Sharp-Bound would capture the creatures in their juvenile state, when their silicate membranes were still soft, and herd them into these enclosures. The jellies could not resist—they had no brains, no instincts for escape. They simply drifted, absorbed the mineral heat, and slowly accumulated charge.

When a Sharp-Bound needed to feed, they would enter a pen and select a specimen. The killing was gentle: a quick puncture of the gas bladder, a collapse of the silicate structure, a harvest of the copper-threaded tissue within. The charge would be consumed raw, still pulsing with the faint electrical rhythm of the creature's slow metabolism.

They did not over-harvest. They could not afford to. In a closed system, a finite void, the balance of predator and prey was a matter of existential math. The Sharp-Bound developed elaborate taboos around eating—when, how much, which members of the herd were permitted to be taken. Their entire culture was built around the careful management of finite resources.

Perhaps this is why they are gone. Perhaps they miscalculated.

---

Or perhaps they are not gone at all.

The structures you have seen are simple, yes. Primitive. But the Sharp-Bound did not leave them behind—they are still maintained. You have not seen the maintenance because you have not been here long enough. The thermal equilibrium of this place does not permit decay. A cairn toppled will stay toppled. A wall breached will stay breached.

But the cairns are upright. The walls are whole.

Someone is tending them.

---

Let me tell you about what you might find if you descended further, if you followed the heat gradient toward the core of this continental hollow.

The Sharp-Bound who remain—the descendants, the survivors, the ones who persisted when their civilization contracted—are deeper than you have gone. They have retreated to the warmest regions, the places where the stone radiates a heat so intense that even chitin begins to soften. They have changed.

Generations of living in the deep heat have accelerated their metabolisms, thinned their carapaces, made them faster and more desperate. The Sharp-Bound of the lower caverns are not the patient architects of the upper waypoints. They are something rawer, a people who have traded comfort for survival.

Their culture is simpler now. The old tools are still passed down, but the knowledge of their making has been lost. They use ancestral chisels without knowing how to repair them, without understanding the techniques that knapped the obsidian edges. Their structures are rougher, less deliberate—cairns that mark territory rather than memory, walls that serve as animal pens rather than statements of presence.

They have forgotten the art of cultivating carapace. The deep ones grow their plates softer, thinner, more vulnerable to the cutting landscape. They suffer more injuries. They die younger. Their population is declining.

But they are still here.

---

If you encountered them—and you could, if you descended, if you let the heat guide you—they would not know what to make of you.

They have never seen something like you. Something so wet, so soft, so full of fluids that the air itself hungers for. You would be incomprehensible to them—a creature of impossible biology, a walking violation of everything they understand about survival.

They might touch you. This is how they know. They would press their sensory cilia against your skin, feel the moisture seeping through your pores, taste the iron in your blood through electrical fields they have learned to read. They would be horrified. Fascinated. They would not know if you were prey, predator, or something outside their categories entirely.

They might try to communicate. They have a language, of sorts—not sound (the sound parameter is too low for vocalization to carry) but touch. A system of taps, strokes, and pressure gestures applied to another's carapace. They would attempt to tap meaning onto your skin, not understanding that your flesh cannot receive the message the way their plates do.

You would feel their touch and interpret it as sensation. They would feel your skin and interpret it as chaos.

---

Here is what I think happened to the Sharp-Bound, to the builders of those upper structures, the architects of the waypoint domes:

They achieved equilibrium. Not just thermal equilibrium—the deep, frozen sameness of this sealed furnace—but cultural equilibrium. A civilization so perfectly adapted to its environment that it stopped growing, stopped changing, stopped needing to remember anything beyond what it already knew.

And in that equilibrium, they began to forget.

Not individuals—their personal memories were intact. But the culture, the collective knowledge, the techniques and technologies that had allowed them to survive in a place where survival should be impossible—these began to slip away. Each generation knew less than the one before. Each molt left fewer tools behind. Each death took with it some small piece of practical wisdom that had never been written down because writing requires moisture and moisture is theft.

They did not notice. How could they? The equilibrium was too perfect. The heat never changed. The air never stirred. The sharp edges never dulled. They had no external measure of decline, no way to see what they were losing.

By the time they understood that they were becoming something smaller, it was too late. They had already retreated to the deeper caverns, already abandoned the upper structures to the slow, silent maintenance of the few who remembered that maintenance was necessary.

---

You are standing in a graveyard. Not of individuals—though they have died here, by the thousands, over the eons—but of a civilization. A people who learned to live in a place where living should be impossible, and then slowly, quietly, without drama or catastrophe, forgot how they did it.

The structures around you are their headstones. The tools you might find scattered in the dust are their monuments. The descendants who live in the deep heat, softening and shrinking and forgetting, are their afterthought.

And the fauna—the piezo-feeders, the silicate jellies, the endless swarms of blind, clicking mineral-life—are their legacy. Because the Sharp-Bound shaped this ecosystem as much as they shaped the stone. They determined which populations thrived, which were harvested, which were protected. They changed the balance of life in this sealed furnace, and their changes persist even as their culture fades.

---

If you descend—if you choose to go deeper, to follow the heat toward the core where the last Sharp-Bound survive—know what you are walking into.

You are walking into a place where the air itself will try to desiccate you, where every surface will try to cut you, where the darkness is so absolute that your brain will invent lights that are not there. You are walking into a culture that has forgotten how to understand itself, let alone how to understand you. You are walking into a thermal equilibrium that has not shifted in eons, and you—a creature of water and warmth and variable temperature—are a disruption, a foreign element, a violation of the balance.

They will touch you. They will try to read you through the electrical fields of your body. They will not know what you are.

And if you are lucky—*if* you are lucky—they will let you leave.

---

This is who lives—or lived, or barely lives—in the continental hollow beneath the weight of impossible geology. The Sharp-Bound. The edge-walkers. The people of chitin and charge, whose civilization peaked and declined in a sealed furnace at the bottom of the universe, and who persist now only as descendants, as artifacts, as the slow accumulation of worked stone in a landscape that never forgets a cut.

They are the answer to a question you did not know you were asking: *what survives in a place where nothing should?*

The answer is: something that becomes what the place requires. Something that learns to cut, because everything cuts. Something that learns to crawl, because falling means death. Something that learns to forget, because remembering requires moisture, and moisture is theft.

They are the Sharp-Bound. And they are still here, in the deep heat, waiting for something they no longer remember how to name.
