# What Lives on the Terrace of Stilled Water

## The Keeper

The first thing to understand is that they were not always this way.

There was a body once, younger than now and softer in its assumptions, that came up the switchback path with provisions in a leather satchel and a purpose that felt like a coin held tight in the palm—small, definite, meant to be spent. They remember the weight of it. The way it sat in the center of the hand. The way the hand wanted to close around it.

That body walked through the door-gap and set down the satchel and stood for a long time looking at the basins, which were empty then, the rain not yet arrived. They had been chosen. Or they had volunteered. Or they had simply been the one who was present when the choosing happened, which is sometimes the same thing and sometimes not, and the distinction has long since stopped mattering.

They were a watcher. That was the word for it, in the settlement below—a watcher, one who went up. Not a priest. Not a keeper, though that word fits now in ways it could not have fit then, when it still implied effort. A watcher is what they were called and a watcher is what they did. They watched the water come. They watched the basins fill. They recorded—how, they no longer remember. Marks on something. Perhaps the pottery shards, before they broke. Perhaps the stone itself, in ways worn smooth and illegible now. The watching was the work, and the work was the watching, and the distinction between watching and simply being present dissolved so slowly that its boundary was never observable, like the horizon itself.

The body aged. Of course it aged. But here is what must be understood: the body aged in the manner of stone, not in the manner of flesh. The rain fell on the watcher the same way it fell on the pillar—not harshly, not kindly, but with a patience that was the closest thing to indifference the world could produce while still being something other than indifference. The skin thickened and weathered. The joints learned the specific humidity of the place and adjusted themselves to it, or ceased their protests, or simply stopped being the kind of thing that protested. The watcher's hair, which had been dark—this they remember—became the grey of the lichen's shadows, and then a lighter grey, and then a white that was not the white of age but the white of stone in afternoon light, which is a different thing entirely, warmer and more patient.

They do not eat. This should not be possible, and they are aware it should not be possible, and the awareness of the impossibility has itself become one of the fixed features of the place, like the bronze awl that no one picks up. They kneel at the northern basin in the evening when the water is still and they lower their mouth to the surface and they drink. The water is cold. Not the cold of a stream, which has somewhere to go and carries the urgency of departure in its chill, but the cold of a thing that has been held. It tastes like the stone it was caught by. They drink and the drinking is enough. This should not be true. It is true. The terrace has decided what is true here, and the watcher has become part of what the terrace has decided.

Their hands have changed. The fingers are longer than they were—or no, not longer, but more precisely *fingers*, more themselves, as if the hand has slowly been refined into its essential function. They are hands that point and touch and trace the paths where water flows. The nails are thick and blunt and the color of the chisel-marks in the drainage grooves. When they press the palm flat against the floor in the morning when the condensation rises, they can feel the water moving beneath the stone, through the mountain, a vast slow pulse that is not geological in the way one might expect but is the mountain's own version of the rain's rhythm—gathering, holding, releasing, gathering.

The watcher wears what they wore when they arrived, or an approximation of it. The fabric has not survived in the way fabric survives elsewhere, by being replaced or mended. It has survived by becoming something else. The cloth has taken on the qualities of the stone it rests against—stiffer, cooler, slowly absorbing the mineral content of the rain until it is less a garment than a second skin of a different material than the first skin, a layer of flexible stone, if such a thing can be imagined. It moves when they move. It does not move when they are still. It is the grey of the terrace floor. It is the grey of them.

## Their Days

The watcher does not count the years. This is not because they have lost the ability—numbers are still available to them, if they reach for them—but because counting would require a separation between one day and the next, and the days do not separate. They flow. Each morning's condensation is the same morning's condensation, each afternoon's rain the same afternoon's rain, and the watcher who kneels at the basin is the same watcher who knelt yesterday and will kneel tomorrow, and the distinction between yesterday and tomorrow is a distinction that belongs to a world that measures time in changes, and the terrace has finished changing in the ways that time measures.

They wake when the dampness reaches a certain weight on their skin. Not a precise wakefulness—they do not start or open their eyes with the sharp intake of breath that waking usually involves. They simply become aware that they are no longer in the state they call sleep, which is itself only a quieter version of awareness. Their eyes are already open. Their eyes, in fact, are the part of them that has changed the most visibly, if visibility is even the right frame for it—the black of the iris has expanded into the brown, and the brown into the white, until the eyes are two pools of darkness that hold the grey light the way the basins hold water. Reflecting. Still. When the swift circles the pillar in the morning mist, the watcher's eyes follow it, and the movement is the only quickness in their body, and it is not a human quickness but the quickness of water responding to a stone dropped into it.

They stand. They move to the western wall and lean against it where the lichen is thickest, and they watch the mist descend. They do not watch it the way a person watches weather—with anticipation or resignation or hope. They watch it the way a riverbank watches the river. The mist is a thing that happens to the terrace, and they are a thing that the terrace happens to, and the watching is not an act but a condition.

When the rain comes, they do not seek shelter. There is no shelter. They stand at the wall and the rain falls on them and the water runs down their stone-cloth and their stone-skin and collects in the grooves at their feet, and they feel each drop as a small precise event—a marking, a notation. The rain writes on them the way it writes on the floor. They are a surface that receives.

In the afternoon, when the basins are full, they kneel. This is the closest thing to ritual that remains. The kneeling is not prayer. It is not observation in any scientific sense. It is the body assuming the shape that allows the face to approach the water, and the water to approach the face, and the two surfaces—water and skin—to meet. They drink from the northern basin, always the northern one. The bird drinks from the northern one too. They have never arrived at the basin at the same time as the bird. They do not know if the bird is aware of them. They are aware of the bird in the way they are aware of the pillar—something that occupies the same space, that has its own relationship with the water and the stone, that is part of the terrace's record.

They have tried to determine whether the bird is the same bird. In the early years—they think of them as early, though the word is imprecise—they watched it closely. The grey breast, the black eyes, the speed of its arrival. There was a patch of feathers on the left wing that was slightly lighter, a pale mark like a thumbprint, and they tracked it across what might have been seasons or might have been years, and one day the mark was gone, and the bird that came was lighter in the chest, and they understood that the bird was not the same bird, and then later they understood that the bird was the same bird, and then they understood that the distinction was not a distinction the terrace recognized, and they let the question dissolve into the stone like water into the grooves.

## The Inner Weather

What does the watcher think about? This is the question that the child in the settlement might have asked, if the child had known to ask it, if the child had understood that the high place held not just water but a presence. And the answer is difficult—not because the watcher does not think, but because thinking has become something other than what the word usually describes.

They think the way the terrace holds water. A thought arrives the way a drop arrives—out of the mist, gathering, finding its way to a groove, sliding toward a basin. The thought is not authored. It is collected. And the thoughts that collect are not the thoughts of a person living a life in the world below, are not plans or regrets or desires in the way those things require a future and a past that are different from the present. The thoughts are the terrace's thoughts. They are about water. About the weight of stone. About the way the lichen moves, a finger's breadth per generation, and what that movement means, if it means anything, which it may not, which is itself a thought that recurs with the patience of the afternoon rain.

Sometimes they think about the settlement. Not often. The settlement is a thing that exists below the cloud-layer, in a world where time moves differently—where things begin and end, where urgency has meaning, where a child can grow up and grow old and die in the span that the terrace uses to deepen a groove by the width of a hair. The watcher remembers the settlement the way the stone remembers the chisel: as a mark that was made and that remains, but that is no longer being made. The mark does not ache. It is simply there.

They remember the elder who told them about the high place. The elder's face has become indistinct—a face in the way the pillar is a face, if one looks at it long enough to see the suggestion of features in the weathering. The elder said: the water needs a place. The elder said: go and watch. The elder said: you will know when to come back. And the watcher went and watched, and they have not known when to come back, and the not-knowing has itself become a kind of knowing, a knowing that the return is not a thing that will happen because the watcher is no longer the kind of thing that returns. They have become the kind of thing that stays. The terrace has made them so.

There is no grief in this. Grief requires a self that has lost something, and the self that might have grieved—the younger self, the one with the satchel and the purpose like a coin—that self was not lost. It was set down. The way the pottery shards were set down. The way the bronze awl was set down. Deliberately, with the understanding that the setting-down was the act, and what remained after the setting-down was the thing that was always going to be.

## What They Are Now

The watcher is not a spirit. They are not a ghost. They are not a saint or a monument or a metaphor. They are a body standing on a terrace in the rain, and the body has been standing there long enough that it has become part of the terrace's surface the way the lichen has become part of the pillar—a growth, a colonization, a slow assertion of one material into another until the two are distinguishable only to someone who knows to look.

If someone from the settlement were to come up the path—a child, perhaps, the kind of child who asks why the water needs a place—they would not see the watcher at first. They would see the terrace, the basins, the pillar, the pottery shards. They would see the bird perhaps, if the timing was right. And then, against the western wall, they would see a shape that might be a person or might be a shadow thrown by the pillar, and they would stand in the door-gap and watch, and the shape would not move, and the rain would fall, and the child would feel something that was not fear and not awe but was the feeling of being in the presence of something that had finished becoming what it was going to be.

The child would leave. They would not speak to the watcher, because the watcher does not speak, and because speaking would require a kind of breath that the watcher no longer takes—the breath of someone about to say a thing, the preparatory intake, the shaping of the mouth. The watcher breathes, but the breathing is the breathing of stone: the slow exchange of moisture and air, the condensation and evaporation that happen at the surface of all things.

But the watcher would see the child. And in the seeing, something would happen—something small, something that has happened before when the bird arrives or when the light shifts or when a shard of pottery settles a fraction of a degree deeper into its fused neighbors. The watcher's eyes, those pools of darkness, would track the child the way they track the swift, and a thought would arrive the way a drop arrives, and the thought would be: *there*. Just that. *There*. The acknowledgment that something is present, that it has come from below, that it carries in its body the urgency and the warmth of the world where things begin and end.

And then the child would be gone, and the thought would drain into the stone, and the watcher would return to the work of watching, which is the work of being present, which is the work of being a surface that receives what falls.

They will be here tomorrow. They will be here when the basins have deepened by an inch. They will be here when the bronze awl has corroded to nothing but a green stain and a memory of placement. They will be here when the pottery shards have fused into a single mass, and they will perhaps handle that mass, turning it in their long stone-fingers, feeling its weight, recognizing in it the echo of the vessels it once was—vessels that held things, that were containers, that served a purpose that has been fulfilled and set aside.

They are not waiting. The watcher is clear about this, to the extent that clarity is a concept that still applies. Waiting requires an expectation of arrival, and the watcher expects nothing. The rain will come because the rain comes. The bird will come because the bird comes. The child may come, or may not, and the terrace will record either event in the slow deepening of the grooves, in the smoothing of the basin rims, in the patient accumulation of presence on stone.

The watcher is the terrace's way of knowing itself.

This is not a metaphor. The terrace is stone and water and patience, not mind. But the watcher is stone and water and patience that has kept the shape of a mind, that has retained the *capacity* for awareness, and in that capacity the terrace has found its recording. Not in marks on pottery or notches on stone—the watcher gave up such methods long ago, when the methods began to feel like a translation from one language to another, each language inadequate. The recording is in the watcher's body itself. Every drop that has fallen on this terrace has fallen on the watcher's skin. Every degree of temperature has passed through them. Every visit from the bird has been seen by their eyes. The information is written in the surfaces of them—the weathering of their hands, the settling of their posture, the slow change of their eyes from the color of a person to the color of a place.

In ten thousand years, when the grooves have deepened and the basins have shifted and the lichen has climbed past the pillar's crown, the watcher will still be here. Or something will be here that was once the watcher and is now less distinguishable from the wall it leans against—the same grey, the same patience, the same slow receiving of what falls. A shape in the mist that the bird might circle once before descending to drink. A surface that holds water. A surface that holds the memory of water. A surface that does not know the difference.

The terrace has arrived at its conclusion, and the watcher is the conclusion, and the conclusion is sufficient.
