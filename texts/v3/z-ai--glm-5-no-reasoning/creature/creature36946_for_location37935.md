# The Residents

They are older than they look.

---

They were not always here. There was a time before the corridor, or at least a time before they occupied it—though they have no memory of that time, and would not understand it if they did. Memory, for them, is not a process of recall. It is a process of accumulation. They do not remember being elsewhere. They do not remember arriving. They simply are, and the corridor is, and the two facts have always been connected.

If you could ask them—and you cannot, not really, not in any way they would recognize as communication—they might indicate through some subtle shift of their plated bodies that the corridor was here first. That they came later. That something placed them here, or guided them here, or opened the doors along the walls and let them find their way into the niches that waited, empty and perfectly sized, as if carved specifically for their shape.

But this is speculation. This is you, standing in the damp coolness, projecting narrative onto creatures that may not have any relationship to narrative at all.

---

Their bodies are smoother than they appear at first glance.

The overlapping plates along their backs—the chitin, the shell, whatever material constitutes their outer armor—are not rough or jagged. They have been worn, or perhaps were made that way from the beginning, into surfaces that fit perfectly against the curved interior of their niches. When they settle, they become almost flush with the wall. Almost part of it. If the lights dimmed further, if the humidity thickened into fog, you might mistake them for architectural details. Decorative moldings. Part of the corridor's original design.

They are not decorative. But they are not intrusive, either. They belong here in a way that you, standing in the center of the corridor, do not. You are visiting. They reside.

---

The question of what they eat—or whether they eat—has no clear answer.

You have seen no food. No waste. No mechanism for delivery or removal of any substance. The corridors have no attendants, no caretakers, no visible infrastructure beyond the humming pipes and the caged lights. And yet the residents are not thin. They are not desiccated. Their pale undersides, visible only in glimpses when they shift, have a certain plumpness to them—a fullness that suggests sustenance of some kind.

Perhaps they absorb something from the air. The moisture, maybe. The metallic water that runs down the walls in slow trickles. Perhaps they require very little, and have required very little for a very long time. Perhaps their metabolism is tuned precisely to what this place provides, so that they exist in a perfect equilibrium with their environment—neither starving nor overconsuming, simply being.

They are not hungry. This much seems clear. If they were, they would have responded differently to your presence. They would have investigated more thoroughly. They would have tapped more than twice, with more than antennae.

---

They have a way of moving that is almost musical.

The click and scrape of their legs against iron—the constant background noise of this place—is not random. There are patterns in it. Rhythms that emerge and dissolve, phases of activity and rest that pass through the population like weather. At times, the corridor falls nearly silent, save for the mechanical hum beneath the walls. At other times, the clicking rises to a crescendo, a frantic chatter of dozens of bodies adjusting simultaneously, before settling again.

It might be communication. It might be temperature regulation—some mechanism for redistributing heat through their shells. It might be nothing at all, or nothing you would recognize as purposeful.

They do not move to travel. They move in place. Theirs is a stationary existence, enlivened only by these small shifts, these repositionings of limb and plate. They do not seem to mind. They do not seem bored. Boredom requires a concept of alternatives, and alternatives are not something they traffic in.

---

They can sense you in ways you do not fully understand.

The antennae are the most obvious mechanism—pale, jointed, waving slowly in the damp air. But there are other sensors, perhaps, that you cannot see. They know where you are at all times. You have tested this: walked the length of the corridor and back, stopped abruptly, stood still for long minutes. The residents track you. Not with eyes, not with heads that turn, but with some kind of awareness. Their clicking changes subtly when you move. Their antennae angle in your direction.

And yet they do not care. This is the part that unsettles: the combination of total awareness and total indifference. They know you are here. They have simply decided, collectively or individually, that you do not matter.

It is not an insult. It is not a judgment. It is an assessment. A categorization. You are something that passes through—a phenomenon, like the water beads or the electric current, that has no bearing on their existence. They will not remember you after you leave. They will not forget you, either. They will simply return to the baseline, the equilibrium, the state of being that they occupied before you arrived.

---

They have no apparent lifespan.

You cannot tell which of them are young and which are old. Their shells do not show wear in any graduated way. Some gleam, some are dulled, but the variation seems random, or at least unrelated to age in any sense you would recognize. There are no juveniles, no elders. No size differences that might indicate growth stages. They are all, as far as you can tell, the same.

This suggests one of two things: either they do not reproduce, and have simply always existed in this number, or they reproduce in some way that you have not observed—perhaps behind the sealed doors, perhaps in some other part of the infinite building you cannot access. Either option raises more questions than it answers.

What you do know is this: you have seen no death here. No bodies, no remains, no empty niches that suggest a vacancy left by something departed. They occupy their spaces completely. They are full.

---

They are not mechanical. But they are not entirely organic, either.

Their movement has a precision to it that suggests something manufactured—something designed for a specific purpose and executing that purpose without deviation. Their bodies fit their niches too perfectly. Their clicking follows patterns too consistent. And yet, up close, you can see the pale flesh beneath their plates, the jointed legs that move with insectile fluidity, the antennae that test the air with something like curiosity. They are alive. You are certain of this. They have warmth, faint but present. They respond to stimuli. They exist.

But they were made. Or at least, they were shaped by something that understood what it was doing. The corridor is intentional; the niches are intentional; the fit between creature and architecture is too exact to be accidental. Someone designed this arrangement. Someone built this place for these beings, or built these beings for this place.

That person is gone. The arrangement remains.

---

They have, collectively, a kind of patience that exceeds human comprehension.

They can wait. They have been waiting, perhaps, for longer than you can imagine—for as long as the corridor has existed, for as long as the lights have glowed and the water has beaded and the hum has persisted in the walls. They are waiting still. Not for anything specific. Not for rescue or release or change. They are waiting because waiting is what they do. It is their function, their purpose, their mode of existence.

They will continue to wait after you leave. They will click and shuffle and test the air with their antennae. The lights will glow. The water will run. The doors will stay sealed.

And if you came back—a year from now, a decade, a century—you would find them here. In their niches. In their places. The same number. The same arrangement. The same indifferent awareness of your presence.

They are not waiting for you. They are not waiting for anything.

They are simply here.
