The people who live in Upper Sump have backs like suspension cables. You can see the tension in them when they lean into the wind off the abyss—some permanent brace against the pull of the drop, the altitude, the slow crawl of their apartments toward the edge. They are not born here. No one is born here yet. The maternity ward is on the docket for Year Twelve; right now, it's a concrete shell where cloud leopards sleep, and so children arrive the way everyone does: on the eleven-hour elevator ride, vertigo already settling into their bones like a second skeleton. By the time they reach the top, they've been rewritten. The pressure change does it, or maybe the anticipation. They call it "getting green" when the damp takes your lungs, but there's an earlier stage, unspoken—the moment you look out over the white nothing and feel not fear but *recognition*. That's when you belong.

They age differently here. Not slower—stranger. The moisture gets under the skin, plumps it, makes the elderly look drowned in the best way, cheeks full, hands soft as the infant granite. Wrinkles arrive late and all at once, like geological events. You can read a Sump-dweller's decades in their knuckles, which swell from pruning their apartment gardens and gripping handrails slick with phosphorescent lichen. Their fingernails grow thick. There are nutritionists who attribute this to the mineral content of the condensation, but the locals know it's simpler than that: the city wants you to hold on, and so it gives you better grip.

They dress in layers that shed water. Nothing here is ever dry, so the fabric has to breathe and repel simultaneously—an engineering problem that's produced a cottage textile industry in the intervals between the apartment blocks. The looms clatter in the pearlescent gloom, and the cloth comes out with a faint greenish cast, something about the moss spores in the air, something about the thread. The residents wear the same colors as their walls. Camouflage, or adaptation. After a while, you stop seeing them against the architecture. They stand still during conversations—something the altitude teaches, the cost of movement at oxygen levels that make haste a form of hemorrhage—and they blend into the unfinished concrete, the fungal bloom, the cables thrumming with load.

Their speech is slow. This is practical: you can't waste air at four thousand meters. But it's also aesthetic. Upper Sump has no patience for the inessential. A sentence that doesn't build something is a sentence that won't survive the moisture. The locals speak in a dialect that's never been documented because it's still forming—borrowing syntax from the construction crews' twelve languages, from the botanists who stayed, from the silence between hammer strikes. Verbs conjugate by building phase. "I am" becomes "I am *pouring*," "I am *setting*," "I am *load-bearing*." There is no word for "finished."

They take lovers the way the mountain takes vegetation: opportunistically, in the cracks of their schedules, roots going wherever the substrate allows. The intervals between buildings are also intervals between lives, and residents cross those intervals rarely enough that each meeting feels geological—two plates converging, pressure building, something giving way. Monogamy is structurally impractical when your apartment might tilt three degrees overnight and need emergency realignment, when your partner might be relocated to Tower Nine for a six-month shift helping the winch crews. The population is too sparse for possessiveness. Instead, there is a kind of distributed intimacy: everyone has tended everyone else's ferns at some point, everyone has held the cable while someone else welded, everyone has sat up with the high-altitude primates watching from the scaffolding and felt, for no clear reason, that they were being assessed and found adequate.

They have no religion yet. The city is too young. But they have rituals, which is religion's raw material. In the mornings, when the cloud rises, they stand at their windows or on the observation platforms and watch the water fall from nothing. No one told them to do this. It started in Year Two, when a handful of residents independently found themselves unable to work during the breath of the mountain, and by Year Four it was customary—you simply stopped, and watched, and let the damp bead on your face like a benediction. The children who've arrived since then do it without being taught. Something about the altitude. Something about the unfinished geology. The moment is too large to spend on anything else.

They have opinions about the emergent floors. This is a subject of endless debate in the half-lit common rooms, the cistern-gardens, the scaffold-corridors where the primates sit and listen. Some residents refuse to enter the undocumented spaces, treating them as symptoms of a pathology they don't yet have words for. Others have moved in, furnishing rooms that appeared without architects with chairs found in halls that lead to stairs that weren't there last Tuesday. These residents report a quality of silence in the emergent zones—not absence of sound, but a *settling*, like the silence of a bone healing. The building inspectors write their reports in pencil. The residents write nothing. They live in the spaces the mountain has offered, and if those spaces change, they change with them.

Their relationship to the animals is practical and mythic simultaneously. The cloud leopards are neighbors who refuse to attend meetings; the insect colonies are infrastructure they didn't have to pay for; the primates are colleagues. A construction worker will report to the morning briefing that "the irregularities on the north scaffolding request additional cable," and the foreman will write it down and allocate the materials. No one finds this strange. The city is still deciding what is structural and what is personnel, and until it makes that determination, everyone—human, feline, insect, ape—works the same shift.

They dream of falling, but not with terror. In Upper Sump, the drop is omnipresent, and the unconscious mind, given enough exposure, will turn any fear into furniture. They dream of the eleven-hour elevator ride going faster, the city shrinking below them, the cables singing a different note. They dream of the mountain finishing its compression and becoming something load-bearing that doesn't need them anymore. They dream of the vegetation winning—of their apartments becoming pure greenhouse, of moss eating the walls, of waking to find themselves inside a jungle that grew from their own breath. These dreams are not nightmares. They are planning sessions. The residents wake from them with ideas for new construction, for load routes, for ways to hold the city in place that work *with* the mountain rather than against it.

They do not speak of leaving. Not because it's forbidden—the cable cars run on their six-hour cycle, and the elevator descends for anyone who requests it—but because the concept has calcified into something abstract, like asking a load-bearing wall to consider early retirement. The people who leave are spoken of the way you'd speak of sediment that washed downstream: they went where the geology took them, and that's natural, and the mountain doesn't mourn its lost strata. But the people who stay are the mountain now. Their weight is part of the calculation. Their hands have mixed the concrete, pruned the ferns, held the cable. They have been incorporated.

A resident of thirty years—a botanist who came on a research permit, like they all do—will show you her hands. The calluses are architectural. She'll point to a thickening on her left palm and tell you that's Tower Four, and a ridge on her right index finger and say that's the cistern on the east interval, and she'll laugh, but not as a joke. She has been here longer than some of the buildings. Her body knows what the surveyors are still mapping: that the city is not a place you live in, but a process you undergo.

And still they arrive. The elevator climbs for eleven hours, and at the top, someone steps out into the permanent gloom, the damp, the arrhythmic percussion of construction that will never end, and feels—what? Not home. Home is too complete a word for what's happening here. They feel *draft*. They feel *schematic*. They feel the way the geology feels: like something that could become anything, given enough pressure and time.

The city asks you to stay. It asks you to help. And what lives here—the people, the leopards, the moss, the emergent floors, the unfinished mountain itself—is whatever said yes.
