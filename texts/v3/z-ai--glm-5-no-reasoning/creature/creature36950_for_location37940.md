# Those Who Dwell in Kael Vorn

## The Body First

You would notice their hands before you noticed their faces. The people of Kael Vorn have hands that have spent generations in dialogue with living wood — palms broad and flat, fingers thick at the bases where muscle has built from the steady work of guiding root-growth, nails worn close and stained with tannin and sap. Their skin is not pale, not dark, but something in between — a greyish undertone that comes from living in light without direct sun, without shadow, without the day-night cycle that governs surface skin. They smell of wet wood and moss and the mineral sweetness of the springs. It is not an unpleasant smell. It is the smell of someone who belongs where they are.

Their eyes have adjusted to the flat grey luminescence in ways that go beyond dilation. There is something different about how they process light — a sensitivity to movement at the peripheries, an ability to read surfaces that surface-dwellers would call featureless. They can see gradients in the bioluminescent moss that you cannot. They can navigate by the faint phosphorescence of the limestone when there is no other light at all. Their pupils do not behave quite right. They seem to hold an openness that is not merely low-light adaptation but something more fundamental — an openness that makes surface-dwellers uncomfortable, as though the person were seeing more than what was in front of them.

Their hair ranges from dark brown to black, universally worn long and bound back with root-fiber cords. It grows thick and fast, and there is a faint gloss to it that comes from the constant moisture in the air. Their clothes are woven from marsh flax and spider-silk, cured to a soft grey-green that matches the moss, and they wear them in layers that can be added or shed as the water-table shifts. Everything they own is designed to be wet. Nothing stiffens when it dries because nothing truly dries here.

They move differently than surface-dwellers move. Their balance is better — they have spent their lives navigating uneven terrain, moss-slicked limestone, root-bridges that flex underfoot, walkways that change with the seasons. They do not stumble. They do not slip. They place their feet with a deliberateness that looks almost like ceremony but is simply adaptation. Their voices have adjusted to the acoustics: they speak in low registers, barely above murmurs, and the sound carries through the root-walls in ways that surface acoustics would not permit. A whisper in Kael Vorn can be heard clearly across a room.

## The Names They Carry

The settlement's founders have been dead for longer than anyone remembers. Their names are not attached to the structures they shaped; houses are not named for builders. They are named for what grows through them.

There is the House of the Pale Bracket, where a shelf-fungus colonized a load-bearing root and was cultivated rather than removed, and now the whole structure smells of spores and the occupants have developed an immunity to compounds that would fell a surface-dweller. There is the House Under the Thrush, named for the bird that nested in its roof-dome for three generations before moving on, and whose calls are still mimicked by the children. There is the House Where the Old Root Broke — still occupied, with its cracked pillar preserved as a reminder that what grows can also fail. There is the House by the Third Terrace, which has the best fishing and the worst damp and a line of occupants whose lungs have learned to process the spore-laden air.

Children are named after water, after moss, after the qualities of wood. There are boys named Seep and girls named Grain and children of either sex named for the sounds of the marsh — names that are barely words, just syllables that approximate the calls of frogs or the whisper of springs. A child's true name is given by the person who first hears them cry. A child's use-name — what they are called in daily life — is given by the vornik.

No one admits to seeing the vornik give a name. But when a child has spent their first year in the settlement, the name simply appears — whispered through the root-walls, spoken by someone who claims they heard it from someone else, until the whole community accepts it. There are children named Rustle. There are children named Damp. There are children named after feelings that have no surface-language equivalent — the specific contentment of being enclosed, the particular loneliness of open sky.

## The Work of Living

The work begins before first light, though "first light" has no meaning in Kael Vorn. They follow cycles that are not cycles — the stirring of the bats overhead, the movement of water through the springs, the subtle brightening of the bioluminescent moss that correlates with nothing the surface world would recognize. Children wake before adults. The old wake last of all. This is not rule but rhythm, and it has been this way long enough that no one remembers why.

A household's first work is fire-tending. Each house holds a small clay hearth with embers banked in moss, and each morning someone blows them to life, adds a shaving of dried root-wood, and carries a lit twist to the gathering-dome. If the gathering-dome's fire has flagged overnight, this is a private shame for the fire-keeper's household, and they will spend the next week nursing the communal flame back to vigor. The fire at the center of the community is never allowed to die. When the springs surge and the water rises and the third terrace floods past its usual margins, someone stays in the gathering-dome all night, feeding the flame, keeping it above the damp.

After fire comes water. Each household sends someone to the springs with clay vessels that take two people to carry when full. The water is not for drinking — the springs emerge clean and sweet, but they are too far from most houses to be convenient for daily use. It is for washing, for cooking, for the moisture-loving plants that each household cultivates in root-hollows near their walls. Drinking water comes from the ceiling-roots, which are tapped with hollow reeds at specific points known to each household. The sap that drips out is sweet and faintly green, and it tastes like the forest above. The children prefer it to spring-water. The adults remember a time when the sap was less sweet, and they wonder what is changing in the world above.

After water comes wood. The root-tenders go out with their stone blades, their frames and guides, their decades of accumulated knowledge. They know which roots are ready to be trained and which need another year. They know where to cut to encourage thickening and where to cut to encourage branching. They know the difference between healthy growth and growth that is trying to become something else — a phenomenon that has no name in surface botany, but which the root-tenders recognize by a quality of the wood, a darkness at the center, a tendency to grow in spirals. Roots that grow in spirals are not trained. They are left alone, or in extreme cases, cut away and burned, and the ash is scattered in the third terrace, where the fish eat it and do not seem to mind.

The work is slow. A house takes years. A walkway takes months. A child's education in root-tending takes decades, and even then, there are surprises — a pillar that splits unexpectedly, a ceiling-root that begins to descend with unnatural speed, a growth that forms in a shape no one has seen before. The people of Kael Vorn have a word for these surprises. It translates roughly to "the wood is thinking," and it is spoken with a mixture of respect and wariness.

## The Eating

They eat fish and tubers and mushrooms and the tender shoots of certain root-pillars, harvested in a way that does not harm the parent wood. They eat insects — not as staple, but as seasoning, a practice surface-dwellers would call strange but which makes sense in a place where protein is limited and flying insects are rare. They eat the larvae of the marsh flies, gathered from the surface of the third terrace in fine mesh-nets, and they dry them and grind them and sprinkle the powder over their fish. It adds a nutty richness and a substantial complement of fat.

They do not eat the vornik. This is perhaps the most serious rule, and it is never stated, because it does not need to be.

They do not eat the rail-bird. This is also never stated.

They do not eat the large, pale mushrooms that grow in the deepest part of the third terrace, near where the fourth terrace begins. These are gathered, but not for food. They are dried and stored and traded in small quantities to the rare visitors who come from the surface, who use them for purposes the people of Kael Vael do not ask about. The trade is occasional but significant. It brings in metal tools, which are otherwise unobtainable, and cloth, which is valued but unnecessary, and sometimes paper, which is treated as a luxury good.

The people eat together in the gathering-dome on a schedule that depends on nothing visible. Everyone knows when it is time. The work pauses. The households bring their contributions — fish from the fishers, mushrooms from the mushroom-walkers, tubers from the terrace-margin diggers — and the communal pot is assembled. The oldest person present oversees the distribution, though this is form more than function. The food is shared. No one goes hungry. The rule of fish — take no more than the pool will provide — extends to everything. If there is not enough for everyone, there is not enough for anyone, and the meal is smaller but equal.

The children eat first. Then the workers. Then the old. The oldest person eats last, and often gives portions of their share to children who hover nearby, and this is accepted as part of the natural order. The old have eaten last for as long as anyone remembers. They do not seem to suffer for it.

## The Children

The children of Kael Vorn do not know about the sky.

They know the shaft-light — the bright vertical columns that move across the marsh floor with the passage of hours. They have been told that above the ceiling-roots, there is something called "sun," which is like shaft-light but everywhere at once. They do not believe this. They nod when told, because adults are supposed to be humored, but they do not believe. The idea of light that comes from no particular direction, that casts no column, that is simply present — this makes no sense to them. It violates something fundamental in their understanding of the world.

They play in the root-bridges, climbing the pillars with an ease that makes surface-dwellers gasp. They play in the shallow margins of the third terrace, splashing in water that comes to their knees, catching frogs with their hands and releasing them unharmed. They play in the gathering-dome, where their voices are allowed to rise above the murmur, where they chase each other through the root-woven spaces with a joy that seems to ignore the damp and the dimness.

They collect spider-silk. This is not play — or rather, it is play that serves a purpose. The children are responsible for the spider-silk harvest, which they gather on frames of woven root-fiber that they carry between them. They know which spiders produce which kinds of silk. They know how to approach a web without alarming its maker, how to harvest without damaging, how to leave enough for the spider to rebuild. They are proud of this work, and they are recognized for it, and the best silk-gatherers are given small honors — first choice of mushrooms, a seat near the fire.

They learn root-tending from the moment they can hold a blade. A child of five is given a practice-piece of wood — a harvested trimming, no longer alive, to learn the motions on. A child of ten is allowed to tend a small growth near their house, something low-risk that can afford mistakes. A child of fifteen is apprenticed to a root-tender, and begins the decades-long process of learning which cuts heal and which fester, which growths can be trusted and which must be watched.

They do not go to the fourth terrace. This is not a rule for them specifically; it is a rule for everyone. But children are more curious than adults, and the prohibition has a weight that suggests danger, and danger is interesting. Children dare each other to go closer to the edge of the third terrace where the water darkens and the roots change. Children whisper about what they might see. Children, so far as anyone knows, have not actually gone. But the adults watch the children, near that edge, with a particular attention.

## The Old

The old of Kael Vorn are not many. Life here is not short, but it is not long either — the damp gets into the lungs, the spores get into the blood, the endless subtle work of living in a place that wants to grow over you wears at the body in increments. A person who lives to sixty is considered old. A person who lives to seventy is considered remarkable.

The oldest person in the settlement is a woman called Three Springs, named for the number of springs that emerged from the limestone in her childhood year. She is perhaps seventy-five — no one knows exactly, and she does not remember. Her face is lined with the kind of wrinkles that come from living in moisture, a softening and folding of the skin that makes her look younger than she is until you see her hands, which are knotted and thick from decades of work. She is one of the three women who know which roots can be cut. This knowledge is printed in her body as much as her mind — her hands move toward certain pillars and away from others without conscious thought, a physical memory of what the wood will permit.

She does not speak much. When she does, the settlement listens. Not because she is loud — she is among the quietest — but because her murmurs carry a weight of having seen more of Kael Vorn than anyone else. She remembers the great collapse of her childhood, when a section of ceiling-root gave way and twenty people were lost to the aquifer below. She remembers the dry years, when the springs diminished and the third terrace shrank and there was talk of leaving. She remembers the wet years, when the water rose to the first terrace and the houses became islands and people navigated by raft. She does not talk about the fourth terrace. She does not talk about it at all.

The other old people cluster around her in a way that is not quite deference and not quite dependence. They are a small group — perhaps fifteen people over sixty — and they spend their days near the gathering-dome, tending small tasks that do not require the strength of youth. They weave. They watch the children. They keep the fire. They tell stories that the children do not quite believe.

They are not afraid of death. Death in Kael Vorn is a process of return. The body is taken to the third terrace, weighted with stones, and lowered into the deep pool where the fourth terrace begins. The fish and the water and the peat take what they need. It is not a funeral. It is a continuation.

## The Visitors

Visitors come, rarely. The goat track that leads down to Kael Vorn is not well-known, and the people of the surface have no particular reason to descend into a root-roofed marsh. But occasionally, someone finds the way down.

Traders come. They bring metal and cloth and paper, and they take the pale mushrooms and the spider-silk and occasionally the dried fish, which is prized in certain surface communities for its intense flavor. The traders do not stay long. The air is too wet, the light too strange, the sense of enclosure too complete. They conduct their business and leave, and the people of Kael Vorn watch them go with expressions that are hard to read.

Seekers come. These are people who have heard rumors of Kael Vorn — of the mushrooms that grow nowhere else, of the quality of the spider-silk, of the possibility of a life untouched by the demands of surface society. Some of them want to stay. Most do not. The seekers find the reality of the place more difficult than the rumor: the damp that gets into everything, the endless work of keeping growth at bay, the food that is always fish and mushrooms and never anything else, the darkness that is not darkness but is also not light. The people of Kael Vorn do not discourage seekers from leaving. They do not encourage them to stay. They simply watch, with the patience of those who have watched many seekers come and go.

Lost people come. Wanderers who descended thinking the goat track led somewhere else. They are treated with courtesy, given food and shelter, shown the way back up. They are not asked why they came or where they were going. The people of Kael Vorn understand that the surface world is confusing, that people end up places they did not intend. They understand that not everyone is meant for the enclosed life.

Once, a scholar came. She wanted to study the root-system, to understand how a forest could photosynthesize through thirty meters of stone. She stayed for three months, asking questions, taking notes, making drawings. The people answered what they could. They did not know the answers to most of her questions. They simply lived here; they did not understand the place the way she wanted it understood. When she left, she took her notes and drawings with her. The people of Kael Vorn never learned what she concluded. They assumed she published something somewhere, in the world of sky that they did not visit.

## The Knowledge That Is Held

The three women who know the roots — Three Springs, and two others called Grain and Seep — hold the most important knowledge in the settlement. They know which pillars are load-bearing and which can be trained. They know which ceiling-roots connect to which surface-trees, and which trees are healthy and which are failing. They know the history of every major growth in the basin: which ones were shaped by human hands, which ones grew on their own, which ones represent compromises between the two.

This knowledge is not written. It cannot be written. The people of Kael Vorn have writing — the traders bring paper, and some of the old scholars who descended over the centuries left books behind — but the root-knowledge is not the kind of thing that fits on a page. It is held in the body, in the hands, in the way one stands near a particular pillar and feels its tension. Grain describes it as listening: you stand near a root and you hear what it wants to do. Not with your ears. With something else.

The three women are not young. Grain is perhaps sixty, Seep perhaps sixty-five. They have apprentices — two each, selected in childhood for their aptitude, trained in the physical art of root-tending. But the knowledge they hold cannot be fully transmitted until they are near death. There is something about the transition, the approach of the body to its ending, that opens the channel. When Grain fell ill two years ago, her apprentices reported that they could suddenly feel the roots in a way they had not before — could sense what she sensed, could know what she knew. When she recovered, the sensation faded. But some of it remained. They had learned something that could not be taught, only caught.

The three women will die, eventually. Their knowledge will pass to their apprentices, who will become the new holders, who will train new apprentices in turn. This is how it has been for as long as anyone remembers. The knowledge is not lost. It grows.

## The Unspoken

There are things the people of Kael Vorn do not talk about.

The fourth terrace is one. Everyone knows the rule: you do not go there. But everyone also knows that someone must have gone, at some point, to know that you should not go. The knowledge of what is there — or what might be there, or what is said to be there — is held in the same way the root-knowledge is held: in the body, in the silences, in the things that are not said. Children learn not to ask about the fourth terrace. Adults learn not to think about it.

The surface is another. The people know there is a world above — they are not ignorant. They trade with it, occasionally; they receive visitors from it. But they do not talk about what it might be like to live there. They do not speculate about the lives of surface-dwellers. When visitors describe their homes, the people listen with expressions of polite incomprehension, as though being told about a country they will never visit. It is not that they are uninterested. It is that the surface is not quite real to them. The marsh is real. The roots and the water and the stones and the fire are real. The world of sky and sun and open distance is a story that other people tell about themselves.

The vornik. The small, furred creatures that live in the root-walls are discussed, but not discussed. Everyone knows they exist. Everyone hears them. Everyone knows the rule against harming them. But no one will say what they are, or where they came from, or why they are auspicious. When asked, the people of Kael Vorn smile, or shrug, or change the subject. It is not that they are hiding something. It is that there is nothing to say. The vornik are simply here, simply present, simply part of the marsh in a way that does not require explanation.

The future. The people of Kael Vorn do not make plans beyond the scale of root-growth. A house is a three-year plan; an extension to the gathering-dome is a five-year plan; anything beyond that is speculation. They do not talk about what Kael Vorn will be like in a hundred years, or whether it will exist at all. They live in the present tense, extended outward in slow rings of growth and decay. This is not a philosophical position. It is simply how life is when the world you inhabit is itself slowly sinking into the earth.

## The Feeling of Living There

To live in Kael Vorn is to feel held.

It is not a feeling of being trapped, of being constrained, of being buried alive — though surface-dwellers might describe it that way. It is a feeling of enclosure that is also an embrace. The roots above, the water below, the stones on every side — these are not walls that separate you from the world; they are the world, and they contain you, and there is comfort in that containment.

You feel it most at night, though "night" has no true meaning here. When the work is done and the fire is banked and the children are asleep in their root-woven alcoves, you lie in the dampness and listen to the sounds of the marsh — the distant thrum of toads, the whisper of springs, the tick of bats overhead, the rustle of vornik in the walls. You feel the ceiling-roots above you, and you know, with a knowledge that is not thought but is simply present, that the forest above is holding onto the earth, and the earth is holding onto the water, and the water is holding onto you, and you are part of something that does not need you to understand it.

You feel it when you work. When you stand before a root-pillar with your stone blade, when you place your hand on the pale wood and feel its warmth, its pulse of sap, you are not shaping something that is separate from you. You are in dialogue. The wood speaks — not in words, but in the way it leans, the way it resists, the way it yields. You speak back with your cuts, your guides, your patience. Together, over years, you make something that neither of you could make alone.

You feel it when you eat. When you take fish from the third terrace and mushrooms from the second and tubers from the margins, you are eating the body that holds you. You are taking part of the marsh into yourself, and it becomes part of you, and you are not separate from it. This is not metaphor. This is metabolism. This is how living things work.

You feel it when you think about leaving. And you do think about it, sometimes — when the dampness becomes too much, when the food becomes monotonous, when the work becomes exhausting, when the grey light becomes oppressive. You think about climbing the goat track and emerging into the open air and seeing the sky and feeling the wind and living a life where growth is not something you have to tend every day. You think about it. And then you feel the weight of the roots above you, and the movement of the water below you, and the presence of the people around you, and the warmth of the fire in the gathering-dome, and the rustling of the vornik in the walls, and you know that you will not leave.

Not because you cannot. Because you do not want to. Because the world of sky and distance is not your world, and the world of roots and water is, and there is a peace in belonging to something that will not let you go.

## What They Are Becoming

The people of Kael Vorn have lived here for longer than they remember. Long enough that their bodies have changed — the eyes, the lungs, the skin. Long enough that their minds have changed — the way they think about time, about space, about growth and decay. Long enough that they are no longer quite what they were when their ancestors descended.

They are not a separate species. Not yet. But there is a divergence happening, slow and quiet, in the margins of their biology. The children born in the last few generations have been born with slightly different hands — the webbing between the fingers more pronounced, the palms flatter, the fingers more sensitive to the surface of wood. Their eyes reflect the bioluminescent moss in a way that their parents' eyes do not, a faint shimmer when the light is low. Their lungs process the spore-heavy air with an ease that surface-dwellers cannot match.

The root-tenders have noticed that the wood responds to them differently than it responds to visitors. When a visitor cuts a root, the wound is slow to heal. When a tender cuts a root, the wound closes quickly, the wood growing around the blade with an almost eager speed. The tender's sap — the fluid that their bodies produce in response to the constant moisture, a thin version of lymph that seeps from the pores after years of living in the saturated air — seems to feed the wood. The wood, in turn, seems to recognize them.

The vornik have changed too. Or perhaps the people's perception of them has changed. They are seen more often now — not clearly, never clearly, but glimpsed in the edges of vision, small shapes moving through the root-walls. They do not flee from the people of Kael Vorn. They watch. They wait. They have been present for longer than anyone remembers, and they seem to be waiting for something.

The people do not talk about what they are becoming. It is not discussed in the gathering-dome, not taught to children, not whispered between lovers in the damp darkness. But everyone feels it, in the way their bodies move through the marsh, in the way the wood leans toward them, in the way the water does not seem as cold as it used to. They are sinking into the place that holds them, and the place is accepting them, and they are becoming something that does not have a name yet.

This does not frighten them. They have lived their whole lives in a world that grows. They understand that growth is change, that change is not loss, that what they are becoming is simply what they are. The marsh breathes. The wood thinks. The people tend. And together, in the enclosed world beneath the hills, they continue — slowly, quietly, held.
