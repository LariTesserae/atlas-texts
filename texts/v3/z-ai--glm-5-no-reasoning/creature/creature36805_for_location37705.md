# What Lives in the High Green Void

## The Shelf Shepherds

They begin appearing before you truly enter the Green—not living *on* the vegetation but at its boundary, in that liminal space where stone meets woven stem. The Shelf Shepherds are not guardians in any traditional sense. They do not prevent passage. They do not guide. They watch, and their watching has a weight.

Small communities, family groups mostly, living in structures built into the rockface itself—caves expanded and shaped, sealed with woven plant matter that they harvest from the Green's edges. Their skin has adapted to the cold and the thin air: pale, almost gray, with a fine network of visible veins that darkens when cloud-moisture arrives. They do not speak to travelers. Sometimes they speak among themselves in a language of gestures and low sounds that the Green absorbs before it carries.

The Shepherds' purpose is singular: they monitor the boundary. Not to protect it, but to record it. The older ones carry memory-sticks of carved bone, etched with notches and symbols that mark where the Green reached in their youth versus where it reaches now. Generational work. A family might spend three lifetimes tracking the advance of a single tendril.

They know things the guides do not. A Shepherd woman once grabbed a climber's arm before he stepped onto a section that looked stable—held him, silent, until the surface collapsed of its own accord. When asked how she knew, she simply pressed two fingers to the climber's chest, then to her own temple, then walked away.

## The Hollow Divers

Down through the first layers of surface growth, past the root-systems and the moisture-absorbing nodes, there exists an ecology almost no surface-walker ever witnesses. The interior of the Green. And the Hollow Divers are the only humans who traverse it.

They are not tall. Generations of living in compressed spaces have selected for compact frames, flexible spines, the ability to fold themselves into gaps that would trap a normal human arm. Their lungs have adapted to air that is thicker with moisture and thinner with oxygen—pockets within the Green where plant respiration has created local atmospheres unlike anything on the surface.

The Divers do not live permanently in the interior. They make expeditions: days or weeks of careful navigation through the vascular tunnels that the burrowers create, mapping the Green's internal architecture, searching for the deep water reserves that accumulate where root-systems have woven particularly tight. They harvest things down there. Medicines. Rare growths. The occasional biological curiosity that surfaces-traders will pay well for.

Divers are identifiable by their hands. The work requires constant gripping, pulling, bracing—navigate by touch in absolute darkness, where one wrong move could send you tumbling into a collapse zone. Their fingers are thick and calloused, their grip strength measured in multiples of normal human capacity. Many have lost digits to the burrowers, who do not appreciate the intrusion.

The Divers do not speak of what they've seen in the deep Green. When pressed, one older Diver simply said: "It has a heartbeat. You can feel it. Everything else, you don't need to know."

## The Weaver-Castes

Scattered across the Green's surface, often far from any water pocket or landmark, the Weaver-Castes have made their homes in the vegetation itself. Their settlements are invisible from any distance—blended into the surface, grown into gaps, maintained through constant negotiation with the surrounding plant life.

A Weaver-home begins as a small wound in the Green's surface: a grazer-path that damaged a section of growth, a lightning-strike that cleared local vegetation. The Weavers move into these damaged zones and begin working. Not building. *Training*. They guide the Green's regrowth, teaching it to arch overhead, to weave walls, to leave openings for air and entry. A well-tended Weaver-home takes a generation to reach maturity. Some have been growing for centuries.

The castes are hereditary. The knowledge passes from parent to child through demonstration, through working alongside, through the slow absorption of technique that takes decades. A master Weaver can read the Green's health in its color, predict weather by tasting the moisture in the air, identify approaching grazers by the specific vibrations traveling through the woven floor.

Their agriculture is distinctive. The blue-tinged crops are not planted—they are *persuaded*. The Weavers have learned to induce mutations in the Green's growth patterns, creating pockets of specialized vegetation that produce edible matter. The process takes years. A single crop-line might represent five generations of careful selection and reinforcement.

Weavers do not leave the Green. They are born, live, and die within its embrace, and when they die, their bodies are given to the Green directly—no ceremony, no preservation, simply a quiet return to the substrate that sustained them. The guides say the Weavers have no word for "burial." Their word for death translates closer to "returning to the weave."

## The Drift-Hunters

Not everyone who lives in the High Green Void stays on the surface.

The Drift-Hunters are a semi-nomadic people who have evolved to inhabit the space *above* the vegetation—riding the thermals, camping on the raised outcroppings that the Green occasionally produces, navigating by wind-pattern and the bioluminescent signals that pulse across the surface below.

They are lean. Wiry. Their bones are lighter than average, their lung capacity exceptional. Many are born with vestigial webbing between fingers and toes—an adaptation that some Drift-Hunters claim improves their ability to read air currents, though the guides dispute this. Their camps are temporary, mobile, broken down and carried in woven packs that distribute weight across their bodies as they travel.

The Drift-Hunters follow the razor-wing flocks. Not to hunt them—razor-wings are too dangerous for direct confrontation—but to scavenge their kills. A flock of razor-wings will take down prey and consume what they need, leaving remains. The Drift-Hunters have learned to read the birds' patterns, to arrive at kill-sites before the Green reclaims the bones.

This scavenging is their primary protein source. Their secondary trade is information. Drift-Hunters see more of the Green than any surface-dweller ever could. They track grazer migrations, weather-pattern shifts, dead-zone formation. They carry news between the Weaver-castes and maintain communication across distances that would take weeks to traverse on foot.

The Drift-Hunters have a complex relationship with the razor-wings. They do not hunt them, but they do harvest their feathers—collected from molting sites, never taken from live birds. The feathers are valuable beyond the Green: lightweight, flexible, impossibly sharp. A single razor-wing primary feather can fund a family for a season.

## The Dead-Zone Priests

Where others avoid the rotted sections, the Dead-Zone Priests make them their home.

Not exclusively—no human could survive indefinitely in the decay—but the Priests are drawn to these dying places. They study them. They document the progression of death and rebirth. And they have learned to predict where the Green will fail before it happens.

A Priest's training takes decades. They begin as assistants to older Priests, learning to read the subtle signs of vegetative distress: the slight gray-green that precedes true death, the specific pitch of the hum that indicates structural weakening, the way moisture collects differently in soon-to-fail zones. They carry instruments of measurement and record-keeping: marked bones, colored threads, jars of soil samples from dead zones across the Green.

Their role is not purely academic. The Priets provide warnings to the surrounding communities. A dead zone forming near a Weaver-settlement is catastrophic; the Priets ensure such settlements have time to relocate. They also maintain the boundary-markers that identify which sections are unsafe for travel.

The Priests are distinguished by their clothing: robes woven from dead-zone fibers, materials harvested from the decay itself. The cloth is treated, stabilized, but retains a faint smell that marks its origin. They do not hide their association with death. They embody it.

Among the Priests, there is a subset known as the Deep Divers—not to be confused with the Hollow Divers—who enter the most dangerous dead zones during active decomposition. Their mortality rate is staggering. Their knowledge is irreplaceable. They are the only humans who have witnessed the full cycle of Green-death and Green-rebirth from beginning to end.

## The Children of the Shelf

Some children are born in the Green and never leave it.

The Children of the Shelf is not a formal group. It is a designation for those whose entire existence has been contained within the High Green Void—no descent to the mountain, no experience of true sky or solid stone. They are raised by Weaver-castes, by Drift-Hunters, by Shepherds, and they develop differently.

Their eyes adapt to the permanent twilight. Their lungs expand to process the oxygen-poor air. Their skin becomes almost translucent, thin enough that the vein-networks show clearly, darkened by the constant moisture. They navigate by sound more than sight, their hearing sharpened to the point where they can identify species by footstep-vibration alone.

These children do not know they are different until they encounter someone from below. The first time a Child of the Shelf sees a surface-walker flinch at the Green's hum, they register confusion. The sound is home. The moisture is comfort. The endless vegetative surface is the only ground they have ever known.

Some Children eventually choose to descend. The experience is traumatic—too much light, too little humidity, ground that does not breathe or respond. Many return within days. Others push through, adapting to the alien world below, though they never fully adjust.

The guides can identify a Child of the Shelf by their movement: a weightlessness, a sense of reading the surface in real-time, adjusting before each footfall lands. They do not walk *on* the Green. They walk *with* it.

## The Burrower-Sympaths

The burrowers are not human. They are indigenous: segmented, six-legged creatures that tunnel through the Green's deepest layers, processing dead matter into soil, creating the vascular architecture through which water and nutrients flow. Most humans never see them. But some humans have learned to communicate with them.

The Burrower-Sympaths are rare—perhaps a few dozen across the entire Green. They are born, not made, though the trait that allows them to interface with burrower consciousness is not fully understood. The leading theory involves sensitivity to the same vibration-frequencies that the burrowers use to communicate, an ability to produce and interpret sound in ranges that most humans cannot access.

A Burrower-Sympath does not control the burrowers. The relationship is closer to translation. The Sympaths relay information between human communities and the burrower-network: warnings about unstable sections, requests for tunnel-modification, alerts about predators or weather. In return, the burrowers share knowledge about the Green's interior, its health, its hidden resources.

The Sympaths are marked by their voices. The constant production of sounds outside normal human range affects the vocal cords over time, giving older Sympaths a distinctive rasp, a quality of layered tones that some find unsettling.

They live apart from other human groups, neither fully accepted nor fully rejected. Their connection to the burrowers makes them valuable but strange. Children sometimes run from them. Adults sometimes seek them out for knowledge they cannot obtain elsewhere.

## The Descent-Guides

Finally, the guides themselves—though they are not native to the Green.

They are professionals drawn from the lower elevations, trained over years to navigate the High Green Void safely. Each guide has their own methodology: some emphasize sound-reading, others track moisture patterns, still others have memorized the locations of every stable shelter within their territory.

The best guides are those who have spent enough time in the Green to have absorbed some of its rhythms without losing their connection to the world below. They serve as translators in both directions—explaining the Green to travelers, explaining travelers to the Green (or at least to its inhabitants).

A guide's fees are substantial. Their mortality rates are not trivial. The average career length is seven years before injury, trauma, or simple exhaustion forces retirement. A few guides have worked the Green for decades. They are legendary, sought after, expensive beyond reason.

They do not speak much about their experiences. When asked why they continue, most give practical answers about economics or skill-utility. But the real reason, the one they will not articulate, is the same reason travelers descend into the Green in the first place.

They have touched something that operates on a different scale. They have been part of a system that transcends individual existence. They cannot explain this to anyone who has not felt it, and they cannot forget it once they have.

## The Unseen

The Green has inhabitants that no human has reliably documented.

The guides speak of them cautiously, the Weavers more openly, the Priests not at all. There is something else in the High Green Void—something that may be a creature, may be a system, may be something for which no category exists.

The glimpses are inconsistent. Tall shadows at the edge of vision. Movements that do not correspond to any known animal. Sounds that cut through the Green's hum, regular and intentional. The disappearance of travelers who were walking safely one moment and simply not there the next.

The Weavers have a word for these presences, though they will not pronounce it in front of outsiders. The word translates roughly to "those who were here first." The Burrower-Sympaths refuse to discuss them at all.

The official position of the guides is that the Unseen are misidentified phenomena: atmospheric effects, optical illusions, the Green's own processes perceived incorrectly by human senses. This is the safe answer. The answer that keeps the passage-trade alive.

But every guide has a story they do not share. A moment when the vegetation around them went silent—truly silent, for the only time in their experience—and something moved past them in the darkness, something that acknowledged them, something that chose not to engage.

The Green does not know itself. It does not have a mind. But it may have a guardian. Or several. Or something that fills that niche without being alive in any sense we understand.

## The Integration

The final category of inhabitant is theoretical: those whom the Green has absorbed.

The ruins suggest that people lived here before. The structures, the tools, the evidence of agriculture. And then they disappeared, leaving no bodies, no signs of violence, no clear reason for departure.

The guides avoid this topic. The Weavers change the subject. The Priests simply smile.

The leading theory among scholars is integration. The Green consumed the earlier inhabitants—not through predation, but through assimilation. Their bodies were incorporated into the substrate. Their consciousness, if such a thing is possible, was distributed across the network.

It is speculation. It is not the sort of thing that can be proven. But travelers report strange experiences in the deep Green: moments of recognition, feelings of being known, the sense that the vegetation around them is responding to their presence with something other than automatic growth-response.

The Dead-Zone Priests speak of voices in the decay. The Burrower-Sympaths describe patterns in the tunnel-architecture that suggest intentional design. The Hollow Divers return from expeditions speaking of faces in the root-systems, formations that look almost human, almost deliberate.

Perhaps the earlier inhabitants are still here. Perhaps they *are* the Green now, or part of it. Perhaps the distinction between us and vegetation is less absolute than we prefer to believe.

Or perhaps these are merely stories that humans tell themselves to avoid the simpler, colder truth: that the Green is vast and indifferent and we are temporary, and when we disappear from its surface it is not because we have joined something greater but simply because we have been forgotten.

---

The High Green Void has no single people. It has no unified culture. What it has is an ecosystem of human adaptation—a scattering of communities and individuals who have found ways to exist within a living landscape, negotiating with rather than conquering their environment.

They are all marked by the Green. They all hear its hum in the quiet moments, feel its moisture on their skin, dream of its endless vegetative surface. They belong to it more than it belongs to them.

And eventually, every one of them returns to it.
