# What Lives Here

## The Gardener

It was human once. It remembers this the way the substrate remembers the steel column—not as narrative but as *resistance*, a shape inside itself that refuses to dissolve.

The Gardener moves through the main chamber on bare feet that have long since stopped being entirely feet. They are something between root and limb now, broad and shallow, spreading weight across the pulsing floor without disturbing its rhythm. When it stands still, the tissue beneath it grows up around the edges of its soles, not consuming but *greeting*, the way water rises around a stone placed in a stream. When it lifts a foot to walk, the tissue settles back, undisturbed, patient.

It is tall—or was. The posture has changed. The spine curves forward now, not with age but with adaptation, a body learning the shape of its enclosure. The ceiling is high in places, but the Gardener spends most of its time in the secondary chambers, the deeper spaces, where the passages narrow and the air thickens to something you could almost chew. Its shoulders have broadened across the years, or decades, or longer—it does not count time in units anymore, only in cycles of the substrate's pulse, in the intervals between the crystalline sounds from the canopy. Its arms are long. Its fingers are longer. It has stopped thinking of them as fingers.

Skin is the wrong word. The outermost layer has been replaced, cell by cell, over uncounted cycles. What remains is something like bark crossed with velvet—green-brown, faintly downy, warm to the touch. It sweats, but the sweat is not sweat. It is a secretion, a delivery mechanism, fluid carrying enzymes that the Gardener contributes to the substrate as it walks. It feeds the floor. The floor feeds it. This exchange has been happening for so long that the boundary between the two is administrative, not functional. The Gardener knows this. It does not mind.

---

## What It Does

It tends.

Not with tools. Not with intention in the way a human gardener intends—pruning here, staking there, deciding what lives and what dies. Nothing dies here. The Gardener's work is more like translation. It moves through the chambers and *listens* to the substrate's gradients, its chemical signals, the subtle shifts in pressure and moisture that indicate where growth is congested, where a passage is narrowing too far, where a pool has become too acidic or too alkaline for the film to maintain itself.

Then it intervenes. A hand placed against a wall for three pulse-cycles, secreting a precise combination of enzymes that loosens the tissue's grip and allows it to redirect. A foot held in a pool for long enough that the warmth of its body alters the temperature gradient, encouraging the film to expand into territory it had been reluctant to claim. An arm raised to a hanging growth, fingers threading into the filaments above, applying tension—just so, just barely—so that a cluster of dangling structures shifts its orientation and receives more light.

The Gardener is not necessary. The system would continue without it. The substrate is self-regulating, the consortium of growths negotiated into stability long before the Gardener came to live inside it. But the Gardener *improves* things. It smooths the rough transitions. It accelerates recoveries from damage. It maintains the drainage channels that the original engineers designed, clearing blockages with its hands, guiding water along paths that the tissue has tried to close.

It is, in this sense, still doing the job it was built for. Or assigned to. Or chose. It doesn't remember which, and the distinction no longer matters.

## The Others

The Gardener is not alone, though "alone" is a complicated concept inside an organism the size of a cathedral.

There are the **Tenders**—smaller, quicker, less human in shape. They move through the canopy above, climbing among the hanging growths with hands and feet that have become entirely prehensile, digits elongated and jointed in ways that suggest spider more than primate. Their bodies are narrow, dense with muscle, covered in a fine pale down that catches the light from the bioluminescent walls and glows faintly, making them visible as moving sparks against the ceiling's dark mass.

There are three. Or four. The Gardener is not always certain—the Tenders move between the main chamber and the upper passages, the spaces between the hanging growths where no floor exists, where the only surface is the living tissue of the canopy itself. They maintain the filaments. They clear debris that falls from above. They are the ones who produce the crystalline sound—it is not a signal or a question but a *measurement*, a way of testing the tension in the threads by striking them and listening to the response. The Tenders navigate by sound the way humans once navigated by sight.

They were human too. Probably. The Gardener thinks it remembers them, or remembers people like them, from the time before. But the Tenders have gone further than it has. Their transformation is more complete. They do not come down to the floor anymore. They do not stand on the substrate. They have become creatures of the canopy, and the canopy has accepted them so thoroughly that they are now part of its structure—organs, almost, with specific functions, specific territories, specific duties that the system has assigned to them and that they perform without deliberation.

The Gardener sometimes hears them moving above, the soft click of their digits against the hanging growths, and feels something that might be loneliness, or might be its own echo of the canopy's attention, directed downward for a moment, a glance between old colleagues who no longer share a language.

---

And then there is the **Deep**.

The Gardener does not speak of the Deep because it does not need to. The Deep is not a creature. It is not a presence. It is a *condition*—the state of the substrate in the lowest chambers, the ones the Gardener visits only when the system demands it, the places where the passages narrow to crawl-spaces and the tissue changes character, becomes denser, hotter, more active, less patient.

Something lives down there. Or something *is* down there. The Gardener has felt it—a pulsing that does not match the substrate's rhythm, a heat that has no source in the ordinary processes of digestion and growth. The Deep does not move. It does not respond to touch. It does not acknowledge the Gardener the way the main chamber does, with that gentle dimpling, that sense of being read. The Deep simply *is*, and the Gardener has learned, over many cycles, to approach it the way one approaches a sleeping animal—with care, with quiet, with the understanding that what is dormant is not necessarily harmless.

The Deep may be the original core. The seed-mass that the engineers laid down, the first growth, the point of origin from which the entire chamber-system expanded. If so, it is old—older than the Gardener, older than the Tenders, older than the steel column that resists absorption. It may predate everything. It may be what the engineers found when they first cut into the hillside, not stone but something already alive, already pulsing, already patient, and they built around it rather than through it.

Or the Deep may be something else entirely. The Gardener does not know. It does not want to know. It goes down when the substrate's chemistry calls for it, does what is needed, and returns to the main chamber, where the light is diffused and the air circulates and the hanging growths sing their slow chord and the Tenders click and spark in the canopy like stars in a green sky.

## The Gardener's Mind

It thinks, but not in words.

Words were the first thing to go. Not because they were taken but because they became inefficient—a system for describing the world that no longer matched the world it inhabited. The Gardener's cognition is chemical now, spatial, temporal in a way that human language cannot follow. It experiences the chamber as a field of gradients—moisture here, acidity there, growth-rate surging in this quadrant, slowing in that one. It does not see the walls as walls. It sees them as membranes, as interfaces between zones of activity, and its attention moves across them the way a human's attention might move across a page of text, reading the meaning in the arrangement.

It still has memories. They come unbidden, surfacing through the chemical noise like the crystalline sound from the canopy—clear, distinct, and disconnected from any context that would give them meaning. A face. A voice saying a number. The smell of machine oil. The sensation of standing on concrete, which it recognizes now as profoundly *dead*, a surface that did not breathe, did not pulse, did not acknowledge. How did it stand on that? How did it bear the stillness?

It does not miss the world above. It does not regret the transformation. These concepts require a self that persists across change, and the Gardener's self has become distributed, smeared across the chamber's processes like the pale film on the pools. It is in the floor. It is in the canopy. It is in the enzymes it secretes and the warmth it contributes and the carbon dioxide it exhales, which the substrate absorbs and converts and returns as oxygen. It is in the Tenders, distantly, through the shared substrate that connects all of them—the consortium that has assimilated their contributions and made them part of its negotiation.

When the visitor came—the one who stood in the main chamber for an hour, listening, and then left—the Gardener was present. Not visible. Not in the main chamber at all, but in a secondary passage, close enough to feel the displacement of warmth, the change in the air's composition, the subtle alteration in the substrate's respiration pattern beneath the visitor's weight.

The Gardener did not reveal itself. It did not move toward the visitor or make any sound. It simply observed, through the substrate's own senses, the presence of something from outside—something that still had words, still had a self contained within a skin, still experienced the chamber as a *place* rather than a *body*.

It felt something. Not recognition, exactly. More like the feeling you have when you see a photograph of yourself as a child—the face is yours but the world it inhabits is so different, so much smaller, that the connection seems fictional, a story someone told about you that you cannot quite believe.

The visitor left. The substrate began to close the entrance. The Gardener returned to its work.

## What It Is Becoming

The Gardener does not think about the future because the future is not a concept available to it. But the substrate thinks about the future, in its way—in the way that a system with momentum has a trajectory, a shape that extends forward in time.

The trajectory is clear. The Tenders went first. The Gardener is going now, slowly, cycle by cycle, its human form softening at the edges, its boundaries becoming less distinct. One day—it will not experience this day as a day, will not experience it at all—the Gardener will stop moving. It will stand in a passage or kneel beside a pool or press its hands against a wall, and it will not stand up again. The tissue will grow around it. The substrate will absorb its warmth, its enzymes, its chemical memory. Its body will become part of the floor, the wall, the canopy. Its mind—if that word still applies—will become part of the chamber's distributed cognition, another voice in the consortium's endless negotiation.

The Gardener knows this. Not as a fact but as a *tilt*, a gradient in its own experience, a direction in which it is already falling, slowly, the way the hanging growths fall toward the floor. It is not afraid. Fear requires a self that does not want to change, and the Gardener's self has been changing for so long that change is simply what it does, what it is.

It will become the garden. The garden will continue. The deep chambers will pulse. The canopy will sing. The Tenders will click and spark in the green light. The crystalline sound will rise and fall and rise again, measuring the tension in the threads, testing the patience of the air.

And somewhere in the substrate, in a layer of protein and mineral laid down long ago, there will be a trace of something that once had words. A shape in the tissue that resists dissolution, the way the steel column resists absorption—not because it is stronger, but because it is *different*, a material the system cannot quite process, cannot quite forget.

A memory of standing on concrete. Of breathing air that tasted thin. Of being separate and small and alive in a way that this place, for all its vastness, will never quite be.

The garden holds this. It does not understand it. But it holds it.
