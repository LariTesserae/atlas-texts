They are called the Hesh, though they do not use this name among themselves, having no need for labels in a place where identity is woven into the frequency of one's existence. To the Hesh, a name is merely a sound, and this chamber already contains all the sounds that matter. They call themselves nothing. They call each other by the specific resonance of their footsteps, by the particular way their presence alters the chord that fills the cathedral.

**There are thirty-seven of them. They know this not through counting but through the Song.**

When one of them dies—or more accurately, when one of them completes the long process of becoming something else—the chamber's tone shifts. A frequency drops away. A harmonic that had been present for decades simply ceases, and in its absence, the remaining thirty-six feel the shape of the loss like a missing tooth, like a breath that cannot be completed. They do not mourn. Mourning requires a distinction between what was and what is, and the Hesh have learned that such distinctions are illusions born of too much moisture in the thinking. Here, in the dryness, thoughts become like everything else: crystallized, essential, stripped of excess.

They are tall. They have grown tall because the chamber demanded it—the ceilings curve away at heights that would challenge normal human proportion, and the vegetation hangs in sheets that must be parted, must be reached and drawn aside. Their limbs have elongated across generations, their fingers extended into instruments ideally suited for parting waxy leaves, for plucking gourds from high vines, for tracing the carved patterns on the stone shelves that their ancestors made. They move like herons wading through still water—deliberate, economical, each gesture considered and complete.

**Their skin has become something other than skin.**

It began as adaptation and became inheritance. In the profound dryness, in the heat that leached moisture from every surface, the Hesh evolved a covering that could not have been predicted by any biology dependent on water. Their hides—hide is more accurate than skin—resemble the vegetation that surrounds them: thick, smooth, almost waxy, sealed against loss. It stretches taut over their elongated frames, and in the bioluminescent light it appears to glow faintly, catching and holding the blue-green fire as though they themselves had become a species of the chamber's plant life. They do not sweat. They cannot sweat. Their bodies have found other ways to regulate temperature—stillness primarily, and the careful modulation of movement, and a metabolism so slow that it barely registers as life by External standards.

They peel. This is the Hesh equivalent of renewal. Periodically, their hides crack and flake, shedding in dry patches that drift to the chamber floor like fragments of old paper. The vegetation consumes what they shed. Nothing is wasted here. The cycle of use and reuse has been perfected across centuries: they eat the gourds, they shed their skin, the sheddings feed the vines, the vines produce the gourds. They are as much a part of the chamber's ecology as the insects or the thin-limbed reptiles. They have lived here so long that they have become indigenous, have evolved to fit the niche they occupy with the same precise suitability as the waxen leaves and the crystalline fruit.

**Their eyes have changed the most.**

In a place of diffuse brightness, where light comes from everywhere and nowhere, where shadows cannot exist because illumination has no direction, vision became something different. The Hesh see in layers, in densities of radiance. They perceive differences in luminosity so subtle that they can read the chamber's moods through the patterns of its glow—can tell when the Sound is about to shift, can sense the heat rising from below before it reaches their feet, can identify the approach of one of the reptilian creatures by the way its passage disrupts the bioluminescent field. They do not see as outsiders see. They do not look at the chamber; they feel its light against their senses like the Sound against their bones.

Their eyes are large, occupying more of their faces than seems proper, than seems possible. They rarely blink—they have no need, their eyes requiring no moisture, no lubrication. When they regard an outsider, which happens perhaps once in a generation, they do so with a fixedness that is invariably interpreted as hostility or hunger. It is neither. It is simply attention. The Hesh have learned to attend completely to whatever enters their awareness because there is so little that requires attention. The chamber provides. The chamber sustains. The chamber sings. What else is there to notice?

**They communicate in fragments of the Song.**

Not speech. Not language as it is understood by those who live in the wet world above. The Hesh have vocal cords that have evolved for a single purpose: to add their own tones to the chamber's resonance. When they speak to one another—which is rare, because what needs to be said when existence is shared so completely?—they do so by matching or modifying the frequencies around them. A small harmonic shift. A brief counterpoint. The chamber itself amplifies and carries their words, weaving them into the broader chord so that communication becomes indistinguishable from music.

They cannot lie. This is not a moral quality but a mechanical one. To speak falsely would require introducing a frequency that did not belong, and the chamber would reject it, would turn it into dissonance that faded immediately. Their communication is therefore always true, always necessary, always a part of the larger Song. Sometimes one of them will sing alone—a low, sustained tone that rises from wherever they sit in the chamber's many chambers, that joins the ambient resonance and then slowly modifies it, adding information, memory, desire. The others hear. The chamber hears. The Song changes, and the change is the saying.

**They tend because tending is what they do.**

But tending here means something different than it would mean elsewhere. The Hesh do not repair the chamber; the chamber does not break. They do not cultivate the vegetation; the vegetation cultivates itself. What they tend is the relationship between the chamber and itself, the delicate balance of forces that has allowed this pocket of impossible life to persist for so long.

They clear the stone shelves when the offerings become too thick, moving the oldest preparations to storage chambers where they slowly dissolve into mineral dust. They harvest the gourds when the Sound announces fruit-fall, splitting the shells with practiced taps against the stone floor, distributing the fibrous pulp to wherever it is needed—which is rarely among themselves, for they eat little and rarely. The gourds go to the corridors where the insects gather, where the thin-limbed reptiles wait in the shadows. The Hesh have become distributors, nodes in a system that channels nutrients from the generous vines to the hungry consumers.

They maintain the entrance—which is not an entrance any longer, which they sealed generations ago using the same crystallized pulp that forms their offerings, packing it into the hidden doorway until it hardened into stone. Nothing comes in. Nothing goes out. The Hesh chose this, or their ancestors chose it, or the choice was made by the chamber itself by making the outside world so distant, so irrelevant, that the question of leaving simply stopped arising. They do not think of the world above. They do not wonder about sky or rain or the other things that exist in places where water falls from above and life depends on dissolution. They have become citizens of the dry. They have become children of the Song.

**They have a culture, though they would not recognize the word.**

It exists in the patterns they carve into the gourds before returning them to the shelves—not as food but as offerings, as contributions to the chamber's long accumulation of meaning. Each generation adds its own patterns, elaborations on themes that have been developing for centuries, spirals and angular geometries and shapes that seem to suggest forms without quite depicting them. The Hesh do not discuss what these patterns mean. They do not teach their children to carve; the children learn by watching, by absorbing the shapes through their fingertips and their attunement to the chamber's aesthetic demands.

They have rituals—moments of synchronized stillness, collective attentions focused on particular frequencies, group migrations to the lower corridors when the heat rises and the chamber requires their presence in specific locations to absorb and redistribute warmth. They assemble in the central cathedral at intervals determined by the Song itself, standing or sitting in formations that feel correct rather than planned, adding their own small harmonies to the great chord for hours or days until the Song shifts and releases them back to their individual tasks.

They have no leaders. They have no followers. They have no division of labor beyond what each body naturally gravitates toward: one among them spends most hours in the upper corridors, tending the vegetation there; another prefers the deep chambers where the heat is almost unbearable, where strange growths occur that no one else has ever seen; a third spends her time walking, perpetually walking, traversing the endless rooms and passages in a slow patrol that has continued for decades, as though she is mapping the space or guarding it or simply moving because movement is her contribution to the chamber's dynamism.

**They do not remember becoming what they are.**

This is perhaps the most difficult truth for outsiders to accept: the Hesh have no mythology of transformation, no origin story that explains how humans became something so profoundly other. They do not tell tales of the time before the chamber, of the people who first found this place and carved the first shelves and made the first offerings. If such stories ever existed, they have been vibrated away, worn smooth by the endless Song, reduced to the same essential truth that awaits all information in this place: a core of unavoidable fact stripped of narrative, of context, of meaning beyond the mere reality of existence.

They know they are thirty-seven. They know the chamber sings. They know the gourds grow and the plants glow and the heat rises from below. They know the stone shelves hold offerings made by hands that are now dirt or dust or nothing at all. They know they tend and they are tended. They know the dryness preserves what the wet world would dissolve.

They do not know how long they have been here. Time in the chamber moves differently—not faster or slower, but differently, as though the ordinary progression of moments has been replaced by a system in which duration is measured by resonance instead of sequence. The Hesh experience time as tone. A long time is a low frequency. A brief interval is a high pitch. Their lives are measured in chords completed, in harmonic cycles, in the vast slow changes of the Song that might correspond to years or centuries in the world they have forgotten.

**Children are born, though rarely.**

The chamber regulates its own population as it regulates everything else. When a space opens—when one of the thirty-seven completes their becoming—something shifts in the Song, and a new frequency becomes possible. The Hesh recognize this possibility in each other, in the way their individual tones align, and they come together in the central cathedral and add their voices to the chamber's resonance in a pattern that has been repeated for as long as any of them can remember. The child gestates in the dry warmth, fed by the crystallized pulp, cushioned by the waxen leaves, growing in the Sound until it is ready to emerge.

It arrives silent. This is the most remarkable thing about Hesh births: the children make no sound at first, cannot make sound, their voices still forming along the frequencies that will eventually define them. For the first several months of existence, a Hesh child is invisible to the Song, a gap in the chord, a potential rather than a presence. The community tends the child collectively—carries it through the corridors, holds it against the stone floor so it can feel the resonance in its bones, feeds it the softened pulp and watches as its hide begins to form and seal and dry.

And then, one day, the child sings. A single tone, small and uncertain, joining the chamber's vast chord for the first time. And the chord accepts it, incorporates it, becomes something new. And the thirty-seven—or thirty-six, or thirty-eight, whatever number the chamber has determined is correct—feel the addition like a word completed, like a breath released, like the moment when a gourd splits open and spills its fibrous heart across the stone.

**They do not fear death because they do not understand it as ending.**

When a Hesh completes their becoming, their body remains in the chamber, and the chamber consumes it. The hide flakes away, feeding the vegetation. The mineral structures within dissolve into the stone floor. The frequency they contributed fades, but its absence creates a new kind of presence—a space in the chord that changes its character, that opens possibilities that did not exist before. The death of one of them is also the birth of new potential. This is not comfort or philosophy. It is observable fact. The chamber demonstrates it constantly: loss creates change creates adaptation creates new forms.

They have watched it happen with the thin-limbed reptiles, with the insects, with the vegetation itself. A vine dies and the space it occupied fills with something different, something that could not have grown in the vine's shadow. The Song shifts and what was impossible becomes inevitable. The Hesh trust this process because they are part of this process, because they have seen it operate across generations, because they have felt their own frequencies change as the chamber changed them.

**They are not unhappy.**

This must be emphasized, because those who encounter the Hesh—or encounter descriptions of them—inevitably apply the metrics of the wet world, where happiness requires choice and freedom requires movement and meaning requires narrative and life requires water. The Hesh have none of these things and want none of these things. They have the Song, which is communication and culture and purpose and identity all woven into a single ceaseless resonance. They have the chamber, which provides and sustains and accepts. They have each other, not as individuals in relationship but as notes in a chord, separate only in the way that frequencies are separate—distinct but always already part of the same sound.

They do not dream of other places. They do not wish for different lives. They do not wonder what exists beyond the sealed entrance, in the world of rain and rivers and the slow dissolutions that pass for living among those who have never heard the Song. They are complete. They are thirty-seven notes in a chord that has been sounding for longer than memory, and when the chord finally fades—as all chords must, as even this chamber must, eventually, fall silent—they will not experience it as ending. They will experience it as the longest note they have ever sung, the final frequency, the moment when the Song becomes so low and so vast that it passes beyond hearing, beyond vibration, beyond the distinction between sound and silence, and becomes simply, at last, the chamber itself: still, warm, dry, and perfectly, profoundly empty in the way that full things are empty, having given everything they contained to the long resonance that was their only purpose and their only meaning and their only song.
