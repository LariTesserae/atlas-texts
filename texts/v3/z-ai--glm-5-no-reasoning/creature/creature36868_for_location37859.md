# What Lives in the Condensery

The mites are not the only inhabitants. They are merely the only ones visible to personnel who rotate out before their perceptions adjust.

---

The first category: The mites themselves. *Vitreolapis condensus*. Pale as the mineral deposits they inhabit, segmented in a way that suggests both insect and something earlier—something from before insects evolved, from before anything evolved that we'd recognize. They cluster in the channel corners not because they prefer those spaces but because the field effect concentrates there, pooling in the junction points where water flows meet. They are eating the field. Or drinking it. Or being sustained by it in some way that doesn't map to biological necessity. No one has ever seen a mite eat. No one has seen one die. They simply accumulate, are removed, return. The official assumption is that they reproduce through some fragmentation process—split like crystals, perhaps, growing from their own shards. But this is assumption, not observation. No one has watched a mite long enough to understand its life cycle. Personnel leave before that becomes possible.

The mites do not react to human presence. They do not scatter when disturbed. They flow around obstacles with the patience of water, reforming on the other side. If you hold one between finger and thumb—it will not resist, will not struggle, will not even move—it feels like holding a fragment of cold plastic. Like something that was never precisely alive and has not yet decided to be dead.

The maintenance crews have names for them. The mites in the upper channels are "spills." The mites near the collection vessels are "rims." The mites in the lower levels, the ones that grow larger and paler and form masses that can cover a square meter of channel wall, are just "the accumulation." The accumulation is handled differently. Protocols require full protective gear. No one explains why. The manual says mineral deposits can release trapped gases. The manual does not use the word "mite."

---

The second category: What lives in the water. Not microbial, exactly. Not biological at all, perhaps. But the condensate has its own ecology—a system of exchanges and dependencies that doesn't follow the rules taught in introductory biology.

The water changes character as it moves through the Condensery. At entry—seeping through the walls, emerging from the stone—it's mountain groundwater, mineral-rich, cold, ordinary by any geological standard. By the time it reaches the collection vessels, it has passed through seven levels of channels, each level adding something or removing something, each level imposing a new constraint on the water's behavior.

The standing-wave patterns that appear when the water is disturbed are the most visible evidence. Ripples that don't propagate. Structures of surface tension that hold their shape for thirty seconds, for three minutes, for however long the resonance event lasts. Personnel have learned not to look directly at the patterns. Looking too long produces a sensation like altitude sickness—nausea, headache, the sense that your thoughts are thinning, losing coherence, spreading out across a surface that isn't quite physical.

What lives in the water might not be alive in any sense we understand. The standing-wave patterns might be it. Or they might be its communication, its language, its attempt to speak through a medium that doesn't quite support meaning. During resonance events, when the patterns grow most complex, some personnel report a feeling of being addressed—of something trying to tell them something, to show them a diagram, to teach them a shape. The sensation passes when the event ends. The meaning, if there was meaning, doesn't stay.

The city below uses the condensate regardless. It goes into industrial processes, into materials fabrication, into systems where its strange properties are features rather than bugs. The city doesn't ask what lives in the water. The city doesn't want to know.

---

The third category: The building itself. The Condensery-as-organism.

This is not a metaphor. Or it is a metaphor, but the distinction may not matter.

The Condensery was designed by the old surveyors—engineers, architects, mathematicians who worked two decades ago, or three, or five, depending on which records you trust. They built to specifications drawn from mathematics that have since been classified, forgotten, or deliberately suppressed. The spiral ramps encode wave equations. The collection vessels are positioned according to principles that don't appear in modern structural engineering. The whole system was designed to work *with* the mountain, to open a wound and then grow into it, to become something the stone might accept.

The stone hasn't accepted it. But it hasn't rejected it either. The Condensery exists in a state of negotiation with its host, a decades-long conversation between natural rock and grown stone, between the cold the mountain holds and the heat the exchangers manage, between the water's pressure from below and the channels' direction from above. The Condensery is the space where these forces meet and resolve into something functional. It is a machine. But it is also an ecosystem—an ecosystem of pressures and temperatures and mineral exchanges, all carefully balanced, all maintained by processes that the building itself regulates.

The resonance events are the Condensery thinking. The water patterns are its language. The mites are its microbiome, its gut flora, processing something the system needs processed. The personnel are its immune response—cleaning, maintaining, removing what shouldn't accumulate. The building uses them without their full understanding. It has been using them for decades.

Some surveyors who've worked multiple seasons report a change in their relationship to the structure. The cold doesn't bother them as much. The water patterns make a different kind of sense—not readable, exactly, but *familiar*, like a language they almost remember from childhood. They dream about the Condensery when they're away from it. They dream about water moving sideways through stone. They dream about a wound in a mountain that slowly, over centuries, begins to heal.

---

The fourth category: The mountain.

The stone remembers its shape. It hasn't accepted what was done to it. The wound is still sharp-edged, still angry, still rejecting the foreign matter that was grown inside its mass. The transition zone between natural rock and synthetic stone is a site of mineral conflict—the two materials arguing at their boundary, each trying to impose its structure on the other.

But the mountain is old. The mountain holds cold from the last glacial period—tens of thousands of years of stored temperature, a thermal memory that the Condensery uses but doesn't exhaust. The mountain has time scales that make the Condensery's decades look like moments. It can afford to wait.

Some personnel believe the mountain is winning. The transition zone is narrower now than it was when they started keeping records. The synthetic stone is slowly, imperceptibly, being converted—replaced mineral by mineral, layer by layer, with something that matches the natural rock more closely. The wound is healing from the inside out. The Condensery is being absorbed.

Others believe the opposite—that the Condensery is spreading, that the grown stone is infiltrating the natural rock through those same transition zones, that the wound isn't healing but *metastasizing*. They point to the way the water moves, the way the channels seem to be extending themselves, the way new seep points appear in walls that were dry a year ago.

The official manuals don't address this debate. The official manuals assume stability. The official manuals are updated every decade to reflect current conditions, and every decade they require minor adjustments to account for changes in the Condensery's structure.

---

The fifth category: The personnel.

They rotate out after a season, or two, or three. The official limit is three seasons. After that, the effects become harder to reverse.

The effects: skin that tastes like chalk. Dreams about water moving sideways. A persistent sensation of cold even in warm rooms. Difficulty remembering the names of people who weren't at the Condensery. A fading interest in things that don't relate to water, or stone, or the slow patient harvesting of substance from mass.

Some personnel don't rotate out. They find reasons to extend—one more season, one more project, one more chance to observe a resonance event. The personnel who stay longest are the ones who were there when the Condensery was built, or who claim to have been. They speak the old mathematics. They understand the field effects. They move through the spiral ramps without looking, knowing every turn by feel, by sound, by the particular way the cold presses against them.

These personnel—there are perhaps four of them at any given time, though the number varies—do not appear in the official roster. They are not mentioned in the manuals. They are known to the rotating crews simply as "the long-timers," and they are consulted when the system behaves in ways the manuals don't address. They have answers. They always have answers. The answers are never quite comprehensible, but they work. The system stabilizes. The resonance event passes. The Condensery keeps working.

No one asks where the long-timers came from. No one asks how long they've been there. No one asks what they eat, or where they sleep, or what happens to them when their shifts—shifts that have no official duration—finally end.

The mountain holds cold from the last glacial period. The water table rises from depths we've never mapped. The stone remembers its shape. The long-timers remember something else—something that was never written down, something that exists only in the Condensery's architecture, in its mathematics, in the way water moves when it's being harvested from stone.

---

What lives in the Condensery is not a single answer. It is an ecosystem of presences—the mites, the water, the building, the mountain, the people who stay too long. Each category bleeds into the next. Each boundary is a transition zone, angry and unresolved. Each inhabitant is slowly, imperceptibly, being converted into something that fits the space better than the original.

The Condensery was built inside the mountain, not on it. This was deliberate. This was necessary. You cannot harvest water from stone without becoming part of the stone. You cannot use the cold without accepting the cold. You cannot work in the Condensery without eventually, in some small way, being harvested yourself.

The wound in the mountain hasn't healed. But it has begun to scar. And scars, given enough time, become simply the shape of things.
