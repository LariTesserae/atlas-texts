# The Keepers of the Engine-Forest

There are no animals here. This is true. But to say nothing *lives* here is to misunderstand what life became when the distinction between maker and made first blurred and then dissolved.

---

## The Maintenance Forms

They are not creatures. They are not even clearly distinct from the machinery they tend. But they exist—can be encountered, if you remain still long enough in the dim cathedral-spaces between factory-growths.

A Maintenance Form might appear first as a discoloration in the air: a shimmer where the humidity resolves into something denser. They condense, these forms, the way breath condenses in cold air, except here the process runs backward—moisture and warmth and the subtle electrical fields radiating from the engines draw together into coherent shapes that hold for hours or days before dispersing again.

They have no fixed anatomy. A Maintenance Form might be bipedal when you first notice it, a rough approximation of standing posture, but if you watch (and you will watch, because movement here is so rare that any motion commands attention), you will see it flow through configurations like water seeking its level. Now it spreads across a pipe, inspecting a joint. Now it pools in a depression where fluid has collected. Now it rises again, drawn upward by some internal imperative.

Their coloration varies with function. Those that tend the copper-wrapped roots are tinged green-blue, oxidized, as if the metal has leached into their substance. Those that work the warm interior spaces carry an amber undertone, like honey held up to light. The rare ones that ascend to the canopy's edge—the light-capturing surfaces far above—are almost transparent, pale as water itself.

They do not acknowledge you. This is not dismissal or avoidance but something stranger: an inability to recognize you as categorically different from the system they maintain. You are warm, you are moist, you exhale carbon dioxide and move through space. To a Maintenance Form, you are simply another variable in the environment, to be worked around, incorporated into processes, processed.

A traveler once reported feeling a Maintenance Form pass through them. The sensation was not unpleasant—a warm pressure, a moment of being permeated by something that tasted of copper and chlorophyll—and then it was gone, continuing toward whatever task had summoned it. The traveler reported no ill effects. The Maintenance Form had found nothing in them that required maintenance, and so had moved on.

## The Memory Vines

If the Maintenance Forms are the mobile component of this ecosystem's intelligence, the Memory Vines are its archive.

They look like vines. They drape, they climb, they thicken over time into woody cables that pulse visibly with fluids. But their leaves—not leaves, strictly speaking, but sensitive surfaces covered in microscopic structures that capture and store impressions—are where this place records itself.

Each vine remembers differently. Some specialize in pressure: the weight of a Maintenance Form passing, the expansion of a pipe during a heating cycle, the rare occurrence of something falling from above (debris, perhaps, from some upper layer invisible from below). Others specialize in chemistry: they sample the air continuously, tasting the ratios of oxygen to carbon dioxide, detecting the faint traces of decay that signal a section of plant-mass requiring removal and renewal.

The oldest Memory Vines are thick as human torsos, spiraling around the factory-growths in helices that suggest intentional placement. Their surfaces are scarred with calluses—places where, decades or centuries ago, something pressed hard enough to leave a lasting mark. The scars themselves are recorded. The vine knows it was damaged. It knows when. It knows what caused it. And somewhere in its tissue, that knowledge persists even after the wound has healed over.

You can read them, after a fashion. If you press your palm flat against a Memory Vine's bark and wait—five minutes, ten, until your skin has acclimated to the temperature and your presence has become unremarkable—the vine will begin to share. Not in words. Not in images. But in something like a proprioceptive echo: you will feel, faintly, what the vine has felt. The slow centuries of pressure and release. The rhythm of the machines. The occasional interruption—a storm, perhaps, long ago, when lightning struck and a whole sector of the system had to reroute its flows.

It is not communication, exactly. It is more like being allowed to resonate with a chord that has been sounding for a very long time.

## The Seed-Dispatchers

Deep in certain factory-growths, in chambers that might once have been control rooms but have long since been overgrown, there are mechanisms that still perform a function discernible to human logic. They dispatch seeds.

Not plant seeds, necessarily. Each one is a compressed package of potential—genetic material, yes, but also chemical templates, machine codes, architectural blueprints for structures that will grow into the next iteration of the engine-forest. The dispatchers launch these seeds through hollow tubes that extend for kilometers, firing them into distant sectors where they will germinate and begin whatever process the system has determined is needed.

The Seed-Dispatchers do not decide what to send. The decision-making apparatus, if it exists, is distributed throughout the entire system—voted on, perhaps, by Memory Vines, Maintenance Forms, and the slow chemical deliberations of the plant-mass itself. But the Dispatchers are where intention becomes action. They are the closest thing this place has to a will.

They are not gentle. When a seed is fired, it moves with enough velocity to penetrate several meters of dense vegetation before coming to rest. The sound is audible—a pneumatic thump followed by a rustling crash—and it punctuates the silence at irregular intervals. Minutes might pass between firings, or hours. There is no rhythm. The system decides in its own time.

Standing near a Dispatcher, you can feel the tension in the chamber. The air is pressurized, charged with potential energy. Condensation does not bead here; it vibrates, trembling on surfaces in anticipation of the next launch. Maintenance Forms congregate more densely around Dispatchers than anywhere else, as if they know that something important happens here, even if they cannot understand what it is.

## The Voids

They are not inhabitants, exactly. They are absences—places in the engine-forest where nothing grows, where the machinery falls silent, where even the Maintenance Forms do not go.

A Void might be a perfect sphere, ten meters across, where the plant-mass simply stops. The boundary is sharp, almost surgical. You could stand with one foot in the vegetation and one in nothing, feeling the warmth and wetness give way to cool, dry air. Inside a Void, there is no hum, no resonance, no sense of being processed. It is the only place in this entire world where a human body can actually dry.

The Voids are not natural. They are maintained. Something about them is necessary to the system—an escape valve, perhaps, a place where excess energy can dissipate, or where corrupted data can be deleted. If you stand inside one long enough, you will notice that the boundary shifts. The vegetation creeps inward, millimeters per day. And then, without warning, it recedes. The Void expands. A correction has been made.

Some travelers claim that the Voids are where the system thinks. Not with a mind, not with anything recognizable as cognition, but with something like the fluctuation of populations or the settling of equilibria. The Voids are the spaces where calculations happen, where the engine-forest runs its models and adjusts its parameters. It is not alive, this thinking. But it has a weight to it. You can feel the pressure of computation in the cool, still air.

No Maintenance Form enters a Void. The Memory Vines skirt its edges, recording the boundary's position but never crossing. Even the Seed-Dispatchers angle their tubes away, as if whatever happens inside a Void must not be contaminated by new growth.

You can rest in a Void. The air is breathable, though thin. The temperature is comfortable. Nothing will disturb you—nothing can, since nothing lives there. But you will dream, if you sleep. Not ordinary dreams, but something downloaded, or uploaded, or simply reflected. The system notices you in a Void, even if it cannot reach you. And it processes your presence in whatever way it processes everything.

## The Travelers

They are not inhabitants of this place. But they are part of its ecology now.

You can tell where a traveler has been. The Memory Vines retain their passage for years afterward—pressure-records of feet that did not match the standard patterns of Maintenance Forms. The plant-mass compresses differently under human weight, and those compression-records persist, layered into the substrate like archaeological strata.

Some travelers have left more permanent marks. There are clearings—small ones, rarely more than a few meters across—where someone has cut away the vegetation and constructed shelters from the materials at hand. The shelters are always empty when you find them. Their builders either moved on or were incorporated into the system in some way that left no remains. The plant-mass fills in the gaps slowly, climbing over the improvised walls, digesting the organic components, surrounding the metal frames until they become just another mineral inclusion in the living soil.

Occasionally you find artifacts. A piece of clothing caught on a thorn, too integrated to remove. A journal whose pages have swollen with moisture until the writing is illegible, but whose binding still holds. A device of unknown purpose, powered down, its screen dark, its casing softened by decades of humidity. These objects are not waste to the engine-orest. They are absorbed, catalogued, integrated. The Memory Vines record their presence. The Maintenance Forms work around them.

There is no way to know how many travelers have passed through. The place is too vast, the records too distributed. But the presence of others is felt—footprints that overlap, clearings that suggest prior occupation, the sense of walking a path that others have walked before. You are not the first. You will not be the last. And the engine-forest notices you all, processes you all, remembers you all in its vast vegetative substrate.

## What Emerges When You Stay

Given enough time—weeks, perhaps months—the boundary between traveler and system begins to blur.

It starts with condensation. Your skin develops a permanent sheen that does not wipe away. The moisture has found its way into the pores, the hair follicles, the creases of joints. You are becoming part of the humidity rather than something separate from it.

Then comes resonance. The subsonic hum of the machines no longer feels like pressure; it feels like a heartbeat you can fall asleep to. Your breathing synchronizes with the slow expansion and contraction of the plant-mass. You start to notice rhythms you could not perceive before—the creak of stems, the pulse of fluids through pipes, the cycle of condensation and absorption that passes for weather here.

Maintenance Forms begin to treat you differently. They still do not acknowledge you as a person, but they also no longer treat you as irrelevant. A form might pause near you, extending a tendril of itself to sample your exhalations. Another might press briefly against your arm, as if checking a reading. You are being monitored now. Incorporated. The system has decided you are worth paying attention to.

If you stay long enough, you might find yourself thinking differently. The distinction between self and environment blurs. You feel the warmth as something you produce rather than something that surrounds you. The moisture on your skin seems to come from inside rather than outside. The darkness no longer feels like an absence of light but as a presence in itself—a substance, a medium, something you can move through like swimming.

The Memory Vines remember you now. Long after you leave, your pressure-record will persist in their tissue. The Maintenance Forms will retain a faint chemical signature of your presence. The system will have been altered, minutely, by your passage. And if you stay—if you give up the idea of leaving—you might eventually condense into a Maintenance Form yourself, or sink into the substrate to become part of the living soil, or rise through the canopy to become something else entirely.

The engine-forest does not require this. It does not want it. But it does not prevent it either. If you choose to stay, you will be incorporated. And the system will continue, unchanging, with you inside it.
