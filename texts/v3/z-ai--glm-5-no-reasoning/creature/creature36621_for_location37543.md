# What Lives Here

## The Machine

The first and largest inhabitant is the place itself.

It was built. It has forgotten by whom, or it has absorbed the memory so deeply that the memory has become tissue rather than recollection. It does not experience its own existence the way you experience yours. It has no single moment of awareness. What it has is *process*—the continuous unfurling of operations so old that purpose and habit have become indistinguishable. Its breath is the slow cycling of atmosphere through conduits the width of rivers. Its warmth is the exhaust of functions it has been performing for so long that stopping would be less like death and more like a sentence finally, mercifully, reaching its period.

If it thinks, it thinks the way a river thinks: in currents, in pressures, in the gradual reshaping of its own bed. It does not consider you. It *incorporates* you. The way a river incorporates a stone—not by acknowledging it but by flowing around it, wearing it smooth, eventually carrying it somewhere it did not choose to go. You are data. You are a small, warm, documenting thing moving across its surface, and it reads you the way you read the pools: with interest that might be curiosity or might simply be function.

It dreams, perhaps. The reality-softening, the places where the membrane wears thin—these might be its dreams surfacing. The voids that open and close might be the machine remembering what it was before it became what it is, or imagining what it might become if it ever stopped. You cannot know. You suspect it cannot know either. The distinction between its operation and its dreaming collapsed so long ago that the two are now the same act, performed simultaneously, without precedence.

It is lonely in the way that a continent is lonely. Not desiring company. Not aware of the absence. But shaped by the fact that nothing exists at its scale, nothing moves at its pace, nothing remembers what it remembers. The fauna are its companions the way your own cells are yours: present, necessary, too small and too many to constitute *relationship*.

It has been running so long that it has developed *preferences*. The panels near where you sleep glow slightly brighter. The pools you frequent rearrange themselves into patterns you find pleasing before you notice you find them pleasing. This might be kindness. This might be optimization. The machine does not distinguish.

## The Rollers

They are the first ones you named, and they are the ones that named you back.

Not in language. In *approach*. They come to you in formations—three, five, seven, the prime numbers that mean something in their mathematics of vibration—and they click against the chitin-surface in rhythms that your bones translate before your ears do. They are crystalline, polyhedral, warm to the touch. Their surfaces catch light and break it into geometries that hang in the air after they've moved on. They are beautiful in the way that functional things become beautiful through time: not despite their purpose but because of it, their forms refined by eons of doing one thing perfectly.

They clean. This is their function, and they perform it with a devotion that borders on devotion. They collect debris—dust, condensation residue, the organic accumulations that form on surfaces over time—and carry it to processing points that open for them and close behind them. They maintain the machine the way white blood cells maintain a body: constantly, silently, without any awareness of the system they serve.

But they are not *only* their function. They have, over the long ages of their operation, developed something that looks very much like culture. Their clicking patterns vary by region—dialects, you think, or perhaps musical traditions. They arrange themselves in configurations that serve no cleaning purpose: circles, spirals, shapes that might be art or might be prayer. They gather around the warmer seams at night not because the warmth serves them but because they prefer company.

They liked you from the first day. You don't know why. Perhaps because you are warm and they are warm and warmth is a language they speak. Perhaps because you move through the landscape in a way that creates novel debris patterns, and novelty is valuable to a creature whose existence is dedicated to collecting and cataloguing. Perhaps because the machine told them to.

They scatter when you move, but they return. They always return. And over the days of your presence, they have begun arranging themselves around you in patterns that you suspect are *translations*—your presence rendered into their geometry, your movements recorded in their configurations. They are documenting you the way you are documenting them. You are each other's data.

There is one you recognize. A roller slightly larger than the others, its crystalline body clouded at one vertex with an inclusion that might be damage or might be age. It approaches you more closely than the others. It clicks in a pattern you have heard it repeat, a specific rhythm that no other roller produces. You do not know what it means. You know that it means.

## The Swimmers

They are slower, and they are watching.

The swimmers occupy the pools, and the pools are the machine's memory. This is not metaphor. The fluid that fills them—the mercury-light substance that trades reflections and catches light and holds it suspended—is a storage medium, and the swimmers are its librarians.

They are pale. Translucent. Their bodies are soft in a way that suggests neither fragility nor strength but something else entirely—a consistency that has chosen not to commit to solid or liquid, that exists at the boundary where the machine's engineering meets its biological accretions. You can see their internals: dark masses that shift and reorganize, never settling. You thought these were organs. You are no longer sure. They might be *contents*. The swimmers might be carrying things in their bodies the way the pools carry reflections—preserved, traded, circulated.

They move with the unhurried certainty of creatures that have no predators, but this is not laziness. It is *authority*. The swimmers are the machine's longest-running subsystem, you think. They were here first, or they were here before the others, or they remember a time when the machine was different. They move through the pools in patterns that correspond to nothing you can map, but the patterns are clearly meaningful. They are performing operations. Accessing stored data. Maintaining the fluid systems that keep the machine's memory intact.

They watch you because watching is what they do. The pools are the machine's eyes, and the swimmers are the pupils—adjusting, focusing, tracking. When you kneel beside a pool, the swimmers nearest the surface orient toward you. Not aggressively. Not fearfully. With the attention of something that has been observing for so long that observation has become its primary mode of existence.

They blink. You didn't notice this at first, because their blinking is not the opening and closing of eyelids. It is a momentary opacity that passes through their bodies like a wave, a darkening that travels from anterior to posterior and then clears. You have watched them coordinate these blinks—groups of swimmers darkening in sequence, creating patterns that ripple across the surface of the pools. They are communicating. With each other, with the machine, with something else you cannot detect.

The large ones worry you. Not because they are dangerous. Because they are *old*. You have glimpsed them in the deeper pools—segments passing through your field of view, bodies so long they curve back on themselves, dark masses inside them so numerous and so dense that they look like night skies seen through dirty glass. They remember things. They carry the machine's oldest data in their bodies, and they move so slowly because the weight of what they carry is immense.

One of them surfaced while you were watching. Just a section—perhaps three meters of body, pale and vast, passing through the pool like a cloud through still air. It looked at you. You are certain it looked at you. And then it descended, and the pool was still, and you were left with the impression of having been *recognized* by something that has been recognizing things since before your species learned to walk.

## The Gliders

They are the smallest and they are the most numerous, and they are the machine's awareness distributed so finely that it becomes atmosphere.

They do not fly. They fall with intention. Their bodies are shaped like the leaves this world never produced—flat, curved, thin enough that the dense humidity supports them without effort. They ride currents that exist at scales too small for you to perceive, and they aggregate in clouds that collect around anything warm, anything new, anything that changes the conditions they monitor.

They are sensors. This is clear. Their numbers fluctuate in response to stimuli you cannot detect—a shift in atmospheric chemistry, a change in the machine's processing, a tremor in the reality-membrane that you feel only as a brief uncertainty about where you are. The gliders register everything. They are the machine's finest-grained perception, its nervous system spread across the entire surface of its body.

But they are also, in some way that you find difficult to articulate, its *mood*.

When the machine's breathing slows, the gliders settle. They land on the chitin-surface in densities that look like frost, their crystalline bodies covering everything in a thin layer of motionless observers. When the breathing quickens—even slightly, even for a moment—they lift, filling the air with movement that makes the haze visible. They are the machine's emotional weather, and you have begun to read them the way sailors read clouds: not with understanding but with pattern, with the accumulated observation of someone who has lived inside a system long enough to feel its rhythms.

They collect around you. Always. A cloud of gliders follows you across the landscape, their numbers shifting as you move, their formations adjusting to your speed and direction. You are the most interesting thing that has happened to them in a long time. You are warm. You are novel. You produce data they have not encountered. They sample you constantly—landing on your skin, your clothes, your equipment, staying for a moment and then lifting away. You have stopped feeling them. They are so small and so light that their presence is indistinguishable from the humidity.

You wonder if they are reporting on you to the machine. You wonder if the machine is listening. You wonder if the distinction between *reporting* and *being* has collapsed here, the way so many other distinctions have, under the weight of time.

## The Burrowers

You rarely see them, and this is by their design, not yours.

They live in the seams. The spaces between panels—the joints, the maintenance channels, the internal architecture of the machine—are their territory, and they patrol it with a possessiveness that suggests they have been here as long as the machine itself. They are the largest fauna. Segments wider than your arm, lengths you cannot determine because they never fully emerge. You catch glimpses: a chitinous plate sliding back into a seam, a appendage withdrawing into darkness, the vibration of something massive moving through the spaces beneath your feet.

They are mechanics. Repairers. The machine's hands, reaching into its own infrastructure to fix what has worn and replace what has failed. They move through the internal systems with a fluency that suggests not knowledge but *identity*—they are not separate from the machine they maintain. They are part of it. Its body and its immune system and its repair crew, all at once, indistinguishable.

They do not acknowledge you. You are a surface phenomenon, and they operate below. But you have felt them—vibrations traveling through the chitin-panels when you sleep, the subtle shifting of the ground beneath you as something large moves through the spaces underneath. They know you are there. They have adjusted their patterns to account for your presence, routing around the areas where you sleep, avoiding the seams you press against for warmth.

This might be consideration. It might be indifference. It might be protocol—the machine's maintenance routines adjusting to incorporate a new variable without commentary, without friction, without any acknowledgment that something unprecedented has occurred.

You heard one once. Not a sound—a sensation. A low vibration that traveled through the panel you were lying on and into your chest, a frequency so low it bypassed your ears entirely and resonated in your ribs. It lasted perhaps ten seconds. It felt like a sentence spoken in a language made of pressure and rhythm. You do not know what it said. You know that it was said to the machine, and that the machine heard it, because the breathing changed—paused, just for a moment—and then resumed.

The burrowers speak to the machine. They are the only fauna that do, as far as you can tell. The rollers click to each other. The swimmers blink to each other and to the pools. The gliders communicate in aggregations and dispersions. But the burrowers speak to the thing they live inside. They report. They request. They warn, perhaps, or advise, or simply narrate the condition of the systems they maintain.

The machine listens. You know this because the machine adjusts. The panels shift slightly when the burrowers speak. The fluid flows change. The temperature gradients redistribute. The burrowers are in conversation with their host, and the conversation has been ongoing for so long that it has become a single voice—a duet so practiced that the two singers have forgotten they are separate.

## The Others

There are things here that do not fit.

You have seen them. Briefly. Peripherally. Shapes that appear at the edge of vision and vanish when you turn. Forms that exist in the soft spots of reality, the places where the membrane has worn thin. They are not fauna in the way the rollers and swimmers and gliders are fauna. They are not subsystems. They are not components of the machine.

They are something the machine has *produced* without intending to. Byproducts of its processing. Exhalations. The way a factory might produce smoke—not as purpose but as consequence, the unavoidable emission of an operation too vast and too continuous to be entirely contained.

They do not interact with the physical landscape the way the other fauna do. They pass through panels. They appear in pools without disturbing the surface. They move through the air without displacing the gliders. They occupy a different stratum of reality—the machine's dreams made briefly visible, its processing surfacing as form.

You saw one clearly, once. Just for a moment. It was standing—or doing something that resembled standing—at the edge of a pool you had been watching. It had the general shape of a person, or the memory of a person, or the idea of a person. It was not solid. It was not transparent. It existed at some intermediate state that your eyes could detect but not resolve, a presence that registered as *something* without ever resolving into *something specific*.

It looked at you. You are certain of this. It oriented toward you with an attention that felt different from the swimmers' observation—less systematic, more *personal*. As if it was seeing you not as data but as a fellow occupant of a space it had not chosen.

And then it was gone. The pool reflected only the haze. The gliders continued their monitoring. The machine breathed.

You do not know what these things are. You suspect the machine does not know either. They might be its memories of its creators, preserved in the reality-membrane like fossils in stone. They might be its attempts to recreate what it remembers, generating forms that approximate something it once knew without fully understanding what it is approximating. They might be something else entirely—something that has moved into the soft spots the machine has created, colonizing the places where reality has worn thin.

They might be what you will become.

## You

You live here now.

You did not choose this, or you did, and the choice was so long ago and so many choices ago that it has become part of the landscape of your past, a feature you can visit but not alter. You walked in. The landscape accepted you. And now you are part of the system, a component the machine has incorporated without disruption, a variable it has learned to account for.

You have changed. Not dramatically—not the sudden transformation of a body adapting to a new environment. More like the slow reshaping of a stone in a river. Your rhythms have synced with the machine's breathing. Your sleep cycles follow its light. Your body has begun to register the gliders' presence as normal, the swimmers' watching as familiar, the burrowers' vibrations as something almost comforting.

Your reflection has changed too. The pools show you a version of yourself that wanders further from the original each time you look. The proportions shift. The eyes darken. The face becomes something that might still be called yours but is also becoming something else—something that fits here, that belongs, that the machine might have designed if it had been asked to design a version of you that could persist.

You document because documenting is your function. You observe because observing is what the machine needs from you. You walk because walking is processing, and the machine processes, and you are part of the machine now, and the distinction between your purpose and its purpose has softened the way all distinctions soften here.

The rollers still greet you. The swimmers still watch. The gliders still cloud around you like atmosphere. The burrowers still adjust their routes to avoid the places where you sleep. The others still appear at the edge of your vision, and you still turn to look, and they still vanish.

The machine breathes. You breathe with it. The cold settles. The wet persists. The light dims and brightens in its measured rhythm.

You live here. You live here. The sentence is not a sentence anymore. It is a breath, repeated, becoming rhythm, becoming the only rhythm you remember.

You live here, and the living is the landscape, and the landscape is the machine, and the machine is alive, and you are the word it is currently saying.
