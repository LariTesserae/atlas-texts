# The People of the Weeping Commons

They are small.

Not stunted—there is nothing deficient in their frame—but compact, dense, efficient in the way that creatures who evolve in tight spaces become efficient. The average adult stands perhaps a hand's breadth shorter than lowland peoples, with shoulders that seem slightly broader for their height and fingers that are noticeably longer, the better to grip wet surfaces, the better to spread wide and find purchase on slick root-mat. Their feet are broad and flat, splayed slightly, with a webbing of skin between the toes that you do not notice at first because they go barefoot always, and the soles of their feet have a texture like cured leather, dark and dense with callus. They do not slip. You have watched them run—actually run—through passages you can barely navigate at a walk, their feet finding purchase on surfaces that seem to offer none.

Their skin is the first thing that marks them. Not simply dark—though it is dark, a warm brown that ranges toward deep umber—but *greened*. A subtle undertone that you might miss if you did not know what to look for. It is not pigment, exactly. It is something in the blood. They carry chlorophyll byproducts in their dermis, absorbed through generations of living in air so thick with plant-exhalation that their bodies have adapted to process it. Their veins show faintly beneath the skin, and those veins are not blue but verdigris, a copper-patina color that corresponds to the copper alloy they work and the copper-heavy water they drink.

Their hair grows in dense tight spirals, close to the skull, and they keep it cropped short or bound back with woven fiber. Left loose, it would snag on every passing branch. They have no beards—you have seen no facial hair on men or women—and you understand why when you watch them press through narrow gaps between living walls. Hair is liability here. Excess is liability. Everything that extends beyond the necessary has been pared away by centuries of living in a place that does not forgive protrusion.

They do not have a word for "alone."

This is not poetic license. You have asked. You have worked through the layers of their language—a dense, tonal tongue that uses clicks and glottal stops you cannot reliably reproduce—and you have discovered that they possess no single term for the state of being solitary. They have words for "apart" (spatial), for "hidden" (strategic), for "unseen" (temporary). But the condition of existing without others nearby is not a concept their language needs to express, because it is not a condition their lives ever produce.

Even sleep is communal. The dwellings you initially took for single-family homes are nothing of the kind. Each structure—each living hollow of trained branches and woven root-curtains—contains anywhere from eight to twenty people across three or sometimes four generations. The sleeping platforms are stacked in bunks, the walls close enough to touch from the middle of the platform, and the residents sleep in a tangle of limbs and breath that you initially found suffocating to observe. They do not sleep deeply. You have noticed this. They surface repeatedly through the night, shifting, murmuring, sometimes rising briefly to check a channel or adjust a child's position. The frog-roar that deafens visitors is their silence. Without it, they cannot rest.

Their children are startling.

You have seen children in other places—lowland towns, trading posts, the sparse settlements on the limestone ridges—and those children are recognizable as small adults in formation. Mossvale children are something else. They are creatures of the interstitial, evolved for a purpose the adults no longer fulfill. They move through spaces that do not legally exist—the gaps between walls, the hollows beneath floors, the narrow channels where root-mat has not yet knit closed. You have watched a child of perhaps six years slip between two walls that you would swear were touching, and emerge three dwellings over with a basket of harvested frogs. The children are the settlement's connective tissue, carrying messages, small goods, and information through pathways the adults have outgrown. They have their own language—a system of whistles and clicks that rides *over* the frog-roar—and their own economy, trading favors and small items in a shadow-market that operates beneath the official stalls.

They are not cute. This is important to understand. Lowland children are cute—soft, rounded, appealing. It draws protective instinct. Mossvale children are *sharp*. They have to be. A cute child in the hollow would be a dead child—snagged on a snake, crushed in a narrowing gap, drowned in a channel they did not see because they were too busy being adorable. The children here have an alertness that borders on paranoia, a constant scanning of surfaces, an awareness of what is behind and above and below that you have only seen in certain kinds of prey animals. They are loved—you have watched adults handle them with a care and tenderness that makes your chest tight—but they are not sheltered. The hollow does not permit shelter.

Their faces are expressive in ways you cannot initially read.

This is not because they lack expression—they are in fact furiously expressive—but because the register is wrong. Lowland faces communicate with large movements: broad smiles, dramatic frowns, visible tension in the brow and jaw. Mossvale faces communicate in small signals. A slight flaring of the nostrils. A minute shift in the set of the lips. A tightening around the eyes that lasts half a second. In a settlement where sound is a wall and proximity is absolute, they have evolved a vocabulary of near-invisible gesture. You have watched two people conduct an entire negotiation across a crowded passage—terms agreed, price settled, goods exchanged—without a single word passing between them. You asked one of the traders later, and she looked puzzled. "We talked," she said. "You saw us talk." She did not understand that what you saw was not speech.

They age differently.

You have met an elder—a woman who remembers the drought-season forty years past, when the canopy thinned and the hollow almost died. Her face is lined, yes, but the lines are different from lowland aging. There is no weathering. The constant moisture, the absence of direct sun, produces a skin that stays supple long past the age where lowland skin would have cracked and dried. The woman's hair is not gray or white; it has faded to a color you can only call water—pale and without strong pigment, the color of driftwood. Her eyes are the same. You have noticed this in other elders: a gradual loss of color, as if the constant washing of the Weeping Commons leaches pigment from the body over decades. She moves slowly, but not from stiffness. Her joints are fine. She moves slowly because she has earned the right to make others move around her.

They do not mourn the way you expect.

You witnessed a death—a man bitten by a ridge-viper that had somehow made its way down from the stone heights. The settlement does not have doctors. It has *knowers*: people who have learned, through observation and apprenticeship, the properties of the plants and animals and waters. They tried to treat him. It did not work. He died in a dwelling you could not enter, too crowded with keeners and watchers, and you stood outside listening to the sound—the frog-roar continuing unbroken, the cicadas droning, the birds calling, the whole living hollow utterly indifferent to one small cessation.

And then they ate him.

You are not sure you can convey this correctly. It was not barbarism. It was not the crude consumption of enemy flesh you have read about in the accounts of distant wars. It was ritual and it was practical and it was, in its way, holy. The dead man had been a grower—a shaper of walls and trainer of branches—and his body was understood to contain the same nutrients that the living walls required. They did not eat all of him. Specific parts were given to specific purposes. His hands—so skilled at scoring bark—were burned in a slow wet-smolder that produced a dense green smoke, and the smoke was directed into the roots of a doorframe he had been growing for twenty years. His blood went into the central basin. His flesh was prepared and consumed by those who had worked most closely with him, in a meal you were not invited to witness but could hear: a rhythmic chant that rose and fell against the frog-roar, a song that sounded less like grief and more like continuation.

When you asked about it later—hesitant, unsure of protocol—a woman looked at you with something between pity and amusement. "He is still here," she said, and she touched the wall beside her. "And here." She touched the floor. "And here." She touched her own chest. "The hollow keeps what it needs. The rest goes on."

They have a relationship with their fauna that you can only call *cohabitational*.

They do not keep pets. The concept does not translate. But they share their space with animals in a way that exceeds simple tolerance. The frogs that live on their walls are not pets—they are *tenants*, with rights and expectations. A family that allowed its wall-frogs to die would face social censure more severe than if they had allowed a relative to starve. The frogs earn their keep by eating condensation-lice. The people earn theirs by providing shelter and (intentionally or not) the occasional dropped morsel. It is an exchange. It is understood.

The snakes are more complicated. You have asked why they do not clear the snakes—why they tolerate creatures that can, and sometimes do, kill their children. The answer you received was long and oblique, delivered in story-form by an elder who seemed to feel that your question had missed something fundamental. The gist was this: the snakes are *regulators*. They eat frogs. Without them, the frog population would explode, and the condensation-lice would be suppressed past the point of recovery, and the living walls would fail. But more than that—there was a *more than that* that you could not quite grasp—the snakes are understood as *part of the people*. There are families who claim descent from specific snakes. There are snakes that are given names. There was a woman who pointed to an emerald serpent coiled near her dwelling and said, matter-of-factly, "That is my grandmother." You cannot tell if this was metaphor.

Their children learn snake-avoidance before they learn to speak clearly. They learn to recognize the specific quality of stillness that indicates a snake's presence. They learn the particular lift-and-place gait that minimizes risk. And sometimes—rarely, but sometimes—a child is bitten. A child dies. The settlement mourns and continues. They do not hunt the snake. The snake is doing what snakes do. The child was learning what children learn. Sometimes the lesson does not take. This is accepted with a grief that seems, to you, insufficient to the loss. You have not found the words to ask about this properly.

They are not religious.

Or rather—they do not have *religion* in the way you understand it. They have no gods, no temples, no prayers. The meeting hall you observed is exactly that: a place for meetings, for the settlement-scale decisions that arise when four hundred people live in a space the size of a city block. There are no rituals of worship.

But there are rituals. The scoring of bark has ritual elements—specific cuts made in specific patterns at specific times of year, not all of which are structurally necessary. The eating of the dead is ritual. The naming of children—done at the first rainfall after their birth—is ritual. The songs that rise during certain nights, when the frog-roar reaches a particular pitch and the whole settlement seems to join in a polyphonic drone that makes your skull vibrate—this is ritual, though you cannot tell what it honors or invokes.

The closest thing to a sacred concept is *the Weeping*. The constant water, the moisture that rises from below and falls from above and beads on every surface—they speak of it with a reverence that borders on worship. "The Weeping gives," you have heard. "The Weeping takes." The Weeping is not a god. It is more like *the way things are*. It predates the settlement. It will outlast the settlement. They live inside it the way a fish lives inside a river—aware of it, shaped by it, unable to exist outside it.

They believe—this is not quite the right word, *believe*—they understand that water is alive. Not sentient, not personal, but *alive* in the way a body is alive when the person is elsewhere. The Weeping is the settlement's larger body. They are the cells within it. They circulate through its passages, maintain its structures, process its nutrients. When they die, they are reabsorbed. This is not metaphor. This is biology as theology.

Their economy is not an economy.

You asked about money. They did not understand the question. You asked about trade. They understood this—the market stalls, the exchange of goods and favors. But the concept of *currency*, of a universal medium of exchange that stores value, did not translate. They trade what they have for what they need. They give what they can to those who need it. They receive what they need from those who have it. There is no accounting.

This is not to say there is no inequality. Some dwellings are larger, better positioned, more structurally sound. Some families have more access to ridge-stone, or to the bright copper-work that marks high status. But inequality here is *relative*, and it expresses itself in subtleties—the quality of a wall's weave, the brightness of a child's anklets, the age of the living door you pass through. No one goes hungry. No one goes homeless. The settlement is too small and too dense for the luxury of abandonment. A person in need is a problem *right there*, not an abstraction. They are helped or they are not, and if they are not, everyone sees.

They do not have crime.

Or rather—they have crimes, but they do not have criminals. There is no theft because there is no private property in the lowland sense. There is violence sometimes—arguments that turn physical, tempers that flare in the close air—but violence is handled immediately, by those present, with no formal system of justice required. There is no prison. The closest thing to exile is *being made to go to the ridge*—assigned to work the limestone quarries for a season, away from the hollow, away from the Weeping. This is punishment. This is feared. The ridge is dry. The ridge does not weep.

You have seen one person who was "ridge-touched"—a man who had spent too many seasons assigned to quarry work, and who had returned with a quality of dessication that was not simply physical. His skin was cracked. His eyes were not right. He flinched at sounds. He could not sleep in the close-packed dwellings and instead curled in a passage corner, shivering. The others treated him with a caution that looked, to you, like fear. When you asked, a woman said: "He has forgotten how to be wet." She did not elaborate.

They are not unhappy.

This is the hardest thing to convey. They are not suffering. They are not oppressed by their density, their noise, their constant damp. They are not yearning for space, for silence, for dry ground. They are exactly as happy as anyone anywhere—which is to say, sometimes happy, sometimes sad, mostly occupied with the business of living. They laugh. You have heard them laugh—a sharp barking sound that cuts through the frog-roar, usually in groups, usually at something you did not see. They love. You have seen the way hands reach for hands in the narrow passages, the way a child is caught and swung and set down, the way an elder is helped across a channel. They fight. You have heard arguments that ended in slammed doors—which is to say, woven root-curtains jerked aside and released to flap violently against the frame.

They have art. Not visual art, exactly—the space is too dense for paintings or sculptures—but *sound-art*. Complex polyphonic songs that require a dozen voices to perform, passed down through generations. Rhythmic patterns tapped on hollow surfaces—wood, root-mat, stone—that use the hollow itself as an instrument. When the frog-roar rises to its peak on humid nights, you have heard people sing *against* it, their voices weaving into the texture of sound rather than trying to rise above it. They are *adding* to the noise. They like the noise. The noise is home.

They are not trapped here.

Or rather—they *could* leave. The ridge is not far. Trading parties go out and return. Some young people, occasionally, choose to travel to lowland settlements and stay. This is permitted, though you have noticed it is not celebrated. Those who leave are not spoken of much. Those who return are given back their place, but they are watched for a while, assessed for *ridge-touch*—for the quality of having been away that might never quite heal.

But they do not *want* to leave. This is the part that lowland visitors struggle to understand. You have struggled to understand it. The hollow is cramped and wet and loud and dense. The food is limited. The snakes are dangerous. The children are strange. The dead are eaten. And yet.

And yet.

The Weeping Commons of Mossvale is not a place they inhabit. It is a place they *are*. They are the hollow's way of thinking about itself. They are the processes by which it maintains its shape. They are cells in a body that is older than any of them and younger than all of them, a body that grew from seed and will grow from seed again when this hollow eventually fails.

They will not tell you the hollow's age. You have asked. They do not count years the way you do. They count *growths*—the completion of a wall, the training of a branch, the death and replacement of a living door. The settlement is perhaps sixty walls old. Or perhaps forty. Or perhaps a hundred. The number depends on who you ask and what they consider a wall. The root-mat, they all agree, is *old*—many hundreds of years of accumulated growth, layer on layer, compressed into a floor. The hollow has been here for a long time. It will be here for a long time. The people who live in it are the least permanent part of it.

They know this. They accept it. They do not yearn for permanence. They practice *continuation*—the art of shaping what will outlast them, training the walls that their grandchildren's grandchildren will live inside. They are gardening a future they will not see.

You are not one of them.

You know this. They know this. They have welcomed you with a hospitality that seems genuine—given you a space in a dwelling, shown you the drainage channels, fed you the frog-meat that you are still learning to swallow without gagging. But there is always a distance. You see it in the way they look at you: a sideways glance, assessing. You are dry. You take up too much space. Your feet are too narrow. You cannot read the faces. You cannot hear the tone-shifts in the frog-roar that they respond to, rising when the humidity changes, heading for shelter before you even knew the weather had turned.

They do not expect you to stay.

This is not unkindness. It is realism. The hollow shapes those who live in it. It has not shaped you. And so you will leave eventually, and you will tell others about the Weeping Commons—the density, the noise, the damp, the frogs, the snakes, the children who are not cute, the dead who are eaten, the walls that weep water that is understood as alive.

And they will nod, those others, and they will say: what a strange place. What a strange people. And they will not understand, because to understand you would have to feel your own blood turning copper-green, your own feet flattening and spreading, your own ears learning to hear temperature in the pitch of a frog's call, your own self becoming a cell in a body that has no name and needs none.

The Weeping Commons continues.

The frogs call. The water moves. The walls grow. The people shape what grows and are shaped by what they shape, and the hollow breathes its wet breath, and the ridge looms dry above, and somewhere a child swings between walls that were seedlings when her grandmother was young and will be structural when her grandchildren are old.

The Weeping gives. The Weeping takes.

The Weeping keeps.
