# The Architects of the Drowned Cloister

They were not made for the light.

---

If you could see them truly—not with eyes adjusted to darkness, but with the vision of their own kind—you would understand immediately that nothing about them is accidental. Their bodies are perfect exercises in economy. No spine to maintain, no skull to protect, no limbs to feed. Just a bell and a train of tentacles, translucent as glass, filled with so little substance that they seem more like ideas of creatures than creatures themselves. But that translucence is their genius. It lets light pass through. It lets them hide in plain view. It lets them layer one upon another without blocking the warmth from reaching those below.

They have no names. They have no need for names. In a world without predators, without prey, without territory to defend or rivals to defeat, identity serves no purpose. Each one is indistinguishable from its neighbors, and each one is utterly itself. They are born, they drift, they reproduce, they die, and they dissolve into the calcium-rich water that will knit their remains into the walls. This is not tragic. This is simply the arrangement. They have been making the same arrangement for ten thousand years.

---

Their life begins in the crevices.

In the places where wall meets wall, where the original builders left gaps too small to fill with stone, the adults anchor themselves and wait. When the time comes—when the warmth is right, when the mineral content of the water reaches a certain threshold, when something invisible and internal decides—the females release packets of genetic material into the current. The males release theirs. The packets meet in the open water, fuse, and sink. They settle in the crevices, in the corners, in the joints between stones. And there they germinate.

The young do not look like their parents at first. They are pinhead-sized discs, hard and smooth, anchored by a single thread to whatever surface caught them. For months they sit there, doing nothing visible, drawing minerals from the water, building the internal structures they will need when they are ready to emerge. You could swim past a thousand of them and never notice. They are that small, that patient, that still.

Then, one by one, they detach. The bell inflates. The tentacles unfurl. And a new architect joins the slow blizzard in the vaults above.

---

They do not swim. Swimming implies direction, intent, a destination. They simply exist in suspension, rising when they contract, sinking when they relax, maintaining their position in the water column through countless tiny adjustments. If you watched one for an hour—lying still, letting your vision blur until the creature became a smudge against the darkness—you would see it pulse perhaps four times. That is enough. That is all it needs. The water does the rest.

The current they create together is almost imperceptible, a thermal wheel turning so slowly that you could measure its rotation in days rather than minutes. But it is there. You feel it against your skin if you hold yourself perfectly still: a gentle pressure moving clockwise, then counterclockwise, then clockwise again, as the collective breath of hundreds draws water through their bells and expels it through their tentacles. They are filter feeders, all of them. The water brings them microscopic particles—mineral dust, organic debris, the dissolved remains of their ancestors—and they strain it through internal membranes so fine that nothing escapes. What they cannot digest, they excrete. What they excrete settles on the floor and becomes part of the sediment. What settles on the walls calcifies and becomes part of the architecture. They are building and eating and building and eating, a continuous cycle of absorption and excretion that has slowly, over millennia, changed the shape of the room.

---

The walls they build are not deliberate. No creature among them decided to form a buttress in the northeast corner or a column near the entrance. But the logic of their bodies produces architecture anyway. Where the currents deposit them thickest, they accumulate. Where they accumulate, they die. Where they die, their calcium-rich remains harden into formations that resemble flowstone, stalactites, the accretions of limestone caves. The living layer on top of the dead, and the living layer on top of them, and so on, century after century, until what began as a few stranded bodies has become a structural element load-bearing in its own right.

Some of these formations are meters thick. If you were to cut through one—a violation, an impossibility, but imagine it—you would find layers like tree rings, strata of compressed remains marking centuries of occupation. The lowest layers are dense and stone-like, the original organic material long since replaced by mineral deposits. The middle layers retain some structural integrity, fragments of tentacle and bell still visible in cross-section. The upper layers are soft, recent, still losing their water weight. And at the very surface, translucent bodies pulse in the darkness, adding their microscopic contributions to the great slow work.

---

They communicate by vibration.

The subsonic hum that fills the cloister is not random noise. It is language—or something like language, something that serves a similar function. When one creature contracts its bell, it sends a pressure wave through the water. That wave reaches its neighbors, who adjust their own rhythms in response. The adjustment sends another wave, and another, and another, until the entire room is vibrating at a shared frequency. This is how they synchronize. This is how they know, collectively, when to feed, when to rest, when to release their genetic packets into the water.

They can also sense distress. If one creature is injured—a rare occurrence, given the absence of predators, but possible—its vibration changes. The others register the change and drift closer, their tentacles brushing against the wounded body, not to help (they cannot help) but to absorb the chemical signals that leak from torn flesh. Those signals carry information. They are remembered. The next generation will be born with a slight aversion to whatever caused the injury, a genetic memory encoded in the proteins that will form their bells.

This is how they learn. Not as individuals—individuals are ephemeral, forgettable—but as a population, a distributed intelligence that accumulates knowledge across millennia. They know the shape of the room. They know the rhythm of the warmth. They know the precise moment when the light from above will shrink to its smallest point and begin to grow again. They have known these things longer than humans have known how to write.

---

There is no death in the way you understand it.

When one of them stops pulsing, the body does not collapse. It simply... persists. The bell remains inflated, the tentacles remain extended, and for days or weeks the creature drifts in the current, indistinguishable from its living neighbors. Only gradually does it begin to change. The translucence clouds. The edges soften. The calcium that once reinforced its internal structures begins to leach outward, forming a brittle shell that will eventually crack and settle.

By the time the remains reach the floor, they are unrecognizable. Just white dust, pale as bone ash, sifting down to join the sediment that has accumulated over ten thousand years. And even then, the creature is not truly gone. Its calcium is absorbed by the water. The water feeds the young anchored in the crevices. The young grow, emerge, and take their place in the slow blizzard above. The same atoms circulate endlessly—bell to water to body to bell—eternal residents of a sealed stone womb.

---

You wonder, as you drift among them, whether they are aware of you.

The question is meaningless. Awareness implies a self, and they have no selves. They have only presence. When you move through the water, they adjust. When your body blocks the warmth, they flow around you. When you exhale, creating bubbles that rise toward the distant ceiling, they absorb the vibration and incorporate it into the collective hum. You are not a visitor to them. You are not an intruder. You are simply another presence in a place defined by presence, another body in a room full of bodies, another note in a chord that has been sustaining since before your species learned to speak.

And yet.

Sometimes, when you lie perfectly still, when your own breathing slows to match the rhythm of the room, you feel them gather closer. Tentacles brush against your skin—soft, dry, almost weightless. Bells pulse within centimeters of your face. The hum drops to a frequency you feel rather than hear, resonating in your sternum, your throat, the bones of your skull. They are not curious. Curiosity requires a self. But they are responsive. They register your presence the way they register everything: as a change in the water, a variation in the pattern, a fleeting irregularity that will be smoothed away by time.

You are part of them now, temporarily. Your body heat joins the thermal wheel. Your movements create currents that ripple through the collective. When you eventually leave—ascending toward the light, breaking the surface, gasping air into lungs that had almost forgotten what it was like to breathe—you will leave behind skin cells, hair, the chemical signature of your existence. The water will absorb it. The creatures will absorb the water. And centuries from now, some fragment of you will calcify on the wall, a microscopic layer in an accretion that has no name and needs none.

---

This is what lives in the Drowned Cloister.

Not a colony. Not a swarm. Not a community in any sense that humans recognize. Just presence, layered upon presence, generation after generation, a continuous living thread that connects the builders who laid the first stone to whatever creature will pulse its final breath in the room's final darkness. They do not remember the builders. They do not know they are preserving a cathedral. They simply live, as they have always lived, in the warm still water, making their slow architecture, breathing their collective breath, waiting for nothing and everything.

The water has been here so long it has forgotten how to move.

And they have been here so long they have forgotten how to leave.
