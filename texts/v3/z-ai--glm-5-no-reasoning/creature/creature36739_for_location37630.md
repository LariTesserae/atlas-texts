# What Lives Here

---

## The People Themselves

They are not thin. That surprised me. I had expected—perhaps from old stories of cave-dwellers—the wasted, the vitamin-starved, the bent. But the basin-dwellers are solid. Compact. Built close to the ground the way bridges are built close to the water they span. Their bodies are adapted to compression rather than reach, to carrying weight up ladders rather than across distances. The women broad across the hips. The men thick in the forearms. The children already developing the overdeveloped shoulders that come from climbing before walking.

Their skin holds a particular quality. Not pale exactly—the warm water and the perpetual lamp-glow keep color in their faces—but it has a translucence, a damp sheen, as though the moisture has worked itself into the upper layers over generations. You can see veins more clearly than you ought to in the crooks of their elbows, the hollows of their throats. Their lips tend toward darkness. Their hair is uniformly black, uniformly straight, and uniformly damp—it hangs in loose pieces regardless of how they tie it, because nothing here ever fully dries.

The eyes, yes. The eyes are the thing you notice first and last and cannot stop noticing. Wholly black. No iris, no white visible in the basin's dim light—or perhaps no iris or white at all, the pupils having expanded so far over generations that they have consumed the rest. The effect is not unpleasant exactly. It is *wet*. Looking into a basin-dweller's face is like looking into still water that happens to be looking back. They blink less often than we do. Their gaze is patient and mineral and does not flinch.

When the occasional trader ascends to the surface, they bind cloth across their eyes and move by touch and memory and the guidance of companions. The light above is pain to them—not merely bright but *assaultive*, they say. Like sound when you expected silence. They descend again as quickly as commerce allows, and when they return, they sit with their faces in their hands for an hour, readjusting to the dark, weeping quietly from eyes that are already wet.

### The Families of the Rim

They live highest, closest to the Salt Gate, in dwellings that receive the most daylight and the most cold. The rim-dwellers are the basin's interface with the world above. They operate the winches. They speak the surface languages with their preserved accents. They know the names of winds and seasons, though they no longer experience them directly. Among themselves they are considered worldly and slightly untrustworthy. They eat the most grain because grain comes through their gates. Their children learn to count earlier because counting is needed for the trade. They are paler than the others—not by much, but enough to notice—and they keep mirrors of polished bone, which other basin-dwellers consider a rim-dweller vanity.

The rim-dweller families tend to be smaller. Fewer children survive the cold that creeps through the timber gates in deep winter, though the basin below stays warm. The rim is a threshold, and thresholds are uncertain places. They say a rim-dweller born during a northern gale will always carry a restlessness. Whether this is true I cannot say, but the rim-dwellers do seem to pace more. They lean toward the gate when it opens. They look upward without meaning to.

### The Families of the Middle Tiers

The weavers are the largest guild and the most visible. They occupy the eastern wall where daylight from the Salt Gate strikes most evenly, and their territory is a hanging landscape of looms and drying racks and fiber bundles suspended from pegs driven into stone. The cloth they produce is rough, durable, slightly damp to the touch no matter how long it cures. It holds dye poorly—the mineral content of the basin water interferes—and so their textiles come in a narrow range: the grey-brown of untreated reed-fiber, the yellow-white of bleached hemp, the dark amber of fat-smoke staining. They weave patterns not through color but through texture. A skilled weaver's cloth has a topography—ridges and valleys that form images to the touching hand. The basin-dwellers read cloth the way surface-dwellers read paintings.

The weavers' children begin early. By five, they card fiber. By seven, they manage the smaller looms. By ten, their fingers move with a speed and precision that makes your own hands feel clumsy and overlarge. The weavers sing constantly—a rhythmic chant tied to the shuttle's movement, back and forth, back and forth, and their voices carry across the basin like the ticking of enormous clocks.

The moss-tenders work alongside the weavers but consider themselves separate. Their territory is the walkway edges, the lamp-niches, the shallow stone trays where the bioluminescent moss grows. They are quiet people. Methodical. They carry clay spray-bottles of seep-water and small wooden trowels, and they tend their moss the way a surface gardener tends roses—with proprietary pride and a willingness to discuss varieties at exhaustive length. The moss comes in several cultivars, they will tell you. This strain produces more blue. This strain produces more green. This one here glows only during the hours of deepest dark, which makes it useless for navigation but valuable for ritual. They speak to their moss. They claim it responds.

I watched a moss-tender press her cheek to a tray of the stuff and lie still for a long minute. When she rose, she said the moss was thirsty. She adjusted the flow of a nearby seep with a wooden wedge. The moss did look brighter the next day, though this may have been my imagination.

The pelt-workers occupy the lower middle tiers, where the waste channels carry the foulest water. They process the wei-pelts—the scraping, the softening, the cutting. The smell of their quarter is dense and animal and not entirely unpleasant, though it will stay in your sinuses for days. They are the basin's largest people—thick-handed, broad-shouldered, accustomed to the physical labor of stretching hides across stone frames. They dye their pelts with mineral compounds from the seeps, producing the rust-red and calcium-white and iron-black that serve as the basin's only colors. They trade pelts for grain, for lamp-fat, for the privilege of having their children apprenticed to other guilds. They want better for their children. Every pelt-worker I spoke to said so.

### The Families of the Floor

The fish-keepers live lowest, where the deep channels collect in carved pools. The fish are pale cave-adapted things—eyeless, or nearly so, with bodies like white fingers that swim in the warm mineral water and eat the moss-scrapings and wei-offal that come down from above. They are not large. A good fish is the length of your hand. The fish-keepers tend their pools with a gentleness that seems incongruous given the roughness of everything else here. They whisper to their fish. They know each one, they claim—or at least they know each pool's population the way a shepherd knows a flock, not by individual name but by aggregate temperament.

The floor-dwellers are the darkest-skinned, the most adapted to absolute blackness, the most foreign in their movements. They navigate by sound and by the texture of the pavement under their feet. They know the basin's floor the way we know our own kitchens. They can feel a new crack through their soles. They hear water change course before anyone else. When they ascend to the upper tiers, they move carefully, touching walls, blinking in the faint moss-glow that to them must seem bright. They are the readers of seeps. The geomancers. The ones who listen to the stone.

The floor-dweller elders carry the basin's memory. They sit in the warm pools with their eyes closed and speak of the layers below—the old constructions, the collapsed chambers, the filled-in drains that once carried water through sections now sealed. They know where the dead are. The basin has no separate burial ground; bodies go into the deep drains with the other offerings, returned to the stone. The floor-dwellers say the dead become water. They say you can hear them in the seeps if you listen long enough. The floor-dweller children are not afraid of the drains.

## The Children

There are many children. The basin needs them. The work is constant and the labor is physical and the replacement of what the moisture claims requires hands at every size—small hands for the narrow channels, medium hands for the loom-work and the trap-setting, large hands for the timber and the stone.

Basin children do not have childhoods in the way we understand them. They have apprenticeships that begin as play and gradually become labor. They are not unloved—this must be said. They are held constantly, carried on backs and hips, sung to, included in every adult conversation. But they are *used*. By eight, they tend lamps. By ten, they catch moths. By twelve, they are fully embedded in the guild economy, their futures determined by their parents' trades and their own aptitudes.

They are loud. The basin's noise is partly their making. They shriek across the tiers, they chase each other through the walkways, they catch wei by hand and hold them up triumphant, their black eyes flashing in the moss-glow. They sleep where they fall—in corners, on platforms, draped across their siblings like puppies. The basin does not provide them with quiet. It provides them with a world that is entirely known, entirely populated, and entirely warm.

I watched a child of perhaps four descend a ladder by herself. The ladder was wet. The drop was considerable. She moved with a casual competence that made my hands tighten on the railing. She did not look down. She did not hesitate. At the bottom, she turned and waved at someone above—a wave of pure social performance, because the person she waved to was barely visible in the dark. She had already learned the basin's geography. She already knew where she was.

## The Hawks

The hawks of the basin are not hawks as we know them.

They are smaller—the size of a large pigeon, built for short bursts in enclosed space rather than the long stooping dives of their surface ancestors. Their wings are broader, more rounded, adapted for the tight corners and sudden obstacles of a vertical world. They are silent flyers. They fold and drop and unfold with a precision that seems mechanical, snatching moths from the air without disturbing the powder on adjacent wings.

Their eyes have changed less than their owners'—still golden, still sharp, though adapted now to the low light rather than the bright sky. They do not hunt above. They do not leave the basin. The rim-dwellers release them occasionally, as an experiment, and the hawks return agitated and unwilling, as though the sky is too large, too empty, too full of nothing to hunt.

They roost in the high corners, in crevices where the original cave ceiling still shows. Their nests are constructions of wei-bones and reed-fiber, built up over generations until some are the size of a man's torso. The hawks return to the same nests year after year. They mate for the basin's long lives. When a hawk dies, its mate sits on the perch outside the dwelling for days, silent, and then either takes a new mate or does not. The basin-dwellers read this as a sign. A hawk that does not re-mate means its owner will soon travel or change trade. Whether this is prophetic or causal, I could not determine—perhaps the owner's grief over the hawk prevents them from mating, and so they travel, and so the prophecy fulfills itself.

A hawk on a perch is an ownership mark recognized by everyone. To touch another family's hawk without invitation is a violation more serious than theft. The hawks know their owners. They sit on specific shoulders. They eat from specific hands. They are named, but the names are private—spoken only between family members, never shared with outsiders. I asked a weaver what his hawk's name was. He looked at me with those wet black eyes and said: "Why would you need to know a thing like that?"

## The Wei

The pale rats. The *wei*.

They are everywhere, and they are not hated. This is the difficult thing to understand. The basin-dwellers do not wage war on the wei. They trap them, yes. They hunt them with hawks. They kill them by hand when they find them in the grain stores. But they do not hate them. The wei are part of the basin. They are recognized as part of the system. They eat what they can find; they are eaten when they are found. The basin-dwellers say: "The wei are the stone's teeth. They keep us from accumulating."

The wei are pale—nearly white, with translucent ears and tails that seem too long for their bodies. They are larger than surface rats, nearly the size of a small cat, and they move with a slowness that seems wrong until you realize they have no predators that hunt by sight in the dark. They navigate by smell and by the vibration of footsteps through stone. They know when a basin-dweller is coming. They know when a hawk is in the air. They know when the grain-store has been opened and when it has been closed. They are the basin's secondary nervous system.

They live in the walls. They live in the gaps between layers. They live in the spaces that the basin-dwellers have abandoned—the collapsed sections, the sealed corridors, the voids between pavements. They eat grain and moth-pupae and the dead and each other. They breed constantly. The basin-dwellers thin them constantly. The equilibrium holds.

I watched a child catch a wei by hand. She was nine. She sat perfectly still beside a grain store with a loop of reed-fiber, and when the wei emerged—slow, confident, its pale body catching the moss-glow—she dropped the loop over its head and pulled tight. The wei struggled. She held it against her body, murmured something I could not hear, and carried it to her mother. Her mother broke its neck with a practiced motion, skinned it in four cuts, dropped the carcass into the stewpot, and hung the pelt on a drying line. The entire process took less than a minute. The child had already returned to her position by the grain store.

## The Moths

The cave-moths are the basin's second breath.

They cluster in the damp corners where the seep-water collects, their pale wings fanning slowly, leaving a fine powder on the stone that catches the moss-glow and makes the corners look dusted with snow. They are large—bodies the size of your thumb, wings broader than your spread hand—and they are stupid, or rather they are single-minded, drawn to moisture and mineral with a dedication that makes them easy to catch. Children net them by the dozen. The hawks take them in flight. The wei eat the pupae.

The basin-dwellers roast them. Threaded on thin reed-spits, held over the fat-lamps, the moths cook in seconds—their bodies popping, their wings crisping to nothing. They taste of dust and salt and something faintly sweet. The children eat them like candy, holding the spits over the lamps with casual expertise, not minding the wings that catch fire and burn to ash in their fingers.

The moths are not cultivated. They simply exist. They emerged from the stone, the basin-dwellers say, when the first seeps opened. They are not native to the surface and cannot survive there. They are the basin's own creature, adapted to nothing else, and their presence is so constant that they have become invisible—part of the sound, part of the movement, part of the damp. You notice them most when you are falling asleep. The soft fanning of their wings in the corners is the basin's lullaby.

## The Dead

I must mention the dead, because they live here too.

Not as ghosts. The basin-dwellers do not believe in ghosts. They believe in water. The dead go into the deep drains with the wei-bones and the hair-offerings and the first cuttings of each harvest. They are placed there with ceremony—the body wrapped in the finest available cloth, the face anointed with seep-water from the family's own channel, the favorite possessions laid alongside. Then the drain-cover is replaced and the water takes them. This is not burial. This is return.

The floor-dwellers say the dead speak in the seeps. They say the mineral content of the water carries something of what the dead were—the iron of their blood, the calcium of their bone, dissolved and distributed through the basin's circulatory system. When a seep runs red, the stone is hungry, and the dead are feeding it. When a seep runs clear, the dead are at peace. The floor-dweller elders listen to the drains with their heads pressed to the stone, and they report what they hear to the families above, and the families adjust their offerings accordingly.

There are no memorials. No markers. No places of mourning. The dead are not remembered in stone but in song—each family maintains its lineage-songs, verses that go back generations, listing names in rhythm, attached to the work-songs so that the dead are spoken every day. The weavers' song includes the names of every weaver going back eight generations. The fish-keepers' song goes back twelve. The dead are not gone. They are in the water and in the music, and they are part of the basin's continuous sound.

## The Basin-Dweller at Rest

I will tell you about one of them. Not a type. A person.

Her name—if the sounds she made when I asked translate correctly—was something like *Telleh*. She was a moss-tender. She lived on the fourth tier of the eastern wall, in a dwelling the size of a generous closet, where she slept on a mat of woven reeds over a frame of wei-bones, wrapped in a blanket lined with white fur. Her hawk—a small, dark-feathered thing with one missing talon—roosted on a peg above her door.

She was perhaps forty. It is difficult to tell. The basin's moisture preserves skin, but the work ages hands, and Telleh's hands were the hands of someone who had been tending moss and climbing ladders for decades. They were rough and damp and precise. She could feel a dry patch of moss across a room.

She woke when the Salt Gate's glow touched a certain point on her wall—a scratched line that marked her morning. She ate moth-cakes and drank from her family's seep. She checked her hawk, spoke its private name, gave it a dead moth from her store. Then she began her rounds, climbing down to the walkway edges with her clay spray-bottle and her wooden trowel, moving through the basin's architecture with the ease of someone walking through their own body.

She sang while she worked. Not loudly—a low murmur, the moss-tenders' song, which was slower and quieter than the weavers' chant. She sang to the moss and to the stone and to herself, and the song was so constant that when she paused to speak to me, the absence of it was startling, like a sudden draft.

She did not understand why I had come. She was patient with my questions—more patient than I deserved—but there was a quality to her patience that I came to recognize as pity. She pitied me for my silence. She pitied me for my aloneness. She pitied me for the fact that I would leave, and that when I left, I would go up into the vast empty light where no one sang and the water did not come from the stone and the dead were not in the walls.

"You will forget us," she said, on my last night. She was not accusing. She was stating a geological fact.

"No," I said.

She looked at me with those black, wet, patient eyes. She did not argue. She adjusted the flow of a seep with her wooden wedge and went back to her singing, and the moss in its tray brightened almost imperceptibly, and the basin went on being itself around her—warm, damp, crowded, alive, descended, descended, descended into a darkness that was not dark at all but only a different kind of light.
