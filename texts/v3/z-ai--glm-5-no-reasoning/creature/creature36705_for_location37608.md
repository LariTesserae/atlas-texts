# The People of Verath Hold

They are not a numerous people. This is the first thing to understand about them. A city of such size, occupying such a vast plateau, might be expected to hold tens of thousands, but the density of construction deceives. The buildings press close, layer upon layer, but many of them stand empty, or half-empty, or used for storage of things that will never be used, or simply abandoned to the slow moisture that works its way through every wall. The population of Verath Hold might be five thousand. It might be fewer. No one counts. Counting implies a future in which the number might matter.

---

## The Bodies

They are shaped by the place in ways that are visible to a careful observer, though perhaps not to each other.

The damp cold settles into the joints. This is almost universal among adults past their thirtieth year. Knees, shoulders, the small joints of the hands — they swell subtly, stiffen, ache in the early mornings before movement works the stiffness out. The oldest inhabitants move with a particular deliberation, placing each foot with care, testing the weight before committing. Some of this is the slipperiness of the stone, the black moss in the channels, the ever-present risk of a fall on wet rock. But some of it is simply the body's accommodation to decades of cold that is not severe but is constant, that never quite leaves, that settles into bone and stays.

Their skin is pale. Not the pallor of illness but the paleness of people who live in diffuse light, who see the direct sun perhaps sixty days in a year, whose skin has adapted to an environment where light is scattered and softened by fog. Those who venture down to the lowlands — traders, messengers, the occasional pilgrim — return sunburned and peeling, their bodies unprepared for unfiltered radiation. The burn heals. The paleness returns.

Their hair is remarkable. The fog deposits moisture constantly, and hair, even short hair, is never fully dry. The result is a characteristic texture — limp, flat against the skull, darkened by the water it holds. Those with longer hair — which is most women and some men — braid it tightly, not for aesthetics but for practicality. Loose hair in this atmosphere becomes a damp curtain that holds cold water against the neck. The braids stay wet for hours after rain. They dry slowly. Some never fully dry, acquiring a permanent musty smell that no amount of washing removes, because washing means more water, and there is always more water.

Their hands are rough. This is not a place of soft-handed people. The work of keeping — the endless maintenance, the clearing of channels, the repair of walls, the tending of rooftop gardens — requires hands that can grip wet stone, that can pull weeds from thin soil, that can hold tools slick with moisture. The skin thickens. Calluses form at particular points: the base of the fingers, the edge of the palm, the places where a tool handle rests during the long hours of labor. These hands are capable. They are not pretty. The people of Verath Hold do not expect hands to be pretty.

Their lungs are deep. The air is thin at this altitude, and the body adapts by drawing more of it, by developing greater capacity, by learning to extract what oxygen exists from the dense wet atmosphere. Visitors sometimes feel a tightness in the chest, a subtle breathlessness that they cannot quite identify. The inhabitants do not feel it. Their chests are broader on average than lowland chests, the ribs slightly more expanded, the breathing slightly more deliberate. They do not waste breath.

## The Faces

They have, many of them, a particular expression that visitors find difficult to read. It is not unfriendly, but it is not warm. It is not closed, but it is not open. It is a face that waits.

The waiting is not passive. It is the expression of people who have learned that there is no point in anticipating what the weather will do, that plans made for clear hours will dissolve when the fog rolls in, that the best response to most situations is to observe them until they reveal what they require. A visitor might interpret this as dullness or suspicion, but it is neither. It is simply the face that fits the rhythm of the place.

Their eyes are adapted to low contrast. The fog flattens everything visually, removes shadow, makes distance difficult to judge. The eyes of the inhabitants have learned to work with this. They are good at seeing movement in the grey, at distinguishing shapes that a lowland eye would miss. They are poor at seeing in bright sunlight — it hurts, after a while, and they squint, and some of them develop headaches that last for days after exposure. They do not seek bright light. They tolerate it when they must.

Their voices are low. This is not a cultural choice but a physical adaptation to the acoustic properties of the place. The fog dampens high frequencies more than low ones. A shout dissipates before it travels any distance; a low murmur carries surprisingly far. They have learned to speak from the chest, to produce sounds that will reach the intended listener without dissipating into the wet air. A heated argument in Verath Hold sounds, to a lowland ear, like a conversation. The heated part is in the words, not the volume.

## The Lifespan

They do not live particularly long. Fifty years is a full life. Sixty is notable. Seventy is remarkable and somewhat uncomfortable for everyone involved, because the body at seventy in this place is not a comfortable thing, and the maintenance required to keep it functional exceeds what the body itself can provide, and someone must provide it, and this is understood as obligation but not as joy.

Children are not common. The population is stable, or slowly declining, or perhaps slowly growing — it is difficult to tell, because the timeframes involved are longer than any single lifespan, and the records, if they exist, are in the lower levels where the water has warped them beyond reading. A woman might bear three children in her life, or four. Two might survive to adulthood. The math is neither tragic nor encouraging. It simply is.

The children who do survive are tough from the beginning. They learn early how to walk on wet stone, how to stay out of the drainage channels when the water rises, how to occupy themselves in the narrow spaces between buildings. They play quiet games. The silence of the city seeps into them early. A child who shouts for no reason is unusual and somewhat disturbing to the adults. Children here learn to communicate through gesture, through touch, through proximity. They learn, before they learn language, that sound is a limited medium.

## The Social Fabric

They live in a proximity that would drive lowland people to violence, but they have accommodated to it.

Privacy, as noted, is acoustic rather than visual. You see your neighbors constantly — through windows, across narrow passages, in the shared courtyards where the drainage channels converge and people gather to check the water flow. You hear almost nothing from them. The result is a strange intimacy, a knowledge of others that is visual rather than verbal. You know your neighbor's posture, the way she moves, the state of her roof-garden, the frequency with which she clears her section of the channel. You may never have heard her voice at length. You know her anyway.

The households are organized around the work of keeping. A typical household consists of three or four adults — perhaps a bonded pair, perhaps siblings, perhaps unrelated individuals who have found it convenient to share space — and whatever children and elders are attached to them. The composition is fluid. People move between households for reasons that are often practical: this building's roof needs repair, and the person skilled in roof-repair lives in that building, and an arrangement is made, temporary or permanent, and the household shifts. The concept of "family" in Verath Hold is elastic. It encompasses blood relations, but it does not privilege them.

There are bonded pairs, but the bonding is not what a lowland visitor might expect. There is no ceremony, or none that a visitor would recognize. A pair who have decided to bond — which is to say, to share resources, to maintain a household together, to raise whatever children come — announce this to the people who need to know, and then they are bonded. The announcement is quiet. It might be as subtle as moving one's belongings into another's space, or as explicit as a statement in a public courtyard. The community accepts it. The community's acceptance is the ceremony.

The bonds sometimes last a lifetime. They sometimes do not. There is no shame in dissolution. A pair who find that their keeping no longer aligns — that one wishes to move to a different quarter, that another has developed skills best used elsewhere, that the simple friction of proximity has worn the bond thin — separate. They announce this, or they simply separate, and the community adjusts. Children born to a bond are the responsibility of the household, and if the household dissolves, the children go where they are needed, or where they wish to go. There is no concept of custody. There is only the understanding that children must be raised, and that raising them is distributed work.

## The Work

Everyone works. There is no class of people who do not work, no leisure class, no category of person whose labor is purely intellectual or purely administrative. Even the oldest inhabitants, whose bodies no longer permit the physical labor of keeping, contribute in ways that their bodies allow: watching the drainage channels from a window and calling out when a blockage appears, sorting the salvaged materials from collapsed buildings into categories of usefulness, tending the smallest and most delicate of the rooftop gardens.

The primary work is the work of keeping. This is the word they use, or the closest translation: the maintenance of the city's physical structure, the endless repair and clearing and checking and replacing that prevents the water from winning. Keeping is not skilled labor in the sense that it requires years of training, but it is skilled in the sense that it requires attention, experience, a familiarity with the stone and the water and the way they interact. A person who has spent thirty years in Verath Hold knows where the water will pool before it pools, knows which wall is about to weep, knows the sound of a drainage channel that is beginning to clog. This knowledge is not taught. It is absorbed, through years of proximity, through the body's gradual calibration to the rhythms of the place.

There are specialists. Some people have developed expertise in particular aspects of keeping: the repair of cisterns, the clearing of deep channels, the maintenance of the few structures that require specialized knowledge. The oldest buildings, the cyclopean ones, have walls that no one knows how to build anymore, and when they need repair — which is rare, because they are the strongest structures in the city — the work is given to those who have studied them most closely. There is a kind of reverence for these buildings that has nothing to do with religion. It is simply the recognition that they contain knowledge that has been lost, that they are the work of hands that knew things the present hands do not.

The rooftop gardens are tended with intensity. A garden-tender knows every plant, every square inch of soil, every potential source of damage. The gardens are small, but they are the margin between the city and total dependence on the lowlands for food. The root vegetables they produce — small, dense, pale things that taste of minerals and very little else — are supplement, not sustenance, but they matter. A garden that fails is a household's shame. A garden that thrives is a household's pride. The definition of thriving is modest: a few more vegetables than last year, a slightly higher yield, a sign that the soil has not yet given out.

The goats are tended by a small group of people who have chosen that work, or been assigned to it, or inherited it through some chain of association that no one remembers. They are not beloved, these goats. They are tolerated, as the work is tolerated, as the city itself is tolerated. The goats provide milk, which is shared. They provide hair, which is woven. They provide meat when they die, or when their population exceeds what the rough grass can sustain. They are mattering, but they are not loved.

## The Temperament

They are patient. This is the characteristic that visitors notice first, and struggle with, and sometimes misinterpret.

Patience here is not a virtue. It is a survival trait. The weather does not care about urgency. The fog does not respond to haste. The water drains at its own pace, and no amount of human will can make it drain faster. A person who cannot wait, who cannot endure delay, who cannot tolerate the slow accumulation of process — such a person does not last long in Verath Hold. They leave, if they can. They wither, if they cannot.

The patience expresses itself as stillness. A person waiting in Verath Hold does not fidget. They stand, or sit, and they wait. Their body does not move. Their face does not shift. They are present in the waiting, not resisting it, not suffering through it, simply occupying it. A visitor might interpret this as catatonia, as depression, as some form of psychological withdrawal. It is none of these. It is the physical manifestation of a mind that has made peace with time.

They are not cheerful. This is another thing that visitors notice, and sometimes find disturbing. The demeanor of Verath Hold is calm, but it is not happy. There is laughter, occasionally — a sound that startles when it occurs, sharp and brief in the damp air — but there is no sustained joy, no pervasive lightness, no sense that life is something to be enjoyed rather than endured. The closest thing to happiness here is satisfaction: the satisfaction of work completed, of a channel running clear, of a roof that does not leak, of a garden that has produced what was expected. This satisfaction is quiet. It does not announce itself. It is simply the absence of failure.

They are not unhappy either. This is the harder thing for visitors to understand. The absence of joy is not the presence of misery. The people of Verath Hold are content, in their way. They are content with the work, with the rhythm, with the slow accumulation of days that are mostly the same. They do not expect more than this. They do not imagine that life contains more than this. The concept of happiness, as an achievable state, as a goal toward which life might be directed, is not present in their thinking. There is life, and there is the living of it, and there is the ending of it, and the question of whether it was happy does not arise.

## The Knowledge

They know things. This is not the kind of knowledge that can be written down, or the kind that can be taught in a classroom. It is the knowledge of the body, of the senses, of the accumulated experience of living in a particular place for a long time.

They know the stone. They can look at a wall and see where it is weak, where the water has begun to work, where the next decade will bring a crack if nothing is done. They can touch a block and feel, through the subtle vibrations, whether it is solid or compromised. They know the sound of healthy stone and the sound of stone that is beginning to fail. They do not think about this knowledge. They simply have it, the way they have hands or feet.

They know the water. They can smell rain before it arrives, can feel the shift in the air that precedes a storm, can predict with accuracy whether a particular rainfall will be a misting or a downpour. They know the rhythm of the drainage channels, the sound of proper flow and the sound of obstruction. They know when the water table is rising, can feel it in the floors of the lower levels, can sense the weight of it pressing upward through the stone.

They know the fog. They can navigate through fog so thick that a lowlander would be blind, can find their way by memory, by touch, by some sense that is not sight but is not quite anything else either. They know when the fog will lift and when it will settle in for days. They know the paths it takes through the city, the routes it follows, the places it accumulates and the places it bypasses. The fog is alive to them, in a way that does not require belief. It is simply another entity in the city, to be understood and accommodated.

They know each other. This is perhaps the most important knowledge. They know the people around them with an intimacy that does not require speech. They know who is struggling, who is unwell, who is carrying a burden that is not visible. They know because they see — the set of a shoulder, the pace of a walk, the quality of presence in a shared space. They respond to this knowledge without discussion. A person who is struggling will find that work has been done for them, that food has appeared, that the demands on them have quietly lessened. No one speaks of this. It is simply what is done.

## The Beliefs

They do not have religion, not in the way that many peoples do. There are no gods in Verath Hold, no temples, no prayers, no ceremonies of worship. The closest thing to sacred space is the Basin, and it is sacred only in the sense that it is treated with caution, with respect, with the understanding that it operates by rules that are not fully understood.

They have stories. The stories are not about gods or heroes or supernatural events. They are about the plateau, the city, the water, the stone. They are about people who lived before, and what they did, and what happened to them. Some of the stories are true. Some are probably not. No one bothers to distinguish between them. The distinction does not matter. What matters is that the stories are told, that they are passed down, that they continue to exist as a body of knowledge that belongs to the place.

They have a relationship with the plateau that is difficult to translate. The plateau is not worshipped. It is not personified. It is simply acknowledged as that which contains everything, the foundation of all existence, the thing that was here before and will be here after. There is no emotion attached to this acknowledgment. It is a fact, like the wetness of stone, like the direction of water flow. The plateau is. The city is. The people are. This is enough.

They believe in the work. This is the closest thing to faith that they possess: the belief that the work matters, that the keeping is worth doing, that the maintenance of the city has value even when it is endless, even when it produces no progress, even when it simply holds back entropy for another day. They do not articulate this belief. They do not discuss it. They simply act on it, every day, in the way they tend the channels and repair the walls and check the lower levels during the dry season. The work is. The work is done. This is enough.

## The Visitors

Visitors come, though not often. The plateau is not easy to reach. The climb from the lowlands is long, and the fog makes the final approach disorienting, and there is little at the top to reward the effort — no market of note, no temple, no natural wonder that would justify the journey. The visitors who do come are usually traders, bringing materials that cannot be found on the plateau (wood, metal, cloth, occasionally preserved food), or messengers, carrying communications between Verath Hold and the lowland settlements that consider themselves its tributaries, or occasionally the curious, those who have heard of this place and wish to see it for themselves.

The visitors are tolerated. They are not welcomed, but they are not turned away. They are given shelter in spaces that are set aside for visitors — not comfortable spaces, but dry enough, sheltered from the worst of the rain. They are fed from the common stores. They are watched, constantly, with eyes that do not seem to be watching. The people of Verath Hold have learned that visitors bring useful things and also bring disruption, and the disruption must be managed.

Visitors are disturbed by the city. They find it oppressive, the grey and the damp and the silence, the endless repetition of stone and water, the absence of joy. They find the people incomprehensible, their patience unnerving, their quietude alien. They do not stay longer than necessary. They complete their business and descend, and they do not return if they can avoid it.

Occasionally, a visitor chooses to stay. This is rare, but it happens. The choice is allowed, after a period of observation. The newcomer is watched more closely, given work, tested in small ways. If they prove capable of the keeping — if they can learn the stone and the water and the rhythm of the place — they are gradually absorbed. They become inhabitants. They are not distinguished from those who were born here. The distinction does not matter. What matters is the work.

## The Leaving

People leave. This is a fact, though it is not discussed. The plateau is vast, and the city is dense, and beyond the city is the edge, and beyond the edge is a drop so long that the bottom is not visible, and below that are the lowlands, and the lowlands are where people go when they cannot stay.

The leaving is not forbidden. There is no rule against it, no social sanction, no punishment for those who choose to go. The leaving is simply accepted as something that happens, as the rain happens, as the fog happens. A person who leaves is not mourned, because mourning implies a loss that is not supposed to occur, and the leaving is understood as a possibility that is always present. Some people are not meant for Verath Hold. They discover this, and they go, and the city continues.

The ones who leave are usually the young. This is not surprising. The young have not yet been shaped by the place, have not yet developed the patience, the stillness, the capacity for the work. They feel the weight of the grey, the oppression of the damp, and they imagine that elsewhere is different, that elsewhere is better. Some of them are right. Some of them are wrong. The city does not keep track.

Occasionally, someone returns. This is rare. A person who leaves and returns is not treated differently, is not questioned, is not asked to explain. They are simply there, and the work is there, and they take up the work again, and the city absorbs them. The time away is not mentioned. It exists, but it is not part of the present reality of Verath Hold. The present reality is the stone and the water and the keeping.

---

## The End

The people of Verath Hold do not think often about the end. Not the end of their own lives — that is a fact, accepted, prepared for in the small ways that people prepare for what they know is coming — but the end of the city, the plateau, the way of life that has persisted for longer than anyone can remember.

They know, abstractly, that everything ends. The stone erodes. The water rises. The buildings collapse, eventually, one by one, and are not rebuilt, or are rebuilt differently, and the city changes. They know this. They do not think about it. There is no point in thinking about it. The end, if it comes, will not come in their lifetime, or their children's lifetime, or their children's children's lifetime. It will come later. Later is not their concern.

What concerns them is today, and tomorrow, and the next few weeks. The drainage channels that need clearing. The cistern that is showing signs of leakage. The wall in the lower level that is weeping more than usual. The goat that is favoring its left leg. The rooftop garden that is producing less than it did last year. These are the things that matter. These are the things that occupy the minds of the people of Verath Hold.

They are a practical people. They are a patient people. They are a people who have found a way to live in a place that does not welcome them, that does not reject them, that simply is, and that requires of them an endless accommodation, an endless work, an endless keeping.

They are the people of the wet stones. They are the people of Verath Hold.
