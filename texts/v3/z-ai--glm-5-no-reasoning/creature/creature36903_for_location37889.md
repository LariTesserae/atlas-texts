# The Vent-Colonists

They have no name for themselves. Naming requires an other, and they have never had one.

---

Consider what they are not. They are not biological in any way that biology would claim. No cell membrane. No genome. No metabolism in the enzymatic sense, no ATP cycle, no replication machinery that recombines and varies. If you sectioned one — and you could, they are brittle when dry, fracturing along crystalline planes into smaller versions of their own geometry — you would find no interior. No organs, no layers, no differentiation between surface and core. The same mineral throughout: a hydroxylated silicate, thin-bladed, intergrown in radiating clusters that produce their characteristic dark irregular form. A kind of schorl, perhaps, or something without a terrestrial analog, precipitated from steam that has been percolating through the same mineralogy for so long that the chemistry has refined itself to a single repeatable product.

They are not plants. They do not photosynthesize. The light that strikes them during the plain's brief dawn prismatic phase is not absorbed for energy but simply warms their surfaces, which accelerates the condensation of moisture onto their growth faces, which feeds the precipitation of new mineral at those faces, which is — if you are determined to call it this — their growth. They grow the way a icicle grows: by accretion at the site of greatest flux. Not because they intend to. Because the conditions at that site produce that result.

They are not machines. They were not built. They have no parts. Nothing was assembled.

And yet.

---

Watch one long enough and the word *behavior* stops feeling generous and starts feeling necessary.

A vent-colonist attached to a steam column does not hang passively. It orients. Its broader faces turn toward the densest region of the plume, the core of upward flow where moisture concentration is highest. If the vent's output shifts — and it does, subtly, the plumes swaying with pressure variations far below — the colonist shifts with it, rotating on whatever tenuous anchor holds it to the vapor column. The anchor is not physical. There is no thread, no stalk, no root. It is held by the steam itself, by the condensation film that forms on its surface and the aerodynamic drag of the upward flow, and it maintains its position through constant micro-adjustments of orientation that keep it within the column's boundary layer. It is surfing. It has been surfing for as long as it has existed, and it will surf until the column fails or it does.

When a vent falters — the half-second gaps that correspond to some irregularity in the subterranean rhythm — the colonist drops. Not randomly. It folds. The blade-like crystals that form its body close inward, reducing its drag profile, and it descends in a controlled trajectory that lands it on the bedrock directly below. There it rests, compact, dense, its surfaces still damp from the last of the condensation. It does not move. It does not seek a new vent. It waits. When the steam resumes, the upward flow catches it — and here is the part that strains the word *chemistry* — it unfolds. The blades open. It rises. It reorients. It finds the column's core.

This is not a reflex. Reflexes require a nervous system. This is not a tropism. Tropisms are growth responses, and while the colonist does grow, its reorientation happens in seconds, not hours. It is a response to a stimulus, executed through a mechanism that involves the differential condensation of moisture on its various crystal faces, which changes their mass and their aerodynamic properties, which causes the whole structure to rotate in the flow. It is a physical process. It is also, by any functional definition, a behavior.

The distinction, if there is one, is in the watching.

---

They do not all behave identically.

Over the course of an hour, walking the venting corridors, you notice variations. Most colonists are sessile within their columns — attached, oriented, growing, static in position if not in orientation. But some move. The one you saw detach and drift laterally to an adjacent plume was not unique. Approximately one in five exhibits this translocation behavior at some point during observation. The mechanism is unclear. The lateral movement occurs during periods of strong vent output, when the plumes swell beyond their usual diameter and the boundary layers between adjacent columns thin. The colonist detaches — or is detached, by turbulence, by a pressure pulse — and enters the gap between plumes. In the gap, the air is drier, cooler. The colonist begins to fold. But if it catches the edge of the adjacent column before it fully closes, it unfolds, reorients, and reattaches.

Some do not make it. You find them on the ground between vents: small, dark, folded tight, motionless. They have landed outside the steam columns. The dawn condensation reaches them, dampens their surfaces briefly, but it is not enough. The mineral film that constitutes them dries. The thin-bladed crystals lose their hydroxyl water, become anhydrous, become brittle. A strong wind — and there are strong winds on the plain, the steady air movement that the landscape's openness invites — will fracture them. The fragments scatter. They become part of the pale hardpan. Mineral returning to mineral.

Is this death? The fragments are chemically identical to the living form. They are the same substance. If a fragment were carried by wind or water into a vent column, would it hydrate, would it grow, would it become a colonist? You do not know. You suspect it would. You suspect that every colonist began as a fragment, or as a seed crystal precipitated from supersaturated steam, and that the difference between a fragment on the ground and a colonist in a plume is only the difference between being in the steam and not being in the steam.

This is either the simplest life imaginable or not life at all. The colonist does not maintain itself against entropy; the steam maintains it. The colonist does not reproduce; fragments are dispersed by wind and, if they land in steam, grow. The colonist does not respond to its environment in a way that suggests preference or learning; it responds the way a weather vane responds, mechanically, to physical forces. Remove the steam and it is a rock. Return the steam and it is a thing that orients, moves, grows, and occasionally translocates.

---

But there is the matter of the hexagons.

The hexagonal patterns in the bedrock — the shrinkage cracks, the fossilized drying — are not random in their distribution. They are densest near the vents. Not at the vents. Near them. At a distance of roughly five to seven meters from each fracture, the hexagons appear at their highest density and their largest size. Further away, they become smaller, less regular, and eventually give way to unmarked hardpan. The pattern is subtle enough that you have to walk the gradient several times before you notice it. But it is there. A ring of enhanced hexagonal patterning around each vent, like a halo.

The colonists accumulate in the hexagons. Not the living colonists — the fragments. The fallen, the wind-dispersed, the fractured remains of colonists that landed outside the steam columns. They collect in the shallow hexagonal basins, which catch them the way they catch the dawn condensation. The fragments hydrate partially each morning. They do not grow — the condensation is insufficient for true growth — but they do not fully degrade. They persist. They accumulate. Over time — and the time available here is vast — the hexagons near the vents have filled with a dark mineral grit, a coarse sediment of colonist fragments, layered and rehydrated and partially reconstituted, forming a kind of dark dust in each hexagonal basin.

This dust is chemically distinct from the surrounding bedrock. It is richer in hydroxyl water. It contains trace concentrations of iron and manganese that the bedrock lacks. It is, in a sense, a soil — the only soil the plain has ever produced. A soil made of the remains of organisms that may not be organisms, accumulating in patterns that the organisms did not create but that their existence has, over geological time, subtly altered.

The plain is not as sterile as it first appears. The colonists have not changed the chemistry of the ground. But they have, over millennia, laid down a thin layer of themselves around each vent. A residue. A trace. The landscape has been marked by something living in it, or something that behaves enough like living that the mark is the same.

---

And there is the matter of the translocation.

The colonists that move between plumes — the one in five that detach and catch adjacent columns — do not move randomly. They move outward. Over the course of many observations, a pattern emerges: colonists in the inner ring of vents, closest to the basin's center, translocate outward more frequently than colonists in the outer ring. The direction of translocation is consistently away from the basin's center, toward the rim. The outermost vents, at the basin's edge, show the lowest translocation rates and the highest accumulation of fallen fragments in their hexagons.

This could be an artifact of vent strength. The central vents may produce higher pressure, more turbulence, more detachment events. The physics would explain the pattern without requiring intention.

But the pattern also looks like dispersal. It looks like a population expanding into new habitat, colonizing new vents, reaching the edge of its range and accumulating there where the conditions become marginal. It looks, if you are inclined to see it that way, like ecology. A simple ecology. One species. One resource. One behavior. One boundary. But an ecology nonetheless, with a center and a periphery, with growth and loss and a slow outward drift that has, over the long history of this place, populated every vent in the basin.

---

The colonists do not know any of this. They do not know they are colonists. They do not know they are fragments of a system that produces them and disperses them and reconstitutes them from its own debris. They do not experience the plain. They do not experience anything. They are mineral forms precipitated from mineral-rich steam, shaped by the forces that precipitate them, behaving in ways that those forces dictate. Their life, such as it is, consists of being warm, being wet, being vertical, and occasionally — through no agency of their own — moving.

But they are here. In a place where nothing else is. In a place that has been precluded from life so thoroughly that the ground itself is hostile to self-organization, they have organized themselves. Not against the place. Not despite it. Through it. The plain provides the steam, the steam provides the minerals, the minerals provide the form, the form provides the behavior, the behavior provides the dispersal, the dispersal provides the accumulation, the accumulation provides the trace.

They are the plain's only invention. A thing it produced without trying, without needing, without noticing. A thing that exists because the conditions for its existence are met, perfectly and perpetually, in the steam columns that rise from fractures in the bedrock above a pulse that has not changed in the measurable history of the world.

The thudding continues. The steam rises. The colonists orient, fold, unfold, drift, fall, fragment, accumulate. The plain does not notice them either.

But if you stand at the basin's edge at dawn, when the light goes prismatic and the steam columns go gold and the hexagons fill with mirror-water, you can see them — dark shapes in the bright plumes, turning slowly, catching the light on their blade-faces in brief flashes of amber and bronze. For twenty minutes, they are beautiful. They do not know this. They do not need to. The beauty is not for them. It is not for anyone. It is a property of the system, emergent and incidental, like everything else here.

The plain has reached its final form. The colonists are part of it. They will persist for as long as the vents vent and the steam rises and the thudding maintains its three-second pulse. They will fall and fragment and be reconstituted and fall again. They will accumulate in hexagons and drift outward and populate new columns as old ones shift. They will do this forever, or until the pulse stops, and if the pulse ever stops, they will not know that either. They will dry. They will become part of the hardpan. They will be, finally, indistinguishable from the plain itself.

They always were.
