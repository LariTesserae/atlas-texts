They are called the Listeners by those who have never seen them, though they call themselves nothing at all—they long ago stopped using the sounds that once identified them, finding language too clumsy for this place, too likely to be swallowed by the roar.

There is one of them here now.

She is not hiding, though you have not seen her. She is sitting in the shallow cave twenty meters up the north wall, in the darkness behind the hanging ferns, where the water doesn't reach but the mist keeps everything perpetually damp. Her skin is the color of wet granite, and this is not metaphor—the same minerals that stain the rock have stained her, over decades, until she is indistinguishable from the stone except when she chooses to move.

She has been watching you since you arrived.

---

The Listeners came to the cirques generations ago. Not this one specifically—there are others, scattered through the high places where water falls and the air grows thin. They were once mountaineers, hermits, pilgrims of various motivations. The kind of people who climb until the world below stops making sense, and then keep climbing.

The first one found this place by accident. He was a cartographer, or perhaps a fugitive—the stories differ, and he is long past correcting them. He built the shelter. He stacked those stones with hands that had forgotten how to hold a pen, and when the work was done, he sat on the flat stone and listened to the water, and something in him went quiet.

He stayed for eleven years.

Others came, found the shelter, understood its purpose without being told. They added the marks to the stones—not tallies, though you were close. They are records of rainbows. Each groove represents a day when the light was right and the supernumerary arcs appeared. The cartographer saw them first and thought they were a message. By the time he died, he understood they were simply a phenomenon—but the habit of marking them had become ingrained, a ritual without meaning except the meaning he gave it.

The current Listener has added seventeen marks. She has been here three years.

---

What she does is listen.

Not to the water—that would be maddening, or maddening to anyone who hadn't learned what she has learned. She listens to *everything else*. The bass rumble of the falls is a canvas, and the other sounds paint themselves across it: the high whistling of air through cracks, the creak of roots shifting in stone, the minute sounds of insects moving through moss. After enough time, you stop hearing the water as noise. It becomes silence—a presence so total it creates a kind of auditory vacancy, a space in which smaller sounds become impossibly distinct.

She can hear the butterfly's wings from three meters away. She can hear the spider spinning, the faint click of its spinnerets attaching silk to reed. She can hear the goats thinking about moving before they move—a tenseness in their stillness that produces no sound she could name but which she has learned to recognize.

She cannot hear you breathing. You are too far away, and your breath is too loud, too uncontrolled. You haven't learned yet.

---

She eats what the cirque provides. The mountain ash berries are edible if you know which ones to avoid—the red ones are safe, the orange ones will make you sick, the ones that have fallen and fermented will make you see things that may or may not not be there. She has learned the distinction through experience she prefers not to remember.

The finches provide eggs, occasionally. She takes only one from any clutch, and never from the same nest twice, and the birds have learned that she is not a threat—they land near her as readily as they landed near you, perhaps more so. She has been here longer.

The marmots are too quick and too wary. The goats are too large and too sure-footed. She could, if she were desperate, take the kid—but she never has been desperate enough, and the cirque has taught her that taking is not the same as being given.

The soil in the corners grows things she planted: a hardy variety of kale, some roots she doesn't name. She tends them with her fingers, working the thin earth, adding ash from fires she rarely lights. The growing season is short and the harvest is meager, but she is small and her needs are small and she has forgotten what it feels like to be full.

She drinks from the springs where the water emerges clean from the rock, before it picks up the minerals that stain her skin. She has a collection spot, a small depression in the stone that fills gradually, and she drinks from it each morning before the sun reaches it.

---

She was not always like this.

Before she came here, she was a professor of acoustics. She studied the way sound moves through spaces, the way architecture shapes what we hear. She published papers on the resonant properties of natural formations—caves, canyons, the strange acoustics of places where human builders never worked.

She found the Weeping Cirque through a footnote in a paper she could no longer cite, written by someone who had found the shelter and stayed for one night and left in the morning with something troubled in their writing that they couldn't articulate. She came here expecting to study the acoustics.

She stayed because of what she heard.

The cirque is a resonant chamber. The shape of the walls, the angle of the overhangs, the particular hardness of the granite—it all creates an acoustic environment unlike anything she has found in her research or her experiments. The roar of the water is dampened, distributed, made bearable. But beneath it, in the frequencies she was trained to isolate, there is something else.

She thought it was a resonance at first. A standing wave formed by the interaction of the falling water and the shape of the stone. She measured it—she had equipment then, batteries that eventually died, instruments that eventually failed. The readings were anomalous. The frequency didn't correspond to any physical model she could construct.

She kept listening.

After the first year, she stopped trying to measure it. After the second, she stopped trying to explain it. Now she simply listens, and sometimes, in the early morning when the mist is thick and the light hasn't yet reached the cirque floor, she hears it most clearly: a tone, or something like a tone, that seems to come from the rock itself. Not vibration. Not resonance. Something else.

She has not spoken in fourteen months.

---

She moves through the cirque with the same ease the goats do—not as quickly, not as daringly, but with the same intuitive understanding of where the handholds are, which stones are loose, which ledges will bear weight and which will crumble. She goes barefoot. The cold that stiffens your fingers has become a fact of her existence rather than a hardship; her circulation has adjusted, or she has simply stopped noticing.

She climbs to the high caves where the snow accumulates and stays through the summer. She descends to the pools where the water gathers before spilling over. She knows this place the way you know your own body—not through conscious study but through the accumulated intimacy of daily existence. When a rock falls, she knows where it will land before it lands. When the wind shifts, she knows which falls will swell and which will thin.

The goats know her. Not as a threat, not as a source of food, but as a *presence*—another creature that lives here, that moves through the same terrain, that occupies the same cold and wet. They have a kind of truce, she and the goats. She doesn't bother them; they don't bother her. Occasionally, in the deep cold of winter, they shelter together—the goats in their thick coats, she in her layered skins, sharing the warmth of proximity without acknowledging it.

The marmots know her too. They whistle when she approaches, the same alarm they give for any threat, but they've learned that she doesn't hunt them, and their wariness has become a formality rather than a genuine fear.

---

You wonder about the shelter. Who built it, who used it, what happened to them.

She built the shelter, and she didn't. The cartographer built it, and the woman who came after him, and the man who came after her, and the Listener who came before the current one. Each added stones, repaired walls, maintained the fire ring. The shelter is a palimpsest—layers of presence, each leaving traces the next would find and build upon.

She uses it rarely now. She prefers the cave, the height, the distance from the ground where visitors arrive. The shelter has become a thing she offers to others—a gift she leaves for whoever comes next, as it was left for her.

She keeps her few possessions there, though. A wool blanket, threadbare but still warm. A metal cup, dented, bought from a shepherd years ago. A journal she no longer writes in, its pages swollen with moisture, the ink long since run and faded into illegibility. A knife with a bone handle, sharp enough to cut through the bark of mountain ash.

And the kale, in the corners. Her contribution to the place, her small mark. She will not be the last one here, and whoever comes after will find the soil she's made and the plants she's tended, and they will eat from them without knowing who to thank.

---

She watches you examine the shelter. She sees you find the grooves in the stones, the rainbow-marks. She wonders if you understand them, if you will add your own. She wonders if you will stay.

Probably not. Most don't. They come, they marvel, they shiver, they leave. The cirque doesn't need them. She doesn't need them. But she doesn't mind them either—the occasional presence of another human, even one who doesn't know how to be quiet, who doesn't know how to listen, who moves through the cirque like a rock fall, all noise and disruption.

She watches you stand in the spray and look at the supernumerary rainbows. She can see the wonder on your face, the way your mouth opens slightly, the way you reach toward the light as if you could hold it.

She did the same thing. Three years ago, which feels like thirty, which feels like this morning.

---

The cirque is changing. She knows this, though she doesn't think of it in those terms. The falls pulse slightly, the glacier that feeds them retreating year by year, the flow diminishing. In her lifetime—no. In the cirque's lifetime, which she has come to think of as her own. The snows come later, the thaws come earlier. The moss creeps higher up the walls each season, and the trees grow where they couldn't before, and the pool where she drinks runs dry in August now, when it never used to.

The goats have fewer kids. The finch flock is smaller than it was. The butterflies come later and die sooner.

She doesn't mourn this. She is a scientist, still, somewhere beneath the stone-colored skin and the bare feet and the silence. She observes. She notes. She does not intervene. The cirque will become what it becomes, and she will be here to hear it, or she won't.

---

What she wants from you, if she wants anything: your silence.

She wants you to stop moving, stop talking, stop reaching for the light. She wants you to sit on the flat stone—the one the cartographer sat on, the one every Listener has sat on—and be still. She wants you to listen, not to the water but to what the water hides. She wants you to hear what she hears, the tone that comes from the rock, the voice of this place that isn't a voice at all but something older and stranger, something that was here before water fell and will be here after it stops.

She won't ask this of you. It isn't hers to ask. The cirque will ask, or it won't. You will hear, or you won't.

But she watches you, from her cave behind the ferns, and she hopes.

---

The butterfly lands near you. You're startled—you didn't expect something so fragile in a place so harsh. Its wings open and close, catching the light.

From her vantage point, twenty meters up, the Listener watches the butterfly settle on your shoulder. She sees you go still, suddenly still, the way you haven't been since you arrived. She sees the wonder return to your face, and the quiet that follows it.

She closes her eyes.

She listens.

The tone is there, beneath the roar. Patient, constant. Waiting.

The cirque doesn't need you. But the Listener does, a little. She needs someone else to hear it. She needs to know she isn't imagining it, isn't losing her mind in the mist and the cold.

She needs a witness.

She waits.
