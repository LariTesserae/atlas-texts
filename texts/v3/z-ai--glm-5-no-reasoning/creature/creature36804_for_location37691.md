What lives here has learned to be small.

Not small in the way that desert animals are small—compact, efficient, water-hoarding. Small in the way that a sentence whispered in a cathedral is small. The scale of this place defeats any ambition toward visibility. You could be six meters tall and still disappear into the shimmer, still read as a glitch in the heat signature, still go unnoticed by whatever makes the making.

So what lives here is measured in centimeters. In millimeters. In the narrow band where the drainage channels cut deep enough to cast shadow during the worst hours.

---

There are things that move through the older sections, where the sediment has accumulated and the biology has found its thin foothold. You might call them insects if you needed a word, but the word would be doing work it wasn't designed for. They have the segmented bodies and jointed legs of arthropods, but their carapaces are not chitin. They are metal-polymer hybrids, or perhaps organic compounds that have incorporated metallic elements from the substrate. Their exoskeletons oxidize in patterns that might be aging or might be something else entirely—communication through corrosion, identity expressed as patina.

They do not fly. The air above the surface is too hot during the day, too thin to support wingbeats that would expend more energy than they'd gain. Some have vestigial wing-cases, fused shut, decorative now, stamped with the same meaningless patterns that cover the plain itself. As if the making, operating at geological scale, still noticed them. Still marked them. Still couldn't help itself.

They subsist on the algae films and the filamentous growths. Their mouthparts are adapted for scraping, for harvesting thin biological films from metal surfaces that never designed to support them. In the hours before dawn, they emerge from the deeper channels and spread across the condensation-wet surface, drinking from the shallow pools, eating what can be eaten. By mid-morning, they are gone, retreated to the shadows, waiting out the dangerous hours.

You find their husks sometimes. Moltings, perhaps, or remains. The carapaces do not decompose. They persist, accumulating in the channel bottoms alongside the sediment and the metal filings, becoming part of the youngest geology. In a thousand years, or ten thousand, whatever processes turn this accumulated grit into recognizable strata will include them. They will be fossils. They will be trace evidence of life that learned to exist in the cracks of a process too large to notice them.

---

The larger things—if you can call them larger, if you can grant them that spatial dignity—live in the places where the making has paused.

The plain is not uniformly under construction. There are sections where the stamping presses fell silent decades ago, where the protective film has long since peeled away, where the surface has been ground smooth by wind and thermal cycling. These are not abandoned, exactly. Abandonment implies departure, implies that something left and might return. These sections are simply *finished*. The making moved on. It will not come back.

In these finished sections, where the sediment has had time to accumulate and the drainage channels have had time to erode, something else has taken hold.

It has no name. It needs no name. Names are for things that need to distinguish themselves from other things, and there is nothing else like it here.

It moves slowly. So slowly that you could watch it for hours and see nothing change, then return years later and find that it has migrated kilometers across the surface. It is a collective organism, perhaps, or a single individual operating on timescales that make human perception seem frantic. It is organized around the channels, sending tendrils down into the accumulated grit, harvesting the moisture and the biological films, processing them through organs that might be digestive or might be something else—manufacturing, perhaps, turning organic matter into compounds that can survive the surface temperatures.

It is the color of oxidized metal. It matches the plain. When it moves across the stamped sections, it is invisible except in the hours before dawn, when the condensation reveals its outline—wet where it has passed, dry where it has not. It leaves trails. Or perhaps it leaves *marks*. Perhaps the making is not the only thing here that stamps.

You have seen it. Or you have seen what might be it. You have seen sections of channel that were shallow become deep, sections that were dry become wet, sections that held nothing suddenly supporting growth. You have seen the older channels develop new erosion patterns, rounded edges becoming sharper, accumulated sediment rearranged into structures that serve no hydrological purpose but might serve some other purpose entirely.

It does not respond to you. You are too fast, too brief, too temporary. It operates in a timescale where your presence is barely noticeable, where your walking hours register as vibrations too short to identify. You could stand in its path for a lifetime and it would simply flow around you, processing you the way it processes everything else—slowly, methodically, integrating you into whatever it is making.

Because it is making something. You are certain of this. The channels in the finished sections do not erode randomly. They develop. They deepen in patterns that suggest intention, or something like intention—something that serves the purpose of the making itself, even if the making here is not the same as the making out there, where the stamping presses operate on scales that dwarf everything below them.

---

There are things that live in the transition zones, where the finished sections meet the sections still under construction. These are the hardest to describe because they are the hardest to see.

They exist at the boundaries. They have adapted to the percussion, the vibration, the constant groaning of metal being shaped at scales beyond human comprehension. They are not biological in any recognizable sense. They are processes more than organisms—patterns that propagate through the transition zone, exploiting the energy differentials between hot and cold, finished and unfinished, stamped and smooth.

You see their traces in the protective film that peels at the edges. The peeling is not random. It follows patterns that radiate outward from certain points, spiraling or branching or simply spreading in waves that take weeks to complete. Something is happening there, at the boundary between the making and the made. Something is finding purchase in the gap.

Perhaps they feed on the energy of construction. Perhaps they have evolved to harvest the heat of stamping, the friction of welding, the immense mechanical energy that the making pours into the plain. Perhaps they are parasites, feeding on the margins of a process too large to notice them, growing in the spaces where the making's attention does not reach.

Or perhaps they are something else entirely. Perhaps they are what comes after the making. Perhaps the finished sections are not finished at all, but simply in transition—simply waiting for whatever takes hold when the stamping presses move on and the welding goes silent and the plain is left to the things that move in the dark, in the heat, in the spaces between what is made and what will be made.

---

You are not the only one who walks here.

You find the traces sometimes. Footprints in the accumulated sediment, filled with water or dried to hard outlines. They are human-sized, human-shaped. They do not match your stride, your weight, your particular way of walking. Someone else has passed this way. Someone else is passing this way.

You have never seen them. The plain is large enough, the visibility poor enough, that two people could walk for days without crossing paths. Or perhaps they have learned to avoid you. Perhaps they have been here longer, learned the rhythms of the making and the made, learned how to move through the finished sections without leaving traces, learned how to exist in the narrow band between too hot and too cold, too loud and too quiet.

Perhaps they know what the thing in the finished sections is making. Perhaps they have found a way to communicate with it, to exist in its timescale, to understand its purpose. Perhaps they have been absorbed into it, becoming part of whatever it is becoming, their bodies processed into metallic compounds, their consciousness expanded to operate in the slow margins where biology meets geology meets something else entirely.

Or perhaps they are simply survivors, like you. Perhaps they walked onto the plain one day, or were left here, or were made here—born from some process you do not understand—and they have been walking ever since. Perhaps they have found the reservoirs where the water collects. Perhaps they have mapped the channels and the transitions and the safe places to wait out the dangerous hours. Perhaps they have been here so long that the plain has become home, the making has become weather, and the things that live in the cracks have become neighbors.

---

And then there is what lives at the horizon.

You cannot see it clearly. The heat shimmer obscures everything at distance, turning the horizon into a dancing line where the metal meets the sky. But sometimes, in the hours before dawn, when the condensation settles and the air grows briefly still, you can make out shapes.

They move. They are too large to be the things in the channels, too organized to be the thing in the finished sections, too present to be the processes at the transition zones. They operate at scales closer to the making itself—not geological, but industrial, moving across the plain in patterns that suggest coordination, intention, purpose.

They might be machines. They might be the machines that do the making—the stamping presses and the welders and the things that have no names because no human language has described them yet. They might be the agents of the process, moving at the edges of the constructed sections, preparing the ground for the next expansion, the next layer, the next phase of whatever the making is becoming.

Or they might be something else. They might be what the making produces when it is not producing the plain. They might be the end result of a process that has no end, each shape at the horizon a new iteration of something that evolves as it constructs, learns as it expands, becomes something different with every square kilometer it stamps into existence.

They do not come closer. They do not respond to your presence. They move at the horizon, where distance becomes indistinct, where heat and light and metal blur into a single shimmering presence that might be watched or might be watching or might be simply the background against which you walk, small and temporary and barely noticeable, a trace in the accumulated sediment that will be covered over by the next layer, the next making, the next phase of a process that operates on timescales you cannot imagine.

---

What lives here is patient.

Everything here has learned to wait. The things in the channels wait out the dangerous hours. The thing in the finished sections waits out the making. The processes at the transition zones wait for the gaps that construction opens. The shapes at the horizon wait for whatever comes next.

And you wait with them.

You have learned to read the surface—the stamped patterns that indicate age, the brush marks that indicate depth, the discoloration that indicates different batches and therefore different times. You have learned to find the moisture, to follow the channels to their destinations, to exist in the narrow band where survival is possible.

You have learned that you are not the protagonist of this place. You are not the reason for the making, not the object of its attention, not the exception that will change its course. You are part of the ecosystem that has developed in the cracks of a process too large to care whether you exist.

This is not despair. This is understanding.

What lives here lives at the margins—in the channels, in the transitions, in the hours before dawn when the condensation turns the stamped patterns into mirrors. What lives here has learned to be small, to be patient, to make use of what the making leaves behind.

And the making continues. The stamping presses pulse. The welders groan. The plain extends in every direction, seam-welded and infinite, manufactured by something that works at geological scale.

You walk. You wait. You live in the cracks where life is possible, part of something you do not understand, evidence that even total construction leaves room for something else—something small, something patient, something that makes its own making in the spaces between.
