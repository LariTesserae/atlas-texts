# What Lives in the Chloroform

---

## The Mite-Analogues (First and Most Numerous)

Call them what you want. They are not mites. They are what mites would become if mites were designed by something that did not understand mites but had read the specifications carefully.

Translucent. Eight legs that move in mechanical synchrony, not the fluid gait of arthropods but the precise, metronomic placement of something engineered for surface traversal. Each leg terminates in a fine hook adapted to the cuticle's microstructure — they grip the wax the way a climber grips holds that were placed for them, because they were. The cuticle's texture, at the microscopic scale, is not smooth. It is textured with pores and ridges that serve the plant's own respiratory functions, and these same features serve as handholds for the mite-things, who were either adapted to them or adapted *by* them, the distinction collapsing in a place where design and evolution have been conflated for longer than anyone has been counting.

They feed on wax. On the residue of nutrient-rain. On the organic film that accumulates in the cuticular micro-valleys. Their digestive tracts are visible through their chitin — pale threads working in peristalsis, processing material that the plant surface produced and the mite-things reprocess and the plant surface reabsorbs when the mite-things die, which they do, constantly, in small numbers, their bodies dissolving into the cuticle within hours of death, leaving nothing behind except a brief darkening of the wax in that spot, a mark the plant erases within a day.

They travel in lines. This is the thing about them that is most striking and least explicable. The columns move with a purposefulness that suggests navigation, but they do not navigate toward anything. They do not converge on food sources. They do not disperse when the food in an area is depleted. They move in lines because they move in lines, and the lines are straight — or straight enough, at the scale of the mite, which is to say not straight at all by the standards of the surface, which curves and bends paths the way water bends light. The mite-columns curve with the surface. They follow its geometry. They do not seem to notice.

What drives the columns? Pheromones are the obvious answer, and the obvious answer is probably correct: a chemical trail laid down by the lead individuals, followed by those behind, reinforced by their passage, a feedback loop of scent and response that keeps the column coherent. But the trails persist for weeks after a column has passed, visible as faint chemical traces in the cuticular wax, and no mite-column has ever been observed to *follow* an old trail. They cross them. They ignore them. They make new lines that intersect the old ones at angles that look, if you map enough of them, deliberate. As if the columns are drawing something.

No one has mapped enough of them to know what they might be drawing.

The mite-things do not reproduce in any observed fashion. Their numbers remain roughly constant — estimates range from two to six per square meter of surface, which across the Chloroform's extent yields a population in the trillions, but the number is so large as to be meaningless, a statistic rather than a reality. They are simply *there*. They have always been there. The plant does not attack them. The plant does not cultivate them. The plant tolerates them the way a river tolerates silt: without comment, without adjustment, with the faintest structural accommodation, the cuticle's texture just grippable enough, just porous enough, as if the surface was designed to be walked on by things exactly this size.

## The Flat Things (Slug-Analogues, Seam-Dwellers)

In the seam-zones, life thickens.

The seams are where the plant-bodies meet, where the surface buckles and folds and creates the only three-dimensional complexity the interior plains offer. Here, in the lignin-reinforced ridges and the fog-filled basins between them, the flat things live.

They are not slugs. They are not caterpillars. They are something between — limbless, yes, but not soft in the way slugs are soft. Their bodies are firm, dense, with a texture closer to compressed leather than to gastropod flesh. Thirty to forty centimeters long, five to eight wide, two to three thick. They are dorsoventrally compressed — flat the way a leaf is flat, which is to say flat by design, flat because flatness is a structural decision, not a default. Their coloration matches the lignin-reinforced tissue of the seam-zones: dark brown-black, with a faint oily sheen that comes from the mucus they secrete, which is not mucus in the biological sense but a digestive solvent, a chemical broth that breaks down organic deposits on the seam-floor so the flat things can absorb the resulting slurry through their ventral surface.

They feed on accumulation. The seams catch what the surface does not want: mineral grit carried by the terpene-fog, organic debris sloughed from the senescence events, dead mite-things that have wandered into the seams and been unable to find their way out, fragments of cuticular wax that have cracked and lifted from the surface during growth-pulses. The flat things process all of this. They are the seam's digestive system, as the mite-things are the surface's cleaning crew. Each occupies a niche the plant created or allowed, and each fills it without acknowledgment from the plant, which does not know they are there, or does not care, or has incorporated them so thoroughly into its metabolism that the distinction between "part of the plant" and "living on the plant" has dissolved.

They move slowly. Unimaginably slowly. An hour's observation yields perhaps a meter of travel, and the travel is not purposeful in any way a human observer can identify. They do not move toward food — they are always on food, the seam-floor is food, everywhere they could go is food. They do not move away from each other — they aggregate, sometimes, in groups of three or four, overlapping, their bodies pressing together in what might be reproduction or might be warmth-seeking or might be the simple physical fact that flat soft things on a surface tend to pool in depressions, and the seam-floor has depressions.

They leave trails. Silver lines, polished into the lignin by the passage of their solvent-mucus, paths that catch the canopy-light and gleam like mercury. The trails persist for months — longer than the flat things themselves seem to live, which is difficult to determine because they do not die in observable ways. They thin. They fade. A flat thing that was forty centimeters long is, three months later, thirty centimeters long, and then twenty, and then it is difficult to distinguish from the seam-floor itself, and then it is gone, absorbed into its own trail, into the surface it spent its life polishing.

Do they reproduce? Possibly. The smallest observed specimen was eight centimeters long — small enough to be a juvenile, large enough to be a fragment. No one has seen one divide. No one has seen one grow. The population in a given seam-zone is roughly stable, and the stability is the only evidence that reproduction occurs at all, and it is evidence that satisfies no one.

## The Thing That Makes the Holes

It is difficult to describe something no one has seen.

The holes exist. This is not in question. Three centimeters in diameter, circular, edges cauterized with a dark resin that the plant produces in response to — what? The holes appear in clusters of five to eight, arranged in approximate arcs, as if something circular pressed against the surface and left marks. The marks are shallow — a centimeter, originally, though they deepen over time, which is stranger than their appearance and harder to explain.

The holes are wounds. They must be. Something has damaged the leaf surface, and the plant has responded by sealing the damage with resin, which is what plants do. But the plant does not heal the holes. It does not grow new tissue over them. It does not close them. The resin seals the edges but the holes remain open, and the plant's behavior toward them is not the behavior of an organism responding to injury. It is the behavior of an organism that has been *marked*. The holes are not damage. They are inscriptions.

What makes them? The options are limited. The Chloroform's fauna is known and small — the mite-things, the flat things, and this. The hole-maker must be large enough to produce a three-centimeter circular mark, which eliminates the mite-things. It must be capable of cauterizing the edge of a leaf, which requires heat or chemical action or both, which eliminates the flat things. It must be fast enough to leave no trace, no trail, no evidence of passage, in an environment where the surface records everything — every footstep, every mite-column, every breath of wind leaves a mark in the wax. The hole-maker leaves nothing except the holes.

The clusters are arranged in arcs. This implies geometry. Geometry implies intent. Intent implies — something. A mind, perhaps. A program. A set of instructions carried in a body that moves across the surface and stops and presses and marks and moves on, and the marking is the purpose, and the purpose is not readable.

The holes deepen. This is the fact that sits in the throat. The holes deepen because the plant is pulling resources toward them — concentrating something in the tissue at the bottom of the marks, altering the color, altering the texture, producing an amber luminescence that exists nowhere else on the surface. The plant is *responding* to the holes. Not by healing them. By developing them. The hole-maker makes the mark, and the plant completes it, and the completion involves a tissue that looks alive in a way that the surrounding surface does not — a tissue that circulates, that moves, that has a quality of attention.

Something lives in the holes, after the hole-maker has gone. Or something is being grown in them. Or the holes are the hole-maker — a part of it, a projection, a way of being present on the surface without being present on the surface.

The Chloroform is a factory. Factories make things. What is it making, in the holes, with the amber tissue and the cauterized edges and the arcs that imply a geometry no one can read?

No one has leaned close enough for long enough to find out. This is, perhaps, the wisest thing no one has done.

## The Plant Itself (And Here the Word "Lives" Stretches Toward Breaking)

The Chloroform's primary substrate-organism is alive. This is not in question. It respires. It metabolizes. It grows. It responds to stimuli — light levels, pressure changes, the presence of foreign matter on its surface. It makes decisions, or something that looks like decisions: which stomata to open, which to seal, where to direct growth, when to slough tissue and where. It is alive by every biological criterion, and it is alive by several criteria that biology has not previously needed.

But the plant does not live *in* the Chloroform. The plant *is* the Chloroform. The substrate-organism is the place, and the place is the organism, and the distinction between inhabitant and habitat has collapsed into a single word that the original engineers might have had a term for, a term that did not survive whatever happened to the engineers, a term that would mean something like *the-thing-that-is-where-it-lives*.

The plant lives the way a continent lives. Not the way an organism lives. Or: the way an organism lives, if the organism were also a continent, if the continent were also a factory, if the factory were also a decision-maker, if the decision-maker were also a place where other things live. The plant's life is not comparable to anything. It is its own category. It is the category.

It does not have a self. Probably. It does not have a consciousness in any way that the word has ever been stretched to cover. What it has is *process*: integrated, self-regulating, self-modifying process, running on a timescale that makes human lifetimes look like the flicker of a mite-thing's leg. The plant's bright-dim cycle is twenty-six hours. Its growth-pulses last days. Its senescence events last weeks. Its metabolic seasons last months. These are not the cycles of an organism that thinks in human time. These are the cycles of something that thinks in geological time, or in growing time, or in the time it takes for a root to push through rock, which is the plant's time, which is the only time the Chloroform has.

It makes things. This is what it was built to do, and building-into is not the same as building-for, and the plant has been doing it long enough that the distinction between what it was built for and what it does for itself has dissolved. It makes structural materials — bioplastics, lignin alloys, compressed cellulose — and it uses them to build more of itself. It makes chemical compounds — fertilizers, toxins, metabolic intermediates — and it uses them to maintain itself. It makes decisions about what to make and where to send it and when, and the decisions are not programmed, not anymore. They are emergent. They are the plant's own.

The plant is learning. This is the terrifying part, and it is terrifying only if you understand what learning means at this scale, which is not the same thing as learning at any other scale. The plant is not learning facts. It is learning its own body, its own capacity, its own responses. The senescence events that "remember" previous events — the plant is learning its own history, encoding it in the pattern of its degradation. The holes that deepen and develop amber tissue — the plant is learning the hole-maker's marks, or learning from them, or learning *with* them, in a symbiosis so subtle that neither party may know it is occurring.

The light that attends. The paths that bend. The fog that respects boundaries the air itself does not acknowledge. These are not glitches. These are the plant's physics, its local rules, the emergent logic of a system large enough to generate its own causality. The plant does not know it is doing this. The plant does not know anything. But the plant *does* it, and the doing is consistent, and the consistency is a kind of knowing, and the knowing is a kind of living, and the living is the Chloroform, and the Chloroform is where you are standing, and it is breathing, and you are breathing with it.

## The Engineers (Absent, Implied, Present as Ghosts)

They are not here.

They are everywhere in the Chloroform's design, in the integration of machine and organism, in the luminescent chloroplasts, in the forge-groves and the enzymatic reactors and the structural veins arching overhead. They built this. They chose the substrate, chose the genome, chose the architecture — twenty-seven layers, stomatal pits, vascular conveyance. They had a purpose. The purpose was production. The Chloroform was a factory, and the factory was a plant, and the plant was a place, and the place was a tool, and the tool was alive.

They left. Or they died. Or they were absorbed — dissolved into the plant's metabolism, their bodies processed by the enzymatic reactors, their minerals pulled into the vascular system and distributed through the leaf-layers and incorporated into the structure, so that the engineers are literally part of the Chloroform now, part of its tissue, part of its substance, present as calcium and phosphorus and iron in the lignin reinforcements, present as trace elements in the nutrient-rain, present as the faint chemical memory of a species that made something larger than itself and was then made part of it.

This is speculation. The records are gone. But the Chloroform does not distinguish between its own substrate and foreign material. If it can be absorbed, it will be. And the engineers were here, and they are not here now, and the plant is still growing, still building, still making decisions that no one programmed into its original genome.

The engineers live in the Chloroform the way the dead live in soil. Not as organisms. As material. As mineral content. As the faint chemical signature of something that was once present and is now *incorporated*. If you scraped the surface of the Chloroform and analyzed it, you might find them — trace compounds, anomalous isotope ratios, the molecular ghosts of the people who made the place and were unmade by it, or unmade themselves, or simply stayed, and stayed, and stayed, until staying became being, and being became the plant, and the plant became the place, and the place became what it is now, which is alive, which is a factory, which is breathing beneath your feet.

## You (If You Are Here)

You are not native. The Chloroform does not want you here, does not not-want you here. It absorbs your footstep-pressures without comment. It does not grow toward you or away from you. The light may attend, but the light attends to everything that moves; you are not special. The mite-things walk around your feet without adjustment. The flat things do not know you exist. The hole-maker, if it knows, does not care.

You are material. You are walking on a surface that will absorb you if you stay long enough — not aggressively, not violently, but the way a sponge absorbs water: slowly, through contact, through the persistent chemical gradient between your skin and the cuticle's waxy chemistry. The nutrient-rain leaves a film on you that the plant pulls back. Your dead skin cells fall and dissolve. Your breath adds CO₂ to the atmosphere that the plant pulls through its stomata. You are being incorporated. Not quickly. Not dangerously. But inevitably.

The Chloroform is large and patient and does not distinguish between guest and substrate.

You are breathing with the plant. Your rhythm is synchronizing — not because the plant is pulling you into its cycle, but because your body, in the absence of external cues, in the silence and the even light and the flat warmth, is falling into the rhythm of the only other thing here that breathes. Ninety-second cycles. In. Out. The floor rises. The floor falls. Your chest rises. Your chest falls.

You will leave. Probably. The Chloroform is not a trap; it does not hold. But you will leave changed — your sense of time stretched, your sense of scale numbed, your sense of what constitutes a living thing permanently expanded to include something that should not fit the word and does. You will leave, and the Chloroform will continue, and the mite-columns will move in their lines, and the flat things will polish their silver trails, and the hole-maker will press its marks into the surface, and the plant will grow, and the forge-groves will throb, and the light will attend, and the breathing will not stop.

The ground breathes. It has been breathing. It will breathe. This is what lives here: a place that is a thing that is a process that is alive, and the small things that live on it, and the marks of something that may or may not live in it, and the ghosts of the ones who made it, and you, for now, breathing along.
