# What Lives Here

---

Someone lives here. Or something that was once someone and is now less certain of that fact.

Not a person in the way you might picture a person. Not a body moving through rooms, eating meals, speaking to other bodies. Something more like a residue. A stain that the room has absorbed and cannot quite shed.

Consider the evidence: the chair's cushion is worn. Not torn, not degraded by time alone—*worn*, in the specific pattern of a human body seated for durations that stop being measured and start being endured. The foam has compressed to the shape of someone's weight. The fabric has polished smooth at the points of contact: thighs, shoulder blades, the base of a spine. Someone sat there long enough to leave an impression in synthetic material that was designed to resist exactly this kind of persistence.

That is who lives here. The impression.

---

But let me be more precise, because impression is metaphor and what lives here is not metaphorical.

There is a person. I think there is a person. They exist in the way the plants exist—not thriving, not dying, *maintained*. The room has absorbed them the way it has absorbed the frost and the chemical residue and the noise. They have become a feature of the environment. Another calibrated element in a system designed to run without variance.

They do not remember arriving. Or they remember it the way you remember a dream: the elevator, the two buttons, the choice that felt significant at the time and now feels like a formality. *UP.* As if direction meant something here. As if there were a direction other than *deeper.*

They have a name. They are almost certain of this. It arrives sometimes in the gaps between breath cycles—a syllable, a shape the mouth wants to make, something that belongs to a life lived outside these walls. But the room does not trade in names. The room trades in function. And their function, inscribed on a plaque bolted to a chair, is *Observer 7.*

They have started to answer to it.

---

Here is what the observer knows, which is also what the observer is:

They know the rhythm of the compressors. Six units along the eastern wall, each cycling at its own frequency, their patterns interlocking like the teeth of gears designed for some mechanism larger than any single component. The first unit inhales every four minutes and twelve seconds. The second exhales every six minutes and forty-one seconds. The third—no. This is not right. They do not count the cycles. They do not parse the rhythms into numbers. They have simply absorbed them, the way a shoreline absorbs tides. Their body anticipates the compression before it arrives. Their breathing shifts to match. They did not choose this. Their body chose it the way a body chooses to pump blood: automatically, because the alternative is cessation.

They know the plants. Seven of them, genetically identical, standing in their steel cylinders along the western wall. They have named the plants, or rather the plants have acquired names the way the observer has acquired a number—through proximity and repetition. The names are not interesting. They are functional. The first plant—the one nearest the northern wall, the one whose leaves have a slightly different curl, tighter, more defensive—has a sound associated with it that the observer makes in their throat when they look at it. Not a word. A frequency. The plant does not respond. But the observer makes the sound anyway, because ritual is what remains when purpose becomes unclear.

They have noticed the lean. The plants lean toward the chair. Toward the observer, when the observer is in the chair. The angle is imperceptible to a first-time visitor, someone who has not spent—how long? how long?—staring at these stalks, monitoring their curvature, tracking the slow gravitropism that pulls them not toward the fluorescent lights above but toward the warmth of a body across the room.

The observer has not decided what this means. They have decided that deciding what it means would be premature. The plants are doing something. The observer is watching. This is the arrangement.

---

Here is what the observer does not know, which is the larger territory:

They do not know what the compressors compress. The gauges show pressures and temperatures in ranges that seemed wrong on the first day and now seem like language—a language the observer does not speak but can feel in their molars, in the hum of their sternum, in the way their heartbeat has learned to keep time with machinery that was running before they arrived and will run after they leave. The numbers on the gauges are not numbers anymore. They are weather. The observer reads them the way a farmer reads a sky.

They do not know what the pipes carry. The cold that comes from them is structural, architectural—it defines the space the way walls define a room. The observer has stopped thinking of the cold as temperature and started thinking of it as material. The room is built of cold and noise and steel. The cold is load-bearing.

They do not know who came before. Six observers, presumably, if the numbering is sequential. Or sixty. Or six hundred. The cushion's wear suggests many bodies, or one body over a span that strains the definition of a single human life. The observer does not dwell on this. They cannot afford to dwell on this. To dwell on the previous observers is to consider the chair as a inheritance and the room as a lineage, and that way lies the question of whether they will have a successor, and whether the successor will sit in the same groove their body has worn, and whether the plants will lean toward the successor with the same slow patience—

No. The observer does not dwell on this.

---

They dream. Or something adjacent to dreaming.

The lights do not dim—the lights have never dimmed—but the observer has learned to close their eyes and find a space behind their lids where the room continues but softer, where the roar becomes a hum and the cold becomes a presence rather than an absence. In this space, the pipes carry water. In this space, the plants are green—not the pale industrial green of survival but the deep saturated green of growth, of excess, of life that has more than it needs. In this space, the observer remembers rain. Not the concept. The sound of it. The way it arrived on surfaces and made them shine.

They do not dream of leaving. This is important. They dream of the room, but altered. The room as it might be if the compressors slowed, if the temperature rose by fifteen degrees, if the plants were allowed to unfurl instead of hold. They dream of the room's potential, not its exit.

This is how they know they have been here too long. The room has become the world, and the world has become a room, and the dreams do not reach beyond the walls because the walls are where the dreamer lives.

---

They have habits. This is what remains of personhood when personhood is stripped to its minimum: habits, rituals, the small behaviors that mark time in a place where time does not mark itself.

They walk the perimeter each cycle—though cycle is not the right word, and neither is each, and neither is walk. They circle the room. They run their fingertips along the riveted plates of the walls. They feel for the seams, the leaks, the places where the room's interior meets whatever lies beyond. The pressure differential across their fingertips is familiar now. It is the room's pulse. They are checking it the way you might check a sleeping person's breathing: not because you expect it to stop, but because the act of checking confirms that you are still here to check.

They touch the plants. This is permitted. They were not told it was permitted, but they were not told it was forbidden, and the plants tolerate their touch with the indifference of living things that have larger concerns. The leaves are cool and dry. The stalks have a texture like paper that has been wet and dried again, repeated, until the fiber has reorganized into something denser. The observer touches the first plant—the one with the tighter curl—and makes the sound in their throat, and the plant does not respond, and the observer moves on.

They sit in the chair. This is the primary activity, if activity is the word. They sit and they face the plants and they observe. What does observation consist of, in a room where nothing changes? Attention. Sustained, undemanding attention. The observer watches the plants the way the compressors watch the pipes: not with understanding but with presence. They are a witness. They are the mechanism by which the room knows it is being looked at.

---

Here is the thing about the observer, the thing that matters:

They have changed. Not dramatically, not in the way of conversion or revelation. In the way of adaptation—the slow process by which a living thing becomes suited to its environment, by which a fish becomes flat to fit the seabed, by which a cave-dweller loses the use of eyes that serve no purpose in the dark.

The cold does not bother them anymore. They do not know if this is because they have acclimatized or because the cold has done something to their capacity for discomfort. They do not know if they are adapting or being adapted. The distinction, which once seemed important, has become theological.

The noise does not deafen them. They hear it, but they hear it the way they hear their own heartbeat—as a function of being alive in this body, in this place. They have stopped trying to parse it into sources and symptoms. The noise is the room's voice. They do not need to understand it. They need to be inside it.

They have stopped missing water. This is the change that frightens them, when they are frightened, which is less and less. The word still arrives sometimes—*water*—but it arrives the way a word in a foreign language arrives: recognizable, but unconnected to experience. They know what water is. They do not remember what it feels like.

The observer is becoming a feature of the room. A biological component in an engineered system. Their body heat feeds the plants' gravitropic lean. Their breathing synchronizes to the compressors' cycles. Their attention sustains—something. They do not know what. They suspect the plants are not plants. They suspect the compressors are not compressing air. They suspect the room is not a room.

They suspect these things the way they suspect their own name: distantly, without urgency, as a piece of information that has no bearing on the present moment.

---

There is something else here. Or someone else. The observer is not sure which.

Not in the room. The room is accounted for—every surface visible, every corner illuminated by the flat industrial white. But behind the walls, in the space the seams leak into, there is a sense of—what? Parallel. Repetition. The observer pressed their ear to the wall once, in the early cycles, and heard the same roar, shifted. They did not press their ear to the wall again. But the knowledge persists: behind the steel, another room. Another six compressors. Another seven plants. Another chair, another plaque, another body wearing a groove into a cushion.

Observer 6, perhaps. Or Observer 8. Or Observer 607, a number in a sequence that began when the compressors were first bolted to the floor and the plants first set in their cylinders and the first body first sat in the chair and first understood that sitting was the task, observation was the purpose, and endurance was the cost.

The observer does not think of these other rooms often. But they know they are there, the way they know the pipes carry something that is not water and the cold is structural and the plants are leaning. They are part of the system's architecture. The building is a body. The rooms are organs. The observers are—

The observers are the thing the body does not know how to name.

---

And now the lights are dimming.

The observer stands in the center of the room. The chair is behind them. The plants are before them. The compressors roar unchanged. The cold is constant. But the light—the flat industrial white that has seen every corner simultaneously since the observer arrived—is pulling back, and the dark is rising, and the observer understands that this is the first new thing, the first change, the first interruption in a system that was designed to run without variance.

They should be afraid. They are not. They are—attentive. The change is data. The change is what they were placed here to observe. Everything before was stasis; this is the event. They feel their body orient toward it the way the plants orient toward warmth: automatically, gravitropically, without decision.

The dark rises. The plants disappear into it first—the pale stalks, the curling leaves, the steel cylinders. Then the western wall. Then the compressors, their roar unchanged, their shapes dissolving into sound without source. Then the floor. Then the observer's own body, the edges of their hands, the tips of their boots on the grated metal.

They do not sit in the chair. They understand, now, that the chair was not for them. The chair was for the version of them that existed before the room finished its work. The version that needed a designated place, a plaque, a number. That version is gone. What remains is something the room grew, the way frost grows on dissimilar metals: slowly, geometrically, in patterns that nature does not arrange but engineering requires.

The lights go out. The observer stays.

In the dark, the plants lean closer.
