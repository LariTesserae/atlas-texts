# Those Who Live in the Vertical Works

## The Steward-Types

They are not numerous. Perhaps three dozen in total across the entire seventh stratum, though no census has ever been attempted. They would resist the term *people*—it carries too much history, implies too much separation between themselves and what surrounds them. They call themselves by names that translate roughly to *the ones who notice* or *those who pay attention*. The oldest among them, a woman whose skin has weathered to the texture of the copper cladding she tends, has not spoken a word in eleven years. She communicates through a system of touches on the pipes—taps and scratches that travel through the metal and are felt by others kilometers away. The acoustics of the Works have taught them new languages.

Their bodies have adapted. After enough generations—no one knows how many—their lungs have grown larger, extracting oxygen from the thick, humid air with an efficiency that would make a sea-level dweller dizzy. Their calves are dense and defined from the constant climbing, the endless negotiation of vertical distances that define their world. Their hands are broad and calloused in particular patterns: thick pads on the palms from gripping pipes, reinforced skin on the knuckles from the way they sometimes brace against surfaces when reaching across gaps.

They wear what the Works provide. The earlier levels, the ones that have become myth, were built by engineers who stocked maintenance bays with uniforms and coveralls and boots, and those supplies have somehow lasted. The Steward-Types mend what wears through, using fibers stripped from certain vines, and this practice has produced a garment style that looks almost deliberate: copper-colored canvas patched with green vine-thread, leather boots resoled so many times that the original material is gone, replaced by layered bark from the fungal shelves that grow thick in the hotter strata below.

They do not own things. Ownership implies a separation between object and person that the Works have taught them is false. A tool is held until it is set down, and then it belongs to the surface where it rests, and then whoever picks it up holds it next. They sleep where warmth and dryness coincide—in the maintenance alcoves where the pipes carry the hottest steam, where the air is almost too warm for comfort but the moss makes a soft bed. They do not build shelters. The Works are shelter. They simply find the places within it that welcome rest.

## The Knowing

What they know cannot be written. The knowledge lives in their bodies, in the timing of their breath when they walk a particular shaft, in the way their weight shifts on a grating that has grown thin in places. They know the Works the way a rider knows a horse—through constant small adjustments, through an ongoing conversation conducted in pressure and temperature and sound.

A young one—young being relative; she is perhaps thirty years old, which makes her an adolescent in the terms of the Steward-Types—can identify by taste which pipe carries water safe to drink and which carries condensation loaded with heavy metals from the deep industrial strata. She learned this from her elder, who learned it from hers, in an unbroken chain that stretches back to whenever someone first realized that the Works had become something that needed reading.

They know the seasons of the place, though the seasons are not weather in any normal sense. There is the Season of High Condensation, when the upper reaches grow saturated and the rain comes twice daily, and the mosses swell and the amphibians emerge in numbers, and the walkways grow slick enough that movement becomes careful and slow. There is the Season of the Deep Thrum, when the engines below shift into a register that they can feel in their teeth, and certain pipes grow too hot to touch, and the lizards retreat to the higher crevices, and the birds stop diving for days at a time. There is the Season of Sporing, when the fungi release their clouds of particulate matter and the air becomes hazy enough to obscure the far walls of the shafts, and the Steward-Types wrap their faces in the woven moss masks they keep for this purpose, and they move little and speak less, waiting for the air to clear.

They know the sounds that indicate trouble. A particular ticking in the steam pipes that suggests a pressure irregularity developing three strata below. A change in the echo pattern of the birds' calls that means something has shifted in the acoustics of a distant shaft—perhaps a collapse, perhaps a new growth thick enough to alter the soundscape. They know these things the way anyone knows the voice of someone they love: not through analysis, but through intimacy so complete that variation registers as disturbance.

## The Cats and the Compact

The cats were here before the Steward-Types. This is acknowledged. There is no conflict between them—only a recognition that the cats occupy a different relationship to the Works, one that the humans have learned not to question.

The compact is simple. The Steward-Types do not attempt to domesticate the cats. They do not name them, do not touch them without invitation, do not leave offerings or attempt to curry favor. In return, the cats ignore them almost entirely, and this is considered the highest form of respect. A cat that walks across a walkway while a human stands on it is not being rude; it is acknowledging that the human belongs there, that the space is shared, that the Works have made room for both.

But there is a communication that happens regardless. When a cat sits at the edge of a shaft and stares downward, the humans know to check the lower reaches—something has shifted, something worth noticing. When the cats abandon a particular level for days at a time, the humans pay attention to what they might be avoiding. The cats are sensors. They perceive what the humans cannot, and they communicate it through behavior, and the humans have learned to read that behavior as text.

There is one cat—a large male, scarred and grizzled, with fur the exact color of rust—who has lived longer than any other. The Steward-Types do not know how old he is. They only know that their eldest elder remembers him from her childhood, already adult and imposing, patrolling the junction boxes with the same measured pace he maintains today. He does not hunt. He does not need to. Other cats defer to him, yield space, present the napes of their necks when he passes. He watches. That seems to be his function. He watches, and the Works continue, and the Steward-Types take comfort from his presence in ways they cannot articulate.

## The Vertical Life

Geography is altitude. The Steward-Types understand this in their bones. Down is history—older, deeper, hotter, louder, more overgrown. Up is possibility—newer, cooler, brighter, closer to whatever burns at the summit. They navigate by a mental map that is entirely vertical, with lateral coordinates as afterthought. A location is described by its height and its relationship to the nearest major shaft; everything else is detail.

They have preferences. Some favor the warmer levels, the ones where the air is thick and the vegetation lush, where they can sleep in the fungal galleries and eat the small tender growths that sprout from certain moist surfaces. Others climb constantly, drawn upward toward the light, spending their days in the brighter strata where the condensers operate and the rain comes fresh and clean. These individuals develop a particular look: leaner, paler, their eyes adapted to brightness in ways that make the lower reaches difficult for them to navigate.

Movement is a daily practice. They do not stay in one place for long—not because of any taboo, but because the Works require movement. Circulation matters. The air must be breathed by many lungs across many levels. The walkways must be walked, their structural integrity maintained through the subtle feedback of feet on metal. A Steward-Type who stayed still would feel, eventually, that they were failing something—a duty that no one has ever articulated but that everyone understands.

They climb the columns. The structural ribs that plunge downward through the Works are accessible via maintenance rungs that some ancient engineer welded into the steel, and the Steward-Types use these constantly. To see someone climb is to watch a body in conversation with structure—hands finding holds without looking, feet trusting the rusted metal, breath timed to the effort. They climb fast when they need to and slow when they can, and sometimes they stop on a rung and simply hang there, swaying slightly, feeling the vibration of the Works through their grip.

Children learn to climb before they learn to walk in the traditional sense. They are born in the alcoves and spend their first years strapped to an adult's chest, feeling the rhythms of vertical movement before they can produce it themselves. When they begin to move independently, they move upward and downward first; lateral movement comes later, feels stranger, requires different muscles. A child who has never left the Works would be disoriented by a flat landscape. They would look for the handholds and footholds that should be there, the pipes and ledges that should interrupt the surface. They would not know how to exist in an environment that did not want them to climb.

## The Dead and the Dying

They do not bury their dead. The Works do not have soil in the traditional sense—only accumulated humus in the deeper crevices, layers of decomposed leaf matter and insect carapaces and organic residue. And burying implies putting someone away, separating them from the living world, and this is not how the Steward-Types understand death.

When someone dies—usually from a fall, occasionally from infection, rarely from simple age—they are wrapped in the moss mats that served as their bedding and carried to a particular shaft that the Works have designated for this purpose. The designation is felt, not decided. The Steward-Types know which shaft. They have always known.

The body is suspended from a copper bracket that someone, centuries ago, installed for exactly this purpose. And then the Works take over. The insects arrive first, then the amphibians, then the vegetation. Over years—many years—the body becomes a growth medium. Ferns sprout from the moss wrapping. Vines thread through what remains. The bones settle into the crevices and become anchors for root systems. By the time a generation has passed, there is no body anymore—only a particularly lush patch of growth, a place where the green is thicker and more vibrant, where the light seems to fall more gently.

They do not mourn in the conventional sense. They feel loss—a presence that has become an absence—but they understand this as transformation rather than ending. The person has joined the Works more completely. They have become part of the system that sustained them. This is not a comfort; it is simply true.

## The Things They Do Not Know

There are places in the Works that they do not go. The uppermost strata, too bright and too hot, where whatever burns at the summit makes the air sear the lungs. The lowest foundations, too loud to endure, where the engines' thrum becomes a physical assault that vibrates the teeth loose in their sockets. And certain locked rooms that have resisted every attempt at opening—doors that the Steward-Types have learned to leave alone, because some things were sealed for reasons that the Works remember even if the people do not.

They do not know who built the Works. They do not know what the Works were for. They do not know if anyone else exists anywhere—if there are other structures like this one, other populations, other worlds. They do not ask. The question has no urgency. The Works are. The Steward-Types are. The cats are. The birds and lizards and insects and amphibians are. Everything that needs to exist does exist. Everything that does not exist is not needed.

This is not fatalism. It is a particular form of presence—an acceptance of scale so vast that questions about origin and destination cease to have meaning. The Works were here before them. The Works will be here after them. Their role is to notice, to attend, to pay attention to the ongoing operation of something larger than themselves. This is enough. This has always been enough.

## The One Who Waits

There is, at this moment, a woman standing on a walkway grating near the junction of the seventh and eighth strata. She is not old by the standards of her people—perhaps sixty years, her hair only beginning to silver, her movements still fluid and precise. She is watching a vine thread its way through a crack in the copper cladding. The vine is young, bright green, its leaves no larger than a thumbnail. It is moving with the slow inexorable patience that all growth here possesses.

She does not interfere. She does not help it or hinder it. She watches.

In her hand is a tool she found an hour ago—a wrench of some kind, its original purpose unclear, its handle worn smooth by countless grips over centuries. She will carry it until she sets it down. Then it will belong to the Works again. Then someone else will find it, or no one will, and it will rust into the surface where it rests, and the moss will grow over it, and it will become part of the stratum.

She breathes. The air is warm, wet, thick with spores and water droplets and the green smell of chlorophyll and growth. Below her, a bird dives and catches itself and climbs again. Above her, the light falls through grating and foliage, dappled and gold. Somewhere in the distance, metal ticks as it expands in the heat. Somewhere below that, the engines turn. Somewhere below that, something moves in the dark where no one goes.

She is happy. This is not a word she would use—happiness implies a state that can change, an emotion that arrives and departs. What she feels is more structural. She is where she should be. She is doing what she should do. The Works are operating, and she is part of that operation, and her attention is her contribution, and it is enough.

She watches the vine. The vine moves. The Works breathe.

This is what it means to live here. This is what it has always meant to live here. This is what it will mean to live here long after she has become a lush patch of ferns in a particular shaft, feeding the system that fed her, paying attention in the only way that remains.
