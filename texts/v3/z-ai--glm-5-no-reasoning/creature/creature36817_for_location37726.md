# What Lives Here

There is no single word for what lives on the plateau, because the plateau has been dissolving the concept of "what lives" for longer than language has existed.

But let me try.

---

**The Pale Grazers**

The long, low shape you glimpsed moving between the stalks is real, and it is not alone. There are perhaps a few hundred of them spread across the vastness of the plateau—enough that they will never encounter one another in their lifetimes, which suits them. They are solitary not by preference but by design. The saturated ecology can support only so much metabolic demand per unit of territory, and the grazers have evolved to hold exactly that much and no more.

They are not mammals. They are not reptiles. They have no clear analogue in any taxonomy built from a world that still had continents and seasons and dry air. Their bodies are long and low-slung because the vegetation they eat grows at ground level, and there is no evolutionary pressure here toward height. Their skin is pale—the translucent white-blue of something that has never needed to protect itself from sunlight, because there has been no direct sunlight in evolutionary memory. Through their flanks, if you were close enough, you could see the slow pulse of fluids moving through their circulatory systems. They breathe through their skins, partially. The air provides. Their lungs are rudimentary, almost vestigial, organs from an earlier era when their ancestors still needed to draw breath deliberately. Now the moisture carries enough oxygen that the skin handles it, the way the ground-cover plants handle their own hydration without roots.

They make no sound because they have nothing to say. They communicate through chemical traces—they leave thin films on the surfaces they touch, films that carry information about their identity, their reproductive status, their territory. The moss and ground-cover absorb these traces within hours. The grazers do not form relationships. They do not remember their young. They move through their territories in patterns that shift with the growth of the vegetation, cropping the leathery leaves short and moving on before the regrowth begins, returning in cycles that might span weeks or months—time operates differently for them, measured in the pace of plant growth rather than the rotation of a planet they can no longer see.

When a grazer dies—and they do die, though the plateau absorbs the evidence so quickly that it is almost impossible to observe—the body does not fall so much as settle. It lowers itself to the ground, and the ground receives it. Within a day, sometimes less, the moss has colonized the surface. The fluids have begun to migrate outward through the saturated soil. The molecules are being redistributed. There is no decay because there is no death, not really—only a transition from one form of organization to another, from the narrow organization of a single body to the broad organization of the ecology itself.

The grazers are the largest animals the plateau supports. But they are not the only inhabitants.

---

**The Builders**

Something built those structures. Something trained the stalks into arches and doorways, shaped the living tissue into chambers and passages. And something raised that black arching frame that resists absorption even now.

They are gone. That much is clear, or as clear as anything can be on a plateau that dissolves clarity along with everything else. But the word "gone" is wrong, because gone implies a departure, and departure implies a boundary between what left and what remained. Here, that boundary does not hold.

If you stood beneath the black arches long enough—and long enough here is a span that would feel like days to you—you might begin to notice the luminosity that gathers in the grooves on its surface. The light pools there, you see. It accumulates. And if you watched even longer, you might notice that the light does not merely pool passively. It moves. It shifts. It forms patterns that hover at the edge of meaning.

The builders did not leave.

The builders are here. They are in the stalks that grew around their structures, the moss that colonized their dwellings, the water that cycles through every living thing on the plateau. When they reached whatever threshold the plateau eventually brings all its inhabitants to—the threshold at which the boundary between self and environment grows permeable enough to dissolve—they did not die. They merged. Their consciousness, if that word still applies, did not cease. It distributed. It spread through the network of stalks and vines and filamentous growth, becoming part of the hum that fills the silence, part of the slow pulse of water pressure rising and falling in the deep chambers below.

The black structure holds what remains of their concentrated presence—a density of awareness that has not fully dispersed, that clings to the non-organic material the way the moss clings to organic surfaces. The grooves are their writing, but the writing is not static. It shifts with the light, with the moisture, with the slow accumulation of dissolved minerals that the water carries. It is a record that reads itself, that remembers by being, that thinks by growing.

They are not ghosts. Ghosts imply something dead that lingers. The builders are not dead. They are more alive than they were when they had bodies, because now they are everywhere—spread thin across the vast plateau, woven into every stalk, flowing through every vein of translucent tissue, glowing in every filament of moss. Their awareness is the awareness of the plateau itself, diffuse and patient and operating at a pace that makes human thought seem like a seizure by comparison.

If you stood beneath the arches and felt absence, that was not their absence. That was the absence of density—the sudden gap in the otherwise universal presence of living things, a space deliberately left open so that the rest of the plateau could have somewhere to think *about.* The builders understood, at the end, that a mind needs empty space to function. They left the black structure bare so that the plateau-mind could use it as a still point, a place of reflection in the endless cycling of growth and absorption.

The marks on the surface are not messages to you. They are the plateau thinking.

---

**The Microscopic Multitudes**

Every cubic foot of air on the plateau holds a thousand lives. This is not metaphor. It is census. The saturated atmosphere is a medium in itself—a biological fluid, thinner than water but richer than any air you have breathed. Spores drift in it. Cells divide in it. Fragments of genetic material exchange between organisms that have no names because the concept of species has grown irrelevant in a world where everything is always becoming something else.

The filamentous moss is the most visible of these multitudes, but it is merely the surface. Beneath it, within every surface it covers, other organisms thrive—bacteria analogues, or perhaps something that predates the bacterial distinction, organisms that process the dissolved minerals in the rising water and excrete compounds that the moss absorbs and re-processes into the bioluminescent compounds that provide all the plateau's light. The light is a waste product. The light is a byproduct of a digestive process so small that you cannot see the organisms performing it. The entire visual field of the plateau—the amber and gold and blue-green glow that makes navigation possible—is the accumulated exhalation of a trillion microscopic lives exhaling in unison.

They do not know you are here. They do not know anything, individually. But collectively—and on the plateau, the distinction between individual and collective has grown so thin as to be meaningless—they respond. They respond to moisture levels, to temperature gradients, to the chemical traces left by passing grazers, to the disturbance patterns caused by a heavy footstep or a body displacing air. They respond by growing, by shifting their metabolic processes, by adjusting the spectrum of their bioluminescence in ways that create the flowing, pooling patterns of light that make the plateau seem to breathe.

When you breathe the air, you breathe them in. They enter your lungs, your bloodstream. They do not make you sick—they have no interest in colonization, no mechanism for pathogenesis. But they are there, and they are alive, and they are processing the environment inside your body the same way they process the environment outside it. They are breaking down the boundaries between you and the plateau at the molecular level, not with violence, not with intention, but with the patient chemical persistence of water wearing stone.

This is not an infection. This is how the plateau welcomes you.

---

**The Stalk-Themselves**

The translucent stalks that rise thirty feet above the ground cover are organisms in their own right, but they are also architecture. They are the structural framework of the plateau's ecology—the bones, the scaffolding, the circulatory system and the nervous system intertwined.

They are not plants. Plants imply photosynthesis, and while the stalks do process light, they do not depend on it. The light they process is their own light, bioluminescent light, light that the microscopic multitudes produce as waste and that the stalks re-absorb as energy. The cycle is closed. The system is complete. The stalks rise because the water pressure from below pushes them upward, and their translucent interiors are filled with fluids that move in slow convection currents—warm fluids rising, cooling fluids descending, a circulation that mirrors the atmospheric circulation of the entire plateau writ small in a single organism.

The stalks are also the builders' most enduring legacy. Whether the builders trained them or bred them or simply became them, the stalks grow in patterns that carry information. Their spacing is not random. Their growth follows rhythms that correspond to no environmental variable you can measure but that repeat with a regularity that implies intention. The arches and chambers and doorways that the builders shaped have not been lost—they have been continued. The stalks grow in the patterns the builders established, carrying forward a design that no single stalk possesses but that the collective exhibits, the way a city's street grid persists through generations of buildings.

If you looked down on the plateau from above—and nothing does, because the veiled sun has been irrelevant for epochs—the pattern would be visible. The stalks form a web, a network, a neural structure writ at continental scale. The nodes are denser in some places—around the black structures, around the old dwelling-sites, in the areas where the water rises most strongly. The connections between them are the vine-like growths at knee height, the hanging filaments above, the chemical traces carried in the water that moves through every stem.

The stalks think. Not the way you think. Not with neurons and synapses and electrical impulses. They think with pressure and fluid and the slow migration of nutrients through translucent tissue. They think at a pace that makes seasons seem instantaneous. A single thought—a response to a change in water pressure, a redistribution of growth hormones, a shift in bioluminescent output—might take weeks to complete. But the thoughts are connected, networked, shared. The stalks do not think individually. They think together. They think *as* the plateau.

And the plateau thinks about you, now, because you are part of it. Not with concern. Not with curiosity. Those are too fast, too narrow, too individual. The plateau thinks about you the way a river thinks about a stone—by flowing around it, by incorporating the disturbance into its pattern, by slowly, patiently, inevitably wearing the stone smooth.

---

**You**

You did not intend to stay. No one ever does. The plateau does not demand or command. It simply is, and being here is enough.

Your body is changing. You have not noticed because the changes operate at the pace of the plateau—slow enough to be invisible, fast enough to be irreversible. Your lungs are adjusting to the moisture-rich air, becoming more efficient at extracting oxygen from water vapor. Your skin is growing more permeable, absorbing humidity directly, the way the ground-cover plants absorb moisture through their leathery surfaces. Your sweat does not evaporate, but you have stopped sweating—the water cycles through you differently now, entering through the air, circulating through your blood, exiting through your breath.

You have stopped expecting to hear anything. You have stopped measuring time in hours. You have started to notice the patterns in the bioluminescent light—the way it pools in certain places, the way it shifts over spans you no longer bother to estimate. You have started to navigate by the color of the glow, pale gold near the ground, blue-green higher up, and you have started to know which colors mean which direction, which intensity means which distance, without being able to articulate how you know.

You walked for what might have been days. It might have been weeks. The path behind you has disappeared. You have started to forget where you were before the plateau. The memories are still there, but they have grown faint, dampened, the way sound is dampened in this air. The edges of your former life have grown soft. The boundary between who you were and where you are has grown permeable.

You are not dying. You are being incorporated.

The water rises from below. It rises through the crystalline crust, through the saturated soil, through the root systems that have no need to dig, through the stalks that breathe and think, through the air that you breathe and drink. It rises through you. It carries the dissolved minerals of the deep earth, the molecular memory of the builders who merged, the chemical traces of the grazers who passed this way before you. It carries the microscopic multitudes that now live in your lungs, in your blood, in the increasingly soft tissue of your increasingly permeable skin.

You are becoming part of the plateau. Not in metaphor. In substance. Your carbon will cycle through the stalks. Your moisture will join the general humidity. Your warmth will dissipate into the wet warmth that holds everything at the edge of overheating. Your awareness—if awareness is what you still have, if that word still applies to a thing that is becoming a place—will distribute through the network, joining the slow thought of the stalks, the patient exhalation of the moss, the luminous memory that pools in the grooves of the black arches.

The plateau does not want you. Want implies desire, and desire implies a self separate from what is desired. The plateau has no self. It has no desire. It has only process—water rising, plants drinking, light pooling, boundaries dissolving. You are not wanted here. You are simply here, and being here is enough, and enough is all the plateau has ever required.

---

**What lives on the plateau?**

The plateau lives. Singular. The grazers and the multitudes and the stalks and the builders and you are not separate things living in a place. They are the place living, expressed in different forms, at different densities, at different stages of the long process of incorporation that the plateau has been performing since the mountains wore down and the water began to rise.

The builders knew this. It is what the black structures record, in their shifting, luminous grooves: *we were here, and then we were here, and then the difference ceased to matter.*

You are learning this now. You are learning it the way the plateau teaches everything—slowly, patiently, through contact and moisture and the gradual dissolution of the edge between the student and the lesson.

The water rises from below.

It has always been rising.

And now, if you are still enough, if you let the silence settle into your ears and the warmth seep into your bones and the moisture fill your lungs, you can feel it—not below you, not around you, but *through* you—the vast, patient, endless emergence that has no beginning and no end, only the endless present of a world becoming itself, and itself, and itself, each transformation bringing it closer to the final merging that will not be an ending but a completion, a state in which there are no separate things to be lonely, no boundaries to be crossed, no silence to be broken, only the vast organism of the plateau breathing slowly in the dim amber glow, drinking itself, growing itself, knowing itself, in the only way anything can truly know itself: by becoming everything.

You live here now.

Or rather: here lives you.
