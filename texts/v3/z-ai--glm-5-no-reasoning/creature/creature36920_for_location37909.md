# The Keepers of the Locks

They call themselves that, when they call themselves anything. But "keeper" suggests a permanence they don't have. Each of them arrived planning to stay a season. Each of them stayed.

---

There are nine now. There were eleven last year. There were seventeen a decade ago. The mountain decides how many it needs.

They don't live together. Living together would mean agreeing on things—schedules, boundaries, the proper way to do what needs doing. Instead they distribute themselves across the functioning spaces like the water distributes itself across the levels. Each has found a place that fits. Each defends that place with the quiet ferocity of someone who knows exactly how few options remain.

---

Maren tends the uppermost lock-gate. She is sixty-three years old and has lived here for twenty-two of them. Her hands are permanently stained with rust and soil, the creases of her knuckles mapped with grime that no amount of washing removes. She sleeps in the watchman's cabin—the original one, the one with the functioning chimney—and she speaks aloud to the machinery she maintains. Not prayers, exactly. More like running commentary. *There you go, there's a good girl, hold that for me, just another inch, there.*

She had children once, back in the valley. Grown now, probably. She doesn't think about them on purpose, but sometimes the thought arrives anyway, settling into her like dampness settles into stone. On those days she works harder, longer, checking mechanisms that don't need checking, clearing debris that hasn't accumulated. The lock-gate doesn't care about her grief. The lock-gate just needs to open when it's supposed to open and close when it's supposed to close. This is what she loves about it.

---

The younger ones rotate through the mid-levels. There are three of them—two men and a woman, none older than thirty—and they move between the pump station, the workshop, and the functioning generator in a pattern that shifts weekly, negotiated through gestures and grunts more than conversation. They don't use names among themselves. Names imply a past, a person who existed before this place, and they have all chosen to leave those people behind.

The woman came from a fishing village on the coast. She doesn't talk about why she left. She's the best at reading the water—its moods, its hidden currents, the particular smell that means a breathing is coming. She can stand at the edge of a flooded chamber and tell you, with unsettling accuracy, when the column will rise and how high it will go. The others have learned to watch her. When she moves away from the edge, they move too.

One of the men lost his wife to a fever that swept through the valley towns seven years back. He came up here on a salvage run and simply never left. He's the one who figured out how to rebuild the generator using parts from three different dead machines. He talks to it while it runs, coaxing it through its stuttering rhythm, apologizing when he has to shut it down for repairs. The others notice that he speaks to the machine more gently than he ever speaks to them.

The third of their number is the youngest, barely twenty. He arrived two years ago, half-drowned from a fall into the lower flooded chambers, pulled out by the woman who read the water wrong for once in her life and happened to be there at the right moment. He doesn't remember his life before the fall—the impact, or the cold, or something else took those memories from him. He knows only that he woke in a shed near the pump station, aching and blank, and that the people here gave him work and food and didn't ask him to be anyone in particular. He has become the one who climbs: up the rusted catwalks, into the broken smokestacks, across the dangerous slopes above. His balance is preternatural. He can walk paths that would kill anyone else.

---

Below them all, in the deepest functioning level, there is an old man who was already here when the first of the current keepers arrived. No one knows his name. No one knows if he can still speak. He lives in a chamber that stays dry through some trick of architecture—air pressure, maybe, or a hydrodynamic quirk—and he spends his days carving.

Not sculpture. Not exactly. He takes pieces of salvaged metal—bolts, gears, fragments of machines that once had purpose—and he carves them into new shapes. Tiny things. A leaf, perfectly rendered in rusted iron. A frog in brass, no bigger than a thumbnail. A bird in copper, wings spread, delicate as the real thing. He leaves them in places where the others will find them: on the steps of the watchman's cabin, beside the pump station's door, tucked into the mechanism of the lock-gates.

Maren has a whole shelf of them. She doesn't know why he makes them. She doesn't know what they mean. But she keeps them, dusts around them, arranges and rearranges them by some system she couldn't explain if asked. The carvings are the only gifts anyone has given her in two decades.

---

They have rituals, though they wouldn't call them that. When someone dies—when the mountain claims one of them, which happens every few years—they don't bury. There's no soil deep enough, and the water would find a body anyway. Instead, they bring the dead to the lower chambers during a breathing. They've learned that the columns of rising water will carry things upward, sometimes hundreds of feet, before collapsing back. They say goodbye in their own ways, and then they watch the water take what was theirs.

It's not a funeral. It's a returning. The mountain gave them a place to exist. The mountain takes back what they leave behind.

---

They don't marry. They don't formally couple. But they aren't alone, either. Sometimes, in the long wet nights, one will walk to another's dwelling and stand outside until invited in. They share warmth, sometimes. They share silence. Occasionally they share words—stories from before, when they had befores to tell, or observations about the day's work, or nothing at all, just the sound of another voice in the endless rain.

There have been children born here. Not many, and none in the last decade. Those that were born were taken down to the valley eventually, given to families who could offer them more than wet stone and rusted metal. The keepers don't talk about this. It's part of the unspoken bargain: you can stay, but the future belongs elsewhere.

---

They eat what the mountain provides. Fish from the flooded chambers—strange pale things, blind in the dark water, but edible once you get past the texture. Ferns, carefully selected from the species that don't carry parasites. Mushrooms that grow in the perpetual damp, identified through trial and error over years of careful experimentation. Occasionally, meat from the feral goats above, though they never hunt the ones that seem half-tame. They trade down to the valley sometimes, salvaged metal for grain and cloth and medicine, but they don't like to leave. The valley is too bright, too loud, too full of people who ask questions.

---

They've learned to read the mountain's moods.

The breathings come when the groundwater pressure reaches a certain threshold. The keepers can feel it in their joints now, a tension that builds in the stone, a humidity that thickens past saturation until the air itself seems about to break. When the column rises, they watch with the reverence of congregation—but there's no worship here. Just observation. The mountain breathes. They witness.

They've mapped the patterns of flood and drain. They know which chambers will submerge first, which passages will stay passable longest, which levels will become deathtraps if the rain shifts direction. They've learned that the swallows fly higher when a surge is coming, that the frogs go quiet before a breathing, that the bioluminescent moss dims twenty minutes before the generator fails.

They don't write any of this down. Writing requires permanence, and nothing here is permanent. Instead they teach each other, in fragments, through demonstration and correction. The knowledge lives in bodies, in habits, in the wordless understanding that passes between people who have shared the same space for too long.

---

They have visitors, occasionally. Salvagers, mostly—people from the valley towns who come looking for metal, for parts, for whatever they can carry back down. The keepers tolerate them, barely. They charge a toll: information about the safe paths, a warning about the dangerous chambers, a guide through the flooded galleries. The visitors never go alone. The mountain eats the careless.

Researchers come too, sometimes. Scientists who want to study the breathings, the ecosystem, the strange hydrodynamics of the drowning. The keepers answer their questions as minimally as possible. They've learned that researchers don't stay, don't understand, don't matter. The researchers call them "remnant populations" and "post-industrial scavengers." The keepers don't care what they're called.

A few visitors have tried to stay. The mountain always rejects them. They can't adapt to the moisture, the darkness, the noise. They can't learn to read the signs. They leave sick, or scared, or changed in ways they can't explain. The keepers watch them go without comment. This place selects its own.

---

At night, in the upper levels, the bioluminescent moss pulses its slow blue-white rhythm. The keepers have learned to navigate by it. They can walk the rusted catwalks without lamps, reading the glow like a language—brighter patches mean moisture accumulation, dimmer ones mean metal fatigue, the particular pattern of pulsing tells them whether the generator is running smoothly or about to fail.

Maren swears the moss has begun to grow in her watchman's cabin. She's seen it spreading across the ceiling in patterns that almost look like writing. She doesn't mention this to the others. She's not sure what it would mean if the mountain were learning to speak.

---

They are not happy. Happiness requires something to compare against, and most of them have forgotten what ordinary life felt like. But they are not unhappy either. They exist in a state of purposeful occupation, each day defined by the work that needs doing, each night defined by the sounds of water and life moving through the drowned machinery around them.

Sometimes, in the brief moments between tasks, they feel something that might be peace. Standing in a flooded gallery as the sun finds a gap in the clouds and the water turns to green-gold light. Watching the lilies bloom in sudden profusion during a dry spell. Feeling the breathings rise and fall, the mountain inhaling and exhaling through the ruined industry that once tried to claim it.

They belong to this place. They know this with the certainty of people who have nowhere else to belong. The mountain keeps them because it needs keeping. They stay because staying is what they know how to do.

---

When they die—when the mountain finally claims them, or when their bodies simply stop working—they will return to the water. The columns will carry them upward, briefly, before collapsing back into the general chaos. Their bodies will settle in the flooded chambers, becoming structure for new growth, nutrient for the relentless forest.

They don't fear this. They don't welcome it either. It simply is, like the rain, like the rust, like the slow patient breathing of the drowned foundries themselves.

They are the keepers. They keep. That is all.
