In the warm, mineral-rich bath of the Drowned Scaffolding, the inhabitants are not separate from the architecture or the vegetation; they are the connective tissue between them. To call them a people is to impose a surface-dweller's understanding of boundaries upon a place that has none. They are the inhabitants the way a heartbeat is the inhabitant of a chest—they are the mechanism by which this place remains alive.

They are called, in the subsonic hum that serves as their language, the *Vellichor*. 

Physically, the Vellichor are difficult to discern from the environment, which is precisely how they survive. They do not have skin in the way we understand it. Their outer layer is a semi-permeable membrane, constantly weeping a thin, organic glaze—the same sulfurous slime that coats the reed-stalks and stone. They are bipedal when it suits them, standing perhaps a meter and a half tall, but their morphology is fundamentally fluid. They possess a central trunk of dense, cartilaginous rings—homologous to the embryonic rock strata they walk upon—from which extend four long, multi-jointed arms. Their legs are thick, pillar-like, ending in broad, splayed feet that grip the dissolving calcified floor without disturbing the sediment.

Their heads, if one could call them that, are overgrown collars of the same translucent, amber-veined leaves that canopy the plateau. These leaves are not decorative; they are sensory arrays and biological engines. They pulse with the same slow, luminescent rhythm as the vegetation, drawing in the faint blue-green light from above and converting the geothermal warmth into chemical energy. To look at a Vellichor standing still in a thicket of reeds is to see only a slightly more organized knot of the same stuff—a creature wearing the forest as its skin.

They do not breathe. The water is their medium, and they filter it through gill-like slits hidden beneath their collar-leaves, extracting oxygen from the mineral-rich currents. Because they are the same temperature as the water, they leave no thermal trail. Because their density matches the medium, they displace no current. They move through the drowned settlement like thoughts moving through a drowsy mind—present, consequential, but physically imperceptible until they choose to be.

The Vellichor are the builders. They are the ones who stacked the dark volcanic stone without mortar, who carved the descending stairs that serve no purpose for air-breathers. These stairs are their thoroughfares. The settlement, viewed from above, seems a chaotic cluster of dense structures, but from below, understood as a three-dimensional lattice of water flow, it is a city designed for a people who navigate by current and vibration. The deep stairs are the capillaries of the community, pulling the warm, rich water from the geothermal vents up through the heart of the city, distributing heat and sustenance. The low doorways are not to keep water out, but to provide calm eddies within the current—places to rest in the slack water, like trout hiding behind stones in a stream.

Their relationship with the stalks is not one of cultivation, but of *integration*. When a Vellichor dies—and they do die, though slowly, dissolving into the same powdery residue that sheds from the rock—they do not leave bodies. They descend into the lower chambers and anchor themselves to the stone, their cartilage hardening into the foam-like rock, their collar-leaves sprouting into the tall, gas-bladdered reeds. The settlement is not built *upon* the vegetation; the vegetation *is* the ancestors. This is why the central cluster glows brightest: it is the oldest part of the city, where the most generations have put down roots.

The Vellichor do not speak in bursts of sound. Sound, in this dense medium, is a clumsy tool. Instead, they thrum. They possess a plate of resonant bone in their chests, capable of producing and detecting the low-frequency vibrations that permeate the plateau. When they gather in the central cluster at night, as the amber glow dims to a pulse, they are conducting the city's metabolism. By thrumming in unison, they manipulate the pressure of the water, encouraging the geothermal vents to release heat, signaling the bladder-leaves to release gas, guiding the flow of nutrients. It is governance by resonance. They are the conductors of a geological orchestra.

Their lives are measured in the slow accrual of calcium. A Vellichor child is not born; it is *secreted*. In the shadowed crevices between buildings, near the warm vents, a mature Vellichor will exude a concentrated nodule of organic glaze, embedding within it a spore of their own cartilaginous pattern. Over decades—human decades—the nodule absorbs minerals from the water, layer by layer, expanding like a pearl. The child "hatches" not by breaking shell, but by slowly extending its limbs, stretching the membrane that contains it, learning to move the water around it before it even fully separates from the wall. To be a child among the Vellichor is to be a protrusion of the architecture, literally growing out of the family home.

They are not primitive. They are radically refined. The surface-dweller looks for metal and glass as evidence of sophistication, but the Vellichor understand that in a place of perpetual becoming, rigidity is death. A metal tool would rust; a glass window would shatter under the slow, immense pressure of geological shift. Their tools are woven from the fibrous stalks, coated in the same calcified cement, designed to be held not by grip but by suction and mutual growth. They use bone—perhaps the cores of ancient ancestors, perhaps the remains of the rare surface debris thatfilters down—carved into hooks and scrapers, kept close to their bodies so the organic glaze keeps them from dissolving.

The dark, multi-limbed Watchers—the size of large dogs—that move through the canopy are their synthetic companions. The Vellichor made them long ago, weaving together the living stalks with the cement, creating autonomous structures that maintain the city. The Watchers are not pets; they are *golems*, biologically imbued with the thrumming language. They patrol the perimeter, investigating debris, retrieving fallen tools, and keeping the gas-bladders clear of parasites. They watch the world with unseeing eyes, navigating by the pressure of the water and the subsonic hum of their makers.

When the water above grows turbulent and debris rains down, there is no panic. The Vellichor feel the pressure changes hours before the event. They secure their tools by pressing them into the sticky mats of their dwellings. They anchor themselves to the stone, wrapping their arms around the pillars of their ancestors, and wait. The debris falls, the current churns, and then it settles. The Vellichor then send the Watchers to examine the new material. These are the moments of novelty in their long, slow lives—the only times the outside world impinges upon their cocoon of warmth and light.

They do not wonder about the surface. To them, the shimmering boundary high above is a ceiling, a membrane they have no desire to breach. They know that the world above is cold, chaotic, and fast. Up there, things happen in seconds; down here, things happen in centuries. The Vellichor are suited to the pace of the Drowned Scaffolding. They are creatures of patience, existing in a state of readiness for an arrival that is not a person or a thing, but a *condition*—the moment when the plateau, in its slow ascent, finally breaks the surface and the air touches the water for the first time.

They are preparing for the exodus. When the plateau surfaces, the water will drain, the geothermal vents will hit the cold air, and the warm bath will become a freezing fog. The stalks, no longer buoyed by water, will collapse under their own weight. The architecture will dry, crack, and blow away in a wind they have never felt. But the Vellichor will be ready. They are storing the warmth in their cartilage, thickening their membranes, learning to breathe the thin, sulfurous air that wafts down from the vents. They are practicing the art of standing upright against gravity unmitigated by buoyancy.

And so they thrum in the dark, in the amber glow of their ancestors, in the warm embrace of the rising earth. They are the scaffolding's breath. They are the keepers of the slow fire. They live in the paused moment of becoming, and they are waiting, with the patience of stone, for the world to finish making itself.
