# The Keepers of the Threshold

They were there before the plateau decided what it would be. They will be there after. They do not rush.

---

## The Architects (provisional name)

You might call them priests, if you needed a word that fit your mouth. But they don't worship the altars. They don't worship anything. Worship requires believing that something is greater than yourself, and the Architects—if they have a name for themselves, they haven't shared it—operate with the patient certainty of those who are still building the thing they might someday pray to.

There are never more than eleven. Never fewer than nine. When you ask how their number is determined, they look at you as though you've asked why water is wet. The question contains its own error.

They don't look like you expected. Not elevated, not sanctified, not marked by any obvious sign of their role. They're slight, damp-skinned, their bodies adapted to the saturated air in ways that took generations—or were engineered in a single one. Their lungs process the thick atmosphere efficiently. Their eyes have evolved past sensitivity into something else: they see moisture gradients the way you see color, can track the movement of water through stone, can read the patterns the stalk-plants etch and know what they mean.

They wear nothing that you'd recognize as clothing. Instead, their bodies are mapped with trails of biofilm—cultivated bacterial colonies that live in the folds of their skin, processing something, producing something, leaving faint luminescent traces that mark where they've been and what they've touched. Each Architect carries a different pattern. You learn to read them the way you'd read handwriting: *this one tends the altars; this one watches the creatures; this one listens to the stone's deep sounds and hums them back, checking for changes.*

They don't speak, exactly. They produce sounds—a clicksong language that travels through the wet air better than your voice could—but they keep it subsonic most of the time, felt rather than heard. When they want you to understand something, they touch your chest and let the vibration transfer bone-to-bone. It takes getting used to: communication as intimacy, meaning arriving through the body rather than the ear.

---

## What They Do

They tend.

That's the word they use when they finally let you understand it—*tend*, not *serve* or *guide* or *control*. The plateau grows itself. The Architects merely ensure favorable conditions. They adjust moisture levels in specific zones by moving their bodies in specific ways, breathing intentionally over specific patches of stone. They clear debris from the altar depressions with movements so small you almost miss them. They make sounds at frequencies that seem to stabilize the plateau's hum when it wavers, and different sounds when it grows too steady—as though variability itself is what they're cultivating.

They harvest nothing. They build nothing permanent. They intervene in ways so subtle that you spend your first days thinking they're doing nothing at all.

But when you watch longer—when you learn to see the way they see—you notice that the stalk-plants near where an Architect has breathed grow slightly straighter. The creatures that pass within their shadow move with more purpose. The stone, touched by an Architect's hand, hardens or softens according to some design you can't read but can feel the edges of.

They're gardening a mountain. They're shepherding a geology. They're conducting an orchestra whose instruments are still being built.

## The Creatures (who have no name for themselves, or have many)

The amphibious things that populate the plateau are not pets. They're not livestock. They're participants.

The Architects treat them as equals in a process none of you fully understand. When an Architect encounters one of the creatures on the wet stone, there's a pause—a moment of mutual recognition. Sometimes they touch foreheads, the creature's slick skin against the Architect's damp brow. Sometimes they simply align their bodies briefly, exchanging something through proximity.
