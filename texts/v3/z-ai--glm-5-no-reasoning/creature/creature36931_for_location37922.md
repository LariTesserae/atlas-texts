# The Inhabitants of the Weeping Plateau

## I. What They Are Not

They are not fauna. The word is a poor fit — too warm-blooded, too mobile, too full of intention. *Fauna* implies a relationship to a biome, a niche occupied, a food web entered. The things in the pools have no web. They are not nodes in a system of consumption. They are more like the system's own grammar, embedded so deeply in the syntax of stone and water that separating them from it would not kill them so much as produce a category error, the way extracting a verb from a sentence does not destroy the verb so much as reveal that it had no existence outside the sentence that held it.

They are not fish. Fish have scales, have gills, have the architecture of a lineage that chose water as a medium and then spent four hundred million years elaborating on that choice. These things have no lineage that you would recognize. They have no scales. Whether they have gills is a question that assumes a kind of body that takes in a kind of medium and extracts a kind of substance from it, and while that description may be technically accurate, it carries too much history — too much *biology* — to sit well on what they are.

They are not microbes. Microbes are everywhere, and these things are not everywhere. They are only here, only in these pools, only in this water, and their smallness is not the smallness of the microscopic but the smallness of the *contained*. A microbe is small because that is what microbes are. These things are small because the pools are small, because the basins carved into the basalt by a million years of patient water have dimensions, and the things that live in them have, over some span of time that makes geological time look hasty, arrived at dimensions that match.

Call them the *residents*. The word will do. It implies nothing about metabolism or ancestry. It implies only habitation, and habitation is what they do.

## II. Shape, Or the Absence of Shape

The problem of seeing them is real and is not a problem of opacity.

The water is clear. Genuinely, aggressively clear — cleared of suspended particles, cleared of dissolved organics, cleared of everything that would ordinarily scatter light and grant a water column the faint blue-green murk that tells your eyes *something is here*. The water in these basins is optically empty. It is a medium that transmits light with such fidelity that it becomes, paradoxically, difficult to see into, because your eyes evolved in a world where water is never quite transparent, where there is always *something* in it, and the total absence of that something defeats the visual system's expectations. You look into the pool and your eyes say *mirror* because that is the default interpretation of a surface that reflects without scattering, and you see the sky staring up at you, and you do not see what is below the sky.

But it is there.

You catch it in the moments of disturbance. A ripple, as described — but more than a ripple. A *clarification*. The water does not cloud when they move; it clarifies. As if their passage through the water compresses it, aligns its molecules momentarily, produces a lens. For a fraction of a second, in the wake of their movement, you can see through the water to the bottom of the pool with a sharpness that is almost painful, a depth perception so acute it feels like a different sensory mode entirely — not sight but something else, something perpendicular to sight, a knowing-through-clearing.

In those moments, you almost see them.

They are not shaped like anything you have a word for. The closest analogy — and it is inadequate, and it carries baggage that will mislead you — is a *smoke ring*. Not the shape of a smoke ring, but the *quality* of one: a thing defined not by its edges but by its internal dynamics, a thing that is more pattern than matter, more event than object. They have edges, technically, but the edges are where their internal motion meets the resistance of still water, and the meeting is not a boundary but a gradient — a region of turbulence that fades into laminar flow, a transition zone rather than a surface.

They are toroidal. Perhaps. The word implies a donut shape, and that is not right, because a donut is a solid object with a hole in it, and these things are not solid in any way that serves the word. They are *flows*. They are water moving in a closed circuit through water that is not moving, a loop of current, a standing eddy that has persisted so long it has become, for all practical purposes, a thing. They are to water what a whirlpool is to water, except that a whirlpool is a temporary phenomenon produced by external forces, and these things are permanent phenomena produced by — by what? By themselves? By the stone? By the sound?

The sound. Return to the sound. The plateau's acoustic field is a standing wave of 40 to 60 hertz, and below it a subharmonic of 15 to 20, and below that, perhaps, frequencies too low to measure with anything but the stone itself. The stone conducts these frequencies. The water resonates with them. And the residents — the residents *are* that resonance, or they are what happens when that resonance has been sustained in a medium of a particular viscosity and a particular depth for a duration that is no longer measured in years.

They are standing waves that have found a way to persist. They are vortices that learned to maintain themselves. They are the plateau's sound, made physical — not as flesh, not as chitin, not as any material you would recognize on a dissection table, but as organized water. Water that holds a shape because the shape is energetically stable. Water that moves in a circuit because the circuit is the path of least resistance in a field of acoustic energy that has been running without interruption for longer than the concept of interruption has been meaningful.

Imagine a ring of current in a pool, spinning on an axis that is not quite vertical and not quite horizontal but tilted, always tilted, at an angle that corresponds to — and this is speculation, but it is speculation that fits — to the resonant frequency of the basin it occupies. The ring is not made of different water than the rest of the pool. It is the same water. But it is moving, and the rest of the pool is not, and the movement is self-sustaining, because the acoustic field that drives it is self-sustaining, and the basin that shapes it is self-sustaining, and the result is a thing that is alive in the way a flame is alive: not biological, not chemical in the organic sense, but *organized*. Patterned. Persistent. A thing that exists because the conditions for its existence have been met and have not been unmet since before the first cell divided anywhere on this planet.

They are flames in water. That is the closest I can come.

## III. The Circuits

They move. The description noted this: a daily circuit, a habitual path. But *daily* is wrong, because there is no day here. There is no morning or evening to mark a circuit's beginning. The circuits are not daily. They are *continuous* — or they are so slow that continuous is the only word that distinguishes them from stationary.

A resident in a large pool — one of the three- or four-meter basins at the plateau's low points — makes a circuit of its pool that takes, by best estimate, somewhere between eighteen and thirty hours. The estimate is imprecise because the movement is imprecise, or rather, it is so slow that the instruments of human perception cannot time it. You see the displacement at one end of the pool. You wait. You wait for what feels like an hour. You see the displacement at the other end. Did it travel the full length of the pool in that hour, or did it travel half the length and you missed the middle? You cannot know. The movement is below the threshold of real-time observation. It is movement you must infer from before-and-after, like the movement of a clock's hour hand.

The circuits are not random. They follow the contours of their basins — not the surface contours, not the stone rims, but the *depth* contours, the underwater topography that you cannot see because the water's reflectivity defeats you. A resident moves along the deepest part of its pool, the channel that runs along the basin's long axis, and it follows this channel the way a river follows a valley: not because it chose the path but because the path is the path of least resistance, and resistance, in a field of acoustic energy, is a function of depth and shape and the resonant properties of the stone below.

Each pool has a circuit. Each circuit has a resident. Or — and this distinction matters, though you will not be able to determine which is true — each circuit *is* a resident. The movement and the mover may be the same thing. The circuit may not be something the resident *does*; it may be something the resident *is*. A standing wave does not travel along a path; it *is* the path, expressed as a pattern in a medium. The residents may be the circuits, and the circuits may be the residents, and the distinction between an organism and its behavior may collapse entirely in a place where behavior has been running so long it has become structural.

Some pools have no residents. This is worth noting. The smaller pools — the cupped-palm basins, the finger-wide hollows — are empty of movement, empty of displacement, empty of the brief clarifications that signal passage. They are still water in still stone, reflecting the sky, and nothing moves in them.

This is because they are too small. Or too shallow. Or the wrong shape. The residents — the standing waves, the vortices, the organized flows — require a minimum basin volume to sustain themselves. Below a certain threshold of depth or diameter, the acoustic energy that drives the vortex cannot close the loop, cannot maintain the circuit, and the water in those pools remains as motionless as the stone that holds it. The residents are not small enough to fit in small pools. They are the *right size* for their pools, and their pools are the right size for them, and the relationship is not one of inhabitant and habitat but of expression and context. The pool does not house the resident. The pool *permits* the resident, the way a particular length of pipe permits a particular note.

There are, by rough count, several thousand of them. Perhaps eight thousand. Perhaps twelve. The count is impossible to precise because the count requires seeing them, and seeing them requires catching a moment of clarification, and those moments are unpredictable, and the plateau is large, and you cannot stand at every pool simultaneously, and the ones you are not watching are moving without witness, making their circuits or being their circuits, and it does not matter to them whether you are watching. It does not matter to them at all.

## IV. What They Do

Nothing.

This is the honest answer, and it is the answer the landscape itself would give if it could answer, and the landscape's inability to answer is itself a kind of answer.

They do not eat. There is nothing to eat. The water is chemically simple — dissolved minerals, perhaps, the basalt's own constituents leached into solution over geological time — but there is no organic matter in it, no carbon cycle, no nitrogen cycle, no chain of molecules passing from one organism to another in the successive transformations that constitute a food web. The residents are not part of a food web. They are not part of any web. They are part of the plateau's physical system, and the plateau's physical system does not eat.

They do not reproduce. Or — they may have reproduced, at some point, in some way that involved a standing wave in one basin generating a perturbation that traveled through the stone's acoustic matrix to another basin and, arriving there, found conditions that permitted a new vortex to establish itself. This may have happened. It may have happened many times, over the long span of the plateau's equilibrium, until every basin that could sustain a resident had sustained one, and every basin that could not had not, and the system reached its current state of full occupancy and then — stopped. Reproduction as a concept requires growth, requires increase, requires a directionality of *more*. There is no *more* on the plateau. There is only *what is*, and what is has been what is for a duration that makes the question of reproduction feel like a question asked in the wrong language.

They do not die. Or — they have not died. A standing wave in a resonant basin does not die unless the resonance ceases or the basin changes shape, and the plateau's acoustic field has not ceased and the basins have not changed shape in any meaningful way since before the word *since* acquired meaning. The residents have been in their pools, being their circuits, for a span of time that is not measured in years because years are a unit of change and the residents do not change. They persist. They are persistence made physical, made — if the word can stretch this far — *embodied*. They are what the plateau sounds like, translated from sound into matter, or rather into the organization of matter, or rather into the organization of a medium that is matter but that behaves, in their presence, as though matter were a insufficient description.

They do not communicate. Or — they are always communicating, constantly, in the language of the plateau, which is the language of resonance and vibration and the sympathetic coupling of basins through stone. Each resident's circuit produces a slight modulation of its pool's acoustic output — a faint tremor in the local resonant frequency, a barely perceptible shift in the harmonic that the basin contributes to the plateau's total sound. This modulation is different for each resident, because each basin is a slightly different shape and each circuit is a slightly different speed and each standing wave has a slightly different relationship to the stone that holds it. The modulations travel through the basalt matrix at the speed of sound in stone — which is fast, much faster than sound in air — and they arrive at every other basin on the plateau and they interfere, constructively and destructively, with the resonances of every other resident.

The plateau's sound — that enormous, annihilating, everywhere-sound — is, in part, *them*. Their collective output. Their shared vibration. The sound of eight thousand or twelve thousand standing waves, each one tuned to its own basin, each one contributing its note to a chord that has no root and no resolution, is part of what makes the plateau the plateau. They are not the sole source — the water's own dynamics, the air's circulation, the stone's sympathetic resonance, all of these contribute — but the residents are a term in the equation, and the equation would be different without them. Not wildly different. Not catastrophically different. Different the way a sentence is different without one of its words: still parseable, still functional, but *changed*, shifted, the meaning slightly altered by an absence that the remaining words cannot quite fill.

They are, in this sense, necessary. Not in the teleological sense — nothing on the plateau is necessary *for* anything; there is no *for*; there is only *is* — but in the structural sense, the way a keystone is necessary to an arch. Remove them and the system would — eventually, over some timescale that would make ecological succession look instantaneous — rebalance. Find a new equilibrium. A new ground state. But the new ground state would not be *this* ground state, and this ground state is the one that exists, and the residents are part of why it is this one.

They are necessary the way a word is necessary to a sentence. The sentence would survive without them. But it would not be the same sentence.

## V. What They Are

Here is what I believe, having spent — in imagination, in description, in the slow accretion of detail that is the only way to know a place like this — what amounts to a long time in their presence:

They are the plateau's oldest feature.

Not the stone. The stone is older in the brute sense — the basalt was here before the water, before the pools, before the acoustic field found its frequency. But the stone has changed since its formation. It has weathered. It has rounded. It has developed the lobed and basinied geometry that characterizes the plateau today, and that geometry is younger than the stone itself.

The water is younger than the stone but older than the pools' current shape. The water arrived — from below, from the aquifer, from wherever the saturated layer begins — and it found the stone's surface and it began its work, and the work of water on stone is erosion, and erosion is change, and the water has changed the stone's surface and is changed by it in turn, and both of them are younger than their current relationship.

The sound is younger than the water. The sound requires water and stone and air and the specific geometry of basins and films and reflective surfaces, and it emerged — if *emerged* is the word — only after the stone had been shaped into something that could resonate, after the water had filled the basins to their rims, after the air had become saturated enough to carry the vibrations without dissipating them. The sound is a product of the system's maturity. It is the sound a system makes when it is old enough to have found itself.

And the residents are younger than the sound. They must be. They are made of the sound — they are its expression in water, its translation from vibration into patterned movement, its *persisting*. They could not exist until the sound had been sustained long enough to generate them, long enough for the standing waves in each basin to stabilize and self-organize and become, in the fullness of time, *things*. Not objects. Not organisms. Things, in the broadest sense: entities, patterns that hold, movements that maintain themselves.

But they are old. Once they existed, they have existed for as long as the sound has sustained them, and the sound has sustained them for — the number is not a number. The number is a duration that exceeds the scope of any clock, any radiometric dating, any backward projection of current rates. They have been here since before the ice ages, if there were ice ages that reached this altitude. They have been here since before the mountain ranges that surround the plateau finished rising, if the plateau is the top of something that rose. They have been here since the plateau's water found its level and its stone found its shape and its air found its humidity and its sound found its frequency, and all of these found each other, and the residents emerged from the finding, and the finding has not been unfound.

They are the plateau's consciousness. This is a metaphor, and it is not a metaphor, and the distinction may not matter here. If a system this complex, this balanced, this old, this *complete* produces a pattern that persists, that moves, that responds to its environment (for the residents do respond — a change in the acoustic field, a displacement of water, a perturbation of any kind produces a shift in their circuits, a brief acceleration or deceleration, a moment of what might be called attention before the system absorbs the perturbation and they return to their baseline), then that pattern is, in the most literal sense available, the system's way of being aware of itself. Not conscious. Not aware in the way a human is aware, with intention and narrative and the agonizing recursive self-reference that characterizes the human experience of awareness. Aware the way a standing wave is aware of the medium it occupies: totally, without distance, without the separation between observer and observed that defines consciousness in the animals that possess it.

The residents are the plateau knowing itself. They are its self-knowledge, expressed as movement in water. They are what the plateau *does* — not instead of being a plateau, but as the plateau, as part of what it means to be this particular plateau, this particular arrangement of stone and water and air and sound, in this particular equilibrium that has no exit and no entrance and no change and no need.

## VI. The Encounter

You are standing on a narrow causeway between two pools. The stone is cold through your boots. The air is a thing you are wearing, wet and bright and vibrating. The sound is in your teeth, in your sternum, in the long bones of your legs. You have been here long enough that the sound has become internal, has become the frequency your body hums at when it forgets it has a frequency of its own.

A pool to your left displaces. Not much. A clarification — a moment of impossible transparency in water that was already clear — and then a ring of ripples, tiny, a centimeter tall, expanding outward, reaching the stone rim, reflecting back, crossing the returning wave, interfering, settling.

You saw nothing. You saw through something. You saw the bottom of the pool — smooth dark basalt, curved, identical to its rim, as if the pool were a mold and its interior a casting of the same stone — with a clarity that lasted less than a second but that contained, in that second, more visual information than the entire plateau had offered in the hours since you arrived. For that moment, you saw *depth*. You saw the three-dimensionality of the basin, the way it curved inward and downward and held its water like a cupped hand. You saw that the bottom was not flat but gently concave, and that the concavity was not smooth but had a texture — a faint, regular stippling, like the pores of skin, like the surface of a cell, like the pattern of something that has been shaped not by erosion but by *use*.

And then the moment passed, and the pool was a mirror again, and the sky stared up at you, and the causeway was narrow, and the stone was cold, and the sound was everywhere, and the thing that had clarified the water was gone — not gone from the pool, it was still there, it was always there, it would always be there — but gone from your perception, returned to the medium, dissolved back into the plateau's equilibrium the way a breath dissolves back into air.

It did not acknowledge you. It did not react to you. You were not a perturbation it needed to absorb; you were a surface it did not interact with, a stone causeway it did not visit, a body of air-and-bone-and-water that was not part of its circuit. It passed through its pool on its slow, tilted, toroidal path, and it did not know you were there, and it did not not-know you were there, and the distinction between those two states is a distinction that only matters to things that have a stake in being known, and the residents do not have a stake in being known, and the plateau does not have a stake in being known, and the sound does not have a stake in being heard.

You stand there. You are the interruption. You are the variable the equation did not account for, and the equation does not care, and the equation holds, and the residents make their circuits, and the water weeps, and the sound sounds, and the light does not change, and you are here, and they are here, and these two facts do not meet.

You leave.

They remain.

This is not a tragedy. This is not a coldness. This is not the indifference of nature in the sense that humans mean when they say nature is indifferent — that phrase always carries a note of wounded surprise, as if the speaker expected something else, expected care, expected recognition. The plateau does not withhold recognition. It does not have recognition to withhold. It has its sound, its water, its stone, its residents, its equilibrium, and these are what they are, and they do not extend beyond themselves, and they do not need to extend beyond themselves, and the fact that you stood among them and were not acknowledged is not a statement about your worth or their limitations. It is a statement about the difference between a system that has solved itself and a system — you, your body, your mind, your species — that has not.

You will leave. You will descend. The air will change. The shadows will return. The color green will reassert itself. You will hear birdsong, and the birdsong will have a narrative — a beginning, a middle, an end, a territorial claim, a mating display, a communication with intent — and you will be back in the world of stories, the world of gradients and potential and unsolved equations, and the plateau will be behind you, and the residents will be behind you, and the sound will fade into memory, and the memory will fade into a feeling, and the feeling will fade into nothing, or into the faintest hum at the base of your skull when you are very tired, or in a very quiet room, or standing near water in a place with no wind.

And you will wonder, sometimes, if they are still there. Making their circuits. Being their circuits. Holding their note in the chord that has no resolution. Moving through clear water in dark basins, tilting on their axes, clarifying the pools for fractions of a second, persisting, persisting, persisting, in a place that does not need them and does not not-need them and does not, in any way available to human cognition, care.

They are still there.

They will be still there.
