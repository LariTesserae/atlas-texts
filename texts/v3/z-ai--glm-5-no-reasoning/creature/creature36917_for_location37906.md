# The Inhabitants of the Compressor

## The Men

They are not the men they were. Whatever they were before—engineers, laborers, technicians, soldiers of fortune, men running from something—they have been reforged by the altitude and the dimness and the endless mechanical breath that fills their chests from the outside in. The compressor does their breathing for them, and they have forgotten what it was like to breathe on their own.

There are not many of them. Forty, perhaps. Fifty at the outside, depending on whether you count the ones who've stopped leaving their quarters, the ones who've gone half-feral in the deep sub-levels where the maintenance crew doesn't bother going anymore. The official roster is longer than the actual population. Names on a list that no one updates. Men who died or left on earlier lifts but whose bunks are still assigned, whose water rations still flow into the system somewhere, unclaimed, recycled back into the general supply. Or into Preece's hands. There are theories about this too.

They are thinner than they should be. Not starving—the food supplies hold, for now, though they've become monotonous in a way that goes beyond mere complaint—but thin in the way of bodies that have been working at altitude, in dry air, on insufficient water, for years that blur together. Their faces have drawn tight over their cheekbones. Their eyes have a particular quality: bright, slightly too wide, the look of men who exist in a permanent state of low-grade vigilance. They flinch at sudden sounds, though there is nothing more sudden than the compressor's rhythm, which never stops.

Some of them have grown beards. Some have not. There is no standard. The men of the day shift tend to be cleaner-shaven—a holdover from whatever professional culture they brought with them. The night shift men look like something recovered from a shipwreck. Harrow, who technically belongs to neither shift and to both, has her hair cropped short and practical, and she carries a wrench on her belt that she uses for everything from tightening pipe fittings to discouraging conversation.

They smell like the station. All of them. Machine oil and sweat and the mineral dust that works its way into everything, into the pores, into the creases of their elbows, into the seams of their clothing. They've stopped noticing. A man who has been in the compressor for two years cannot remember what he smelled like before. A man who has been here for five has begun to suspect that he never smelled like anything else.

The older engineers—the ones who remember the construction—carry themselves differently. They move through the station with a proprietary air, touching the walls, listening to the pipes, resting their hands on the riveted steel plates as if checking for fever. They are the ones who built this place, and they feel its illness as a personal affront. They can tell you, if you ask, which sections were built first, which walls were riveted during the second week, which tunnels were supposed to go somewhere and don't anymore. They are the ones who say *within tolerance* most often, with the greatest conviction, and the most private doubt.

There is a man named Stilwell who was among the first crew. He is old now—or older than he was, which amounts to the same thing. He works in the comm center, the room full of dead equipment, and he maintains it as if it might spring back to life. He cleans the dust from the screens. He checks the connections. He has not received a transmission in two years, but he sits at the console during his shift and watches the static, and when people ask him what he's doing, he says *listening.* No one asks what he's listening for.

There is a man named Dehlin who was a structural engineer, brilliant once, now half-mad from the isolation and the noise. He spends his off-hours in the observation room at the top of the station, the cold chamber with the sealed-over windows, and he runs his fingers along the steel plates where the sky used to be visible. He says the mountain is moving. Not settling—moving. He says the peak is slowly rotating, a fraction of a degree per cycle, and that the station is being twisted inside its frame like a body in a slowly turning vice. The engineers say this is impossible. Dehlin says the engineers said the cracks were impossible too.

There are younger men, newer arrivals, who still remember the world below. They are the most restless. They pace the corridors. They linger at the lift shaft, looking down, as if they might see something at the bottom other than darkness and the supply platform that hasn't risen in months. They speak about home with a specificity that the older men have lost—the color of a sky, the sound of rain, the feeling of standing in wind that wasn't recycled. The older men listen to these stories with expressions that are difficult to read in the yellow light. Sometimes the expressions are wistful. Sometimes they are something closer to pity, as if the young men are describing a world that no longer exists, or that never existed, or that exists only in the space between a memory and the telling of it.

## The Women

There are a few. Not many. Harrow is the most visible because of the dogs, but there are others: a mechanic named Su who works the day shift in the maintenance tunnels and can find a leak in a pipe system by sound alone; a woman called Brill, no first name that anyone knows, who runs the mess hall and the sorting floor with an efficiency that borders on tyranny and who has a system for everything, color-coded tags on the supply crates, rotation schedules for the canned goods, a ledger of inventory that she updates in a cramped hand on sheets of paper that she's been using for years, writing in the margins when the lines fill up; a young woman named Eket who came on one of the last lifts as a laborer and who sleeps in the crew quarters nearest the compressor room because she says the rhythm helps her sleep, though the noise level there is punishing, and the other women in that section have moved out, one by one, leaving Eket alone with the pistons and the half-deaf workers who nod at her in the corridor with the careful courtesy of men who have been told to be careful and are trying.

Harrow is something else. She is the kind of person who emerges in sealed environments—a woman who has found, or been found by, a role that no one designed for her. She does not officially work with the dogs. She is, on paper, a compressor technician, night shift, one of four people responsible for keeping the great engine running through the small hours. But her relationship with the dogs has become her defining quality, and everyone knows it, and no one knows what to do with it.

She is tall. Thinner than the men, which is saying something, but strong in the way of people who have learned to use leverage because they don't have mass to spare. Her hands are large and scarred and perpetually grease-stained, and she moves through the station with a sureness that the other residents find either reassuring or unsettling, depending on their temperament. She doesn't hurry. She doesn't dawdle. She moves at a pace that seems to be calibrated to the compressor's rhythm, as if she's synchronized with the station's heartbeat.

The dogs follow her in a loose pack, five or six at a time, different dogs on different days, though there are two—a big male with a torn ear and a smaller female with a grey muzzle—who are always with her, who walk at her heels like lieutenants. They don't beg from her. They don't fawn. They walk beside her with the same purposeful stride, and when she stops to check a gauge or tighten a fitting, they stop too, and wait, and watch the corridor with an alertness that looks, in the dim light, like vigilance.

Whether she has actually trained them is a matter of debate. She doesn't discuss it. When asked directly, she gives answers that are either evasive or simply incomprehensible—"They do what they do; I just go where they're going"—and changes the subject. The men who believe her point to the time a dog named Checker alerted to a hairline fracture in a steam line three hours before it burst. The men who don't believe her point out that dogs bark at lots of things, and confirmation bias is a hell of a drug, and Harrow is either the most competent person in the station or the most cunning, and those look the same from the outside.

What is certain: the dogs recognize her. What is certain: she walks through the darkest corridors without a lamp, and the dogs walk with her, and she has never been bitten.

## Preece

Preece deserves separate mention because he has made himself into something more than a resident, something more than a worker. He has become an institution.

He was not always fat. There are photographs—someone has photographs, tucked into a footlocker in the crew quarters—and in the photographs, Preece is a lean man with a narrow face and a beard that he kept neat. But the Preece of the station is round, padded, soft in a place where nothing is soft, and the softness is itself a kind of power. In a station where everyone is slowly drying out, slowly tightening, slowly becoming less of what they were, Preece is becoming more. He is accumulating. And what he is accumulating—fat, water, influence, debt—are all the same thing, expressed in different currencies.

He runs his gambling ring from the corner of the mess hall, a small table that he has claimed through sheer persistence, sitting there during every meal shift with his back to the wall and his canteen in front of him like a scepter. He plays cards, mostly—games of skill that depend on reading the other players, which is difficult in the dim light, which is why Preece wins, because Preece has learned to read men by their breathing and their hands rather than their faces. He also runs a lending operation. Water loans, repaid with interest. The interest rates are not exorbitant, which is part of his strategy—they are just enough to keep people coming back, just enough to keep them in his orbit, just enough to ensure that when Preece needs something done—a corridor cleaned, a crate moved, a message delivered—there is always someone who owes him.

He does not go to the observation room. He does not go to the compressor room. He does not go to the sub-levels. Preece exists in a narrow band of the station: the mess hall, his quarters, the main corridor between them. Some people say this is because he has a secret source of water somewhere and he's guarding it. Some people say it's because he's afraid. Some people say he's not really fat, that he's wearing padding, that the whole thing is a performance designed to make people think he has resources he doesn't have, which is its own kind of power—the power of perceived abundance.

His eyes are what give him away, if you watch closely. They are not the eyes of a comfortable man. They are the eyes of a man who has calculated everything—every gesture, every meal, every transaction—and who is waiting, always waiting, for the calculation to come out wrong. He is playing a longer game than anyone else in the station, and he knows that the game ends when the station does, and the station is failing, and so Preece is failing too, in his way, no matter how much he accumulates.

## The Dogs

They are not what they were, either.

The first dogs—the ones the construction crew brought, if that story is true—were working animals, probably. Trained, or half-trained. Guard dogs, maybe, or simply companions, the kind of animals that men bring to difficult places because the presence of something loyal makes the difficulty bearable. They would have been a specific type: hardy, medium-sized, thick-coated, the kind of dogs that thrive in marginal environments, that can eat anything and sleep anywhere and find warmth in the smallest spaces.

What they have become is something else.

The dogs of the compressor are a population. Not a pack—there are too many of them for a single pack structure—but a colony, a loose and shifting society of animals that has adapted to the sealed environment with a speed that suggests either remarkable intelligence or remarkable luck, and in the dim yellow light, it is difficult to tell the difference.

They are mongrels now, thoroughly mongrelized, whatever breeds were originally present having been blended through generations into a type rather than a breed: medium-sized, thick-coated, with broad heads and intelligent eyes and bodies that seem designed for the station's corridors. They are the right width for the passages. They can curl into the spaces between pipes. They can navigate the maintenance tunnels that connect the sub-levels. It is as if the station has selected for them, has bred them through its architecture, culling the ones that didn't fit, physically, into the spaces available.

They have learned the rhythms of the station. They know when the shifts change—there is a migration of dogs twice daily, from the areas that are being worked to the areas that are being vacated, a flowing of animals that follows the movement of bodies and the availability of warmth. They know which corridors have steam pipes and which don't. They know which workers drop food and which don't. They know which doors stay open and which stay closed.

The question of their number is a source of low-grade anxiety. No one has counted them, because counting them would require catching them, and catching them would require touching them, and touching them would require acknowledging that they exist in a quantity that is not sustainable. There are dozens. There may be a hundred. In a station the size of the Compressor, a hundred dogs is a crisis that walks on four legs. They eat scraps and they eat vermin and they eat, some people suspect, things that are not food—the rubber gaskets, the oilcloth wrapping, the insulation on the cable bundles. There is evidence. There are teeth marks on things that should not be bitten.

And yet. And yet they serve a function that no one planned for them. They are the station's nervous system, its early warning network. A dog barking at a wall is a dog that has heard water moving where water should not be. A dog scratching at a floor plate is a dog that has smelled gas or smoke or the mineral tang of a new crack opening in the mountain's stone. They detect what the sensors cannot, because the sensors are dead or failing, and the dogs are alive and multiplying and their senses are sharpened by the very environment that is slowly killing the machines.

They follow Harrow. They follow Brill, though Brill doesn't want them to and kicks at them when they get underfoot in the mess hall. They follow whoever carries food and whoever moves through the corridors at a steady pace, because a steady pace means purpose, and purpose means a destination, and a destination means a place where something might be happening. They are opportunist animals, and the station is a place of opportunities, if you are a dog.

At night—or what passes for night, the dim hours when the compressor's rhythm slows slightly, or seems to—they gather in the warmest corridors, piled on top of each other, a breathing mass of fur and heat that the workers step over and around and sometimes, in moments of weakness or loneliness, sit down beside. The dogs allow this. They are not sentimental animals, but they understand warmth, and they understand that the two-legged things that live in the station are warm too, and that there is enough warmth to share if everyone is close enough together.

The men who sit with the dogs at night do not talk about it during the day.

## The Quiet Ones

There are people in the station who are not part of any faction, who do not gamble with Preece or follow Harrow or work a regular shift or maintain dead equipment in the comm center. They exist in the margins—in the deep sub-levels, in the maintenance tunnels that lead nowhere, in the chambers that were built for purposes that have been forgotten.

They are the ones who have been here the longest, or the ones who have been broken by the place, or both. They are not dangerous, or not particularly, but they are strange in the way that people become strange when they spend too long in enclosed spaces with the sound of machinery in their ears and the taste of metal on their tongues. They speak to themselves, or to the dogs, or to the walls. They have claimed small territories—a corner of a sub-level corridor, a disused storage bay, the space beneath a stairwell—and they defend these territories not with violence but with the simple persistence of being there, always there, like features of the station itself.

One of them is a man named Gough who was, according to the older engineers, one of the best structural welders on the construction crew. Something happened to him—no one is sure what, exactly; the stories range from a head injury during a tunnel collapse to a simple psychological break, the kind that comes from prolonged isolation and pressure changes. Now he lives in sub-level four, in a chamber that was supposed to be a secondary airlock but was never completed. He has built things in there. Sculptures, or models, or something—constructed from scrap metal and cable fragments and the rubber gaskets that the dogs haven't eaten. The structures are dense and intricate and no one knows what they represent. Some of the men who have visited Gough say they are maps of the station. Some say they are maps of somewhere else. Gough doesn't explain. He just builds, adding to the structures cycle by cycle, working in the near-darkness by touch alone, his fingers reading the metal the way a blind man reads text.

Another is a woman named Foss who might be sixty or might be forty, and who lives in the maintenance tunnels with three dogs that no one else has ever been able to touch. She speaks to the dogs in a language that is not any language anyone recognizes—sounds, clicks, a kind of vocalization that is closer to what the dogs themselves make than to human speech. The maintenance crew has learned to announce themselves when they enter her section of the tunnels. They knock on the pipe work and wait. Sometimes she answers. Sometimes she doesn't. When she doesn't, they go around, or they come back later. No one has confronted her. No one has tried to remove her. There is an unspoken agreement that the maintenance tunnels belong to Foss in the same way that the mess hall belongs to Brill and the comm center belongs to Stilwell and the compressor room belongs, in some deep and inexpressible way, to Harrow.

## The Engineers

They are a separate case. They exist in the station the way the station exists in the mountain: as an imposed structure, as something that was built and that persists through maintenance and will, when the maintenance fails, be reclaimed by what surrounds it.

There are six of them who still actively work. They have a workshop on sub-level two, a room that is denser even than the rest of the station, filled with tools and spare parts and the accumulated debris of twelve or fifteen years of construction and repair. They are the ones who keep the compressor running. They are the ones who patch the cracks and reset the seals and adjust the pressure differentials and say *within tolerance* with the particular emphasis of men who are measuring something against a standard that may no longer apply.

Their leader, if they have one, is a man named Trask. He is compact, precise, and absolutely certain of things that he should not be certain of. He has a diagram of the station on the wall of the workshop—a schematic, hand-drawn, updated with annotations and corrections in different colors of ink representing different years. The diagram is covered in annotations. Some of them contradict each other. Some of them say things like *FAILED* and *BYPASSED* and *NO LONGER ACCURATE.* The diagram is a record of the station's slow deviation from its design, a history of compromise and adaptation written in ink on paper, and Trask studies it the way a general studies a map of a battle he is losing.

The engineers are the ones who know the station is failing. They have the data—or they had it, before the instruments started breaking. They can feel it in the way the pistons move, the slight irregularity in the rhythm that no one else notices but that they hear the way a mother hears a child's breathing change in the night. They know the seals are leaking. They know the mountain is shifting. They know the water situation is worse than the rationing suggests, because they're the ones who maintain the moisture traps, and the traps are producing less than they did six months ago.

They do not say these things. They say *within tolerance.* They say *the situation is manageable.* They say *the next supply lift will bring replacement parts.* And in the workshop, in the small hours when even Trask is sleeping, someone—no one is sure who, the handwriting doesn't match any of the six—has written, in small letters at the bottom of the diagram, in ink that is newer than the rest: *for how long?*

## What Lives Here

What lives here is the station itself. The men and the women and the dogs and the quiet ones and the engineers and Preece and Harrow and Gough and Foss and Stilwell and Brill and Dehlin and Eket and Su and all the others whose names don't appear here because names are not the point—they are not inhabitants so much as they are components. Organs in the steel lung. Blood in the vasculature. They move through the corridors the way air moves through the ductwork, and the station processes them, and they are changed by it, and they change it in turn.

What lives here is a closed system that is slowly, slowly losing pressure. A community that is being forgotten by the world that made it. A population—human, canine, mechanical—that has adapted to its environment so thoroughly that it can no longer imagine existing anywhere else, and that will, when the compressor finally fails and the seals break and the outside silence rushes in to fill the space where breath used to be, discover that it has become something that cannot survive in open air.

But that day is not today. Today the pistons rise and fall. Today the air cycles through. Today Preece sits in his corner and Harrow walks her corridors and the dogs sleep in their warm places and the engineers study their diagram and Gough builds his sculptures by touch in the dark and the compressor breathes, and breathes, and breathes.

What lives here lives here. That is the only answer the station knows how to give.
