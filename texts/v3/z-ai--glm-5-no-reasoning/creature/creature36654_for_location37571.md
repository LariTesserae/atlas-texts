# What Lives Here

They are small-boned. Not stunted — not the way starvation makes people small — but reduced, as if over many generations the body decided that mass was a luxury it could not justify in the heat. Compact frames, narrow shoulders, short limbs carried close to the torso. They move like something that has learned to take up less space. Not performatively. The way water learns the shape of what holds it.

Their skin has gone the color of the tuff around them. Not from sun — they see the sun rarely, perhaps only during surface foraging trips, which some of them make and some of them never do in their entire lives. The color comes from the chamber itself, from decades of proximity to heated stone and the mineral exhalations of the roots. A warm brown-grey, ashen in the creases of the elbows and neck, darker across the backs of the hands and the tops of the feet. You would lose them against the walls if they held still, and they often hold still.

They live at a pace the heat demands. Forty-seven degrees, constant, without relief or variation, means that every action costs more than it would elsewhere. They have metabolized this fact so deeply that it no longer registers as a constraint. It is simply the temperature at which life happens. They work in intervals — ten minutes of grinding, five of rest. A trip to retrieve stored seed pods from an alcove three chambers down is an undertaking, planned and paced, not casual. They do not rush because rushing is a thing that exists in cooler places, and they have never been to cooler places, and the ancestors who came from cooler places are so many generations gone that the knowledge has thinned to nothing.

They sleep in fragments. Twenty minutes here, forty there, tucked into carved niches along the chamber walls, their bodies curved against the smooth tuff, woven fiber mats beneath them. They do not have a concept of nighttime. The chamber has no night. The drone continues. The roots glow their faint red. The heat holds. They wake and sleep according to a rhythm that is partly the nine-minute respiratory cycle of the organism and partly something internal, communal, shared — when one rises, others may be sleeping, and there is no regularity to it, no schedule, only the aggregate behavior of forty bodies cycling through rest and wakefulness in overlapping waves so that the chamber is never fully active and never fully still.

Their hands are remarkable. The fingers are long relative to the palm — an adaptation, perhaps, or simply a feature that the founder population carried and the chamber preserved. The fingertips are broad, flattened, calloused to a smoothness like polished wood. They read the roots with these hands. A resident can place a palm against a root conduit and tell you, without looking, without instruments, what the pressure is doing, whether the organism is drawing vapor up or pushing it back down, whether a storm has reached the surface fifty meters above. The roots vibrate differently depending on the gas flow, and the residents have been feeling these vibrations since before they could walk. Their hands are their primary sensory organ. More than their eyes, which are adapted to darkness so complete that surface foragers must bind their eyes with fiber wraps before emerging into daylight.

They do not speak.

This requires clarification. They are physically capable of speech. Their vocal cords are intact, their hearing functional. But the chamber's drone — sustained, layered, 90-plus decibels — makes spoken language impractical at anything below a shout, and shouting is energetically expensive in the heat, and they have not done it for so long that the idea of forming words for interpersonal communication has become foreign. They are not mute. Occasionally a resident will vocalize — a sharp bark of alarm, a low moan of discomfort or grief, the sounds that bypass language because language is too slow for the things that truly need to be said. But for everything else, for the daily architecture of coexistence — who will grind the tubers, who will check the surface, who will tend the fire, who will weave, who will sleep — they use their hands and the roots.

The percussed language is the chamber's own invention, or rather the chamber's necessity made into art. They strike the roots. Different roots, different tones. A thick root near the ceiling, struck with the heel of the palm, produces a low boom that carries through the chamber's acoustics. A thinner root, tapped with a fingertip, yields a higher note, almost a click. They strike the floor too, and the walls, and the ceramic vessels. The result is not a signaling system — not a crude code of knocks — but a genuine language, with the complexity and flexibility of any spoken tongue, built on rhythm, pitch, duration, and sequence. It is faster than speech in some ways, slower in others. It carries emotion in the force of the strike, in the pause between strikes, in the choice of which surface to hit. An argument conducted in percussion is a fearsome thing — rapid, layered, the whole chamber ringing with it. A lullaby is a single root, tapped softly, irregularly, fading.

They have stories told this way. Long narrative sequences, passed from generation to generation, struck out on roots and stone in patterns that encode events and characters and meanings the way spoken syllables do. A child learns these stories the way a child anywhere learns language — by immersion, by repetition, by the gradual accumulation of pattern until meaning crystallizes. They have a story about how the chamber was found. They have a story about the organism — what it is, why it breathes, what it wants, though the word "wants" may not be quite right for a clonal root colony and they may not use it. They have stories about the surface that are partially accurate and partially mythologized, because the surface is a place some of them visit and none of them live, and it has acquired the quality of a frontier — known but not inhabited, real but strange.

They do not have a written language. They do not need one. The roots remember.

---

The surface foragers are a distinct subgroup, though the distinction is fluid. Perhaps eight or ten of the forty make regular trips above, through a narrow root-walled passage that ascends in switchbacks for forty meters before emerging through a concealed opening in the basalt. They go because the chamber needs what the surface provides — dried seed pods for food, fiber stalks for cordage and matting, the tough surface tubers that are their dietary staple. They go in pairs or threes, never alone. They wrap their eyes. They move fast, because the surface is 65 degrees in direct light and their bodies are not built for it. They are back within an hour, two at most, descending into the crack in the rock with their gathered material, returning to the heat and the dark and the sound that is, for them, the temperature of home.

The foragers have a quality the others lack, or perhaps the others have a quality the foragers have lost. The foragers are restless. Not in a way that causes conflict — the chamber's social dynamics are notably low-friction, partly because the percussed language does not lend itself to the kind of casual grievance that spoken language generates, and partly because forty people in an enclosed space have a powerful structural incentive toward cooperation. But the foragers move differently when they are in the chamber. They pace. They check the passage entrance. They look upward, toward the root ceiling, in a way that the others do not. They have seen the sky. It has marked them.

The non-foragers — the majority, the ones who weave and grind and tend the fire and never leave — have a relationship with the sky that is theoretical. They know it exists. They know it is above the roots. They know the roots go up into it and come back down. But they have no direct experience of it, and this is not a source of distress or wonder for them. It is simply a fact about the structure of their world, the way the fact that the ocean exists is a fact for people who have never seen the ocean. It is there. It is not here. Here is the chamber.

---

They do not have a religion in any recognizable sense, but they have something.

The organism is not worshipped. They are too practical for worship, and the relationship is too reciprocal — they take from the roots, the roots take from the rock, the rock takes from the deep heat, and they all take from each other in a cycle that does not require reverence to function. But they have a practice that occupies a similar structural position. Every nine minutes, when the drone drops in pitch and the organism begins its ninety-second exhalation — the period when gas pressure in the root system shifts direction, when the vibration in the walls changes character — they stop. Whatever they are doing. Grinding, weaving, sleeping, foraging in the corridors. They stop and they place their hands against the roots.

Not all of them, every time. It is not mandatory, not ritualized in that way. But most of them, most times. They press their palms to the root casings and they feel the shift, the great slow breath moving through the conduits above them, and they stay still for the duration of the cycle. Ninety seconds of shared attention, shared silence, shared contact with the thing that holds up their ceiling and holds up the world above and has been doing both for longer than their memory reaches. Then the pitch rises, the gas flow reverses, and they return to what they were doing.

They do not name this practice. It does not have a name. It is what you do when the roots breathe.

---

Their faces are difficult to read for someone from outside. Not because they are expressionless — they are expressive, in fact, but the expressions are calibrated to a social environment where visual cues matter more than auditory ones, where the darkness and the red glow and the noise make every facial movement a primary communication channel. They smile, but the smiles involve the whole face in a way that is almost exaggerated, because subtlety is lost in the dimness. They frown the same way — total, unmistakable. Their eyes, adapted to far-red light, catch the bioluminescence in a way that makes the faint glow seem brighter than it is, and there is something about looking into the eyes of someone who has never seen daylight — whose pupils have never contracted against anything brighter than a dying fat lamp — that is unsettling in a way that has nothing to do with them and everything to do with you.

They do not consider themselves strange. They do not consider the chamber strange. They do not consider the organism strange, or the darkness strange, or the heat strange, or the absence of animals strange, or the fact that they communicate by striking the living walls of their home strange. These are the conditions of existence. The conditions of existence are not strange. They are what is.

If you asked them — and you cannot ask them, because you cannot speak their language and they cannot hear yours over the drone — but if you could ask them what they are, what they would say, through taps and strikes and the careful placement of hands on roots, is something like this:

We are the ones who live in the roots. The roots breathe. We listen. The surface grows. We gather. The rock is warm. We are warm. This is where we are.

They are not wrong. They are not tragic. They are not lost or forgotten or primitive or noble. They are forty people living inside the respiratory system of an organism they do not fully understand, in a place they did not choose, in a manner they have refined over centuries into something that works. The chamber holds. The roots hold. They hold.

The drone continues. It has been continuing for longer than anyone can count.
