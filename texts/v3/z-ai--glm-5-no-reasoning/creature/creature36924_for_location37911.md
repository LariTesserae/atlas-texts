# What Lives Here

There is no singular answer to that question. Or rather — the answer changes depending on what you mean by *lives*.

---

**The insects live here the way weather lives in a valley.**

Not as individuals. There is no individual. The small flying things — diaphanous, pale, none larger than a grain of rice, none with a name you would recognize — exist in a state of pure *number*. A single one, isolated, held in cupped hands, would be nearly nothing: a twitch of wing, a wet body, a lifespan measured in hours. But they do not exist singly. They exist as a fact. A condition of the air. They are born in the moss, in the uncountable tiny pools of moisture caught in the stems and the leaf-litter, and they live their entire lives without leaving the ten cubic meters around where they were born, and they die on the same surfaces, and their bodies become the dark soil that feeds the moss that catches the water that makes the next generation. They are a cycle that happens to have a physical form.

They have no eyes. Or rather, they have structures that were once eyes — small, dark, vestigial points on either side of the head — but these have flattened and clouded over, unnecessary for so many generations that the organs have simply been allowed to drift. What they have instead is an extraordinary sensitivity to air pressure, to vibration, to the particular humidity gradients that mark the boundary between one terrace level and the next. They navigate by feel. They navigate by the feel of the air itself. The mist, when it comes, is a wall to them — they move around it, not through it, and the subtle turbulence of their avoidance creates eddies in their own density, so that the mist seems to carve a hollow in the swarm as it passes. For a few minutes, you can see through them in the mist's wake. Then they close again.

They do not sleep. The concept is meaningless to them. The swarm has a constant density, a constant volume, a constant roar, because the swarm is not made of discrete lives but is itself a single organism, and its metabolism never rests. The individuals are cells. They are born and they die and the organism doesn't notice. It doesn't need to notice. It has been this thing — this vibration, this saturation, this medium — for as long as there has been a here to be a thing in.

**The beetles live here the way stones live in a field.**

*Pseudotanarus profundus* — or something near enough that the distinction is academic. Large. The size of a closed fist, though the body is low-slung and broad, the legs splayed wide and short, the profile flattened as if pressed down by the weight of all that stone above. The carapace is the same dark as the basalt it moves across, and it is wet, always wet, and the moisture on its surface catches the blue-green bioluminescence so that the beetle, when it moves, appears to glow faintly, a dark shape outlined in cold light, moving through darker moss.

They are grazers. They move across the fungal patches in slow, methodical sweeps, mandibles working in a rhythm that is audible only if you press your ear to the stone — a wet, grinding sound, the texture of the substrate being sheared away. A beetle can clear a patch of fungi the size of a dinner plate in several hours. It does not hurry. It does not need to hurry. The fungi grow back. They always grow back. The beetle has food in front of it and food behind it and food beside it and the concept of scarcity is foreign to its biology, its nervous system, its slow and patient somatic intelligence.

They navigate by smell. The air is so saturated with insect — with the pheromones and the waste and the body-heat of ten million small flying things — that chemical navigation seems impossible, and yet they move with precision, following gradients that are invisible to any sense but their own. When a fungal patch is exhausted, the beetle turns, not randomly, but along a vector that has, statistically, always led it to another patch. They have done this for so long that their movement patterns have worn visible paths in the moss — dark channels where the vegetation has been crushed by the passage of a million abdomens, a million sets of clicking legs, each following the same track because the track works and the track has always worked.

The carapace is not just armor. It is substrate. Several species of moss — a dwarf variant, tight-grained, pale — grow nowhere else but on the backs of the larger beetles. The beetles carry their own ecosystems on their shells, miniature gardens of lichen and liverwort that are seeded, it is thought, by the spores that saturate the air and that germinate in the hairline scratches on the carapace, the micro-abrasions from years of pushing through tight vegetation. The beetles do not notice. They do not need to notice. The moss is on them the way barnacles are on a whale — a passenger, a rider, neither invited nor unwelcome, simply present, simply part of the arrangement.

**The salamanders live here the way statues live in a church.**

They do not move. Or rather — they move, but the movement is so infrequent, so minimal, so precisely rationed that a human observer would need hours of stillness to confirm that the dark, wet shape on the retaining wall was, in fact, alive. *Australoblepus troglodytes* — or something that has descended so far from any surface-dwelling ancestor that the connection is speculative. Eyeless, yes — flat, smooth patches where the eyes would be, the skin grown over them, unpigmented, faintly translucent, so that in the strongest bioluminescent light you can see the dark pulse of blood beneath. The skin is dark, almost black, but it has a quality of wetness that makes it shine — they are always wet, always gleaming, the moisture on their bodies as constant and as ambient as the moisture on the stone.

They are the reason the insects do not grow more numerous than they are. The salamanders eat them. They sit on the retaining walls, throats pulsing, and they wait, and the insects — the insects are everywhere, the insects are unavoidable — land on them, brush against them, accumulate on the dark wet skin like debris, and the salamander turns its head, slowly, with a patience that is geological, and extends a tongue, and the tongue is fast, and the tongue is the fastest thing in the undercanopy, the one gesture in this entire ecosystem that occurs at a speed you would recognize as *quick*. The rest is glacial. The tongue is a flash. Then the throat pulses again. Then the waiting resumes.

They have been sitting on the same wall for a very long time. The stone beneath them — beneath the specific individuals, the specific salamanders, the specific dark shapes in their specific positions — has been worn smooth, polished to a sheen, by the pressure of their bodies. They have favorite positions. They return to them. They have been returning to them for years, decades, perhaps longer. The salamander is not territorial — it does not defend a position, it does not fight — but it is *persistent*. It has a place and it goes to the place and it sits in the place and it eats what comes to the place and it has been doing this for so long that the stone has shaped itself around the animal, a faint impression, a shallow cup in the basalt, the exact depth and contour of a salamander's belly.

They breed, but the breeding is slow, infrequent, and the young are small and dark and they find their own walls and they sit on them and they grow and the stone shapes itself to them and the cycle continues at a pace that makes the beetles look impetuous.

**The things in the upper dark live here the way echoes live in a canyon.**

You do not see them. That is the first thing. That is the essential thing. You hear them — the leathery flap of wing-membrane against thick, still air, a sound that arrives and departs in under a second, swallowed immediately by the insect-roar, leaving behind only the memory of a shape moving overhead, the displacement of air, a brief coolness on the updraft. And the chittering — a sound that is lower in frequency than the insects, lower than the beetles' clicking, a sound that exists in a band of the spectrum that is nearly tactile, felt in the jaw and the teeth rather than heard in the ear.

They are not bats. They are not birds. They are not anything that has a name, because the things that have names are things that have been seen, and these have not been seen — not clearly, not in any way that produces a description more specific than *dark* and *fast* and *above*. They are warm-blooded, or nearly so. The air they displace is warmer than the ambient temperature of the terrace. They have mass. You can hear it in the way the air moves around them — not the flutter of something light, but the displaced rush of something with weight, with a body that occupies space, that pushes the medium aside as it moves.

They live in the upper terraces, where the ceiling rises and the light fades to nothing and the air is cooler and the moss gives way to bare stone. They come down to feed. You hear the feeding — brief sorties, rapid incursions into the lower levels, the chittering spiking in frequency and intensity, a burst of activity, and then silence, or what passes for silence here, which is the insect-roar reasserting itself, the equilibrium restoring, the brief disturbance absorbed.

They are the apex of this system. They take from the lower levels — insects, amphibians, anything small enough to be taken on the wing — and they carry it up, to the dark, to the ceiling, to the places where the bioluminescence does not reach and the stone is still warm and dry and old. They do not come down often. They do not need to come down often. Their metabolism is slow for a warm-blooded thing — slower than a bat, slower than a bird — and they can go for long stretches between meals, hanging in the dark, whatever they hang from, whatever they cling to, invisible and patient and above.

If you are very still and very lucky and the mist comes through and the bioluminescence brightens to its peak, you might — might — see a shape. A silhouette. For a fraction of a second. Dark against dark, a membrane, a curve of body, something that is not a wing and not an arm and not a fin but something that serves the function of all three. Then it is gone, and the light dims, and the shape was never there.

---

**And the moss. The moss lives here the way a government lives in a country.**

It is the substrate, the surface, the architecture, the law. Every other thing in the undercanopy lives *on* the moss or *because of* the moss or *despite* the moss. The moss is the first thing and the last thing and the thing that will be here when everything else — the insects, the beetles, the salamanders, the things in the dark — has changed or shifted or died away. The moss will not die away. The moss will find the gap. The moss will fill the gap. The moss is the patient, the inevitable, the slow tide that covers everything and makes it its own.

There are seventeen species of bryophyte in the undercanopy, and the taxonomist who counted them was working from specimens and from dead-air photographs and from a kind of informed desperation, because the undercanopy does not invite counting. You do not count the moss. You accept it. You understand that the surface beneath your hand, the green-brown-black mat of vegetation, is not one thing but seventeen things, layered and interleaved and growing in and through each other, a fabric so complex that the concept of species is a human convenience applied to a system that does not recognize human conveniences.

The moss metabolizes the stone. It is — slowly, over centuries, over millennia — eating the basalt. Its rootlike structures, the rhizoids, are not strong, but they are persistent, and they work their way into the grain of the rock, into the hairline fractures, and they expand when they absorb water, which is always, and the fractures widen, and the stone weathers, and the moss absorbs the minerals, and the moss grows, and the next generation of rhizoids finds the widened fractures and enters them and expands again. This is how the terrace is becoming the moss. This is how the built becomes the grown. It is a process that has no conclusion. It will continue until the stone is gone and the moss is floating on nothing, and then the moss will become the stone, because dead moss is soil, and soil compresses, and compressed soil is, eventually, rock.

This is the cycle. Stone to moss to soil to stone. And on the surface of the moss, the insects are born and die, and the beetles graze, and the salamanders sit, and the things in the dark come down to take what they need, and all of it — all of it — falls eventually to the moss, and the moss absorbs it, and the moss grows, and the cycle does not pause and does not end and does not require observation or acknowledgment or understanding.

It simply continues.

---

**Nothing here is in a hurry. Nothing here is lonely.**

The undercanopy is not a sad place. It is not a place of exile or abandonment, despite its darkness, despite its depth, despite the overwhelming sense that you are somewhere you were not meant to be. The things that live here are not suffering. They are not trapped. They are not waiting for something to change. They are the change. They are the process itself, the slow grinding of life against stone, the patient accumulation of moss on surface, the constant vibration of ten million wings in warm, wet air. They are complete. They are sufficient. They are a world that asked nothing of the outside and received nothing from it and became, over ten thousand years of darkness and damp, entirely itself.

You are the thing that does not belong. You, with your eyes that expect light and your lungs that expect fresh air and your skin that expects to be dry and your metabolism that expects to eat more than once a day. You are the anomaly. You are the disruption. And the undercanopy will absorb you the way it absorbs everything — slowly, patiently, without malice, without urgency. The insects will land on you. The moss will find you. The moisture will settle on you. And in time — in the time scale of the undercanopy, which is not your time scale, which is not anyone's time scale — you will become part of the surface, part of the substrate, part of the patient, layered, incomprehensible thing that lives here.

But not yet. For now, you are still you. For now, you are still the observer, the visitor, the thing that does not fit. And the undercanopy goes on around you, and the sound continues, and the mist drifts through, and the light waxes and wanes, and the moss grows, and the beetles graze, and the salamanders sit, and the things in the dark come down and go back up, and the insects vibrate, and the stone is very slowly becoming something else, and none of it — none of it — needs you to notice.
