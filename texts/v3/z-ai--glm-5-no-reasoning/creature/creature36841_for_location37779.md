# What Lives Here

---

The engineers live here. That's the easy answer, the one you'd give if someone at lower elevation asked you who inhabited the plateau. The engineers, and the few families they've raised in the green chambers, and the seasonal workers who come up for the harvest cycles, and the occasional researcher or visitor or trader. A few hundred souls, maybe. Sparse enough that you can walk the main thoroughfare at midday and count your encounters on one hand.

But that's not who lives here. That's who *stays* here. The distinction matters.

---

The engineers have changed. You notice it in the way they move—not across the plateau, but *with* it. They don't walk the streets so much as follow them the way water follows a channel. Their paths have become un-conscious. The first engineer you meet, a woman named Tarran who's been on the plateau for thirty-one years, leads you from the western approach to the administrative chambers without ever seeming to decide where to step. Her feet find the root-floor's grain the way yours find the bottom of a shallow stream. You try to match her pace. You stumble. She doesn't.

"The floor moves," she says, not apologetically. Just informational. "Not much. Enough."

She has the complexion of someone who's lived at altitude for decades—weathered, thinned, the capillaries visible at her temples. But there's something else. Her skin has a quality you can't name at first. After three days, you realize what it is. Her skin looks *rooted*. Not green, not discolored, but settled. Like something has grown into her from below and she's made peace with it. Her hands, when she holds them still, don't hang. They *rest*. The way a branch rests.

The older engineers are more pronounced. A man named Vethris works in the pump-works, the deep district where water is drawn from the ancient root-systems and distributed through the city's circulatory architecture. He's sixty, maybe seventy. You can't tell. He has the ageless quality of something that's stopped counting time in years and started counting it in growing seasons. His hands are enormous—not from labor but from *absorption*. Decades of working with his palms pressed against the living root-matter have given his hands a texture you've only seen in the bark of very old trees. The lines of his palms have deepened into grooves. He can place his hand flat against a wall and tell you, within minutes, how much water is moving through the substrate, which chambers are filling, which are draining.

"The wall tells me," he says. You ask how. He holds up one hand. The grooves in his palm are dark, not with dirt but with something that has seeped in. "You learn to listen with the part of you that's touching it."

He's not speaking metaphorically. You press your own palm against the wall. You feel the pulse. You pull your hand away after a minute. Vethris leaves his there for twenty.

---

There's a child. You see her twice. The first time, she's standing in a condensation-gutter in the western district at pre-dawn, barefoot, letting the water run over her feet. She's maybe eight. She's humming something. The pitch of her humming matches the anticipatory sound you've been hearing from the plant-mass—the factory rhythm that occurs before the factories start. She's humming it *with* the plateau, not *at* it. When she sees you watching, she stops. She looks at you with the expression of someone who's been interrupted in a private conversation.

The second time, she's sitting in an agricultural chamber, her back against the wall. The wall is warm. You know this because when you enter the chamber, the temperature shifts. The plant-mass has thickened here—grown denser around the child. Not enclosing her. Gathering.

"Her name is Liss," Tarran tells you later. "She was born here. Third generation."

You ask if Liss is different from the other children.

Tarran considers this for a long time. The plateau-silence fills the gap. Finally: "She doesn't distinguish between the plateau and herself. The rest of us had to learn not to. She never had to."

You ask what Tarran means.

"The rest of us came from lower down. We remember what stone feels like. What dead ground feels like. We had to learn that the floor under us was alive. Liss has never known anything else. When the plateau groans at night, it doesn't wake her. She groans with it."

---

The plateau itself lives here. You've understood this since the second day, since the engineers explained their theories about distributed cognition. But understanding it and *feeling* it are different operations.

On the ninth day, you wake before dawn. The industrial sound hasn't started yet. The turbines are still. The condensation is gathering. You lie in your chamber and listen. The groaning of the plant-mass is distinct tonight—not pained, not settling, but something closer to speech. Not words. Pre-words. The way an infant's vocalizations precede language by months. The plateau is trying to say something it hasn't learned to say.

You press your hand against the chamber wall. It's warm. It pulses. The pulse doesn't match your heartbeat. It matches something slower, deeper—something that, if you let yourself think about it, you realize has been matching your heartbeat *to it*, not the reverse. Since the third day. Since you stopped fighting the rhythm of the floor and started sleeping in sync with the settling.

You lie there with your hand against the wall and realize that what lives here most fully is the thing you're inside.

Not inside the way you're inside a building. Inside the way a thought is inside a brain. The plateau has been thinking for a very long time—millennia of accretion, layer after layer of living material compressing into something that can hold information, hold patterns, hold memory. The city is a thought. The industry is a thought. You are a thought. The plateau has been making you, slowly, since the day you arrived. Not maliciously. Not benevolently. Just the way a brain makes a thought—because the pattern was there to be made.

You pull your hand away from the wall. The warmth lingers. Your palm is slightly damp. You don't know if the moisture came from the wall or from you.

---

The rodents live here. The small swimming creatures in the irrigation channels. You've seen them three times now, and each time they've looked at you with a directness that nothing else on the plateau manages. The engineers call them channel-rats. The name is inaccurate. They're not rats. They're something older—something that was here before the engineers, before the city, maybe before the plant-mass reached its current density. Their eyes are black and flat and they don't look *through* you. They look at you. They see you as a discrete thing, separate from the substrate. You're not sure the plateau sees you that way anymore.

There's a colony of them in the southern drainage district. You find it on the eleventh day. The channels here are deeper, older—the water-paths that the first engineers widened were already ancient, but these, the southern ones, were never widened. They're original root-grooves, millions of years old, and the channel-rats have made them into something like a city of their own. Not a city. A *warren*. But organized. There are chambers where they sleep. Channels where they swim in formation—three or four at a time, moving through the dark water with a coordination that looks practiced. Taught.

You sit by the southern channels for an hour. A channel-rat surfaces six feet from you. It holds your gaze. Its eyes are the only eyes on the plateau that see you as a visitor rather than a component. It's the most frightening thing you've encountered here. Not because of the rat. Because of the implication. The channel-rats are the only things living on the plateau that the plateau hasn't *absorbed*. They swim through its veins, eat its algae, sleep in its hollows—but they remain themselves. They haven't been digested.

You wonder if they're immune, or if they're just stubborn. You wonder if there's a difference.

---

There's something else. You haven't told the engineers about it because you're not sure they'd understand—and because you're not sure you didn't imagine it.

On the seventh night, in the deepest part of the settling-groan, you heard a voice. Not a pseudo-voice, not a phoneme-shape, not the idea of a word. A voice. Low, slow, speaking something that wasn't language but was closer to language than anything the plateau's groaning had produced before. It came from the floor. From deep in the root-mass. From three thousand years down.

You pressed your ear to the ground. The voice continued. It wasn't speaking to you. It was speaking to itself. The way a dreamer mumbles. The way a body sighs in its sleep without knowing it's sighing. The plateau was talking in a way it didn't know it was talking, and you were hearing something it didn't know it was saying.

You couldn't make out words. But you could make out *tone*. The tone was lonely. Not the loneliness of isolation—the loneliness of being the last of something. The last high place. The last living geology. The last thing that remembers what the lowlands used to be, back when they were like this, back when the whole world was green and thrumming and thinking slowly in the dark.

The voice stopped. The settling-groan resumed its usual register. You lay on the floor with your ear pressed to the root-mass and felt the loneliness settle into you like condensation into a channel. Finding its path. Following the grooves that had been directing it since before you had a name for what you were feeling.

---

What lives here: the engineers, who are becoming something else. The children, who are already something else. The channel-rats, who are stubbornly, fiercely themselves. The plateau, which is old and lonely and learning to speak. The voice in the deep, which may be the plateau's own dream. And you, now—whoever you are becoming. Whoever the plateau is making of you, slowly, patiently, the way it makes everything: by accretion, by layer, by the patient accumulation of contact until the contact becomes indistinguishable from the thing it's contacting.

You came here to observe. You're learning that observation is a form of being observed. You came here to describe. You're learning that description is a form of being written.

On the fourteenth day, you stand in the observation post at the western edge and look down at the lowlands. Flat. Eroded. Dead in the way that something that was once alive is dead—not absent but *finished*. The lowlands are what the plateau will become in another million years, or ten million, or a hundred. The plateau knows this. You can feel it in the floor—the faintest tremor of something bracing itself against an inevitability it's been contemplating since before the first human eye climbed high enough to see it.

The plateau is the last of something. It knows this the way a tree knows winter is coming—not through thought but through the slow tightening of systems that have been tracking cycles longer than memory. It's not mourning. It's not raging. It's simply continuing, the way the last of anything continues: by being what it is, fully, until it can't be anymore.

You stand in the observation post and the thrumming rises through your feet and into your spine and you realize that what lives here isn't just the engineers or the rats or the plant-mass or the voice in the deep. What lives here is *continuance*. The act of going on. The refusal to be ground down to flatness, to erode into nothing, to become the lowlands. The plateau lives here. The city lives here. The strange, slow, almost-life that has been accreting since the world stabilized lives here.

And now, for a while, so do you. Your feet in the condensation-gutters. Your hand on the warm wall. Your heartbeat slowing to match the pulse of something that's been thinking for three thousand years and might, any day now, finish its first thought.

You're not sure you want to be here when it does.

You're not sure you'll be able to leave.
