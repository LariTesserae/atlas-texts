# The Keepers of the Cistern

They are not what you would expect. They are not guardians in any sense that implies watching, nor stewards in any sense that implies care. They do not maintain the cistern. They do not repair its erosion or direct its water or tend its strange vegetation. They do not, in any active way, keep it.

And yet they are called keepers. This is what they call themselves, though not in words.

---

There are five of them.

The number is important. Not four, which would suggest a cardinal arrangement. Not six, which would suggest symmetry or completion. Five. The number of digits on a hand. The number of senses. The number of points needed to draw a star without lifting the stylus.

They have been five for as long as any of them can remember. They do not remember being any other number. They do not remember becoming five. They do not remember not being.

---

They are not human. This is clear from the first glance, though the second glance introduces doubt, and the third — if you have time for a third — complicates everything.

Their bodies are shaped like human bodies, in the way that a reflection in troubled water is shaped like a human body. The proportions are almost correct: two arms, two legs, a head, a torso. But the proportions are almost correct in the way that a tune hummed from memory is almost correct — you can recognize the song, but something has shifted, flattened or sharpened, in the transfer from one mind to another.

Their skin is grey. Not the grey of ash or cloud, but the grey of stone that has been wet for a long time — the particular grey of the cistern walls. It has a sheen to it, a mineral glaze, like the moisture that covers the lower reaches of the basin. When they move, the sheen shifts, and for a moment their skin looks almost like scales, or like the bark of a tree that has grown under water.

They are hairless. Their heads are smooth and slightly elongated, the backs of their skulls extending further than a human skull would. Their faces are the faces of statues that have been worn by centuries of weather — the features present but indistinct, like something remembered rather than seen. They have noses, but the noses are flat and wide, hardly rising from the plane of the face. They have mouths, but the mouths are small and rarely used. They have ears, but the ears are shallow depressions rather than projecting cartilage.

Their eyes are the only feature that is not indistinct. Their eyes are large — too large for their faces — and dark, and wet, and they reflect everything they see. Their pupils are not round but horizontal, like the pupils of a goat or a sheep, and when they look at you, they seem to be looking at you from several directions at once.

---

They do not speak.

This does not mean they cannot make sound. They make sound constantly. Their bodies produce a low, resonant hum that is not quite a voice and not quite a breath — a vibration that seems to come from their chests and resonate in their skulls, so that their whole bodies become sounding chambers. When they move, the hum shifts in pitch and timbre, tracking their motion like an auditory shadow. When they are still, the hum drops to a frequency that you feel more than hear, a pressure in your own chest, a resonance in your own bones.

They communicate through the hum, but not in the way that speech communicates. The hum does not carry words or concepts. It carries states. Attentiveness. Agreement. The simple fact of presence. When one keeper's hum shifts, the others shift in response, and the total sound of the cistern changes — not dramatically, not enough that you would notice if you weren't listening for it, but measurably. The keepers are constantly adjusting each other, the way the instruments in an orchestra adjust to each other, the way the snails and swimmers and fliers adjust to the cistern's total sound.

They also communicate through touch. They touch each other frequently — a hand on a shoulder, a forehead pressed to a forehead, a brief contact of palms. The touches are not affectionate or hierarchical. They are functional. When they touch, their hums align, and something passes between them — not a message but a synchronization, a brief merging of perspectives that allows them to operate as a single entity spread across five bodies.

---

They have no names.

Or rather, they have a single name, which is the name of the cistern itself. They are all the cistern. They are the cistern's way of being aware of itself.

This is not metaphor. They are not, in any meaningful sense, separate from the place they inhabit. Their skin has the same mineral composition as the walls. Their blood — if you could call it that, the dark fluid that pulses through their visible veins — has the same iron content as the water. They breathe the dense wet air, and when they exhale, their breath joins the moisture already present, and the cistern absorbs it, and they absorb the cistern, and the cycle continues.

They do not eat, or not in any way you would recognize. They drink from the weeping walls, pressing their mouths to the stone, drawing the moisture directly into themselves. Sometimes they wade into the central pool — all five of them, together, in a slow ritual motion — and lower themselves until the water covers them to their necks. They stay there for hours. The swimmers circle them. The snails climb their submerged bodies. The fliers land on their heads and drink from the moisture that collects in the hollows of their skulls.

When they emerge, they are slightly different. Their skin is brighter. Their hum is clearer. They have absorbed something from the pool, or the pool has absorbed something from them. It is not clear which.

---

They have no leaders and no followers. They have no hierarchy, no gender, no age that you can determine. They appear to be the same age — an age that is neither young nor old but simply *present*, the age of stone that has been shaped and then stopped being shaped.

They move together, but not in unison. They occupy different parts of the cistern at different times, but their movements are coordinated, as though each knows where the others are and what they are doing without needing to look. They seem to take turns performing certain functions — drinking from the walls, wading in the pool, standing motionless at the opening and looking up at the sky.

But these are not really functions. They are not performing tasks. They are simply being where they are, doing what they do, and the doing has no purpose beyond itself.

---

They do not sleep.

Or rather, they do not sleep in the way that humans sleep, with a clear division between waking and unconsciousness. They have periods of stillness, during which their hum drops so low that it almost disappears, and their eyes remain open but unfocused, and their bodies become so motionless that you might mistake them for carved figures left in the cistern by its original makers.

During these periods, they are still present. Their awareness has not shut down but expanded. They are listening to the cistern — not to individual sounds but to the total sound, the way the whole place vibrates with water and stone and life. They are feeling the moisture move through the walls. They are tasting the air. They are experiencing the cistern as a single vast sensation, spread across time and space, and their five bodies are simply the points at which this sensation concentrates.

When they emerge from this state — and they emerge together, always, as though some invisible signal has passed through them — they are refreshed, but not in a way that suggests rest. They are refreshed the way a still pool is refreshed when water begins to move through it again. They are cleared of sediment. They are ready to hold what needs holding.

---

They are not immortal.

This is clear from the marks on the walls. In the alcoves that were not made for drainage, the alcoves that are too small for a person and too large for a snail, there are hollows that fit the shape of a human body. The stone in these hollows is smoother than the surrounding stone, polished by long contact with skin and mineral sweat and the slow shifting of limbs. In some of them, there are deposits — pale crusts of calcium, dark stains of iron, the same mineral tracks that mark the weeping walls. Something spent a long time in these alcoves. Something that was held by the stone and released something back into it.

The keepers do not speak of these alcoves. They do not look at them. They do not avoid them either. The alcoves are simply there, part of the cistern's architecture, and what happened in them is part of the cistern's history, and the keepers are not historians. They are present. What was is not their concern. What will be is not their concern. What is — this breath, this drop of water, this vibration of stone — is all they attend to.

But if you watched them long enough — if you watched for years, or decades, which is the timescale on which they change — you would notice that they are slowly becoming more like the walls. Their skin is thickening, developing a texture like the carved tuff. Their movements are slowing, their hum deepening, their eyes becoming less distinct. They are not dying, exactly. They are *settling*. They are becoming part of the place they keep.

And sometimes — rarely, perhaps once in a generation — one of them stops moving entirely. The hum ceases. The eyes glaze over with mineral deposit. The body assumes a position of rest, often in one of the empty alcoves, and then remains there, indistinguishable from the stone around it.

When this happens, the other four continue their routines. They do not mourn. They do not mark the transition. But within a year — somehow, from somewhere — there is a fifth keeper again. Younger, perhaps, or smaller, or with a slightly different cast to its features, but unmistakably the same kind of being, occupying the same place in the cistern's five-part harmony.

Where the new keeper comes from is not clear. Perhaps it grows from the stone itself, mineral and moisture and the cistern's slow will to be aware of itself. Perhaps it emerges from the pool, one of the swimmers grown large and strange. Perhaps it is always there, a potential in the place that only becomes actual when the number drops below five.

Or perhaps the keeper never really left. Perhaps it simply *shifted* — from the mode of being that moves and hums and watches to the mode of being that holds and waits and remembers. Perhaps the cistern keeps its keepers as thoroughly as they keep it.

---

They are aware of you.

If you enter the cistern, they will know. They will not acknowledge your presence in any obvious way — they will not approach you or speak to you or make eye contact. But their hum will change. The total sound of the place will shift to accommodate your breathing, your footsteps, the moisture your body adds to the air. You will become part of the cistern's awareness, a new thread in its woven sound.

If you stay long enough — if you sit quietly and let the place absorb you — you may begin to feel what they feel. The slow pulse of the stone. The constant weeping of the walls. The rhythm of the snails and the swimmers and the fliers. The way the light enters through the opening and changes as the day progresses, the way the moisture catches it and scatters it, the way the cistern's whole surface becomes a single diffuse eye, looking up at the sky, looking down at itself, looking out at nothing and everything.

If you stay longer — days, perhaps, or weeks — the keepers may approach you. Not in friendship or hostility. In curiosity. They will stand near you and hum, and the hum will be a question: *what are you?* And you will have to answer with your own presence, your own vibration, your own way of being in the world. And they will listen, and they will judge, and they will decide whether you belong.

Most do not belong. Most are too dry, too solid, too separate. Their boundaries are too sharp. They do not weep. They do not absorb. They do not resonate. The keepers will tolerate them for a time, and then they will gently guide them out, herding them toward the opening with shifts of hum and slight movements of body, until they emerge into the dry world above and the cistern closes behind them.

But some — a few — might belong. The ones whose edges are soft. The ones who hold moisture in their lungs and grief in their chests and silence in their throats. The ones who know what it is to keep something that cannot be kept. The ones who have been hollowed out by time and weather and loss, until they are more empty space than solid matter, more vessel than content.

If you are one of these, the keepers may offer you a place. Not to stay forever — they know that humans are not built for permanence. But to return. To visit. To be held by the cistern for a time, and to hold the cistern in return.

They will teach you, if you are willing to learn. Not through instruction — they do not teach the way humans teach. They teach through resonance. They will hum, and you will feel the hum in your chest, and slowly, over time, you will begin to understand what the hum means. You will learn to listen to the stone. To taste the water's history. To feel the snails' slow thought as they move across the walls. You will learn that the cistern is not a place but a being, and that beings are not always what we expect them to be, and that keeping is not about possession or control.

It is about attention. It is about staying present to something that does not stay present to you. It is about being a vessel for something larger than yourself, and letting that something fill you and empty you and fill you again, in a rhythm that predates your birth and will continue after your death.

It is about becoming a keeper.

---

And if you are not one of these — if you are too solid, too certain, too sure of what you are and what the world is — you will leave the cistern unchanged. You will see the snails and the swimmers and the fliers. You will hear the hum and the click and the trill. You will touch the weeping walls and taste the iron water and breathe the dense wet air. And you will carry these things with you, stored somewhere in your solid body, part of you without being integrated into you.

And the keepers will watch you go. They will note your departure. They will adjust their hum to fill the space you leave. And they will continue their work, which is not work at all, but presence. They will stand in the cistern's hollow and hold what needs holding. They will be the stone's awareness of itself. They will keep the cistern, and the cistern will keep them, and the cycle will continue, has continued, will always continue, for as long as there is water to weep and stone to hollow and sound to amplify.

They are five. They have always been five. They will always be five.

This is what they are. This is what they do. This is what it means to be a keeper of the Cistern of Lungs.
