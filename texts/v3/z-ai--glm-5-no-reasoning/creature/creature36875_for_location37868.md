What lives here is not separate from the making.

Start with the grazers, because they are easiest to see. Six legs because the stone is uneven and three points of contact will not do on a surface that shifts between one moment and the next. Low-slung because the cold lives at joint level and they have minimized their joints, tucked them inward, made themselves into something closer to stones than to animals. Their hides are not skin exactly. They are membranes, permeable in a controlled way, designed to take in the plateau's constant moisture and filter from it the minerals they need. They feed by standing still. They press their bodies to the exposed veins and they drink the rock, slowly, the way the rock drinks the aquifer.

They follow the geology like text. This is not metaphor. The mineral deposits read to them as sentences read to you—structures with meaning, with sequence, with something that functions grammatically. A group of them will trace a path that looks wandering and then you see that they have been moving along a fault line, pausing at each point where the bedrock changes composition, taking what they need from each paragraph of stone before moving to the next. They are editing as they go. Consuming the draft, refining it, leaving behind waste products that mineralize and become part of the next layer.

Their eyes are lateral, set wide, and they do not focus. They do not need to. Nothing here is far enough away to require depth perception, and nothing here is close enough to be a threat. They perceive the plateau the way you perceive weather—as something surrounding, as medium rather than object. When they blink, the membranes close with a sound like wet stone against wet stone.

The swimmers-in-air are harder to describe because they resist the language of birds. They have more legs—eight, though two are vestigial, folded against their flanks, used only during the transitions between formations when the air currents shift and they need to make sudden corrections. Their bodies are long and flat, designed for the medium they move through, which is not quite air and not quite water. The plateau's atmosphere holds too much moisture. It is dense. It resists them and supports them simultaneously, and they have evolved for exactly that contradiction.

They swim because flying implies a commitment to a destination. The swimmers have no destinations. Their formations tighten and loosen according to rules that are not social but mathematical—they are computing something as they move, solving something, and the solutions are written in the patterns of their bodies against the grey sky. You watch them spiral inward and then scatter outward and you realize you have watched an equation complete itself and reset.

They do not land. There is no reason to land. They take their moisture from the air, their minerals from the condensation that forms on their surfaces, their warmth from the friction of movement itself. When they die, they dissolve. Their bodies are not built to persist. They are built to calculate and to disperse, and when the calculation is finished, the medium that held them reclaims them.

The creatures in the cracks are the ones you will not see clearly. They live in the weeping fissures, in the water that runs through every crevice, and they are too small and too fast and too strange for your eyes to hold. They have too many eyes and not enough skeleton—this is accurate. They are mostly fluid, held together by surface tension and by something that functions like intent. They have nine eyes, or twelve, or a number that changes depending on how you count, distributed across bodies that are more like concentrated water than like organisms. They move through the cracks the way water moves through the cracks, because they are nearly water, and the distinction between them and the medium they inhabit is a matter of organization rather than material.

They feed on the minerals that the grazers miss. They feed on the organic residue that the swimmers leave when they dissolve. They feed on each other, though this is not violence. It is chemistry. One absorbs another and the absorbed one's patterns persist in the absorber's structure, layered, archived. They are the plateau's memory, in a sense. Everything that dies here passes through them and is retained.

They chirp. The sound is below your threshold of attention until you hold very still, and then you hear it—a clicking, a wet percussive sound, repeated in sequences that might be language or might be sonar or might be something for which you have no category. They are communicating with the stone. They are telling the bedrock what to become next.

This is the part that takes time to understand.

The animals are not inhabitants. They are processes. The grazers are the plateau's editing, consuming and refining the mineral text, leaving deposits that become new layers of stone. The swimmers are the plateau's calculation, solving the atmospheric equations that determine where moisture will condense next, where the next structures will be printed into existence. The crack-dwellers are the plateau's memory, archiving every pattern that has passed through, feeding information back into the stone through their chirping, their small percussive instructions.

And the city—the city is the plateau's statement of what it intends to become.

It is not built by anyone. It is not built for anyone. It is the plateau growing toward a form it has not yet achieved, the way a plant grows toward light. The structures are crystallization events, matter instructed into architecture by the accumulated information that the animals process and return to the stone. The city is the plateau's future, printed one quantum instruction at a time, arriving at a speed that exists outside your perception of speed.

The half-finished walls are not unfinished. They are in the process of finishing. The scaffolding of impossible fineness is not exposed—it is visible because the matter around it has not yet decided to be opaque. The doorways that are not quite doorways are openings for bodies that do not yet exist, forms that the plateau is still composing. The rooms that your brain refuses to map correctly are spaces that have not yet committed to their dimensions.

What lives here is the plateau itself.

The animals are its organs. The city is its skeleton, forming. The water is its blood, the stone its tissue. The cold is its temperature, the metabolism of something vast and slow and still in the process of becoming. The sparseness is not emptiness. It is the efficiency of an organism that has not yet developed redundancy. Every element here is necessary. Every creature, every structure, every pool of standing water and every weeping crack and every cloud that forms and dissolves—it is all part of a single process of self-construction.

The plateau caught itself in the act of becoming. And when it noticed you noticing it, what it considered was not whether you were a threat or a guest or an intruder. What it considered was whether you were an ingredient.

You were. You are. Your exhale, hanging in the air longer than it should, studied by the atmosphere before being allowed to dissipate—the plateau was tasting it. Learning from it. Incorporating the information you carried into the draft of what it was writing itself into.

This is what lives here: something that is still deciding, and that has not yet decided to stop.
