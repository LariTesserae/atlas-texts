# The People of Vash Morrain

## I. The First Thing You Notice

They do not look up when you approach.

This is not rudeness. It is adaptation. In a place where the light comes from everywhere—a diffuse white haze that flattens the world and erases shadows—looking up means looking into brightness that does not distinguish between sky and air. The residents of Vash Morrain have learned to read the ground instead. They watch for movement in their peripheral vision. They track disturbances in the thin films of water that coat every surface. Footsteps here announce themselves in ripples long before they arrive at the ear.

So when you walk the path from the high road into the settlement's edge, the first person you encounter—a figure crouched beside a condensary's collection basin, hands submerged in warm water, checking the filter mesh—does not look up. They track your approach through the water's surface. Their head tilts slightly, angling an ear toward you, and this is acknowledgment enough. You have been noticed. You have been catalogued. You are neither threat nor priority.

They return to their work.

## II. Bodies Shaped by Wet Heat

The people here have a specific look. It takes time to recognize it as distinct—time away from the plateau, time in places where the air cools at night and the sun casts real shadows and the ground does not breathe warm water. But once you have left and returned, you see it clearly.

They are not tall. Generations of living at altitude have not stretched them upward; if anything, the opposite. Compact bodies, efficient bodies, with dense musculature that does not show until it moves. Their skin has a particular quality—a translucence, almost, as though the constant moisture has softened the barrier between inside and outside. Veins show more clearly here than elsewhere, particularly at the temples, the wrists, the inner arms. This is not frailty. It is simply that the skin has stopped trying to seal itself against an environment that will not be sealed against.

Their skin is also warm. You notice this if you touch hands in greeting—or rather, in the local custom, if you press your palm flat against theirs, a brief contact that communicates warmth and wetness and the mineral taste of the water that touches everything. A resident's palm is always slightly damp, always body-temperature or slightly above, and after the contact you will find your own hand carries a faint film that takes minutes to evaporate.

Their eyes have adapted in ways that are harder to describe. They do not squint at the white haze. Their pupils adjust to brightness without apparent strain. If you ask them about this—if you comment on how bright it seems to you, how the light seems to press against your eyes like a physical weight—they will look confused, not by your discomfort but by your framing. To them, the light is not a thing that presses. It is a thing that fills. It is simply present, the way air is present. It carries no weight.

Their hair varies in color but shares a texture: fine, straight, lying flat against the skull as though smoothed by constant moisture. Some residents shave their heads entirely, finding it cooler, more practical, less likely to trap condensation against the scalp. Others grow it long and bind it in simple cloth wraps that wick water away from the neck.

Their hands tell the longest story. Even the youngest adults have hands that look older than their faces—fingers slightly thickened at the joints from decades of working wet stone, palms marked by the faint white scars of mineral burns, nails kept trimmed so short they barely clear the fingertip. These are hands that have spent lifetimes in warm water, and the water has left its signature.

## III. The Craft of Maintenance

What do they actually do, the six hundred people of Vash Morrain?

The question seems simple. It is not.

The condensaries run themselves, mostly. The engines—brass and iron and ceramic, steam-driven, simple enough to be understood by any child who grows up beside them—require regular maintenance but not constant attention. A team of three can service an engine in a morning, checking valves, cleaning intake filters, adjusting the timing of the exhaust cycle. There are perhaps forty condensaries of significant size, so engine maintenance occupies perhaps sixty people, rotating through schedules that keep every machine running without exhausting any worker.

The plates inside the condensaries need attention. The dark stone slats that catch the rising vapor accumulate mineral deposits over time—calcium, magnesium, iron in trace amounts, all drawn from the basalt below and carried upward by the warm water. Left unchecked, the deposits would eventually crust over the plates' surfaces, reducing condensation efficiency, changing the way water flows through the collection channels. Removing the deposits requires a specific technique: careful scraping with bladed tools made from a harder stone, followed by rinsing with a mild acid solution distilled from minerals harvested elsewhere on the plateau.

Plate maintenance is skilled work. The scrapers—there are perhaps twenty who specialize in this craft, recognized by their steady hands and their intuitive understanding of each stone's grain—move through the condensaries on a schedule optimized around each building's performance. They can tell by looking at a plate whether it needs attention, whether it has weeks left or days, whether the water is running correctly or pooling in a developing fault.

Then there are the filters.

The collection basins at the base of each condensary hold the water that has condensed on the plates and run down through the channels. But the water arrives carrying more than H₂O. It carries traces of the mineral film that grows everywhere on the plateau—the thin, gelatinous living layer that coats wet stone surfaces. The film clogs pipes, contaminates storage, interferes with the chemical processes that the settlement's trade partners require. It must be removed.

The filters are mesh screens, fine-grained, arranged in layers through which the collected water passes before entering the distribution pipes. The film accumulates on these screens—thickens, spreads, becomes a gelatinous mass that must be scraped away and collected and disposed of. The disposal is important. The film cannot simply be discarded anywhere; it would regrow, spread, colonize new surfaces. It is taken to designated waste sites at the plateau's edge, downwind, down-slope, where the outflow carries it away from the settlement and its water supply.

Filter maintenance is the settlement's least desirable work. It is not difficult, but it is unpleasant. The film has a texture—raw egg white, people say, though not everyone here has encountered raw egg white—and a faint smell, mineral and vegetal both, that lingers on the hands and clothes. The people who do this work rotate through it, sharing the burden. No one is assigned to filters permanently. It would be considered cruel.

Beyond these core tasks, there are the support systems: the workshops where engine parts are fabricated and repaired, the distillation arrays where minerals are refined, the distribution network that moves collected water to storage cisterns and then to the loading platforms where it is transferred to transport vessels from the lower country. There is cooking—food arrives from elsewhere, but it must be prepared—and there is child care, and there is teaching, and there are the small tasks that hold any community together.

But through all of it, there is a quality that strikes outsiders as strange: the work does not seem urgent. No one rushes. No one hurries. The condensaries have run for generations; they will run for generations more. The water rises, the vapor condenses, the engines hum. The people of Vash Morrain have achieved something that most communities never do: they have solved the problem of survival, and they have been living in the solution for so long that it has become a kind of stillness.

## IV. Silence as Speech

They do not speak much during the bright hours.

This is the first thing a visitor must learn, and the hardest. In the heat and light of midday, when the haze goes incandescent and the world flattens into white and dark, the residents communicate through gesture. A pointing finger. A flat palm pressed downward: *wait, stay*. A hand waved in a slow circle: *all is well, continue*. Two fingers touched to the chest above the heart: *I understand*. The same two fingers touched to the temple: *I will remember*.

These gestures are not arbitrary. They are a language, evolved over generations, as precise in its way as speech. A resident can tell another resident to check the third plate from the top on the eastern face of condensary seven, and do it without making a sound. A workshop foreman can direct a team through a complex repair using only hand positions and eye movements.

Why silence? The reasons are practical and cultural both. Practically, sound behaves strangely in the bright haze. The diffuse light comes with a diffuse acoustic environment—sounds seem to come from everywhere and nowhere, distances are hard to judge, and voices carry further than expected but arrive indistinct, as though heard through fabric. Speaking feels like shouting into a void. It is uncomfortable.

Culturally, the silence is tied to attention. In a place where looking up is painful, where shadows barely exist, where the ground tells you more than the sky, the residents have learned to listen to the sounds that matter: the engine-thrum, the hiss of condensation, the subtle shift in a condensary's rhythm that signals a developing problem. Human speech would cover these signals. It would be interference.

So they save speech for the dim hours—morning, evening, night—when the haze softens and the light becomes directional and the air feels less like a medium and more like a gas. Then they gather in the workshops, the shaded spaces, the covered platforms where residents eat together. Then they talk.

Their voices are quiet. Not whispering—that would require too much breath, too much effort—but soft, a conversational register that never rises above a murmur. They have forgotten how to project. They have forgotten that speaking can be a performance, a display, a means of dominating a space. Here, speech is like the water: it moves between people, shared, distributed, never gathered or hoarded.

Visitors find this unsettling. In the bright hours, the silence feels heavy, pregnant, a pressure against the ears. Visitors want to speak, to fill the void with their own noise. When they do, residents turn—slowly, without expression—and wait. The waiting is its own communication. *You may speak if you wish*, the waiting says. *But we will not pretend we do not notice*.

## V. Children of the Plateau

Children here grow up with wet feet.

This is not metaphor. From the moment they can walk, they are walking on stone that is always slightly damp, always slightly warm. Their small feet learn the texture of wet basalt before they learn the texture of dry. They learn to grip with their toes, to test surfaces before committing weight, to move across slopes that would be treacherous if the water made them truly slippery—but the water here does not make things slippery, not exactly. It makes them consistent. Every surface has the same coefficient of friction, the same warm wetness, the same mineral taste if you happen to lick something you should not.

Children do lick things they should not. This is universal.

The children of Vash Morrain do not play with the animals, but they watch them. They learn to track strider movements, to predict where the outflow pools will expand and contract, to read the patterns of crawler trails across the mineral film. They develop games around this knowledge. One game involves following a crawler's path backward, tracing its grazing route to find the thickest patches of film. Another involves standing perfectly still near an outflow pool until a strider approaches, then seeing how close it will come before noticing the child's presence.

The striders do not seem to mind children. They do not approach, but they do not flee either. They tolerate human proximity up to a certain distance—perhaps three meters—and then they move away, unhurried, their long legs lifting and placing with mechanical precision. The children learn this distance the way children elsewhere learn the boundaries of a playground. It is simply where the striders end and the humans begin.

Children learn the engines before they learn to read. Not formally—there is no school for engine maintenance, no curriculum—but through proximity and absorption. A child who grows up near condensary three will know its rhythm, its particular voice in the polyphonic thrum, the way its vibration feels in the soles of the feet when standing on the stones nearby. If the engine falters, the child will notice before the adults do. The child may not know what to call the fault, but they will know that something is wrong. They will tug at an adult's sleeve and point.

Reading and writing happen in the dim hours, taught by elders in the workshops. The script here is a simplified variant of the lowland alphabets, optimized for carving into wet stone. Letters are angular, with few curves—easier to cut with the bladed tools the settlement uses for everything. Children practice on scraps of basalt, their first attempts clumsy, their progress slow but steady. Literacy is not universal in Vash Morrain. It is useful, valued, but not considered essential for a full life.

What is essential is the knowledge that children absorb without trying: the layout of the plateau, the path from any condensary to any other, the location of every outflow pool and collection basin and storage cistern. Children here can navigate the settlement blindfolded. They have no need for maps. The place is written into their bodies.

## VI. The Old Ones

There are residents of Vash Morrain who remember more than they say.

This is not suspicion or conspiracy. It is simply that the settlement has a history, and the history has not been written down, and the people who carry it are old now, their memories long, their stories held in the way breath is held—released slowly, carefully, only when someone is listening.

The oldest resident at present is a woman named Teret, who estimates her age at approximately ninety years but does not know precisely. She was born on the plateau, as were her parents, as were their parents. She has never left. She has never descended the high road to see the lower country or the people who trade food for water. She has no desire to do so.

Teret lives in a sheltered platform attached to the eastern wall of workshop five, a space she has occupied for sixty years. Her days are spent in the workshop, not working—her hands are too stiff now for fine maintenance—but watching. She observes the younger workers. She sees their techniques, their habits, their small innovations. Sometimes she makes suggestions. Sometimes they listen.

She remembers when there were fewer condensaries. She remembers when the striders first appeared—not individuals, but the species itself, arriving from somewhere else, finding the plateau, deciding to stay. She remembers debates about whether to drive them away. The decision to tolerate them was not unanimous. There were residents who saw the animals as competition for the water, as contamination, as a threat. They were overruled, not by argument, but by inertia. No one could be bothered to do the work of removing them.

She remembers when the gliders appeared. This was later, perhaps forty years ago. The gliders came from above, descending from the high haze, and no one knew what to make of them. They were not animals in any recognizable sense. They seemed more like fragments of some larger thing, broken free and floating. Some residents wanted to catch one, dissect it, understand what it was. Others argued that this would be a violation, though they could not articulate what was being violated. In the end, the gliders were left alone. They have been left alone ever since.

Teret remembers when the settlement still received visitors from outside. This was in her childhood. People came up the high road—traders, messengers, occasionally someone who claimed to be an official of some distant authority. They asked questions. They inspected the condensaries. They seemed interested in the water's mineral content, its thermal signature, its potential applications. Then they left, and fewer came the next year, and fewer the year after that, and eventually no one came at all except the regular traders who brought food and carried away water and asked no questions.

When asked why the visitors stopped coming, Teret shrugs. "They forgot us," she says. Or: "We forgot why they came." Or: "The world changed, and we did not change with it." She does not seem troubled by any of these explanations. She offers them as possibilities, not conclusions.

What does trouble her—if trouble is even the word—is the mineral film. She remembers when it was thinner. She remembers when it did not grow so fast, so thick, so iridescent. She remembers when the filter maintenance took less time. She does not say this is a problem. She simply notices it. The film is growing. Something is changing. The balance of Vash Morrain is not what it was.

Whether this matters, she does not say.

## VII. Relationships Without Naming

There are couples in Vash Morrain. There are families. There are friendships that span decades and rivalries that simmer across generations. But the settlement does not have words for these relationships in the way that other places do.

The local dialect—a variant of the lowland trade tongue, simplified and compressed by generations of silence—has no term for marriage. It has no term for divorce either. People live together or they do not. They separate or they do not. The settlement records nothing, celebrates nothing, mourns nothing publicly. What happens between people happens between them, and the rest of the community observes with the same tolerant attention they give to the animals: *we see you, we acknowledge you, your arrangements are your own*.

Children are raised collectively. This is not a policy; it is simply what happens. A child born to two parents will be fed by neighbors when the parents are working, taught by elders when the parents are resting, watched by older children when the parents are absent. The child will grow up knowing every adult in the settlement as a source of food, knowledge, and attention. The child will not distinguish between parents and other adults in any categorical way.

This does not mean that parents have no special bond with their children. They do. But the bond is expressed through proximity and shared work, through the specific knowledge a parent passes to a child, through the way a parent's hand rests on a child's shoulder. It is not expressed through exclusivity. A child here never belongs to anyone. A child belongs to the plateau, to the water, to the community that sustains them all.

Friendships form around work. Two people who maintain the same condensary will know each other's rhythms, anticipate each other's movements, communicate through glances and gestures. They will eat together in the dim hours, share observations, occasionally disagree. Their bond is not named. It does not need to be. It is simply the accumulated weight of hours spent in the same vibration, the same warmth, the same silence.

Romantic and sexual relationships exist. They form and dissolve with the same quiet attention that characterizes everything here. Two people may share a sleeping platform for decades, or they may spend a single night together and return to separate routines. The settlement observes and does not judge. What matters is whether the relationship affects the work—whether the condensaries run, whether the water flows, whether the balance holds. If the balance holds, the relationship is acceptable. If it does not, the community intervenes—not with rules or punishments, but with conversation, adjustment, gentle pressure toward a solution.

This is how the people of Vash Morrain live: without categories, without ceremonies, without the structures that other communities use to contain the chaos of human desire. They contain it instead through attention, through patience, through the unspoken understanding that every relationship exists within a larger relationship—that each person is an organ of the settlement, and the settlement's health is the only measure that matters.

## VIII. Leaving and Returning

People do leave Vash Morrain.

Not often, but it happens. A young person reaches an age of restlessness—the age where the white haze feels suffocating instead of comforting, where the silence feels like weight instead of peace—and walks down the high road toward the lower country. They may have heard stories from traders about cities, about crowds, about places where the sun casts real shadows and the night goes truly dark. They want to see these things.

The settlement does not stop them. There is no ritual of departure, no gathering to send the traveler off. The leaver simply leaves, carrying a small pack of provisions, a pouch of refined minerals for trade, and the knowledge that they can return if they choose.

Some do not choose. They find the lower country congenial. They learn to live with cool nights, with directional sunlight, with the complex social structures of other communities. They marry, breed, work, die. They become people of the lowlands, and Vash Morrain becomes a memory—a strange white dream they occasionally recall, telling stories about the plateau where the water comes from the stone and the animals have no names.

Others find the lower country unbearable. This is not because it is worse—objectively, many lowland settlements have more comfort, more variety, more pleasure. It is because the people who return have been shaped by Vash Morrain in ways they did not recognize until they left. Their bodies, adapted to wet heat, struggle in dry cold. Their eyes, adjusted to diffuse light, ache under direct sun. Their ears, trained to the engine-thrum, find silence hollow and disturbing.

And they miss the water. They miss the feeling of walking on stone that breathes warm moisture, of touching surfaces that acknowledge you with dampness, of existing in an environment that does not dry you out but holds you in a constant, gentle, mineral embrace.

Those who return are not judged. They are not questioned. They are simply absorbed back into the settlement's routines, assigned tasks according to their abilities and the community's needs, given space to sleep and work and live. If they left as children, they return as adults and are treated as adults. If they left as adults, they return as themselves-plus-absence, and the absence is not mentioned unless they choose to mention it.

A returned resident will sometimes notice things that the permanent residents have stopped noticing. The way the mineral film has thickened. The way the striders' patterns have shifted. The way the engine-thrum has changed pitch over the years. They may mention these observations. They may be thanked, or they may be told that the observation has already been noted and filed, not acted upon because action is not yet required.

The settlement has a tempo, and the tempo is slow. Generations slow. What returns to Vash Morrain after a decade away is not what left. What remains after they have been absorbed is a sense, difficult to articulate, that the settlement is not quite what it was—and that the change is neither good nor bad, simply accumulated, like mineral deposits on condensary plates.

## IX. The Wet Dream

There is a phenomenon that visitors report, and that the residents do not discuss.

After leaving Vash Morrain—after descending the high road, returning to the lower country, resuming whatever life they had before—visitors often dream of the plateau.

The dreams are consistent in their structure. The dreamer stands on the white stone, surrounded by bright haze, and the condensaries rise around them like dark pillars. The engine-thrum fills the air. Water runs somewhere nearby, unseen but felt—a constant, warm presence. The striders move at the edge of vision, their long legs placing themselves with mechanical grace. The mineral film coats every surface, including the dreamer's skin, a thin iridescent layer that tingles but does not hurt.

And the dreamer feels a sense of rightness. Not happiness—happiness is too specific, too small for what the dream conveys. Rightness. Correctness. The feeling that this is where things belong, that the plateau and the water and the settlement and the silence are arranged exactly as they should be, that any deviation from this arrangement would be an error.

The dreamer does not want to wake. When they do wake—when they open their eyes to a dark room, a cool morning, a world where shadows have edges and the air does not hold them—they feel a loss. Not grief. Emptiness. The absence of a fullness they had briefly experienced.

The residents of Vash Morrain do not have these dreams. They do not need to. They are already inside the rightness, the correctness, the arrangement that the dreams imperfectly convey. They do not know what they are missing because they are not missing anything. They are present.

But visitors—visitors carry something away from the plateau that they cannot name and cannot release. It sits in their bodies like the mineral taste of the water. It returns in dreams. It asks, without words, without demands, without any particular urgency: *was that not better? Was that not more?*

Most visitors ignore the question. They have lives, obligations, purposes. They cannot live on a plateau where time moves differently and nothing ever seems to begin or end.

But some visitors return. And some of those who return stay.

And the plateau receives them, and the water acknowledges them, and the condensaries continue to hum, and the striders continue to walk, and the engine-thrum continues its patient chord, and nothing changes except that one more organ has been added to a body that was always large enough to hold it.

---

*This is what lives in Vash Morrain: people who have forgotten why they came, animals who were never invited, and a machine that runs because it runs, harvesting water from stone, drinking the sky, waiting for nothing in particular, content in the way that only things without purpose can be content.*
