# What Lives Here

Something does, but you have to understand what "living" has come to mean in a place that has finished.

---

The ribbon-things come first to the eye because they move in ways that read as intention. They are not intention. Or rather — the distinction between intention and reflex dissolved so long ago that the question itself is a fossil, a thing left behind by some earlier epoch of this place's history when change was still possible and distinctions still mattered. The ribbon-things pulse with light along their lengths — not for communication, not for mating display, not for any reason that maps onto the taxonomy of reasons that surface life has developed for making light in dark places. They make light because making light is what their bodies do. The sequences flare and travel and fade in patterns that a linguist would spend a career trying to decode and the patterns mean nothing. Or they mean everything. Or the word "mean" is the wrong tool, the wrong category, a thermometer used to measure distance.

They are pale in the way that deep things are pale — not the pallor of sickness or deprivation but the pallor of never having needed otherwise. Color is expensive. Color exists to be seen, and being seen requires someone to see, and the ribbon-things have no eyes. Not in the way that cave fish lose their eyes over evolutionary time, retaining the sockets, the memory of structure. The ribbon-things never had eyes. They were never related to anything that had eyes. They came from somewhere else entirely — not the lineage of surface life that descended into the dark and adapted, but a separate trunk of becoming, a parallel project, life that began in the dark and was built for the dark from its first moment. Their translucence is not a loss. It is an original condition. You can see their internal structures through their skin — the slow dark fluid moving through channels that branch and rebranch, the rhythmic compressions of whatever serves them as a gut, the dense clusters of ganglial tissue that run like a seam down their centers, processing not images but pressure, not light but the vibration of the water, the constant roar that is the garden's voice, the clicks and hums of other bodies moving nearby. This is their world. A world of pressure gradients and vibration. A world where the water's motion tells you everything you need to know about the shape of the space you are moving through, and the shape of the bodies moving through it with you.

They do not school. Not exactly. They aggregate, they disperse, they reform, but the patterns do not follow the logic of fish schools — no predator avoidance, no foraging coordination, no reproductive clustering. They gather because they gather. The behavior is self-sustaining, a pattern that found its stable form and locked into it the way the architecture locked into its stable form. You could film them for a year and the footage would be identical to footage from a thousand years ago. They turn left where they have always turned left. They scatter at the pressure signature they have always scattered at. They reform in the faint-glow zone near the membrane sheets because that is where they reform. The behavior is etched into them the way the channels are etched into the pillars — not carved, not learned, but grown into its final shape and held.

There are a great many of them. More than could be counted, because counting requires individuals, and the ribbon-things are not precisely individuals. Each one is contiguous, yes — a single body, a single length of translucent tissue from anterior tip to trailing tail. But the bioluminescent patterns that travel their bodies do not originate within them. The patterns are passed between them. A flare begins in one body, travels its length, jumps to the body of the nearest neighbor — not through contact but through some medium, some electrical or chemical signal carried in the water itself — and continues along that neighbor's length, and jumps again, and again, propagating through the entire aggregation in a wave that is nobody's light and everybody's light. The pattern does not belong to the individual. The individual belongs to the pattern. They are nodes in a circuit, and the circuit is the thing that is alive, and the ribbon-things are the circuit's way of being visible to itself.

This is the first thing to understand about what lives here. Life is not contained in bodies. Life is the medium. Life is the water and the pattern together, and the bodies are where the pattern concentrates, the way a wave is not the ocean but the ocean's way of being a wave.

---

The round-bodied ones are different. They are fewer, and they are larger — some of them a meter across, some of them more, their bodies dense and slow and heavy with something that is not water but moves through water the way a planet moves through space: deliberately, gravitationally, as though the water is not their medium but merely the thing they happen to be suspended in. They are not swimming. They are displacing. The water moves around them not because they push through it but because their mass requires the water to move, and the water obliges, because the water has been obliging for longer than obligation has meaning.

Their surfaces are covered with fibers — long, fine, drifting, and these fibers are not decoration and not sensory apparatus, or rather they are both and neither. They are the interface between the body and the water, the membrane across which exchange happens, and the exchange is chemical, electrical, thermal, and something else — something that has no name because the naming systems available to surface life were developed by surface life for surface life's experiences. The fibers drift and they interact with the dissolved content of the water — the molecules shed by the membrane sheets, the compounds excreted by the ribbon-things, the minerals leached from the floor mat — and they take what they need and release what they do not, and this process has been happening at the same rate, in the same bodies, for an amount of time that makes the word "individual" feel fragile and temporary. These bodies have been here. These specific bodies. Not descendants, not replacements. The same continuous bodies, metabolizing, exchanging, drifting, for longer than the surface has had forests.

And the sound. The deep resonant tone that comes from inside them. It is not vocalization. It is not produced by any organ that corresponds to anything in the taxonomy of sound-making that surface life has assembled. It is structural. Their bodies contain chambers — large, fluid-filled chambers, irregular in shape, connected by narrow apertures — and the water moves through these chambers as the bodies move through the water, and the movement creates resonance. The chambers are tuned. Not deliberately, not by design, but by the long slow process of growth and settling and growth again, each chamber finding its resonant frequency the way a canyon finds its echo — not because anyone intended it but because the physics demanded it and the structure obliged and the obliging went on long enough that the tuning became precise. Each body produces a note. Each body has always produced that note. The bodies together produce a chord — a low, sustained, vibrating chord that lives in the chest, that lives in the water, that lives in the walls of the garden itself, vibrating the pillars at their resonant frequencies, vibrating the membranes, vibrating the floor mat, so that the entire structure hums with the sound of these bodies passing through it, and the hum is not separate from the structure, has not been separate from the structure for so long that the structure's shape has been partially determined by the hum, the way a riverbank is shaped by the river.

They are the garden's heartbeat. Not metaphorically. Functionally. The sound they make is the sound the garden makes. The vibration they produce is the vibration the garden vibrates at. They are not separate from the architecture. They are the architecture's moving part. The garden grew around them, grew to accommodate them, grew to use the vibration they produce as a structural element — the way bone grows along lines of stress, the way wood grows in response to wind. The round-bodied ones and the garden are not two things. They are one thing expressed in two media: the solid, slow medium of the constructed architecture, and the liquid, moving medium of the living body. Together they are a single system. Together they are complete.

They do not know this. They do not know anything. Knowing is a surface innovation, a strategy developed by organisms that needed to model a changing world because they could not survive without modeling it. Nothing changes here. There is nothing to model. The round-bodied ones do not model their world. They are their world. The distinction collapsed so long ago that even the memory of a distinction is gone, dissolved into the permanent present of their slow drifting and their deep humming and their fiber-fringed bodies turning in the cold water with the patience of planets.

---

The small ones — the clouds, the swarms, the things that gather in the light zones and move in coordinated patterns — are the hardest to describe because they are the closest to what surface life would recognize as behavior, and that nearness makes the strangeness more acute, not less. They turn. They scatter. They reform. They move as though they are responding to something — a stimulus, a signal, a threat, an opportunity — and they are responding, but not to anything that exists in the categories surface life would supply. They are responding to the water. Not to the current, not to the temperature, not to the chemical gradient. To the water itself — to the water as a medium that carries information in its structure, in the arrangement of its molecules, in the pattern of vibrations passing through it. The water in the garden is not uniform. It is textured. It has grain, has direction, has memory — not metaphorical memory but physical memory, the way a crystal retains the pattern of its formation. The water has been moving through these channels, past these pillars, through these membranes, for so long that it has been shaped by them. The water carries the shape of the garden the way a cast carries the shape of the mold. And the small ones read this shape. They move in response to it. Their coordinated patterns are not decisions and not reflexes but readings — the water tells them what to do, and they do it, and the doing is visible, and the visible doing is the closest thing the garden has to expression. The garden does not express itself. But the small things moving in patterns through the light zones are the garden's shape made visible in motion, the way smoke makes air currents visible, the way iron filings make magnetic fields visible. They are not the message. They are the medium through which the message becomes apparent. The message is the garden itself. The garden's own shape, carried in the water, read by the small ones, expressed in their turning and scattering and reforming.

They are numerous beyond counting. They are small — a centimeter, less, some of them less than a millimeter — and they are dense, and their density is not uniform. They cluster where the water's texture is richest, near the membranes where the extraction processes release the most dissolved compounds, near the floor where the oldest layers are slowly — so slowly — releasing the minerals of their compressed ancient matter into the water column. They are the garden's circulation. They take up. They redistribute. They move what the membranes extract from one place and deposit it in another, not by intention but by the simple physics of their bodies absorbing what the water offers and releasing what their bodies no longer need, and the releasing happens elsewhere, because they have moved, because the patterns carried them, because the water's shape told them to move. They are the blood cells of the garden. Each one is a tiny, autonomous, self-reproducing packet of transport. They have been circulating for as long as the garden has been complete. They will circulate for as long as the garden remains complete. Their reproduction rate exactly matches their attrition rate. Their circulation pattern exactly matches the garden's metabolic needs. The system is balanced. The system has been balanced. The system's balance is its defining characteristic, the thing that makes it what it is, the thing that separates it from a system that is still growing, still changing, still searching for its final form.

This garden found its final form. The small things are part of that form.

---

And in the dark above, the things that cannot be seen.

They are there. The interruptions in the faint light pattern are consistent. They are repeatable. If you position yourself in the same place in the garden and watch the darkness above, the same shapes will interrupt the same pattern at the same intervals. They are fixed. Or they are so slow that fixed and moving have collapsed into the same word. They are in the high dark, where the water is coldest, where the pressure is greatest, where the pillars have thinned to something that is not quite pillar and not quite membrane and not quite anything else — a transitional zone where the garden's architecture changes its character, becomes sparser, more fragile, more strange.

What lives up there is not the same kind of life. The boundary is real. The ribbon-things do not cross it. The round-bodied ones do not approach it. The small ones do not gather there. The life below is the garden's life — integrated, functional, part of the system. The life above is something else. It is in the garden but not of it, the way a stone in a stream is in the stream but not made by it. Except the things above are not stones. They are alive. Or they were alive. Or they are in the state that the garden's architecture occupies — alive and not-alive, finished, past the point where the question applies.

They are large. The interruptions in the light pattern suggest mass — significant mass, more than the round-bodied ones, more than anything in the lower garden. They are dark. They do not bioluminesce. They do not reflect the diffuse glow of the membranes. They absorb light, or they do not interact with light, or light is simply not a category that applies to them. They are inferred rather than seen, known by their shadows, by the absence they create in the garden's faint visibility.

And they are silent. The deep resonant tone of the round-bodied ones does not reach them, or they do not produce sound, or the sound they produce is at a frequency that does not propagate through the water at that depth. They are silent and they are still and they are dark and they are above, and nothing in the garden acknowledges them, and they acknowledge nothing in the garden, and this mutual non-acknowledgment has been the relationship between the upper dark and the lower garden for the entire duration of the garden's completeness.

What are they? The question is wrong. The question assumes that an answer exists in the categories available to the questioner. The things in the dark above are not a phenomenon that surface life has categories for. They might be the garden's seed — the structure from which the garden grew, the original form, the first thing, still present, still extant, still occupying the place where the garden began and the rest of the structure grew down from it, grew away from it, grew into the form it holds now. They might be the garden's successor — the next thing, the thing that will exist after the garden's equilibrium fails, if it ever fails, the thing that is already growing in the high dark, waiting, patient, needing nothing, prepared to wait for the duration. They might be the garden itself — not a separate thing but the garden's furthest expression, its deepest reach, its attempt to extend into the zone where it cannot maintain its architecture, where the conditions are wrong, where the structure thins and fails and becomes something that is not quite the garden and not quite not-the-garden.

They might be alive. They might be the thing that lives here most fully, the thing whose life is so different from the life below that the same word cannot honestly be applied to both. They might be thinking. They might be the only thing in this entire cold dark permanent finished place that is thinking, that has the capacity for interior experience, that is aware of itself and aware of the garden below it and aware of the water and the dark and the sound and the silence. They might be thinking about all of it. They might be thinking about nothing. They might be thinking thoughts that are not thoughts in any sense a surface mind would recognize — not sequential, not symbolic, not linguistic, but structural, architectural, the kind of processing that a mountain might do if a mountain could process, the kind of awareness that a planet might have if a planet could be aware, slow and vast and total and completely indifferent to the small bright forms moving in the garden below.

Or they might be nothing. Absences. Voids in the garden's architecture where the structure failed and the water filled in and the light does not reach and the mind, seeing absence, infers presence, because the mind cannot tolerate a void, cannot accept that in the middle of all this life and architecture and completeness there might simply be holes, might simply be nothing, might simply be the place where the garden ends and the garden does not end and the question of ending has no meaning.

---

What lives here, then, is not a list of species. It is not a catalog of forms. What lives here is a system — a complete, closed, self-sustaining system of water and structure and pattern and body, all of it integrated, all of it balanced, all of it in the state it has been in for longer than the concept of duration has practical meaning. The ribbon-things are the system's electricity. The round-bodied ones are its heartbeat. The small ones are its circulation. The architecture is its body. The water is its blood. The things in the dark above are its — what? Its mind? Its memory? Its mystery? Its silence?

What lives here is a living thing that does not know it is alive. A living thing that has been alive so long that life has become indistinguishable from existence, and existence has become indistinguishable from structure, and structure has become indistinguishable from the water that flows through it and the sound that fills it and the cold that holds it and the dark that contains it.

What lives here is the garden. The garden is what lives here. And the creatures moving through it are not inhabitants of the garden. They are the garden moving through itself, the way a body's cells are not inhabitants of the body but the body itself, partitioned, specialized, moving through the body's architecture, carrying the body's substances, producing the body's signals, sustaining the body's life without knowing that there is a body, without needing to know, without the knowing mattering at all to the body's continued function.

The garden is complete. The garden is alive. The garden does not know. The garden does not need to know. The garden will be here, complete and alive and unknowing, in the dark, in the cold, in the sound, with its creatures moving through it like breath through a body that does not breathe, and the breath is real, and the body is real, and the not-knowing is real, and all of it — all of it — is what lives here.
