# The High Bramid

You come up through the roots.

That is the only way to describe it — though the word *roots* is insufficient. For the last three hours of ascent, the ground beneath your feet has not been ground. It has been living wood, living fiber, a woven tensile mass of ancient xylem and phloem and cambium in layers so deep you could bore for days and never reach mineral substrate. The path threads between buttress structures the size of cathedral walls, but these are not buttresses of any single tree. They are the exposed edges of a continent-scale rhizomatic mass — a plant body so old and so intertwined that the individual organism concept collapsed here sometime before human memory began.

The Bramid is a plateau. Or it was once. Forty million years ago — maybe sixty, the estimates vary — this was a mountain range. Real rock, real peaks, real granite pushing into cold thin air. Then the plants came. Not plants as we know them, not exactly, but something ancestral to what now covers everything: a clade of hyper-woody, deeply networked vascular organisms with no single name because the people who live here do not name what is simply *the world*. The roots cracked the rock. The roots held water against the rock. The water froze and thawed and the roots cracked it more. Fifty million years of this. The mountains came down. The plants stayed. The plants stayed and grew into the rubble, and grew into each other, and grew into their own dead ancestors' bodies, layer after layer after layer, until what remained was a high flat living plain the size of a small sea, elevated two and a half miles above the lowlands, held in shape not by geology but by the structural tension of ten billion interlocked trunks and stems and aerial roots that have never known anything but other plant matter beneath them.

You step out onto the surface and the world opens.

---

The sky here is enormous. At this elevation, with the horizon stretching flat in every direction and nothing — no hills, no peaks, no ridgelines remaining — the sky sits on the world like a dome of pale glass. The light is full daylight, unremarkable, but it falls onto a green so vast and so uniform that the color seems to generate its own luminescence. The surface of the Bramid is a living mat. Not grass — closer to a tight weave of woody creepers and broad flat succulent leaves and a continuous carpet of moss-analogues that fill every gap. It is walkable. It is firm underfoot, slightly springy, like walking on a drumhead stretched over a hollow body. Because it is, in a sense, exactly that. Beneath the surface mat, the interior of the Bramid is hollow — not empty, but structured like a sponge, vast chambers of dead and living wood alternating with spaces where roots have grown apart and left gaps. The water collects in those gaps. You can hear it when you stand still: a low, subsonic trickle and drip far below your feet, moisture percolating through fifty meters of living structure.

The air is warm. Not hot — a thick, humid warmth, like stepping into a greenhouse. Moisture sits on your skin immediately. Not sweat — condensation. The Bramid generates its own microclimate. The transpiration of ten billion plants at this altitude pushes warm wet air upward where it cools and falls back as a fine, intermittent mist. It rains here seven or eight times a day, but never for long. Ten minutes of dense warm rain, then sun, then mist, then rain again. The cycle is so regular you could time your watch by it. The people who live here — and there are people, a few — do time their lives by it. They call the rain-cycles *breaths* and they speak of the Bramid breathing, which is not entirely a metaphor.

---

And the sound.

The sound is the first thing that actually hurts. You have been hearing it since the upper reaches of the ascent, a wash of noise that grew from a hum to a buzz to a roar to a physical pressure against your eardrums, and now, standing on the surface, it is nearly unbearable. It comes from everywhere. It comes from the mat beneath your feet. It comes from the air. It comes from the low woody canopy structures — shoulder-high thickets of branching stems — that dot the surface in irregular clusters. It is the sound of life at a density that defies comprehension.

Insects. The insects are the foundation. The Bramid supports an arthropod biomass that exceeds anything in the lowland forests by orders of magnitude, because the substrate itself is food. The surface mat is constantly growing, constantly being eaten, constantly regrowing. Billions of boring beetles chew through the woody creepers. Something like caterpillars — but soft, pale, translucent, the size of your thumb — work through the moss carpet in waves, their mandibles producing a continuous granular scraping that, multiplied by millions, becomes a low roar. Flying insects cloud the air at knee height: things like midges, things like wasps, things with no earthly analogue — four-winged, iridescent, moving in synchronized patterns so dense they look like weather. The sound of their wings alone is a sustained high-pitched whine that sits at the edge of what the human ear can process.

Then the birds. The Bramid's birds are small, brilliantly colored, and absurdly numerous. They flock in groups of ten thousand or more, moving across the surface in loose clouds, landing on the canopy thickets and stripping them of insects in minutes. Their calls are sharp, repetitive, metallic — each species with its own rhythm, dozens of species overlapping, producing a layered percussion that has no silence in it, no gaps, no rests. It is a wall of sound. When a flock passes directly overhead the noise spikes to a level that makes you flinch, that makes your sternum vibrate.

And beneath all of this, lower in the register, the amphibians. The water pockets inside the Bramid's structure host vast populations of something between a frog and a salamander — smooth-skinned, broad-mouthed, pale. They sing from the interior chambers, their voices rising through the porous wood and the surface mat as a deep, resonant chorus, a throbbing bass note that you feel more than hear. At dusk — when the bird flocks settle and the flying insects thicken — the amphibian chorus rises to dominate, and the Bramid seems to pulse with it, the whole living plain thrumming like a drumhead.

---

The emptiness is what confuses you. Everything you have read about the Bramid — the fragmentary explorer's accounts, the oral histories from the lowland towns — describes abundance. And there is abundance. The life here is overwhelming, a saturation of biomass and sound and moisture. But the space between things — the actual physical distance from one feature to the next — is vast. The canopy thickets are spaced hundreds of meters apart. The surface mat stretches in unbroken flatness to every horizon, and walking across it is a meditative, almost lonely act. You can walk for an hour and encounter nothing taller than your ankle. The bird flocks appear as distant smudges on the horizon before they reach you. The few surface water features — wide, shallow pools that form in natural depressions where the mat sags — are kilometers apart.

It is the paradox of the Bramid: there is more life per cubic meter here than anywhere else on the planet, and yet the place feels hollow. The flatness. The silence between the sound-sources, which is not silence at all but a ringing absence where your brain expects echoes and gets none because the surface absorbs everything. The sky pressing down. The knowledge that beneath your feet is fifty meters of living structure and then nothing — just a void where the mountains used to be, the whole plain suspended on its own tension like a web.

---

You find the first structure on the second day.

It is a platform, raised about two meters above the surface mat, supported on stilts made from the Bramid's own wood — not cut, but grown. You can see where the stems were trained, bent, grafted, guided over years into the shape of legs and a floor. The platform is perhaps four meters across, open to the sky, with a woven roof of broad leaves that have been layered and stitched with some kind of fibrous thread. There is a fire pit in the center — a ring of dense blackened wood, not stone, because there is no stone here. The wood has been treated somehow, soaked in something, because it has not rotted despite what must be years of rain and humidity.

Near the platform, a few tools: a digging stick made from a sharpened branch, its tip hardened by slow heating. A woven basket, tightly constructed, still sound. A cordage of twisted plant fiber, knotted at intervals, perhaps a measuring line. These are the artifacts of a people who work with the Bramid and not against it, who have no metal and no stone and no need for either, because the living world around them provides every material: fiber for cordage, wood for structure, leaves for shelter, and food in the form of insects and amphibians and the starchy inner tissue of the mat itself.

There are no people at this platform. You find two more over the next day, each similar, each unoccupied, each spaced at intervals of several kilometers across the surface. They are not abandoned — the roofs have been recently repaired, the fire pits cleaned. But the population is so thin, so widely distributed, that the chance of encountering anyone is small. Perhaps a few hundred people live on the entire Bramid. Perhaps fewer. They are distributed across an area the size of a small country, living in ones and twos on their trained-wood platforms, moving between them along paths that are not paths but simply slightly firmer lines in the mat where feet have walked for generations.

---

On the third day, the Bramid moves.

Not metaphorically. You are lying on the surface mat at midday — the rain-cycle has just passed and the sun is out and you are resting — when you feel it. A slow undulation. The mat rises beneath your back by perhaps ten centimeters, holds, and settles. Then again, a hundred meters to your left, a slightly larger movement, a visible ripple in the surface as the living carpet flexes.

You understand, then, that the Bramid is not a static structure. It is a slow, continuous system in motion. The interior chambers fill with rainwater and the weight deforms the surface. The water drains — slowly, over hours, through the deep structure — and the surface rebounds. The whole plain is breathing, flexing, settling and rising in cycles that match the rain. The amphibians in the interior chambers adjust their calls to the movement — you notice that the bass chorus shifts pitch slightly with each undulation, as the chamber volumes change and the resonance shifts. They are singing in the Bramid's breath.

And the insects adjust to the birds, and the birds adjust to the insects, and the mat grows where it has been eaten and is eaten where it has grown, and the people move between their platforms and repair their roofs and harvest their food and the whole system turns and turns and has been turning for fifty million years on the bones of a mountain range that no longer exists.

The sound does not stop. It has not stopped since you arrived. It will not stop. It is the sound of the Bramid being alive, which is the sound of the world being itself, which is so loud here, at this altitude, on this ancient living plain, that you have begun to lose the distinction between the sound and the silence, between the vibration and the stillness, between the thing and the void it fills.

You lie on the surface and feel it breathe beneath you. Above, the sky is pale and huge and empty. Below, fifty meters of living wood. Below that, nothing. The mountains are gone. What remains is this.
