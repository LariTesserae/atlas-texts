# The Hollow of Moving Stones

You descend into it the way you descend into any old wound — gradually, without a clear moment of arrival. The passage widens, the ceiling lifts beyond your light, and then you are there, standing in a city that was old before the concept of age had settled into the world's vocabulary.

---

The cavern is enormous. Not cathedral-enormous — that word implies a ceiling, a sense of enclosure. This is something else. The roof, if there is one, has been lost to a pale luminous haze that hangs thirty or forty meters up and diffuses light downward without revealing its source. The light is blue-white, steady, the color of moonlight if moonlight had been refined and concentrated. It casts no shadows. Everything is lit equally from all angles, which gives the place a flattened, dreamlike quality — you can see very far, but depth becomes difficult to judge. Distances telescope. What looks like a nearby wall might be a distant one, and you only realize this when you've been walking toward it for ten minutes and it hasn't grown.

The city — and it is a city, or was — spreads across the cavern floor in a pattern that might once have been grid-like. The erosion of inconceivable time has softened that geometry into something more organic. Streets are shallow channels worn into the rock, their edges rounded to gentle curves, their surfaces polished to a dull sheen by the passage of... what? Water, perhaps, in some earlier age. Or wind, if wind ever moved down here. Or time itself, which has its own kind of abrasion. The buildings are low — most stand no higher than two meters, their walls thick, their doorways wide and squat. Whatever people built this place worked in stone and nothing else. No metal survives. No wood could survive. The walls are the same grey-white limestone as the cavern floor, and in many places you cannot tell where building ends and bedrock begins. The structures have been worn down to their essence — a suggestion of rooms, a hint of division, the memory of architecture.

There are hundreds of them. Thousands, possibly. They extend in every direction, sparse and patient, with wide gaps between clusters. The emptiness between buildings is not neglect. It feels deliberate, ceremonial almost — as though the builders understood that space itself was a material to be worked, and they used it generously.

The ground is damp. Not wet — damp. A film of moisture clings to the stone, collects in the shallow grooves of the streets, beads on the surfaces of walls. It comes from the air. The cavern breathes, slowly, on some geological timescale, and each exhalation deposits a layer of condensation on everything below. You can see it gather on your hands. It is not cold, but cool — the kind of cool that settles into cloth and stays. The temperature is stable, unchanging, the kind of thermal consistency that only happens underground, where the rock has had long enough to decide what it wants to be and has settled on this: twelve degrees, forever.

There is no plant life. None. Not a moss, not a lichen, not a thread of algae. The mineral world holds this place completely. The stone is bare and clean, as though scrubbed. In the absence of vegetation, the colors are all geological — the white-grey of limestone, the amber-brown of mineral stains where water has traced paths down walls, the occasional flash of something crystalline embedded in a surface, catching that sourceless light and throwing it back in a different color. Rust-red. Pale green. A purple so dark it looks black until you turn your head and the angle changes.

---

The fauna, by contrast, are everywhere.

They are not animals in any familiar sense. They are... constructs might be the closest word, but that implies a builder, and you cannot tell if these things were built or if they emerged. They are made of the same limestone as everything else, or something very like it — a pale, chalky substance that seems to grow and reform and dissolve according to rules you cannot parse. Some are the size of your fist. Some are the size of a horse. They move, but the movement is so slow that you only notice it by watching a single individual for a long time and then looking away and back. They have shifted. They have rearranged themselves.

Their shapes are irregular, provisional. A body that might be a shell, appendages that might be legs or might be roots or might be feeding structures. They gather in the low places — the worn streets, the shallow bowls in the cavern floor, the doorways of buildings — and they seem to feed on the mineral film that the condensation leaves behind. You can watch one settle against a wall and, over the course of an hour, leave a faint bright patch where the stone has been cleaned. They are grazing. They are grazing on the rock.

They make no sound. This is the most disconcerting thing about them. There are hundreds visible at any time — thousands, probably, across the full extent of the cavern — and they are all silent. Their bodies do not click against the stone. Their appendages do not scrape. Even when two of them meet, which happens occasionally, there is no collision, no friction. They simply merge, briefly, and separate, each slightly changed by the encounter. The silence is total. You hear your own breathing, your own footsteps, the faint creak of your own joints, and nothing else. The cavern is as quiet as a room that has been sealed for a very long time, except it hasn't been sealed — it is alive with movement, with activity, with the constant slow reshuffling of these mineral grazers going about their business.

---

And the ground moves.

Not the way ground moves in an earthquake — not sudden, not violent. The ground moves the way a crowd moves: slowly, in shifting currents, with local turbulence that propagates outward and then subsides. You feel it through your feet first. A faint unease, a sense that the floor is not quite where it was a moment ago. Then you see it. The street you are standing in is widening. The buildings on either side are drifting apart, centimeter by centimeter, their walls grinding against the bedrock with a sound so low it is more vibration than noise. A crack opens in the floor, runs twenty meters in a direction that seems random, and then closes again. A mound rises in the middle of an intersection, lifts fifteen centimeters, holds for a moment, and sinks back. The stone is not solid. The stone is a very slow liquid.

This is the turbulence of the place — not atmospheric, not biological, but geological. The cavern is not stable. It is in a state of constant, slow, chaotic rearrangement. The buildings shift. The streets migrate. New channels open in the floor and old ones close. The mineral grazers move with these changes, riding the currents of stone the way birds ride thermals, clustering where the ground is active and dispersing where it is still. They are adapted to it. They are, perhaps, born from it — you begin to suspect that the grazers are not separate from the stone at all, that they are the stone's way of being alive, its method of sensing and responding to its own turbulence.

The ancient builders, whoever they were, must have known this. The wide streets, the thick walls, the low structures — these are not aesthetic choices. They are accommodations. You build low because high things fall. You build thick because the ground will push and pull. You leave space between buildings because the buildings will move, and if they are too close together, they will collide. The city is not ruins. It is a working response to a working problem, still standing because it was designed to stand in motion.

---

You walk for an hour and the city does not end. The pattern shifts — the buildings cluster more tightly here, sparser there, and in one region they stop entirely, replaced by a wide plain of bare stone pocked with shallow basins of clear water. The water is still and cold and reflects the luminous haze above. The grazers are thicker here, clustered around the basins, their pale bodies half-submerged. They are drinking, or dissolving, or depositing something — you cannot tell. The water in the basins closest to them is slightly clouded, faintly milky. A fine sediment, mineral in origin, drifting in the still water.

In the distance, across the plain, another cluster of buildings begins. And beyond that, another plain. And beyond that, the haze swallows detail and you are left with the sense of a place that goes on and on, region after region, a landscape that was shaped by hands and has since been reshaped by time and stone and the slow logic of geology into something that is neither built nor natural but some third thing.

The light does not change. There is no cycle here, no day, no night. The temperature does not change. The dampness does not change. The only change is the movement of the ground, the drift of the buildings, the endless silent reshuffling of the grazers. The place is in constant motion and yet nothing ever seems to arrive anywhere. The turbulence is cyclical, perhaps — patterns that repeat on a scale too large for a single visit to perceive. Or perhaps it is truly chaotic, and the city you see today will be different tomorrow, different in a year, different in a century, always shifting, always settling, never settled.

---

You find a building that is taller than the others. Three meters, maybe three and a half. Its walls are intact, its doorway narrow, and inside there is a shelf carved into the stone, and on the shelf there is a row of objects — hand-tools, you think, though their shapes are strange. Stone implements, carefully shaped, worn smooth by use and by age. A chisel. A hammer. Something that might be a measuring device, two arms of stone joined at a hinge that has long since mineralized into rigidity. The tools are the same color as the walls. They have been here so long that they have begun to reunite with the architecture that produced them.

Outside, a grazer the size of a dog has settled against the building's wall. It is cleaning the stone, slowly, methodically, leaving a bright patch of exposed limestone in its wake. In another thousand years, perhaps, it will have consumed this building entirely. In another ten thousand, the tools on the shelf will be indistinguishable from the shelf itself. The city will be a memory in the stone's memory, if stone remembers. The grazers will still be here, still moving, still silent. The light will still be on.

The ground shifts under your feet, a long slow wave that travels from somewhere behind you to somewhere ahead, and the building groans — a sound so low it is felt more than heard, the first sound you have heard from anything other than yourself — and then settles. The grazer does not react. It continues its work. The city continues its work. The stone continues its work.

You are the only thing here that is temporary.
