# The Xylomatic Continuum

There is no water here. There hasn't been for longer than the word "long" can carry. What moves through these plants is not water but a dense amber alcohol — methanol-cut with terpene resins and mineral salts — that flows through xylem tubes the diameter of subway tunnels. It smells like varnish and ozone and the inside of a electrical transformer running at capacity. It smells like a forest fire that decided not to happen and instead became a permanent condition.

---

You stand on bark.

That is the first thing to understand. The ground — the ground you walk on, the ground for hundreds of meters in every direction, the ground for thousands of kilometers in every direction — is bark. Not soil with bark on it. Bark. The living outer sheath of a single organism whose body fills the accessible crust of this world from the mesosphere down to whatever serves as bedrock when the bedrock itself has been colonized and replaced, layer by layer, over geological time. The surface under your feet is a ridged, fissured hide, warm to the touch despite the cool air, pulsing faintly with the pressure of the sap moving in conduits below. It creaks when you walk. Not like wood creaking. Like a ship creaking. Like something enormous adjusting to your weight and deciding, each time, to tolerate it.

The bark is grey-brown where it is old, which is most of it. In the crevices, where the fissures run deep — some of them three, four meters down into the surface — the bark darkens to black and grows soft, almost fleshlike, and there you find the moss-equivalents: flat, scale-like organisms that are technically part of the same organism but have specialized into a kind of settled parasitism, living on the surface of the bark the way barnacles live on whales. They are not separate from the Continuum. Nothing here is separate from the Continuum. But they have their own color — a deep mineral green, almost teal — and they crackle underfoot like thin ice when you step on them, releasing a puff of desiccated spores that hang in the cool air and catch the light.

The light. The sky is not a sky in any sense that implies distance. It is a ceiling. Or rather, it is a canopy so high and so dense and so structurally complex that it has become a weather system of its own. You are at high elevation — the bark surface sits at roughly four thousand meters above the nominal sea level that no longer exists — and the canopy above you is another six to eight thousand meters higher still. Between you and it, there is air. Cool air, bright air, air that tastes of resin and metabolic exhaust. The light comes through the canopy in shafts so defined they appear solid, like pillars of pale gold dropped from a great height. They move. The canopy moves. The canopy is always moving.

## The Sound

You cannot hear yourself think. This is not a metaphor. The noise is such that the interior monologue of a human mind — which runs at a frequency and volume calibrated for quiet rooms, for conversation, for the soft predator-prey ambience of Earth — simply cannot maintain itself against the acoustic pressure. The noise is total and it comes from everywhere and it has layers.

The lowest layer is a thrum. A bass-frequency pulse, perhaps fifteen or twenty cycles per second, that you feel in your sternum and your molines before you properly hear it. This is the sap. The amber alcohol moves through the great xylem conduits in pressure waves driven by photosynthetic pumping stations kilometers above your head, and each wave passes through the bark beneath you and radiates outward through the surface like a stone dropped into a membrane. The Continuum is the membrane. The thrum is the stone. It never stops.

Above the thrum, a roar. A continuous, broadband roar, like standing behind a jet engine that has been running since the Cretaceous. This is the gas exchange. The canopy — that eight-thousand-meter-high ceiling of leaf and branch and photosynthetic machinery — breathes. Not metaphorically. The stomata of the Continuum's upper surface number in the trillions per square kilometer, and they open and close in synchronized waves driven by the planet's rotation and the temperature differential between day and night sides. When they open, they pull. The air above the canopy — thin, bright, stripped of moisture — is drawn inward through the stomatal fields with a force that generates sustained wind in the canopy zone, and that wind, channeled through the architecture of branches and buttresses and leaf-plates the size of city blocks, produces the roar. It is the sound of a continent breathing through its skin.

And above the roar, a shrieking. Higher, sharper, coming in bursts and patterns. This is the mechanical layer. The industrial layer. Because the Continuum is not merely a plant. It is a plant that has, over evolutionary timeframes so vast that erosion has become a fact of its own existence rather than something done to it, developed industry.

---

## The Machines

There are no factories. There are no buildings. There are no artifacts. The tech parameter registers at industrial levels because the Continuum's own metabolism has, over cons, scaled up to industrial output. The photosynthesis happening in the canopy above you is not the gentle green chemistry of an oak tree. It is a high-throughput, mechanized, production-scale conversion of light and atmospheric carbon into structured hydrocarbons, running at a rate that would power a small nation if nations existed here. The "engines" are the xylem and phloem networks — pressurized sap-delivery systems that move fluid with the force and volume of hydroelectric infrastructure. The "factories" are specialized growth zones, thousands of them scattered across the continental bark surface, where the Continuum produces complex molecules — resins, oils, reactive compounds — at rates and purities that match any chemical plant.

You see one of these growth zones from a distance, across a kilometer of flat bark plain. It rises from the surface like a blister: a dome of living tissue, perhaps sixty meters high and two hundred across, its surface glistening with secreted oils, venting steam — not water steam, but methanol vapor, condensed by the cool air into white plumes that stream sideways in the constant wind. The dome pulses. With each pulse, a quantity of processed resin is forced out through fissures in its crown and hardens in the air into long, black, wire-like filaments that fall outward and embed themselves in the bark surface like porcupine quills. These filaments — the Continuum produces them by the millions — are conductive. They form a planetary-scale electrical grid that routes the charge generated by the canopy's photosynthetic machinery to wherever the Continuum requires it. They are the wires. The Continuum is the power plant and the grid and the factory and the product, all in one.

The shrieking you hear is the venting. At each growth zone, at each processing node, at each junction where the pressure of the sap system exceeds the tolerance of the bark and must be released, there are stomatal exhaust ports — organic valves, meter-wide, that open and slam shut with a frequency that generates standing waves in the audible range. Some of them cycle at hundreds of hertz. The sound they make is not unlike a steam whistle, except lower, wetter, with a quality of organic distress, as if something alive were being squeezed through a nozzle. Thousands of these ports, each cycling at its own frequency determined by the local sap pressure and the elasticity of the surrounding tissue, produce a soundscape that is, in aggregate, deafening. Not loud. Deafening. The threshold at which hearing becomes pain and pain becomes simply another texture of the environment.

## The Spaces

Despite the totality of the vegetation — despite the fact that every surface is plant, every structure is plant, the air itself is plant-exhalation — the density is low. This is the paradox of the Continuum: it is everywhere, and yet there is vast empty space. Between the growth zones, between the processing nodes, between the great buttress-roots that rise from the bark surface like the walls of canyon, there is nothing. Flat bark plain. The grey-brown surface stretching to every horizon, unmarked, unbroken, pulsing faintly with the sap-thrum, lit by the shifting columns of canopy-light, and empty.

You can walk for hours and see nothing change. The bark surface maintains its texture: the same ridge-and-fissure pattern, the same teal moss-scaling in the crevices, the same scattered black filaments from the nearest growth zone, lying where they fell. The sound does not diminish or increase. It is constant because it is generated by the whole, not by any local source. You are always at the center of the noise. The light does not change. The canopy above is uniformly dense, uniformly active, uniformly bright. There is no weather in the conventional sense. There is no water to make clouds. There is only the canopy, and the canopy is its own weather.

But if you walk long enough — and the scale here is continental, so "long enough" might be days — the emptiness begins to feel deliberate. Not absent. Held open. You notice that the bark surface in these vast plains is not growing. The moss-scaling is not spreading. The filaments are not taking root. The Continuum, which is everywhere alive and which grows with a ferocity that matches its industrial output, has chosen — or has been shaped by erosion, by time, by the deep physics of its own scale — not to grow here. These plains are the Continuum's emptiness. Its silence, if it had silence. The negative space of an organism so vast that it can afford to leave most of itself fallow.

You stand in the middle of one of these plains and you feel it: the way the emptiness is held. The way it is not absence but reserve. The bark beneath your feet is alive. It pulses. It is warm. But it is resting, in the way that soil rests between seasons, except that the season is geological and the rest has lasted for millions of years and may last for millions more. The turbulence — the constant growth, collapse, and regrowth that characterizes the active zones — does not reach here. This is the deep body of the Continuum. The part beneath the storm.

## The Fauna

They are small. Everything that lives here, apart from the Continuum, is small. This is not a place that rewards size.

The most visible are the chitin-shelled grazers: flat, oval creatures, perhaps fifteen centimeters long, the color of the bark they live on, with overlapping plates along their backs and too many legs — not arthropod legs but something softer, more like roots, like fine pale fingers that grip the bark surface and pull the animal forward. They eat the moss-scaling. You see them in the crevices, in the deep fissures, clustered in groups of dozens, methodically stripping the teal organisms from the black bark and processing them through mouthparts that are not mouths so much as grinders: circular, rotating, like a borers, reducing the moss to paste which they absorb through their ventral surface. They make a sound. A fine, dry, clicking. You cannot hear it over the roar of the Continuum, but when you kneel and place your hand on the bark near a group of them, you feel the clicking through the surface, like morse code tapped into the skin of something sleeping.

There are others. In the canopy — in that high, inaccessible, shrieking, bright world above — there are fliers. You see them only as shapes. Dark shapes, small against the canopy-light, moving with a jerkiness that suggests they are riding the gas-exchange currents rather than flying under their own power. They are the Continuum's pollinators, or its predators, or both. From this distance, you cannot tell. Occasionally one falls — tumbles from the canopy, spiraling, and you track it against the light until it disappears into a fissure or strikes the bark plain with a sound you also cannot hear. The canopy does not let go of its inhabitants easily. The ones that fall are the dead, the dying, the miscalculated. They fall like fruit from a tree that is eight thousand meters tall.

In the growth zones, where the processing domes vent and shriek and extrude their black filaments, there are other fauna. Heat-tolerant organisms, red-shelled, flat, living in the glistening oil-films on the dome surfaces, feeding on the complex molecules the Continuum secretes. They are the equivalent of the bacteria that live in a hot spring or a deep-sea vent. Extremophiles. But they are not microbial. They are visible. They move in patterns — spiraling up the dome surfaces, following the channels where the oil flows thickest, clustering at the exhaust ports where the heat and the chemical output are highest. They seem to thrive on the noise. When a port vents, the blast of methanol steam that scours the dome surface does not dislodge them. They clamp down, ride it out, and resume feeding the instant the valve closes. They are adapted to industrial-scale turbulence in the same way that their world is adapted to it: completely.

## The Erosion

This place is old. Not old in the way that mountains are old. Old in the way that oceans are old — old enough to have erased the evidence of its own beginning. The Continuum has been here long enough that the bark surface has weathered into a landscape. The fissures are not cracks; they are valleys, carved by millions of years of wind and chemical erosion into the living surface. The plains are not clearings; they are plateaus, the result of a process so slow that it has become indistinguishable from stability. The growth zones are not new growth; they are features, as permanent in their way as volcanoes, cycling through their production rhythms with the patience of geological processes.

The erosion is the key to the paradox of this place. The Continuum is turbulent — hyperactive, storm-state, constantly growing and collapsing and regrowing. But it has been doing this for so long that the turbulence has become the landscape. The growth zones erupt and subside and erupt again, but they erupt in the same places, over and over, because those places are where the sap pressure is highest, and the sap pressure is highest there because the conduits beneath them have been reinforced by millions of years of growth-and-collapse cycles. The filaments fall and embed and conduct, and when they degrade, new filaments fall in the same places, because the growth zones have not moved. The noise is constant because the sources have not moved. The light is constant because the canopy has not moved. The turbulence is real — on the surface, at the growth zones, in the canopy, the activity is frenetic, chaotic, Solaris-like in its constant creation and destruction — but the turbulence has been flowing through the same channels for so long that the channels have become permanent.

This is what the erosion parameter means here. Not that the landscape is being worn away. That the landscape has been worn into. That the turbulence has carved its own shape into the living surface and the shape has become the place.

## The Atmosphere

The air is cool. The air is bright. The air is dry — so dry that your lips crack within an hour of arriving, and your eyes sting, and the moisture in your breath condenses into the cool air and is immediately absorbed by the bark surface beneath you, which is not thirsty exactly but which is chemically active enough to strip water vapor from the air the way a sponge strips liquid from a surface. The Continuum does not use water. But it does not refuse water. What falls on it, it takes. What it takes, it breaks down and uses. The water is gone from this world not because the Continuum rejected it but because the Continuum consumed it, millions of years ago, and converted it into something else — structural hydrocarbons, sap-alcohol, metabolic byproducts — and the cycle closed, and the water never came back, and the Continuum learned to do without it and then forgot that it had ever needed it.

You breathe the air and it tastes of terpene. Of varnish. Of ozone. Of the electrical discharge that crackles along the filament-grid when the canopy's photosynthesis peaks at midday and the surplus charge seeks ground through ten thousand kilometers of black wire. The air vibrates. Not from the sound — the sound is so constant that you stop perceiving it as vibration and start perceiving it as a condition of the air itself, a standing wave in which every molecule is slightly more agitated than it would be in a quieter place. The air is warm where it rises from the growth zones, cool everywhere else. The air moves, constantly, drawn by the canopy's respiration, but it moves in patterns so large that from the ground it feels like stillness. You are inside a lung. The lung is breathing. You are too small to feel the breath.

---

## What This Place Is

It is a world that has become a single organism. It is an organism that has become an industry. It is an industry that has become a landscape. It is a landscape that has been here so long that it has eroded into permanence, and the permanence is indistinguishable from turbulence, and the turbulence is indistinguishable from silence, and the silence is deafening.

You stand on the bark. The bark pulses. The filaments crackle. The canopy roars. The light moves in pillars. The grazers click in the fissures. The growth zones shriek and vent and produce. The air tastes of varnish. The emptiness holds you.

This is the Xylomatic Continuum. It does not know you are here. It does not need to know. It has been here for longer than the concept of "here" has meant anything, and it will be here for longer than the concept will mean anything again. It is the plant and the machine and the ground and the sky and the sound and the silence, and it is all of these things at once because it has had time — time beyond reckoning — to become all of them, and to settle, finally, into the shape of its own storm.
