# The Still Becoming

The first thing is the light. Or rather, the light's refusal.

It comes down through sixty meters of warm ocean — not crashing, not dramatic, just thinning. Cyan into teal into something between blue and memory of blue, until it reaches the plateau and arrives changed: soft, diffuse, as though it has forgotten where it came from. The sun is a rumor here. You look up and you see it, or its idea — a pale disc, featureless, like a coin seen through cathedral glass. It does not cast shadows. It merely makes darkness less dark.

Below that filtered non-light, the plateau spreads.

---

## The Tableland

It is called, where it is called anything, a tableland — though the name belongs to the people who live on its surface and implies a flatness that isn't quite right. The plateau is high, is the point. It rises from the deep floor of this nameless sea like the back of a sleeping animal, two and a half kilometers of vertical ascent from the abyssal plain below, and it nearly breaches. Nearly. Its summit still lies under that sixty meters of warm water, which means that from the surface — from a boat, from the air — there is nothing here. Open sea. A slight discoloration, perhaps, if you know what you're looking for. A murkiness where the water becomes greener, denser with particulate, the breath of the living substrate below.

From beneath, swimming down toward it, the plateau announces itself gradually. First the water warms. Not dramatically — a degree, perhaps two, over a hundred meters of descent. Then the color shifts. The pale blue-white of surface water gives way to something richer, and the water carries a taste: mineral, faintly sulfuric, like old coins held in the hand. Then — and this is the moment, the one that changes everything — the seafloor appears below, and it is wrong.

It should be dark sediment, flat and dead, the usual patient accretion of marine snow over millennia. Instead, the ground *breathes*.

---

## Living Geology

The plateau is still forming. Not in the way that all land is technically still forming, eroding one grain at a time — this is active, visible, ongoing. Volcanic vents pulse along fissures that run across the plateau's surface like the lines on a palm, and from those fissures, new rock emerges. It comes out soft.

That is the strangeness, and it takes time to understand what you're seeing. The rock, when it first extrudes from the vents, is not rock in any familiar sense. It is more like a dense, warm clay — deep red-brown, iron-rich, pliable to the touch. You can press your thumb into it and leave a print. You can, if you are patient, shape it. It cools slowly in the warm water — the temperature differential is small, here, which is part of why it stays soft so long — and over the course of weeks, sometimes months, it hardens into something like basalt. Darker, denser, with a glassy fracture when it breaks.

But in those first weeks, while it is still becoming itself, it is workable.

This is the foundational fact of everything that follows. The plateau is a construction site. It has been a construction site for longer than anyone can remember, and it will remain one for longer than anyone can foresee. The earth is still being made here, and the making is slow enough to live with, fast enough to matter.

The vents pulse irregularly but constantly. A low vent might produce a few cubic meters of soft stone in a year. A major vent, during an active phase, might extrude that much in a week. The fissures shift over time — old vents quiet, new ones open, the pattern of emergence reconfiguring the plateau in slow motion, like a face aging. Over decades, the terrain changes. Hills appear where there were none. Channels fill. The ground rises, sometimes unevenly, and what was flat becomes sloped, and what was sloped becomes terraced by the accumulated layers of successive flows.

Erosion, in the traditional sense, barely exists here. The water moves slowly over the plateau — the thermocline above creates a still layer, a pocket of calm water that barely mixes with the surface above. Currents are weak, diffuse, more suggestion than force. What shapes the plateau is not erosion but accretion, not wearing-down but building-up, and the building is continuous.

The plateau is, in a literal and ongoing sense, under construction. The geology is embryonic. It has not yet decided what it wants to be.

## The Gardens

And into this warm, dim, mineral-rich water, the vegetation has come.

It is not kelp. It is not seaweed. It is not any form of marine flora that belongs to any known taxonomy, though it shares qualities with several. The closest analogy, if one were needed, is to terrestrial grasses — but that comparison fails almost immediately, because these plants do not root in sediment. They root in the soft stone itself.

They are called, by the people who tend them, *thara* — a word that means, roughly, *the thing that grows where the stone is still soft* — and they are the second foundational fact of this place.

*Thara* is a photosynthetic organism, or near enough. In the dim light that reaches the plateau, it grows broad, flat fronds — not blades, not leaves, but something between: wide, thin, translucent sheets of tissue that spread horizontally to maximize their catch of the attenuated light. The fronds are dark green at the center, where they attach to the stalk, and fade to a pale yellow-green at the edges, almost white, and in the dim water they catch what light there is and hold it, so that a mature *thara* stand glows faintly, a cold phosphorescence that is not bioluminescence but simply the concentrating of scarce photons. Walking through a dense garden at midday is like walking through a room lit by green stained glass.

The stalks are the useful part. They grow thick — a mature stalk can be the width of a human thigh — and they are composed of a dense, fibrous material that hardens over time, becoming something like wood. Not quite wood: denser, more flexible, with a waxy outer surface that resists water. The roots, though, are the true marvel. They penetrate the soft stone while it is still pliable, threading through it, and as the stone hardens around them, they become part of it — literally integrated into the rock, running through it like rebar through concrete. The roots do not break the stone when the stone hardens. They become *inclusions* in it, strengthening it, giving it a fibrous toughness that pure basalt would lack.

This is the symbiosis that underlies everything. The *thara* roots the soft stone and holds it together while it hardens. The stone, in return, gives the *thara* a mineral substrate rich in iron and silicon and warmth, the volcanic nutrients that let it grow thick and tall in water where light is barely sufficient. The plants shape the geology; the geology shapes the plants. They are co-emergent.

A dense garden is a dense thing. The stalks grow close — within arm's reach of each other, sometimes closer — and the fronds overlap overhead like a canopy, so that swimming through a mature stand is like moving through a forest with a low, tangled ceiling. The water between the stalks is still, sheltered from even the weak currents above, and it is warmer here, trapped by the canopy, than in the open. Sediment — mineral particulate from the vents, organic debris from the plants — settles slowly between the stalks, building a soft layer on the stone, and in this layer smaller organisms grow: microbial mats, thin filaments of something like algae, a slow ecology of decomposers that break down fallen fronds and return their nutrients to the water.

But there is not much else alive here. The fauna is sparse — a few small fish, pale and slow, that shelter in the canopy; some sessile filter-feeders on the harder stone, transparent tubes that catch particulate from the water; occasionally something larger passing through above, a shadow against the dim light, gone before it can be identified. The plateau's ecosystem is young, like its geology. It has not yet been colonized. The *thara* is the pioneer, the first thick presence, and it has the place nearly to itself.

## The Settlement

The town is built where the gardens are densest.

This requires some explanation, because it is not obvious why one would build a settlement in a thick underwater forest on a geologically active plateau in dim water far from any surface. The answer, like everything here, is about the soft stone.

The people — there are perhaps four hundred of them, maybe more, spread across several clusters of construction — have built their settlement by a method that is unique to this place and that would be impossible anywhere else on Earth, if this is Earth. They build with the stone while it is still soft, and they build *into* the *thara* stands, using the plants' root systems as living infrastructure.

Here is how it works:

When a new vent opens and begins extruding soft stone, the *thara* gardeners — there are people whose entire role is this, who are called *soft-ground tenders* — guide the flow. They shape the emerging clay with their hands and with simple tools: flat stones for smoothing, pointed sticks for cutting channels, woven-fiber screens for directing the flow of the thicker material. They build walls this way, low and curved, following the natural fissures. They build channels for water flow, basins for catching sediment, platforms for drying and hardening. And they build the foundations of dwellings — not the dwellings themselves, but the stone bases, the floors, the lower walls, the parts that will harden and last.

But they build these foundations *around the thara stalks*. They leave gaps, channels, openings in the soft stone where the stalks and their roots can pass through. As the stone hardens, the *thara* becomes part of the structure — the stalks are living pillars, the roots are living rebar, the canopy above is a living roof. The dwellings that result are not built *from* the plants. They are built *with* them, *through* them, *among* them.

A dwelling is a low stone chamber, its walls curving and organic — because they were shaped by hand from soft clay, not cut from hard stone — with *thara* stalks rising through gaps left for them, their roots threading through the walls and floor, their fronds spreading overhead. The chamber is open to the water; it is not sealed. It is not a dry space in a wet place. It is a *shaped* space, a space where the current is slowed, where the water is a little warmer, where the light is filtered twice — once by the ocean above, once by the canopy — into something very quiet and very green.

The people do not breathe water. This is the other fact that needs explaining, and it is the one that is hardest to convey because it is the one that makes this place what it is, the strangeness at the center.

## The Air

The vents do not only extrude stone. They also release gas.

Not all of them, and not all the time, but many of the fissures produce, along with the soft rock, bubbles of volcanic gas — mostly carbon dioxide, but with other components: sulfur compounds, nitrogen, trace hydrogen, and — critically — pockets of gas that are rich enough in oxygen to be breathable. Not clean air. Not surface air. Thin, mineral-heavy, tasting of iron and sulfur and warmth, like breathing inside a furnace that has been cooled just enough to survive. But breathable, for short periods. For longer periods with practice and with the adaptations that these people have developed over generations.

The gas is captured. This is the third foundational technology of the settlement, after stone-shaping and *thara* cultivation, and it is the one that everything else depends on. The people build capture chambers over the active gas vents — domes of shaped stone, held in place by *thara* roots threading through their edges, sealed with fiber-and-clay mixtures that are not watertight but slow the exchange of water and gas enough to maintain a pocket of air within the dome. These air pockets are small — a few cubic meters at most — and they are not pure. They are warm, wet, mineral-thick. But they are air, and the people use them.

They take breaths. This is how they live underwater: they take breaths from the capture chambers and hold them while they work in the water. A trained adult can hold a breath for four or five minutes of moderate activity, longer if they are resting. The work of the settlement — shaping stone, tending gardens, building, maintaining — is organized around this limitation. Everything is slow. Everything is paced to the rhythm of breath: work, return, breathe, work again.

The capture chambers are clustered, and around the densest clusters, the settlement is densest. This is the urban pattern: air sources determine density. Near a productive vent, the chambers are close together, and the dwellings are close together, and the population is close together, and the place feels — if not crowded, then *full*. There are many presences in a small volume. The chambers, the dwellings, the *thara* stalks, the people, the tool-caches, the fiber-weaving stations, the stone-shaping yards where the soft clay is worked before it hardens — all of it layered, near-touching, a dense and interpenetrating arrangement of stone and plant and person.

Further from the vents, the settlement thins. Outlying dwellings, single chambers built around isolated gas sources. Gardens that are less tended, more feral. The terrain is more raw here, the stone newer, the *thara* younger. The plateau's edge is visible in the dim water — a drop-off, a precipice, beyond which the plateau descends into the dark, and the water cools, and the light dies, and nothing grows. The edge is not a boundary the people cross. Below is the deep, and the deep is not their place.

## The Tools

The technology is primitive, which is to say it is immediate, and local, and made from what is at hand.

Stone tools are the primary implements. Hard basalt — old stone, stone that has fully cooled — is fractured and shaped into flat blades, points, scrapers. These are not sophisticated. They are not worked with precision; they are made quickly, used hard, and discarded. The raw material is everywhere — the plateau is made of it — and the effort of making a tool is less than the effort of maintaining one. The result is a lithic culture of abundance: tools are scattered across the settlement like leaves in a forest, used where they are found, left where they are dropped.

Fiber tools are the second category. *Thara* fiber — stripped from the stalks, dried, twisted into cordage — is the universal binding material. It is used for everything: lashing stone tools to handles, weaving screens for directing stone flow, making the nets and baskets that carry particulate and sediment, constructing the lightweight frames that support fresh stone while it hardens. The fiber is strong when new, weak when waterlogged, and is replaced constantly. The settlement generates a continuous rain of discarded fiber, which settles to the ground and becomes part of the sediment layer, which becomes part of the stone, which becomes part of the place. Nothing leaves. Everything is incorporated.

Wood — or the *thara*-wood of the mature stalks — is used for structural elements. The hardened stalks are cut with stone saws (a laborious process) and shaped into beams, posts, and frames. But the cutting kills the stalk, and the stalk's root system begins to decay within the stone, weakening the structure it was part of. So *thara*-wood is used sparingly, and only from stalks that have already died, and the living stalks are left intact whenever possible. This creates a strong cultural preference: the people build *around* living things rather than *from* them. The settlement is shaped by what lives in it.

There is no metal. There is no fired clay (the stone is shaped, not fired). There is no glass, no wheel, no written language. There is fiber and stone and the living plant and the soft emerging rock, and from these four things, the people have built a town.

## The Quiet

The sounds of the plateau are few and slow.

The dominant sound is the vents. Not a roar — the gas emerges under low pressure, more a seep than a jet — but a continuous low murmur, a bubbling, a sound like something breathing in its sleep. In the capture chambers, the gas makes a higher sound, a thin hiss, barely audible. The vents are always going. They never stop. The murmur is the baseline of all sound here, the ground tone, and after a time you stop hearing it, the way you stop hearing your own pulse.

Above the vents: the *thara*. The fronds move in the slow currents, and they make a sound — not the rustling of terrestrial leaves, which is dry and sharp, but something softer, wetter, a brushing sound like fabric underwater. When the current shifts — which it does slowly, over hours, responding to temperature changes far above — the fronds shift with it, and the sound changes, a slow modulation, like a chord changing in a piece of music that takes a day to play.

The people are quiet. They communicate with gesture, mostly — a system of hand and arm signals developed in an environment where speech is impossible (you cannot speak underwater, and in the capture chambers, speech is wasted breath). The gestures are efficient, dense, capable of conveying complex information, but they are silent. When the people work, the only sounds are the scrape of stone on stone, the slap of hands shaping clay, the occasional click of a tool against a *thara* stalk. These sounds are small, localized, swallowed quickly by the water.

Occasionally, something larger: a section of stone, cooling and contracting, cracks. The sound is a sharp, low *thock* that travels through the water and through the stone itself, felt in the body before it is heard. When this happens, people pause, listen, assess. A crack can mean a new fissure opening. A crack can mean a vent shifting. A crack can mean a structure failing. The sounds of the plateau are information, and the people read them the way a sailor reads wind.

Mostly, though: quiet. The deep, patient quiet of a place where nothing is in a hurry.

## The Slow

Time moves differently here. Or rather, time moves the same way it always does, but the things that fill it move slowly, and the slowness compounds, and the experience of being here is the experience of inhabiting a pace that is not human-normal but is place-normal.

A dwelling takes months to build. The stone must emerge, be shaped, harden. The *thara* must grow into the structure, its roots threading through the soft clay, establishing the living connections that will hold the walls together when the stone sets. The capture chamber must be built, sealed, tested, adjusted. The gas flow must be stabilized — too much and the chamber vents uselessly, too little and the air becomes unbreathable. All of this happens in a timescale of seasons, not days.

A garden takes years. The *thara* grows slowly — a mature stalk takes five to seven years to reach useful thickness, and a stand that can support a dwelling takes a generation to establish. The gardeners plant, tend, wait. They guide the roots toward emerging stone, directing the symbiosis, encouraging the plant to grow where the next structure will be built. They think in decades. They plan for buildings they will not live to see completed, planting *thara* now for dwellings that their children, or their children's children, will shape the stone for.

The settlement changes over decades. New vents open, and new clusters of construction follow them. Old vents quiet, and the clusters around them are abandoned — not demolished, just left, the stone slowly hardening, the *thara* slowly dying without the warmth of the active vent below, the dwellings becoming empty shells that slowly fill with sediment and are incorporated into the plateau's surface. The town moves. Over a lifetime, the center of the settlement shifts by hundreds of meters, following the moving geology. Over generations, it migrates across the plateau in a slow drift, leaving behind a trail of old foundations, dead gardens, empty chambers — a record of habitation written in stone, legible to anyone who knows how to read it.

The people are born here, live here, die here. They do not leave the plateau. They do not know what lies beyond it, in the deep water below or the open ocean above. Their world is this high, warm, dim, slow place — the stone that is still becoming, the plants that root the becoming, the air that seeps from the earth, the town that builds itself in cooperation with the forces that make the ground. They do not think of themselves as primitive. They think of themselves as patient. They think of themselves as the people who live where the world is still being made, and who help make it, and who are made by it in return.

## What It Looks Like

If you could swim here — and you cannot, not really, not without the adaptations and the generations and the patient accretion of knowledge that these people carry — if you could, you would see this:

A high place, under deep water, under dim light. The ground is warm stone, dark and rough, fissured with lines of dull red where the vents pulse. The stone is soft in places, hard in others, and the boundary between soft and hard shifts over time, so that the ground has a quality of incompletion, of process, of not-yet-finished. It is not a landscape of features. It is a landscape of *becoming*.

Across the stone, dense stands of *thara*: tall, thick-stalked, their broad fronds spreading overhead in overlapping canopies of pale green. The canopies filter the dim light into something softer, greener, more private. Between the stalks, the water is still and warm and tastes of iron.

Among the stalks, the settlement: low stone chambers, curved-walled, organic, shaped by hand. They are not uniform. Each is shaped to its site, to the *thara* that grows through it, to the vent that feeds it, to the person who built it. They cluster where the gas is densest, and the clustering gives the settlement a center — a dense, layered, many-structured core, with outlying dwellings thinning toward the edges. In the center, the capture chambers: stone domes, modest, functional, from which small bubbles of gas occasionally escape and rise, slowly, toward a surface they will never reach as bubbles, dissolving into the warm water on the way.

The people move between the chambers and the dwellings and the gardens. They are dark-skinned, dark-eyed, lean, adapted to the cold that isn't cold here (the water is warm, but it is water, and it pulls heat from the body faster than air). They carry stone tools and fiber baskets. They move with the unhurried deliberation of people who are working on a breath they must make last. Their hands are always moving — signaling, shaping, touching the stone to test its hardness, feeling the *thara* stalks to gauge their health. They are tactile people. They know their world through contact.

The light is always dim. It does not change much between day and night — sixty meters of water smooths the diurnal cycle into a gentle modulation, the dimness deepening slightly and then lightening slightly, a breathing of light rather than a switch. The people do not mark days. They mark the growth of stone, the hardening of *thara*, the shifting of vents, the slow pulse of the plateau. Their calendar is geological.

At night — which is to say, at the dimmest — the settlement is almost invisible. The stone is dark. The water is dark. The *thara* fronds catch the last of the light and hold it as a faint green glow, and in this glow the people move, dark shapes in green light, silent, patient, attending to the slow work of living where the world is still being made.

The vents murmur.

The *thara* breathes.

The stone grows.

The people tend what grows, and what grows tends them, and the place continues becoming itself, slowly, slowly, in the warm dark under the quiet water.
