# The Reliquary of Falling Water

There is a place inside the mountain that the mountain is still deciding about.

You reach it the way water reaches everything — by falling. A fissure in the granite high on the eastern flank of the range, narrow enough that you enter sideways, and then a drop that is not a cliff so much as a throat. Water has been pouring through this throat for longer than anything can remember, and the water has made a room.

Not a cave. A room. The distinction matters. Caves are accidental. This place is being *built*, though by what and for what, no one who has entered it can say. The rock is still warm from its own becoming. You can feel it under your palms when you press them against the walls — a low, atrial heat, like the inside of a closed fist. The stone is basalt that hasn't finished being basalt. It is dark, vitreous, and in places it has the texture of cooled caramel, still slightly yielding, still remembering when it was something else. Columns of it descend from the ceiling like the wet teeth of some enormous and patient mouth, and between them, water falls.

All of it is water. This is the first and last fact of the place. Water occupies nearly every volume the room contains — falling in sheets from the fissures above, pooling in basins that the rock has only just now decided to make, rising in clouds of steam from the floor where the falling water meets the still-forming stone. You are submerged in it as much as you stand in it. There is no dry place. There is no dry *concept*. The air itself is so saturated that breathing is an act of drinking, and the water on your skin is indistinguishable from the water in your lungs, and all of it — falling, standing, breathing, drinking — is the same event viewed from different angles.

The light should not be here. That is the second fact. The chamber is buried deep enough inside the mountain that no sun should reach it, and yet light fills the place with the ordinary persistence of a Tuesday afternoon. It comes from no source. It has no direction. It is simply present, flat and white and faintly blue, the way light is present behind closed eyelids — ambient, sourceless, as if the stone itself is remembering what light looked like and projecting that memory into the room. Under this light, the falling water becomes visible in a way that falling water almost never is. You can see the individual threads of it. You can see where threads merge and braid and unmake each other. You can see the turbulence patterns that form in the basins — spirals and counter-spirals, fractals repeating at smaller and smaller scales until the water becomes foam and the foam becomes something finer than foam, a kind of wet smoke that moves according to rules you can almost read but not quite.

The room is not large. You could lie down in it, and your feet would touch one wall while your hair brushed the other. In every direction, the space is intimate, almost confessional, the kind of room that makes your own body feel like an intrusion. And yet within this smallness, the emptiness is vast. There is almost nothing here. The water does not count as a thing; it is the medium. The stone is still becoming, not yet a thing. Between these two non-things, the space yawns hollow. Your presence fills it more than any object does. You are the largest fact in the room.

Almost the largest.

There are structures. Three of them, maybe four — it is hard to count because they are unfinished, and an unfinished structure can be mistaken for geology or vice versa. They are small. The tallest comes to your hip. They are built from the same dark stone that the room is made of, which means they look like the room grew them, which may be what happened. They are rough, hand-shaped, primitive in the way that first attempts are primitive — not crude, but *uncommitted*, as if whoever made them was still deciding whether to continue. One has the suggestion of a doorway. One has a flat top, like a table or an altar. One is simply a column of stacked stones, held together by nothing but the water's permission, which is to say barely held together at all. Whoever built them worked with their hands. You can see finger-marks in the still-soft stone, pressed there the way a child presses marks into wet clay, and the stone kept them. The stone is keeping everything here. The water erases nothing. It only carries it around.

There is life. Barely. A pale filament — you might call it moss if you needed a word — clings to the ceiling where the water falls thinnest. It is not green. It is the color of the light, which is the color of nothing in particular. It does not photosynthesize; there is no sun to synthesize. It eats the stone, or the water, or the sound. There are things in the basins, too — small, translucent, nearly invisible, like the embryos of something that will eventually become shrimp or will eventually become nothing. They drift in the turbulence and do not seem to have opinions about it. They are rare enough that when you see one, you feel as if you have been shown something, and when you lose it in the foam, you feel as if you have failed a test.

The sound.

The sound is the room's true inhabitant. Everything else — the water, the stone, the structures, the pale filaments — is secondary. The sound is what lives here. It is not one sound. It is the accumulated noise of every drop of water hitting every surface simultaneously, reverberating through stone that has not yet hardened into a shape that would dampen the noise, bouncing off walls that are still forming and therefore still ringing, the way a bell rings because the metal is still remembering being struck. The sound is physical. You feel it in your sternum before you hear it with your ears. It fills the small room until the room cannot contain it and the sound pushes outward into the rock, and the rock, still soft, absorbs it, and carries it deeper into the mountain, and the mountain shakes with it, and this shaking may be what is making the room — the vibration, the constant percussion of falling water against becoming-stone, carving the space not by erosion but by *insistence*, by repetition, by the patient assertion that this room should exist, spoken in a language so loud it has become a physical force.

The sound changes. Not in volume — it is always at the threshold of pain, always at the edge of what your ears can metabolize — but in texture. It shifts. There are frequencies that rise and fall like tides. There are moments when the sound deepens below the range of hearing and you feel it only in your teeth, in the fluid of your eyes, and then it rises again into a shriek that is not a shriek but a chord, a chord made of ten thousand impacts of water on stone, harmonics stacking on harmonics until the air itself seems to vibrate visibly and the falling threads of water shimmer and blur.

Nothing here is still. The water falls. The stone forms and reforms — you can watch it happening, a ridge rising over the course of minutes like a slow breath, a basin deepening like a thought, a column extending itself downward by fractions, reaching toward the floor with the blind patience of geology. The structures shift. The stacked column of stones falls, rebuilt, falls again — or maybe it is not rebuilt, maybe it simply reassembles itself, the stones tumbling in the turbulence and settling back into their original configuration, a pattern that the water remembers and enforces. The foam patterns in the basins dissolve and reconstitute continuously. The pale filament on the ceiling extends a tendril, withdraws it, extends it elsewhere. The transparent creatures in the basins drift and tumble and do not die but also do not settle, as if they too are still being decided.

This is a place that is under construction. Not by hands, not by water, not by geology — or by all of those, which may be the same thing. It is being made by sound. By the accumulated statement of falling water, repeated so often and so loudly that the mountain has begun to agree with it, has begun to hollow itself out in assent, to make the room that the water has always been describing. The structures are what happens when the sound reaches a human who has been caught in it long enough to want to participate — to add a small, stammering stone voice to the chorus. The filament is what happens when the sound reaches something that was not alive before it heard the sound. The creatures are what happens when the sound reaches the water itself.

You will not stay long. No one does. The sound makes it impossible to think in straight lines, and the room is too small to pace in, and the water is too pervasive to stay dry in, and the stone is too new to trust. But when you leave — climbing back up through the throat of the fissure, pressing your body sideways through the granite, the sound dimming behind you like a dream you had once about a place that was still deciding whether to exist — you will carry the vibration in your bones for days. You will feel it in quiet moments. In the hum of a refrigerator. In the silence after rain. You will press your hand to a wall and feel it there, faintly, the mountain still singing to itself in a language it learned from water, still making the room, still deciding.
