# The Terraces of Constant Sound

The plateau sits above the reach of anything green. Nothing grows here. Nothing has ever grown here — or if it once did, it left no trace, no fossil imprint pressed into the mineral floor, no ghost of root or stem preserved in stone. This is a world before or after biology, a place that belongs to water and mineral alone, and it has been this way long enough that the concept of change has worn smooth and become meaningless.

---

You reach it by climbing, though the approach matters less than the arrival. The land rises in long, folded steps — not stairs, not terraces carved by intention, but the natural architecture of a mineral deposit laid down over spans of time that make the word "ancient" feel frivolous. The formations are dense. Packed. There is no empty space here, no gap between features, no resting place for the eye. Every square meter of surface is occupied by something: a rim of calcite, a pool of trapped water, a ridge of deposited mineral curving like a frozen wave, a channel cut through stone by centuries of flow. The density is almost claustrophobic despite the open sky, because the ground itself offers no vacancy. It is a landscape with no margins.

And it is bright. The light here is not gentle. At this elevation the atmosphere is thin enough that the sun arrives with an edge to it, a sharpness that makes shadows crisp and surfaces vivid. But the real brightness comes from the ground. Every surface is wet — not damp, not occasionally moistened, but perpetually, constantly wet — and the water catches light and throws it in every direction. The mineral deposits are pale, cream and ivory and a faint ochre where iron has stained the stone, and the water film over them turns the whole plateau into a single vast reflector. On clear days the glare is nearly blinding. You squint and the landscape still burns. The pools act as mirrors, the thin sheets of flowing water act as lenses, and the mist — there is always mist, condensing out of the cool air as it rises and meets the colder layer above — scatters light into a diffuse glow that fills the air itself. The sky and the ground seem to trade places. You walk on brightness.

The coolness is a relief after the climb but a surprise after the light. The air sits at a temperature that makes you aware of your own warmth — not cold enough to threaten, but cool enough that your skin registers the difference constantly, a gentle insistence that you are a heat source in a place that is not. The water is colder than the air. When you touch a pool — and you will, because the temptation is irresistible and there is nothing to stop you — the shock travels up your forearm and makes you gasp. The mineral water carries a mineral taste, faintly alkaline, faintly metallic, the flavor of the stone it has been passing through.

---

And the sound.

The sound is the defining fact of this place, and nothing prepares you for it. You hear it from the climb, of course — a growing roar that you mistake for wind, for a waterfall, for a river in flood. But when you reach the plateau and stand in it, you realize it is none of those things. It is everything at once.

The water here does not flow in one direction. It does not gather into a single stream or pour over one edge. Instead, it moves across the entire plateau in a thousand paths simultaneously, and each path produces its own sound, and the sounds layer on top of each other until they become a single thing — a wall of sound, a physical presence in the air, a vibration you feel in your sternum before your ears make sense of it. There are sheets of water sliding over smooth mineral ramps that produce a low, sustained hush like the inside of a shell. There are narrow channels where the flow accelerates and the pitch rises to a bright, hard hiss. There are drops — small ones, a few centimeters, water falling from one terrace level to the next into a pool — and each drop makes its own plink, its own note, but there are thousands of them, tens of thousands, and they merge into a continuous crackle like rain on a metal roof amplified past any scale you've experienced. There are pools where water rises from below, bubbling up through fissures in the mineral floor, and these make a low, resonant blup-blup-blup that you feel more than hear. And there are places where the water is so thin — a film only millimeters deep, spreading across a flat expanse of wet stone — that it makes almost no sound at all, just a whisper, and these quiet patches are somehow worse, because the silence within them is relative, not absolute, and the surrounding roar presses in from all sides like pressure changes in an airplane cabin.

The cumulative effect is deafening. Not metaphorically. Your ears ring after an hour. Conversation is impossible even at a shout. You learn to communicate by gesture, by pointing, by the expressions on your face. The sound becomes a medium you inhabit, like being underwater, and after a while your mind stops trying to parse it into components and simply accepts it as the ambient condition of existence. This is what the world sounds like. This is what it has always sounded like.

Because it does not change.

---

That is the strangest thing, and it takes time to notice it because the sound is so overwhelming that it masks the absence of variation. The water flows. The pools fill. The channels carry their load. And none of it shifts. The flow rates do not increase or decrease. The pools do not rise or fall. The channels do not migrate. The mist forms at the same altitude every morning and dissipates at the same hour every afternoon, and if you come back tomorrow it will be identical, and if you come back in a year it will be identical, and if you could come back in a thousand years — you suspect, standing there — it would still be identical. The system is in equilibrium. The water condenses from the atmosphere at exactly the rate it evaporates. The mineral deposits grow at exactly the rate they erode. The flow paths are established, settled into their courses the way a river settles into its deepest channel, and there is no flood to reshape them, no drought to dry them, no freeze to stop them, no earthquake to redirect them. The plateau exists in a state of dynamic equilibrium so precise and so stable that the word "dynamic" feels misleading. It is a frozen process. A river caught mid-pour. A rain that never started and never stops.

The erosion parameter — 1.5, established, in-balance — is visible in the smoothness of the stone. Everything is worn but not wearing away. The edges are rounded, the channels are polished, the rims of the pools have the softness of things that have been touched by water for so long that sharpness itself has been dissolved. But the erosion is not progressing. It has reached its resting state. The water moves but does not cut. The stone dissolves but re-deposits at the same rate. The landscape is not aging. It is not young. It simply is.

---

Life, here, is an afterthought. A rumor. You see no animals for the first several hours, and you begin to believe the place is truly empty — that the absence of vegetation extends to an absence of everything alive, that this is a mineral kingdom with no subjects but stone and water. Then, in the late afternoon, when the light shifts and the mist thins, you see something move.

It is small. A pale shape against the pale stone, difficult to distinguish from the mineral formations until it moves again. A crustacean of some kind — or something crustacean-like, something adapted to this specific chemistry, this specific temperature, this specific absence of shelter. It lives in the pools, you realize, in the mineral-saturated water, and it is the color of the stone because there is no reason to be any other color, no predator to hide from, no mate to display for. It moves slowly across the bottom of a shallow pool, grazing on — what? You look closer. There is a film on the submerged stone, a thin biological layer, barely visible, a community of microorganisms so sparse and so simple that the word "ecology" feels too grand. But it is there. Life persists at the margins, in the thinnest possible sense, in the interstices between mineral and water. The crustacean — if that is what it is — feeds on it. And something else, you notice, feeds on the crustacean: a bird, circling high above, a raptor of some kind, riding the thermals that rise from the warm stone — but the stone is not warm, the air is cool, and the bird seems to be there from habit or from necessity, scanning the pools for movement, finding it rarely. The fauna are rare here. Rare and incidental. They are not part of this landscape. They are visitors who have learned to extract a living from its edges, the way a beggar extracts coins from the margins of a crowd.

You see the crustacean twice more, in different pools. You see the bird once more, diving, missing, climbing again. You see nothing else alive.

---

The scale of the place becomes apparent only when you walk. The plateau extends past the immediate, past the first cluster of terraces and pools, past the first ridge of deposited mineral, into a broader landscape of the same character — more terraces, more pools, more channels, more sound. You walk for an hour and the terrain does not change in kind, only in detail. The formations vary — here a broad flat expanse of shallow water, there a steep cascade of small steps, there a field of rounded mounds each crowned with a pool — but the essence is the same. Wet mineral. Bright surfaces. Roaring silence. No plants. No structures. No evidence that any human or human-like intelligence has ever been here or wanted to be. The place is fully occupied by its own processes, saturated with its own logic, and there is no room for anything else. It does not need you. It does not notice you. You are a temporary pattern of warmth moving across a surface that has been here for longer than your species has been making patterns.

The sound does not diminish as you move. It does not increase. It is the same everywhere, because the water is the same everywhere, and the water has been the same everywhere for longer than the stone can remember. You walk across a landscape that is, in every meaningful sense, timeless. It will be this way when you leave. It was this way before you came. The water will flow and the stone will hold and the light will reflect and the sound will fill the air, and none of it will know that you were here, and none of it will care.

That is the final fact of this place. Not its beauty, though it is beautiful — the pale terraces, the bright water, the mist catching gold in the late light. Not its strangeness, though it is strange — a world without plants, a landscape without silence, a river without banks. But its indifference. Its complete, settled, equilibrium indifference to anything that is not itself. You stand on the plateau in the deafening roar and the blinding light and the cool mist, and you feel what it feels like to be irrelevant to a place that does not know it is being visited.

You turn back. The sound follows you down the mountain. It follows you for a long time. Then it fades, and the world becomes quiet, and you are not sure the plateau was real.
