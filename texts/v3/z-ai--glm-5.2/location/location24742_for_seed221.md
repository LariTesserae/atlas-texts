# The Humming Pocket

You enter through a hatch that was already open. You did not open it. No one opens it. It is open the way a mouth is open — by design, permanently, the hinge long since fused into its fullest arc by a crust of mineral deposit that has grown *over* the mechanism rather than corroding it. This is not decay. This is completion.

---

The space beyond is not a room, exactly. It is more accurate to call it a *cassette* — a modular pocket perhaps four meters across at its widest, two and a half at its tallest, wedged between what you must take on faith are larger structures. You cannot see the larger structures. You can only feel them through the vibration in the walls, which is constant, which has always been constant, which is so constant that you begin to understand it is not vibration at all but a property of matter in this place — the way steel hums at sixty hertz because it was born humming and has never been told to stop.

Everything here is made. There is no floor that is not poured. There is no wall that is not milled. The ceiling — low enough to touch if you stand on the bundled conduit that runs the perimeter — is a stamped lattice, some kind of ventilation panel from a fabrication run you cannot identify because the part numbers are in a typeface that looks almost familiar, almost legible, but resolves into nothing when you try to read it. The walls are riveted sheet metal over what feels like concrete, but the concrete is too smooth, too uniform, and when you press your palm flat against it you realize it has no aggregate. No gravel. No sand. Just a grey matrix that was mixed and cured and set against the back of the steel and has been sitting there, uncracking, unmoving, since whatever event constituted its beginning.

This place is new. Not new to you. New *to itself.* There is no wear. The rivets are bright. The metal has no patina except for the water, which is everywhere — beaded on every surface, sheeting down the walls in thin slow films that do not speed up or slow down, pooling in the seams between panels, collecting in the shallow depression where two conduit bundles cross. The air is saturated. You can see it — not as fog, not as mist, but as a visible density, a greyish thickening that makes the far wall seem slightly farther than the near one. Water condenses on your eyelashes. Water condenses on the inside of your lungs. The atmosphere is at or very near its dew point and has been there, exactly there, for a duration that the word "long" does not describe because duration here is not experienced as passage. It is experienced as *state.*

The temperature is just above the point where breath would fog — or perhaps just below; it is hard to tell because the air is so wet that your breath has nothing left to give. Cold, but a wet cold, a cold that comes from the water having absorbed whatever heat the metal once held and redistributed it so evenly that no gradient remains. You cannot find a warm spot. You cannot find a cold spot. Every surface is the same temperature: your skin, the wall, the water, the air. Six degrees, maybe five. The cold does not bite. It *saturates.* It is the cold of a refrigerator that has been running undisturbed for the entire span of its functional life.

---

And the sound.

The sound is the first thing you noticed and it is the thing you cannot stop noticing, though after a time — and time here is difficult to measure because nothing changes — after a time you realize that you do not hear it so much as inhabit it. It is not one sound. It is many sounds layered so tightly that they have become a single texture, a standing wave, a wall of frequency that fills the pocket the way water fills a glass — completely, without gaps, touching every surface simultaneously.

At the base, there is the hum. Sixty hertz, perhaps, or close enough. Transformer hum. The sound of electricity doing what electricity does inside a coil that was wound by a machine and energized by a source you cannot trace and has been energized since the winding was completed. Above that, a mechanical thrum — rotational, steady, the sound of something spinning in a bearing that was packed with grease and sealed and has never been opened since. It could be a pump. It could be a fan. It could be a flywheel storing angular momentum for a purpose that was designed into the system at the same moment the system was designed and has never been revisited. Above that, a hiss — pressurized fluid or gas moving through a narrow channel, the channel being a tube, the tube being one of the bundled conduits, the fluid being something that does not leak and does not stop. And above *that,* or perhaps woven through all of it, a high thin whine that lives at the edge of perception, tinnitus-like, constant, the sound of a frequency you cannot quite identify coming from a component you cannot quite locate.

The noise is so dense you can almost lean against it. It has mass. It fills the pocket the way the water fills the air — not as an addition but as a condition. You try to clap and the sound of your clap is absorbed before it reaches the nearest wall. You try to shout and your shout is a ripple in a medium that is already moving faster than you are. The sound does not echo because the pocket is too small and too lined and too wet for echoes. It does not reverberate. It *pervades.*

And woven through the mechanical noise, biological noise.

---

Because the pocket is full of life.

Not plants. The vegetation parameter is nearly zero and it is accurate: there is no photosynthesis here, no chlorophyll, no green. What grows here grows without light and without soil and without any logic you associate with growth. What lives here lives in the seams.

They are insects, or they are what insects would be if insects had been designed by the same process that designed the pocket — manufactured, modular, optimized for a niche that did not exist until they were placed in it. You see them first as movement on the walls: a constant, streaming, granular movement, like the surface of the metal is alive, and then you focus and you realize the metal is covered — *covered* — in small dark bodies, segmented, wet, each one the size of a rice grain, each one moving with a deliberateness that suggests not foraging but *commuting.* They are going somewhere. They have always been going there. The somewhere is the seams where water collects, and they drink — or they process the water in some way, or they extract something from the water, or the water extracts something from them — and the flow of their movement is as constant and as unvarying as the flow of the water itself.

In the ceiling lattice, larger forms. Not spiders. Not exactly. Eight-legged, yes, but the body is flat and pale and translucent, and you can see — if you look up, which is difficult because the ceiling is low and the light is poor — you can see organs inside, dark filaments that pulse with something that is not quite blood, moving in a rhythm that matches the sixty-hertz hum exactly, as if their circulation is synchronized to the electrical cycle, as if they were tuned to the grid at birth.

On the floor, in the pooled water, in the shallow depression where the conduits cross, there are things that are harder to classify. They are perhaps three centimeters long, perhaps less, and they are flat and oval and they move by expanding and contracting — a peristaltic motion, undulant, slow — and they are the color of the concrete, which is the color of the water, which is the color of the air, which is the color of the light, which is dim and grey and comes from no source you can identify.

The light. Yes. It comes from everywhere and nowhere. The walls are faintly luminous — not glowing, not lit, but *releasing* light, as if the metal itself was charged with some phosphorescent property during fabrication and has been emitting at this same low level ever since. It is enough to see by if your eyes adjust. It is not enough to cast a shadow. Nothing here casts a shadow. The light is too uniform, too ambient, too evenly distributed. It is the visual equivalent of the cold — a saturation, not an application.

---

And the fauna is overwhelming. Not in the sense of danger — nothing here attacks you, nothing here acknowledges you — but in the sense of *density.* Every surface that is wet, which is every surface, is also occupied. The insects on the walls do not crowd each other but they are at the limit of spacing, each one exactly far enough from its neighbor that their bodies do not touch, each one maintaining its position in the stream by some signal you cannot detect. The translucent things in the ceiling are spaced at regular intervals, which means the spacing is either territorial or architectural — they are at the junctions of the lattice, each one at a node, each one occupying a position that was either chosen or assigned. The flat undulant things in the water are the densest — hundreds of them, perhaps thousands, in a pool no larger than a dinner tray, each one layered over and under the others, a living sediment, a biological accretion that sits at the bottom of the water and processes it in ways you cannot observe and would not understand.

You realize, standing here, that the water and the life and the sound and the cold and the light are not separate phenomena. They are one system. The machinery pumps the water. The water feeds the life. The life processes the water into something the machinery can pump. The machinery generates the heat that the water removes. The water carries the heat to the walls, which release it, or do not release it — the equilibrium is so complete that you cannot tell. The light is a byproduct. The sound is a byproduct. The cold is a byproduct. Everything here is a byproduct of everything else, and the whole is so perfectly balanced that it does not *run* — it *exists.* It is a clock that tells no time. It is an engine that does no work. It is a biome that does not evolve.

Nothing here is eroding. Nothing here is being built. The pocket is *finished.* It was finished the moment it was sealed, and it has been in this exact state — this exact wetness, this exact cold, this exact noise, this exact population — for a span of time that the pocket does not record and does not care about. The rivets are still bright. The concrete is still smooth. The insects are still commuting. The water is still condensing. The hum is still humming.

You are the only thing in this pocket that is changing. Your breath. Your heartbeat. Your eyes adjusting. You are the only gradient here, the only thing with a direction, the only process that has a beginning and will have an end. And you can feel — or you imagine you can feel — the pocket's indifference to that. Not hostility. Not welcome. *Indifference.* The same indifference it has to the water, to the insects, to the hum. You are a perturbation. You will pass. The pocket will not adjust. The pocket does not adjust. The pocket *is.*

You sit on the conduit bundle because there is nowhere else to sit. The metal is cold and wet and vibrating under you. The insects stream past your hand on the wall beside you, parting around your fingers the way a river parts around a stone, closing again on the other side without pause or comment. Something small and wet falls on your shoulder from the ceiling. You brush it off. It is already gone.

The sound continues. The water continues. The light continues. Everything continues. You are inside a finished thing, and the finished thing does not know you are here, and if it did know, it would not care, and if it cared, it would not change.

You have entered a room that was complete before you arrived and will be complete after you leave and is complete right now, in this moment, in every moment, in the only moment it has ever known, which is this one, which is all of them, which is *now.*

The hum fills your chest. The cold fills your skin. The water condenses on you the way it condenses on everything, and you begin to understand — not think, *understand* — that you are not in the pocket.

The pocket is in you.
