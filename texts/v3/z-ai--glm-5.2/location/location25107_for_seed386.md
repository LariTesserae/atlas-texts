# The Plateau of Weeping Engines

You come up onto it through a cut in the ridgeline — a natural pass, choked with tree ferns whose trunks are wrapped in moss so thick it looks like green felt padded over something mechanical, and in places it is, because the old pipes run through here, half-buried, half-consumed by the forest, their iron skins flaking into the soil like rust-colored dander.

The plateau opens wide. You can see across it — several miles of uneven ground tilting gently southward, bounded on three sides by volcanic escarpments that drop thousands of feet into cloud forest. The elevation is enough that the sky feels close, a pale dome stretched tight overhead, and the air is thin enough that you notice your breathing. But it is not cold. It is profoundly, saunically hot. The plateau sits on a geothermal basin — you can feel it through your boots, a warmth radiating up from the rock itself, and in the lowest pockets of the terrain, where the ground dips into shallow basins, the air shimmers with heat that has nowhere to go.

---

Water is everywhere but does not flow in any organized way. It seeps. It weeps. The rock here is a porous basalt, old and settled, and water presses up through it from below — not in geysers, not dramatically, but as a constant exudation, a slow sweating from every horizontal surface. The flat rocks are slick with it. The steeper rocks run with it in thin parallel threads that catch the light like tinsel. In the shade of the larger fern groves, the water has nowhere to evaporate, and the air itself becomes dense — not fog exactly, not mist, but a heavy wetness, a visible thickening, so that objects fifty yards away have a soft edge to them, as if the world is slightly out of focus.

It rains in the afternoons. Not storms — the sky doesn't seem to have the energy for that — just a steady, tropical downpour that begins and ends with no particular announcement, lasting an hour or two, after which the plateau steams. That is when it is most alive: the rocks breathing out white vapor, the ferns dripping, the standing pools in the basins sending up columns of steam that lean in whatever direction the thin high air moves.

---

The vegetation is the first thing you notice and the last thing you stop noticing. It is everywhere, and it is not shy. Tree ferns dominate — thirty, forty feet tall in places, their crowns spreading in great dark green umbrellas that filter the already-pale light into something green and underwater. Between them, thickets of broad-leafed shrubs form waist-high tangles, their leaves glossy and dripping, always dripping. Moss covers everything horizontal. Epiphytes — bromeliads, orchids, things you don't have names for — cluster in the crotches of every trunk and branch, their roots dangling in the wet air, drinking directly from it. There are vines, but they are not aggressive; they drape rather than strangle, hanging in loose curves between trees like rigging on an abandoned ship.

The ground between plants is not bare. It is layered — leaf litter decomposing into black muck, overlaid with moss, overlaid with fern prothalli, overlaid with the thin roots of plants growing on top of plants growing on top of other plants. You cannot find the original surface. The original surface was probably rock. It has not been visible for a very long time.

---

The engines are part of the landscape now. Nobody built buildings here — whatever the machines were originally meant to service, it was not a human habitation. There are no foundations, no walls, no roads. Just the machines themselves, scattered across the plateau in a pattern that might once have been systematic and is now indistinguishable from the arrangement of boulders in a talus field.

They are pumps, mostly. Great iron pump housings the size of cattle, bolted to flat rock outcrops, their impeller shafts frozen in place but still holding the shape of whatever fluid they once moved. Some still turn — slowly, groaningly, driven by geothermal steam that vents up through the same fissures the water uses. You can hear one nearby, a low rhythmic chuff, chuff, chuff, barely louder than a heartbeat, its piston moving through a arc so slow you have to watch for a full minute to confirm it is moving at all. It has been doing this, probably, for decades. Maybe longer. The steam enters through a pipe that is now more moss than metal. The exhaust vents into the air with a soft hiss, adding to the wetness.

There are other machines. A generator housing, split open by root action, its copper coils green with patina that is indistinguishable from the green of the moss around it. A series of valves along a pipeline that runs for a quarter mile across the plateau's midsection, each valve a different size, each caked in mineral deposits from the water that has been seeping over them for so long that they look like stalagmites — organic, formed, as if they grew there. In one place, a pump station's frame has become a trellis for a flowering vine, and the red-orange blooms hang from the iron crossbeams in heavy clusters, attended by insects.

The machines do not belong here and they do not not-belong here. They have reached the same state as a shipwreck on a reef — still recognizable as something made, but no longer foreign to the place that holds them. The plateau has decided they are geography.

---

The sound world is sparse and deliberate. The chuffing of the nearest working pump. The constant small liquid sounds — drips landing in pools, water trickling over stone, the faint crackle of bubbles rising through standing water heated from below. Occasionally a bird — there are birds, small dark things that move fast through the fern canopy, and something larger that calls from the escarpment edges, a low descending note that sounds like a question asked once and not repeated. Insects buzz in the warm pockets but go silent in the steam. After rain, everything is quiet except the water, and the water sounds like breathing.

You notice, after a while, that there are no human sounds. No aircraft. No distant engines. No hum of power lines. The machines that still function do so without electricity — they are purely mechanical, driven by the earth's own heat, and they make the kind of sounds the earth would make if it had gears.

---

The animals are here but they are not showy about it. The small birds. A mammal — something low-slung and dark — that you see once, disappearing into a fern thicket with a fluid motion, leaving behind only the shaking of fronds. There are frogs; you hear them in the warm pools, short percussive notes, not a chorus but isolated calls, territorial. Insects work the flowers on the vine-covered pump station — something like a bee, but darker, longer, its flight pattern different from what you expect. On the rocks near the hot springs, there are lizards, or things like lizards, flat-bodied, dark-eyed, watching you with an indifference that suggests they have never learned to fear large moving shapes, which means they have never needed to.

There is something else — you find tracks in the mud near one of the basins. Broad, padded, four-toed. Something heavy comes here to drink. You do not see what made them. You get the sense that the plateau supports a full ecosystem in its own quiet way, a closed loop of heat and water and green growth that has been running for longer than anyone has been counting.

---

The rules of this place, as far as you can tell: water comes from below and falls from above and has nowhere to leave, so it sits and seeps and steams. Heat comes from below also, and meets the water, and the two of them make the air into something you wear rather than breathe. Things grow in this. Things eat what grows. Machines run on the same heat that grows what things eat. Nothing has been built here in a long time. Nothing has been removed. The plateau holds what it holds — rock, water, fern, iron, steam, frog, vine, rust, pollen — and lets the boundaries between them soften.

You could stay here. You could find a dry rock near a working pump and sit and watch the steam move and listen to the chuffing and the dripping and the occasional bird. The place would not mind you. It would not welcome you either. It would simply continue, as it has been continuing, in its own slow mechanical-breathing way, until the last pump stops and the last vine takes down the last iron frame and the plateau is only rock and water and green again, which is what it was before the engines came, and what it will be after, and what it almost is now.
