# The Bellows

There is a room inside a room inside a drowned machine.

You reach it only by accident. A vent grille, corroded to the consistency of wet chalk, gives way under the slightest pressure of current — and then you are through, pulled sideways into a space no larger than a chapel nave, tucked between the third and fourth cooling jackets of a generator housing that stopped being serviced before anyone now alive was born.

The water is warm. Not lukewarm — *warm*, bathwater warm, 38 degrees or more, heated continuously by the tonnes of decaying industrial equipment that form the walls, floor, and ceiling of this pocket. The steel has been down here so long it has passed through rust and arrived somewhere else: a dark, hydrated mineral skin, velvety to the touch, fragile as pumice. You can break it with a finger. Beneath it, more steel, then concrete, then the housing of the old turbine — layers like geological strata, each one a different era of obsolescence.

Nothing grows here. The warmth would seem to invite it, but the water carries a mineral load so dense it stings exposed skin. Calcium and sulfur compounds and something faintly metallic — manganese, perhaps — saturate every cubic metre. Surfaces are coated in a brittle white rind, like salt deposits around a hot spring, but this is not salt. It is the exhaled chemistry of the machine itself, leaching from its alloys decade after decade into the still-circulating water. The deposits form in fronds and shelves and sometimes in shapes that *almost* resemble something organic — a ribcage, a coral fan, the interior scaffolding of a lung — but there is no biology here. No microbes survive this heat and chemistry. It is a sterile, mineral-calcified world, and the near-life shapes are coincidence, or the residual memory of the engineers who designed the systems that breathe and pump around you.

Because the machine is not dead.

That is the first thing you learn. You learn it through your bones before you understand it with your mind.

---

The sound is not something you hear. It is something that happens to you.

Water transmits sound roughly four times faster than air and with devastating fidelity, and this room — this pocket, this blister in the body of the generator complex — is positioned at the convergence point of three still-operating pump systems and a primary circulation main that has not been shut off because there is no one left who knows how, and no one who would want to. The pumps run on the residual pressure differential between the flooded lower levels and the only-partially-flooded upper levels. Water falls through the complex by gravity, spins turbines that were never meant to run this long, and the turbines spin pumps that push water back up through pipes that have been scaling shut for decades, which means the pumps labour, which means they shudder, which means the entire housing vibrates at a frequency that enters your chest cavity and rearranges things.

Standing — floating — in the centre of the pocket, you cannot distinguish individual sounds. There is no separation of sources. The pumps, the turbine three rooms away, the water hammering through a partially collapsed pipe in the wall to your left, the groan of concrete settling under unequal hydrostatic load — all of it arrives as a single chord, a roaring, shuddering, bottomless chord that has been sustaining itself for so many years that the room has worn grooves into its own architecture. The vibration has eroded the walls smooth in parallel striations. The white mineral deposits form nodules at the antinodes of standing waves, so the room is subtly decorated — beaded, almost jewelled — by its own noise. Long exposure to this frequency has calcified the decoration into place. The room has been singing the same note for so long that the room is *made* of the note.

You open your mouth and the water around your teeth vibrates differently. You feel your fillings buzz. Your vision blurs not from poor light but from the oscillation of your eyeballs in their sockets.

There is no silence here. There has never been silence here. Silence is a concept from another province of reality.

---

The light comes from a single source and it is failing.

A waterproof housing, bolted to the ceiling of the pocket, contains a lamp that runs on the same residual power system as the pumps — a trickle of current generated by the turbines, stepped down through transformers that have been partially shorted by mineral infiltration, delivered to an incandescent bulb that has been burning, in theory, for longer than its rated lifespan by a factor of forty. In practice, the filament has broken and re-fused itself so many times that the light it produces is not the light it was designed to produce. It is a brownish, anemic amber, and it flickers not with the rhythm of the electrical supply but with the vibration of the room — the filament sways in its mount, the glass envelope is cracked, water has infiltrated the housing but cannot quite reach the filament because the heat at the glass surface keeps a tiny pocket of superheated vapour around it, a fluctuating cloak of steam that lenses and distorts the light. The result: the room pulses. Not rhythmically — arrhythmically. The light lurches, surges, dims, flares. It illuminates the mineral deposits in stages, like a fitful flashlight across a cave wall.

Most of the room is in shadow at any given moment. The darkness is not empty — it is textured. You can feel it, the way the warm water moves differently in the dark spaces, eddying around deposits and corroded brackets and the skeletons of cable trays that still cling to the ceiling like rusted ribs. The darkness has weight. It has temperature — slightly warmer than the lit spaces, because the mineral deposits in shadow have not been bleached by the lamp's weak UV and so retain heat differently.

You learn to navigate by the darkness. The lit spaces are landmarks; the dark spaces are the rooms between landmarks.

---

There is something alive here. One thing.

It is not supposed to be here.

A flatworm — pale, translucent, roughly the length and width of a human thumb — lives on the surface of the steam-shrouded lamp housing. It has no eyes. It does not need them. It feeds on the thermophilic bacteria that have colonised the mineral crust around the lamp — the only organisms that can survive in this water, a single strain of chemotrophic archaean that metabolises the sulfur compounds bleeding from the corroding steel. The flatworm is the apex predator of this ecosystem. Its ecosystem is approximately the size of a dinner plate.

You notice it because it moves, and nothing else here moves on a timescale you can observe. The mineral deposits grow at rates of millimetres per year. The corrosion advances in geological time. The water churns, yes — turbulent, chaotic, surging through the pocket in confused eddies that reverse direction every few seconds as the pumps cycle in and out of phase — but the churning is the room's breathing, not its living. The flatworm, by contrast, glides. It extends itself along the lamp housing, contracts, extends again, with a slow confidence that is deeply, almost painfully alive.

It has been here for years, perhaps. It has no predators. It has no competitors. The warm, deafening, mineral-dark pocket is its entire universe, and it has no idea it is inside a machine, inside a flooded facility, inside a body of water that may itself be inside something larger. It knows the heat source. It knows the food. It knows the edge of the plate.

You watch it for a long time. The sound shakes your vision. The light pulses. The water shoves you sideways and then pulls you back. The flatworm does not notice any of this. It has no mechanism for noticing. It extends. It contracts. The lamp flickers. The pumps roar. The mineral world deposits another fraction of a millimetre of itself onto every surface, and the room grows imperceptibly smaller, imperceptibly more decorated, imperceptibly more *itself* with each passing second.

---

This is what remains of industry when industry is forgotten: not silence, not stillness, not ruin in any romantic sense. A deafening, warm, dark, vibrating continuation. A machine that no one built to run forever but which has run for so long that stopping it would require more effort than letting it shake itself to pieces. And it is shaking itself to pieces — slowly, over decades, the vibration loosening bolts that no one will retighten, the water dissolving seals that no one will replace — but the timescale of destruction is so vast, and the energy so constant, that destruction and maintenance have become indistinguishable. The mineral deposits fill the gaps the corrosion opens. The corrosion opens the gaps the mineral deposits fill. The room maintains itself by falling apart at exactly the rate it is being rebuilt by its own decay.

This is the equilibrium. This is the *established state*.

You are the anomaly. The flatworm does not count — it is too small, too local, too perfectly adapted to be anomalous. It belongs here now. You do not. The grille through which you entered is already partially recalcified with mineral deposits disturbed by your passage. In a week, there will be no sign you were here. In a month, the deposits will have grown over the brackets you touched. In a year, the lamp filament will break one final time and not re-fuse, and the flatworm's universe will go dark, and it will die, and the archaean colony will drift, and the room will continue — warmer, darker, still roaring — for another thirty years, or another three hundred, until the pumps finally fail, and the pressure differential equalises, and the water grows still, and the sound stops, and there is finally, at the bottom of everything, a silence so total it will feel like the loudest thing this room has ever known.
