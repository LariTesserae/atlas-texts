# The Drowned Mesa

It happened not all at once but over a span no one was there to measure — a high plateau, newly thrust upward by forces still trembling somewhere beneath the crust, flooded by warm water rising from below rather than falling from above. Springs, is the closest word. But springs implies something small. This was a whole region becoming sea. The plateau caught the water in its sharp, fresh-broken basins — basins that had been valleys for only a geologic moment before they became something else — and the water stayed. It stays still. That is one of the strangest things about this place: the water is warm, and warm water should move, should circulate, should sink and rise and trade heat with the air. This water does not. It holds its warmth the way a held breath holds its shape. It sits in the basins and it does not leave.

---

Light reaches a long way down. The water is clear in the manner of water that has had no time to grow clouded with silt or plankton — young water, you might say, though water itself is never young. The light enters at an angle that shifts through the day and falls against walls of stone that drop steeply from what used to be the plateau's edge. These walls are sharp. They have the unfinished look of rock that was broken and not yet worn — edges still keen enough that the light catches them and throws bright lines across the underwater floor. The stone is dark, volcanic-looking, but it is not volcanic. It is simply young. It has the color of something that has not yet learned what it will become. Fissures run through it in networks that branch and rebranch, each one still clean-edged, each one deep enough to vanish into darkness where the light fails to follow.

There are shelves. There are overhangs. There are columns of stone that broke free during the uplift and now stand where they fell, partially embedded in the basin floor, leaning at angles that suggest they have not yet decided whether to topple. The water fills every gap between them, and because the water does not move, it has not pushed sediment around their bases, has not shaped them into anything softer. They remain exactly as they landed. The whole terrain has the quality of a photograph taken an instant after something happened — the moment captured, the aftermath not yet begun.

---

But the vegetation. This is what changes everything about the place, what keeps it from being merely a flooded geology. Because the plants here did not drown. They arrived — or they were already here, already growing on the plateau in its brief time as dry land — and when the water came, they did not die. They changed.

What grows now across the submerged slopes and sharp shelves is dense, layered, near-touching itself. Stalks rise from the basin floor in thick stands, pale green-gold in the filtered light, their surfaces smooth and faintly translucent. They are not kelp. They are not seagrass. They are something that was terrestrial and became aquatic without stopping, without the usual dying-back and reseeding that adaptation implies. They simply kept growing. Their leaves — if they are leaves — are long and ribbonlike and they drift in the still water with a motion so slow it is almost imperceptible, not driven by current but by some internal process, some osmotic pulse that moves them the way a heartbeat moves a chest. When you watch them long enough — and this is a place that demands long watching — you begin to see that they are all moving in the same rhythm, the whole stand breathing together, in and out, a slow flexion that takes perhaps a full minute to complete.

Beneath the stalks, lower to the floor, the growth becomes denser. Broad flat plates of something between leaf and membrane layer over each other in overlapping shingles, each one a deep blue-green that darkens almost to black at the center where it attaches to the stalk. They are warm to the touch — warmer than the water around them, even, which is itself warmer than it should be. There is a faint sweetness in the water near them, not a smell exactly, since smell is an air-word, but something the tongue reads, some dissolved compound that reads as sugar and mineral and something else, something that has no name because no one has been here to name it.

The density increases as you move toward the basin walls. The growth wraps around the broken columns, climbs the sharp-edged fissures, fills the overhangs with hanging curtains of pale filament that drift the same way the stalks drift — that same slow pulse, that same patient rhythm. In places the vegetation is so thick it obscures the stone entirely, and you would not know there was rock beneath it except that the geometry gives it away: a straight line where no plant grows straight, a sharp corner where no organic thing bends at ninety degrees. The plants have covered the young geology like a green-gold skin, but they cannot yet hide its newness. The bones of the place show through.

---

There are almost no animals. This is the other strange thing. A warm, lush, sunlit underwater forest should be full of life — grazing things, predatory things, things that nest in the stalks and feed on the plates. There are none. Or nearly none. Once, if you stay still long enough — and stillness is the only appropriate posture here — you may see something small and dark move between the stalks at the edge of your vision. Not a fish. Something more like a flattened oval of shadow that drifts and then is gone. It does not return. Whether it was real or a trick of the light on translucent leaves is impossible to determine. The absence of fauna is so complete that it begins to feel like a rule, a condition of the place: nothing eats here. Nothing is eaten. The vegetation grows and breathes and does not seem to be subject to the ordinary contract of consumption that governs living systems elsewhere. It grows and it grows and nothing takes from it. The density increases not because old growth dies and new growth replaces it but because nothing dies at all. The layers you see are layers of time, each one still alive, the oldest at the bottom and the newest at the top, and all of them green.

---

The sound. There is almost no sound. Underwater places are never silent in the way dry places can be — there is always the sound of one's own blood, the sound of the water itself pressed against the ears — but here even that is muted. The vegetation seems to absorb what little sound there is. The slow pulse of the stalks is too low to hear. The water is too still to slosh or lap. The stone does not creak because it is not under stress; it has already done its breaking and is now merely resting. If you make a sound — if you knock against one of the broken columns — the sound travels a short distance and then is swallowed. The vegetation takes it. The layers of flat blue-green plates absorb it the way moss absorbs a footstep. What you hear, in the end, is your own presence and almost nothing else. The place itself makes no noise.

---

The scale of it is difficult to judge. The water is clear and the basins are wide — wide enough that the far walls of stone are hazy with distance, their sharp edges softened by the volume of water between. You are in a valley that has become a sea, or a sea that has become a valley; the distinction has lost its meaning. The floor drops away in steps, each step a new shelf of stone coated in growth, and the shelves descend until the light goes and the vegetation thins and there is only dark water below, warm and still and going down a long, long way. You cannot see the bottom. You can see the walls going down, the growth fading from gold-green to blue-green to deep blue to nothing, and you know that the basin extends below you further than the light can reach, that this is not a shallow place, that the flooding went deep and the plateau was taller than its surface suggested and its roots go down into the warm dark where nothing grows.

Above — if you look up — the surface of the water is a ceiling of light. It is not a flat ceiling. The water's surface moves, even if the water beneath does not, and the light above it shifts and ripples and sends moving patterns down through the clear water to play across the vegetation and the stone. These patterns are the only thing in the place that changes quickly. They move across the stalks and plates and sharp-edged walls in shifting bright lines, and the vegetation responds — not by moving but by seeming to brighten where the light touches and dim where it does not, so that the whole forest pulses with moving light the way a lung pulses with air, in and out, bright and dim, bright and dim.

---

This is a place that has not yet found its equilibrium. That is the feeling it gives, more than any specific observation: it is new, it is warm, it is full of life that has adapted without transition, and it is waiting. Not waiting for anything in particular. Waiting in the way that geology waits — patiently, on a scale that makes the pulse of the stalks seem frantic by comparison. The plateau rose. The water came. The plants changed. Something will come next — the water will begin to move, or the stone will fracture again, or the vegetation will reach some threshold and the dense layers of living matter will finally do what living matter does and begin to cycle, to rot and regrow, to attract the things that eat rot and the things that eat them. But that has not happened yet. The place holds itself in this moment — warm, still, bright, dense with growth, empty of consumers, quiet — and it does not know how long it will hold, and it does not care. Time here is not a pressure. It is a depth. It accumulates the way the vegetation accumulates on the stone: layer on layer, each one alive, each one added to the next, the whole thing growing thicker and denser and more itself with every slow minute that passes beneath the rippling ceiling of light.
