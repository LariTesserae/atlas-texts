# The Rookery of Throats

## First Approach

You hear it before you understand it.

Not from one direction — from the ground, from the air column above, from inside your own chest where your ribs press against soft tissue. A sound like a thousand wooden flutes all boiling at once. Like the groan of a house settling, but the house is a county, and it is being built and demolished in the same breath. You are standing at the edge of something and the edge keeps moving.

The land is low. You can tell by the way the sky sits on things — not distant, not高高 suspended, but draped, a grey-warm membrane sagging into the tops of what grows here. And what grows here is everything.

## The Architecture

There are streets. You can walk them. But they are not made for walking in any way you understand — they are made by the walking, by the pressure of feet having once passed and the plant matter having remembered the passage and hardened in those channels, laid down lignin like cobblestone. The streets are roots, are exposed heartwood, are living alleys between walls that were fences that were hedges that are now four-story load-bearing structures of braided stem and pulped bark.

It is a city. It was a city. It is becoming a city again, differently, right now.

The buildings breathe. Not metaphorically. The walls are diaphragmatic — great sheets of living leaf-matter stretched over frame-wood, and they flex inward and outward on a cycle that might be minutes, might be hours, might be tied to something that is not time as you know it. Each chamber you pass is a lung. Each street is a trachea. The whole settlement is respiratory, and the sound — that sound — is the air being moved, the sap being pushed, the fiber being grown at rates that should be impossible but are happening with such force that the cellulose screams as it elongates.

There are no tools here. No cut beams, no joints, no mortise and tenon. The construction is entirely biological. Branches grew where rooms needed to be. Leaves wove where walls needed to close. The geometry is organic in the strictest sense — it was *grown*, not built, and it is still growing, and the distinction between construction and organism has dissolved entirely.

You touch a wall. It is warm. You feel the vascular pulse beneath your palm, a thick slow throb of sugared fluid moving upward. The wall moves against your hand. Not pulling away. Pressing back. Confirming you.

## The Water

It is not wet here, not exactly. But nothing is fully dry.

Moisture comes from everywhere and nowhere. The air itself is humid to the point of being substantive — you can almost hold a handful of it, squeeze dew from it. Droplets form on every surface, gather along the grooves of bark and the undersides of leaves, and run in thin rivulets that join other rivulets and become shallow streams that run along the gutters of the living streets. These gutters are not carved — they are channels where the root-pavement naturally dips, following the old water paths, and the plants have shaped themselves to accommodate and accelerate the flow.

In the low places, where several streets converge, the water pools into shallow basins no deeper than your ankle. The basins are lined with a sort of moss that is not moss — it is something that has no name yet, something that evolved here in the last improbable week. It is translucent, gelatinous, and it glows faintly in the way that rot glows, though nothing here is rotting. Everything here is too alive to rot. The water in these pools is warm, almost body-temperature, and it moves even when there is no current, circulating through some capillary action of the living substrate beneath it.

Rain comes, but it comes wrong. It falls in sheets from the sagging sky, but only in certain corridors — you can walk from a street where rain is hammering down in a solid curtain into an intersection where the air is merely damp and still, then into another avenue where the rain falls upward. Not all the time. But sometimes. The rain that falls upward disappears into the canopy above, which absorbs it, drinks it, and the leaves swell visibly, pulse, and the sound — the sound — gets louder.

## The Sound

You cannot think past it. You try. You fail. It is not that it is painful — it is that it is *full*. There is no silence here, no quiet pocket, no momentary lapse. The sound is continuous and layered and comes from every surface and every volume of air.

The lowest layer is a hum, felt more than heard, in the bones of your legs and the base of your spine — the vibration of the root system that underlies everything, the whole settlement's foundation, which is a single interconnected organism pulsing with sap-flow and incremental growth. Above that is the creaking, the groaning, the splintering-into-growth — the sound of wood becoming more wood, of fibers lengthening, of cell walls forming and hardening and being pushed aside by new cell walls forming behind them. It is the sound of construction at a speed that defies what you know about plants.

Above that is the rustling, the tearing, the flapping of leaves at scales from microscopic to the size of doors. The canopy above — three layers deep, at least, a stratified ecosystem of light-catchers — is in constant motion, and each motion produces its own acoustic signature, and they overlap into a wash of white noise that is not white but green, if green were a sound, which here it is.

And above that, or beneath it, or woven through it, are the voices.

Not animal voices. There are no animals. But the plants make sounds that your brain cannot help but parse as language. Clicking, popping, rasping — produced by tension in seed pods, by air trapped in vascular cavities and released, by the friction of bark against bark as stems sway. It is patterned. It repeats. It responds. If you stand still long enough, you begin to feel that the sounds nearest you have shifted, have oriented toward you, have started to — not mimic, but reference your breathing, your footsteps, the wet sound of your eyes blinking.

It is deafening and it is coherent and it does not care whether you understand it.

## The Reality

This is the part that is hardest to write.

The settlement — the Rookery, you have started calling it, though you don't know why, though the name came from the plants themselves, from the pattern of their clicking — the Rookery is not stable in the way that places are stable. It is stable in the way that a river is stable: it persists, but it does not stay.

You walk down a street and reach a plaza. The plaza is circular, thirty meters across, floored with a tight-woven mat of living grass that gives slightly under your weight and springs back. Around the plaza are structures — two stories, three stories, one that might be five if you count the way it spirals upward — all of living wood, all breathing, all warm.

You turn around. The street you came from is not there.

There is a wall. A new wall, grown in the seconds you were not looking, already breathing, already warm, already integrated into the vascular network of the surrounding structures. Behind it, you can hear the street — the sound of it, the particular acoustic signature of that corridor — but you cannot get to it. The wall is solid. It is real. It is made of the same stuff as everything else.

You turn again. The plaza has changed. Not dramatically — the proportions have shifted, the structures have rearranged, one that was on your left is now in front of you, and the gap between two of them has narrowed or widened, you cannot tell which because you cannot remember exactly how it was before, because the place does not want you to remember, because the place is not a place, it is a *process*.

This is what the breakdown looks like: not a falling apart, but a falling *through*. The Rookery is a thing that is happening, not a thing that has happened. Every surface is provisional. Every structure is a temporary convergence of growth-patterns that will dissolve and reform and dissolve and reform. The streets remember being streets, and they will be streets again, but not the same streets, not in the same configuration, not with the same relationship to the streets around them.

You walk through the wall. Not around it, not over it — through it. The fibers part for you, or you become less solid, or the wall becomes less solid, and you are on the other side, and the street is there, and the sound shifts to accommodate your presence, and a vine descends from the canopy and touches your shoulder and withdraws, and you realize that the vine was checking whether you were real, and it concluded that you were, and it concluded that it was, and both of these conclusions are temporary.

## The Light

It is normally lit, which here means something specific. The sky is not occluded — you can see it, grey-warm, sagging — but the canopy takes most of the direct light and redistributes it. The light that reaches the streets is filtered, shifted, green-gold, and it moves. Not just dappled — the canopy is too dense, too layered, too turbulent for mere dappling. Instead, the light arrives in beams and sheets that rotate, shift, sweep across the living architecture like searchlights. You are in light, then in green shadow, then in light again, and the intervals are irregular because the canopy is not moving with wind — there is no wind, not really, only the respiration of the settlement itself — it is moving with *intent*.

The light falls on things and the things grow faster where the light falls. You can watch it happen. A wall that is in a beam of light for ten seconds gains a centimeter of thickness. A branch that is in shadow loses leaves, withdraws, and the branch beside it, in light, extends to fill the gap. The settlement is photosensitive and it responds to its own light-distribution by redistributing itself, which changes the light, which changes the settlement, which changes the light —

You are inside a loop. You are inside a living equation that has not resolved because it cannot resolve because resolution would mean stillness and stillness would mean death and this place is not dead, this place is the opposite of dead, this place is so alive that it has lost the ability to be a place and become instead a *becoming*.

## The Warmth

Everything is warm. The air is warm. The walls are warmer. The water in the shallow pools is warmest of all. The warmth comes from below — from the root network, from the metabolic engine of the whole organism, which is burning sugar and growing tissue and producing heat as a byproduct, and the heat rises through the vascular structures and radiates from every surface, and you are standing inside the body temperature of something that does not have a body but is, somehow, a body.

You are inside something alive. You have been inside something alive since you arrived. The Rookery is not a place you visit. It is a place that ingests you, temporarily, and releases you, temporarily, and the boundary between those two states is not clear.

## What Lives Here

Nothing with a face. Nothing with a skeleton, with a brain, with a nervous system. But alive, all of it, every surface and fiber and drop of sap. The ecology is pure plant — not plants as you know them, not any species you could name, but a system that has decided that plants are sufficient, that the kingdom Plantae contains everything needed for complexity, for architecture, for sound, for what might be thought if thought were not bounded by neurons.

There are layers. Down low, the moss-that-isn't-moss, the gelatinous groundcover, the shallow warm pools. Above that, the root-pavement, the streets, the low structures, the gutters with their capillary streams. Above that, the load-bearing walls, the breathing chambers, the spiraling towers. Above that, the canopy, three layers, each a different scale of leaf and stem, each absorbing a different wavelength of the filtered light. And above that, the sky, sagging, grey-warm, occasionally raining in the wrong direction.

But the layers are not fixed. A street can become a canopy. A wall can become a pool. You watch it happen — a load-bearing structure on your left begins to soften, its fibers loosening, its bark going translucent, and over the course of what might be minutes it sags, liquefies, and flows into the gutter, and the gutter carries it to a basin, and the basin absorbs it, and somewhere above you a new branch begins to extend from the canopy, and the branch has the same structural memory as the wall that was, and it grows in the same shape, and the place has moved a wall from the ground to the sky and called it progress.

## The Rules

You are learning them, slowly, by accident, by survival.

1. Do not stand still for too long. The settlement will grow around you. It will not hurt you — it does not seem capable of hurting you, or interested in it — but it will enclose you, and the chamber it makes around you will be warm and dark and breathing and you will not be able to find the door because there will not be a door, and you will have to wait until the wall-cycle rotates and the fibers soften and you can push through, and this might take hours, and the sound will be very loud inside the chamber, and you will not enjoy it.

2. Follow the water. The streams in the gutters move, and the places they go are the places that are stable, at least temporarily. The water knows where the settlement is solid and where it is not, because the water is part of the settlement, and it will not flow into itself.

3. Do not touch the warm pools. You will want to. They glow. They are the right temperature. But the moss-that-isn't-moss will adhere to your skin and it will grow on you and it will become part of you, and this is not dangerous but it is *permanent*, and the Rookery will know you afterward, will recognize you, will grow toward you when you approach, and this will be either a comfort or a horror depending on what you are.

4. The sound is not for you. It is for the settlement. It is how the parts coordinate, how the walls know when to breathe, how the roots know where to grow, how the rain knows where to fall. You can listen but you cannot speak, because you do not have the equipment, and the settlement does not want your answer, it wants your presence, your weight, your warmth, your carbon dioxide.

5. Leave before the sky touches the ground. This happens. The grey membrane above descends, slowly, and when it reaches the canopy the light changes and the growth accelerates and the sound becomes something that is not sound anymore but a physical pressure, a vibration that enters your bones and reorganizes them, and you do not want to be here for that, believe me, believe the place, believe the warning that the place does not know it is giving you in the way the roots pulse faster when the sky descends, in the way the walls close slightly, in the way the streets narrow —

Leave.

## What Remains

You are at the edge now. The sound is behind you, still deafening, still layered, still alive. Ahead of you is something else — another landscape, quieter, cooler, and you do not know what it is yet. Behind you, the Rookery breathes. You can hear it. You will always be able to hear it. The sound does not stop at the edge. It follows you, not as a noise but as a memory of a noise, a phantom pressure in your chest, a warmth that you cannot account for.

You were inside something alive. It let you go. Or it hasn't let you go and you only think you left. The wall you passed through on the way out — did it part for you, or did you pass through it because it was no longer entirely solid, or did it absorb part of you and replace it with part of itself?

Your shoulder is warm where the vine touched it.

You walk forward. The sound does not diminish. You are still in the Rookery. You will always be in the Rookery. The Rookery is not a place. It is a condition. It is a way of being alive that, once you have been inside it, does not release you entirely, because release would mean a boundary, and the Rookery does not believe in boundaries, and the Rookery is correct.

The sky is sagging.

Walk faster.
