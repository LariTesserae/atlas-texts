# The Hollow Works

You descend through a gap in the limestone — a fracture wide enough for a body, not much wider — and the sound reaches you before the place does. A low, composite roar, not aggressive but *omnipresent*, the way a river's noise fills a valley. Except this is deeper. Mechanical. Organic. You cannot separate the two.

---

The stairs begin about twenty feet down. They are wood, but not cut wood — grown wood, each step a transverse ridge in a living root that has been trained, over what must be decades, into a switchback descent. The grain is still running. If you press your thumb into the edge of a step, it gives slightly, warm, the way living tissue gives. Moss — or something that functions like moss — has been allowed to colonize the vertical faces between steps. It is dark green, almost black in this light, and perpetually wet. Water moves across it in films too thin to call flow. More like the surface is *weeping*.

The air is warm. Not hot — warm the way a greenhouse is warm, or a room where many bodies have been for a long time. It carries a smell that is hard to place at first: wet cellulose, tannin, the iron tang of standing water, and beneath it all something sweeter, almost fermentative. Yeast, maybe. The slow exhale of decomposition that has found equilibrium with growth.

---

The staircase opens into a chamber.

That word — *chamber* — is wrong. It implies emptiness, a void with walls. This place has no emptiness. You step off the last root-stair onto a floor that is also root, also moss, also packed living matter, and the space ahead of you is *full* in a way that redefines the word. Full the way a body cavity is full. Full the way a termite mound is full. There is structure, there is passage, there is architecture — but there is no room that is merely room.

What you can see — and visibility is low, maybe twenty feet in any direction before the humid air solidifies into a brownish murk — is a cross-section of something that has been growing and being built for a very long time. Maybe a century. Maybe longer. The two processes — growth and construction — have become indistinguishable.

---

Here is what you can piece together:

Once, there was industry here. Actual industry. The kind that requires engines and piping and production lines and a labor force. The evidence is everywhere, but it has been *absorbed*. A cast-iron pipe, four inches in diameter, runs along what might be a ceiling or might be an elevated root-bole — it's hard to tell which is which because a fig-root has grown around the pipe, conformed to its shape, and now holds it the way a hand holds a rope. The pipe is warm. Something moves through it — steam, perhaps, or heated water from somewhere deeper. The root has grown to accommodate it, has shaped a groove for it, has in some places *fused* with the iron through a crust of oxidation and calcium deposit so that the two materials form a single composite strut.

This is not ruin. Ruin implies neglect. This is *incorporation*. The industry did not fail and then get overgrown. The industry and the growth reached a settlement. They are co-operating.

---

You move through what can only be called a street, though it is a street the way a vein is a street — a channel between masses of tissue, following a path of least resistance. The masses on either side are structural. They are walls. They are also living plants: layered root systems, woody and bark-covered on their outer faces, soft and pale where they face the passage. Some have been shaped — you can see where ties or wires once constrained growth, leaving constrictions and bulges that record decades of cambial pressure. The wires are still there in places, embedded, green with patina, functionally part of the wall.

Doorways exist. They are not cut. They are *grown* — gaps left open by the directing intelligence of whatever cultivates this place, maintained by constant pruning, you'd guess, though you see no tools and no hands. The doorframes are living wood, smooth, calloused where repeated contact has worn and healed and worn again. Some thresholds are shallow steps down into spaces that are darker, smaller, more private. Others are simply alcoves.

Through one doorway you see a room — a *room*, with walls, floor, and ceiling all formed from the same dense intergrown root-mass — and in the center of it, an engine. A small one. A pump, maybe, or a compressor. It sits on a plinth of calcified root tissue and it is *running*. A slow, patient, chugging rhythm, two beats per second, and with each beat a faint vibration travels through the floor and into the walls, and the walls — you realize — are vibrating in sympathy. Not damaged. Resonant. The plant tissue has thickened at the vibration nodes, grown dense where the stress concentrates, and thinned at the antinodes into membranous windows that let in a filtered, brownish glow.

The engine is not maintained in any way you recognize. It has no visible fuel source. Pipes — some iron, some grown woody tubes that may once have been roots and may still function as roots — connect it to the surrounding mass. It is possible the engine is being *fed* by the plants. It is possible the plants are being *run* by the engine. It is possible this distinction has not been meaningful here for a long time.

---

The sound.

The sound is what stays with you. It is not one sound. It is a *saturation* of sound, the way the air is a saturation of water. Layers:

The lowest layer is a throb — mechanical, deep, felt in the chest more than heard. It comes from below, from somewhere further down than the chamber floor, and it pulses at perhaps one beat every two seconds. Slow. Like a heartbeat at rest. Or like a large pump cycling. It is the rhythm that everything else organizes around.

Above that: a continuous rumble, mid-register, composed of many smaller mechanical sounds — the chugging of the small engines you can see in the rooms, the hiss of steam or heated water moving through pipes, the creak of metal expanding unevenly in warm humid air. This layer is the *industrial* voice of the place.

Above that: the organic sounds. The creak of woody tissue under tension. The faint, wet sound of fluid moving through living tubes — not fast, not pressurized, just the slow capillary creep of water being pulled upward through root systems the size of buildings. A soft ticking, almost like rain, which is condensation — water forming on every surface and dropping into every lower surface, a constant fretting of small impacts.

And above all of that: a high, thin, almost-electrical whine. You cannot find its source. It might be resonance — the whole chamber, all its masses and pipes and living walls, vibrating at a frequency just at the edge of hearing. Or it might be something else, something you haven't found yet.

It is loud. Not painfully — not like a factory floor. Loud like a crowded room where every voice is a machine voice. You have to raise your voice to be heard. There is no one to hear you.

---

You go further in. The street narrows, widens, branches. You choose branches at random, or by some instinct toward the slightly more open path. The place has the logic of a town — there are intersections, there are what appear to be larger and smaller thoroughfares, there is a sense of *districts* — but the logic is not human in any simple way. The wide streets are wide because something large once needed to move through them. You see grooves worn into the root-floor — metal wheels, you think. Carts, or trolleys, or some kind of tracked vehicle. The rails, if there were rails, have been grown over, but the grooves persist in the hardened callus tissue of the floor like fossils.

In one district — you think of it as the deep district, though you're not sure you've descended much further — the architecture changes. The walls are less woody, more fibrous. Softer. Pale. The color drains out of everything here — the murk goes from brownish to greyish, the plant tissue goes from bark-brown to a fleshy off-white, and the air is thicker, wetter, so saturated that droplets form on your eyelashes and you have to blink to see. The surfaces are slick. Not with water — with a film of something more viscous. Sap, maybe. Or lymph.

There are no engines here. Instead, the walls themselves seem to be doing something. They bulge and contract — slowly, so slowly you have to hold still and watch for a full minute before you're sure it's happening. Rhythmic. Synchronized with the deep throb from below.
