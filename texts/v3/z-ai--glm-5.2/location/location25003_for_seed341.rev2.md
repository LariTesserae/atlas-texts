# The Sump Gardens at Veyl

You come down into it. That is the first thing — the descent. Not a dramatic descent, not a cliff or a ladder, just a gradual downslope along a paved road whose center has been patched so many times the patches are the road now, the original surface surviving only as a faint darker vein running between the repairs. The air thickens as you go lower. Not just humid — *thick*, the way a room gets when a kettle has been boiling too long, except here it is the whole basin, the whole low place, and the kettle has been boiling for decades.

---

The complex is called Veyl, or was called Veyl, and it occupies a shallow natural depression where three drainage channels once converged on their way to a river that has since shifted course two kilometers east and left behind a long, flat, impermanent lake that comes and goes with the season. The settlement was built to manage water. That is its origin and its ongoing purpose, though the management has changed character over the years and now the water manages the settlement about as much as the other way around.

It is small. You can stand at the pump house on the north edge and see the far wall on the south — a retaining wall, poured concrete, stained black-green with algae in the permanent wet — in one unbroken view. The whole place spans maybe two hundred meters at its widest. A hamlet. A single feature. A garden with engines in it.

---

The engines are the first thing you hear, and they never stop. There are four of them, housed in a long brick building with a corrugated tin roof that has rusted to a deep arterial red. Three of the engines run constantly; the fourth kicks in when the water table rises past a certain point, which is often, and when it does the sound changes — not louder exactly, but lower, a new fundamental note entering the chord. The sound is not a roar. It is a sustained, vibrating, composite drone that you stop hearing consciously after about fifteen minutes but that continues to act on your body. You feel it in the soft tissue of your throat. You feel it in the fillings in your teeth if you have them. The locals — there are perhaps thirty people who still live here, maintaining the works, though you will not see many of them at once — the locals say you dream the sound after you leave, for nights afterward, a low hum in the bones of the dream.

The engines drive pumps. The pumps move water from the sump — the low central collection basin, a rectangular concrete cistern about forty meters long and three meters deep — up through a network of iron pipes that route the water out of the depression and into the old drainage channels, which carry it east toward the phantom lake. The system was built sixty or seventy years ago. The brick is mortared and set. The iron pipes have developed a patina of oxidation so thorough and so uniform that they look almost intentional, a dark bronze-brown that matches the mud. The concrete is cracked in places, and from the cracks, water seeps in thin, sheeting curtains that catch what light there is and hold it for a moment before the water falls. The weeping is constant. The walls weep. The pump house weeps. The retaining wall weeps. Everything that is built here has become a permeable membrane.

---

The light is low and stays low. The heat rises off the water and the wet ground and forms a permanent haze — not fog, not mist, but a luminous suspension, a bright dimness, like looking through waxed paper. Above the depression, the sky is presumably blue or grey or whatever the sky is being that day, but from inside Veyl you see only a diffuse pale ceiling at the height of the surrounding trees, which are enormous. They are *Ficus veylensis*, a variety that does not exist outside this basin, or if it does, it has not been recorded. They grow from the edges of the depression and lean inward, their canopies overlapping in a loose but effective dome that filters the already-diffuse light down to a greenish grey, an aquarium light, the light of a place that is always underwater even when it isn't. The leaves are broad, dark, and waxy, and they drip. Not from rain — the dripping is mechanical, automatic, the leaves condensing moisture from the saturated air and channeling it down their stems to their roots. The trees drink the sky. They are the largest living things here, and they are the reason the built structures have not been torn apart by roots: the ficus roots grow laterally, shallowly, spreading across the ground in thick serpentine cables that go around and over obstacles rather than through them. The result is a landscape where the ground itself appears to be moving, frozen in mid-surge, cables of root crossing the paths and the open ground between buildings like the rigging of a ship laid flat.

---

Between the structures — the pump house, the pump operator's residence, the two storage sheds, the old control room that is now used for nothing but still has its panel of Bakelite switches and dials on the wall, green with verdigris — between these, there is space. Open, wet, flat space. The ground is paved in places with old brick, now mossed and shifted, and in other places it is bare earth, dark and granular, the kind of soil that stays wet without becoming mud because the organic content is so high it acts like a sponge. The vegetation here is lush but not crowded. It grows in deliberate-seeming clumps: a stand of ferns against the east wall of the pump house, their fronds waist-high and beaded with condensation. A colony of something that looks like moss but is not moss — it is a vascular plant with leaves the size and shape of a thumbnail, packed so densely it forms a carpet — colonizing the flat roof of the nearer storage shed. Climbing vines on the retaining wall, but only on the south face, as if they have made a collective decision about exposure. The effect is of a garden that has been designed by a process that does not include designers: each plant where it can thrive, each gap between plants a site where, for now, nothing has succeeded in establishing.

There are no animals. None. This is the strangeness you notice second, after the sound. You hear the engines and the dripping and the hiss of water moving through pipes, you feel the heat and the wet, and then you realize what is missing: no birds, no insects, no frogs in the sump, no gnats in the air, no skinks on the warm brick. Nothing with eyes or legs or wings. The absence is total and it is conspicuous because the place otherwise feels so *alive* — the plants, the water, the mechanical processes all humming with activity — but the animal niche is simply empty, a hole in the ecosystem the shape of everything that moves and breathes and eats other living things. The people who live here are the sole exceptions, and they do not count; they are visitors, maintenance staff, not fauna.

What has replaced the animals — or what fills the role that animals would fill, if the thought of replacement applies — is movement. The place is in constant motion. Water moves. The trees' leaves shift in the thermal updrafts that rise from the warm ground, creating a slow circling of air in the basin, a lazy convection that makes the hanging condensation drift in wide spirals. The pumps cycle. The fourth engine kicks in with a shudder you feel through your feet, and the pipes tick as the pressure changes, and the sump water, which is clear and dark-tea-colored, rises half a centimeter and then falls again as the additional pump catches the load. The ficus roots are not actually moving, but they look like they are, the way a frozen river looks like it is moving. The ferns unroll new fronds at a rate you can almost see, the climate here so constant and so optimal that growth is not seasonal but continuous, a slow green explosion held at the threshold of perception.

---

There is one more thing. The sump itself, the central cistern, has something growing in it that should not be there and that no one in Veyl will explain to you clearly. From the walkway that runs along its edge — an iron walkway, grilled, the grilles filled with leaf litter and small standing pools — you can look down into the water, which is dark but not opaque, and see, on the bottom, a mat of something that is not algae, not a plant, not a fungus, not anything that the locals have been able to name. It is white. It grows in radial patterns, like a crust, covering the concrete floor of the sump in branching fractal filaments that glow faintly in the dim light. When the pumps cycle and the water level shifts, the mat pulses — expands and contracts slightly, rhythmically, as if it is breathing, or as if it is responsive to pressure in a way that suggests a membrane rather than a colony. It is the color of milk. It is the color of bone. The water that flows over it is clearer than the water that flows in.

The operators have been told to leave it alone. The instructions are old — handwritten, filed in the control room in a folder that has been wet and dried so many times the paper has the texture of parchment — and they say only: *Do not disturb the substrate. Note any changes. Report annually.* No reports have been filed in over a decade. The substrate has not changed. It breathes, or seems to. The water passes over it and comes out clean.

---

You stand on the iron walkway. The engines hum their chord. The air deposits small cold drops on your skin from the condensation that forms on every surface, including you. The ficus leaves turn in their slow spiral above. The light is green and low and even and has no source you can identify — it comes from everywhere and nowhere, filtered through haze and canopy until it is just the ambient condition of being here, the light-equivalent of the sound, something you are inside rather than something you see. The white mat breathes below. The water moves. The place does not need you and is not hostile to you and does not care, and this — the profound indifference of a system that is complete without animal life, that runs on heat and water and engines and growth and something white at the bottom of a sump — is what stays with you after you climb back up the road, out of the basin, into the ordinary dry air above, where you can hear birds again and the sky is just the sky and the sound of Veyl is only a memory that will hum in your dreams for the next three nights before it fades.
