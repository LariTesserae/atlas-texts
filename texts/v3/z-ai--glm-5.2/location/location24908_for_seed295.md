# The калькаmere

It begins as sound before it begins as place.

You hear it long before you reach it — a low, continuous roar that you mistake at first for wind, then for water, then for something mechanical, and then finally for what it is: voices. Tens of millions of voices. A sustained, layered, unbroken wall of biological sound that rises from the landscape like heat from pavement. It is not one sound but so many sounds compressed together that the ear stops trying to separate them. Chittering. Clicking. Deep rhythmic thrumming. High keening whistles that might be insects or might be amphibians or might be something in between. A bass note that you feel in your sternum before you hear it, pulsing slowly, once every several seconds, as if something very large were breathing or speaking at a frequency just at the edge of perception. Over it all, a constant dry rattling like rain on leaf litter — but it is not rain. It is movement. Legs and wings and bodies shifting across surfaces, over and across and against each other, a ceaseless friction of life against life against life.

Then the place itself.

---

You are high. You know this before you see it — the air has that thin quality, that slight deficit that makes you aware of your own breathing. But it is not cold. The warmth is pervasive and wet, clinging to your skin, carrying the smell of something organic and fermenting, sweet and rotten simultaneously, like fruit left too long in a damp room. The warmth rises from below and from the surfaces around you. It is the warmth of decomposition, of respiration, of countless bodies generating heat in an enclosed space.

Because this is an enclosed space. Or nearly so.

You are standing on a surface that is not ground. It is not rock, not soil, not wood, not metal. It is *made* — unmistakably, pervasively made — but made the way a reef is made, the way a termite mound is made, the way a coral atoll is made: by accumulation, by secretion, by the patient labor of organisms across spans of time that dwarf any individual life. The surface beneath your feet is hard but slightly yielding, with a texture like very dense cartilage or old bone gone green with biofilm. It is warm to the touch. It is damp. A film of condensation coats everything, collecting in the low places, running in thin trickles along shallow grooves that seem too regular to be accidental — channels, perhaps, or the architectural remnants of something that was once functional and is now simply part of the structure.

The structure extends in every direction.

It rises around you in great curved walls that arch overhead and meet — or nearly meet — far above in the darkness, forming a canopy or a roof or a vault, depending on which metaphor you reach for. None of them are quite right. The walls are thick, layered, built up over what must be geological time into strata of material that range from pale and chalky to dark and almost woody. In places the layers have eroded into overhangs and shelves and shallow caves. In places they bulge outward as if something grew behind them and the growth was incorporated rather than rejected. The walls are not smooth. They are textured at every scale — ridged, pitted, perforated with holes ranging from the size of a pinhead to the size of a doorway, each hole its own microenvironment, each ridge its own coastline.

And on every surface, in every hole, along every ridge and shelf and overhang: life.

---

The vegetation is the first thing you process because it is what you can see, even in the low light. The light here is dim and indirect, a greenish-gray glow that seems to come from everywhere and nowhere — perhaps bioluminescence, perhaps light filtering through the structure from some distant opening far above, perhaps some combination. It is enough to see by, barely, after your eyes adjust. And what you see is green. Green in every direction, green in every shade from near-black to a pallid yellow-green that looks almost fluorescent in the gloom.

The plants — if that is the right word; they may be something else, something that has converged on the same solutions — grow in dense mats and hanging curtains and tangled masses that cover the walls, drape from the ceiling, carpet the floor. They are not like forest plants. They are flattened, compressed, as if the density of this place has forced them to grow in two dimensions rather than three. They cling to surfaces. They layer over each other, each layer a slightly different shade, a slightly different texture, a slightly different leaf shape. Some are broad and flat, maximizing surface area for whatever light they can catch. Some are thin and wiry, reaching out into the open air like antennae. Some are nothing but crusts and films, barely distinguishable from the surface they grow on.

They are wet. Everything is wet. The condensation is constant — warm, moist air rising from below meets the cooler surfaces of the structure and deposits a fine layer of water that never quite dries. It beads on the leaves. It runs down the stems. It collects in the channels and grooves and flows in thin, slow streams toward lower ground. The air itself is close to saturation. You can feel it on your face, in your lungs. You are breathing water as much as air.

And from this moisture, the plants have built something exuberant. Not a forest — too dense, too dark, too horizontal. More like a terrarium overgrown, a greenhouse abandoned to its own devices for centuries. The growth is lush but constrained, filling every available niche, competing for every square centimeter of surface. Where two surfaces meet — wall and floor, shelf and wall, root and stone — the vegetation is thickest, piled up in drifts and ridges of living material, layer upon layer, the oldest layers dying and composting beneath the newest, feeding them, the whole system recycling its own mass with an efficiency that borders on the mechanical.

But the plants are only the visible architecture. The living architecture. Beneath them, around them, through them, the fauna moves.

---

They are everywhere.

This is the thing that takes time to understand, because your mind will not accept it at first. You see a surface and you think: surface. Rock, or chitin, or whatever this material is. And then the surface moves. Not all of it. Not dramatically. But a patch of it shifts, and you realize it was not surface at all but animals — animals pressed so close together, so tightly layered, that they formed a continuous covering. A living skin over the living wall.

The insects come first in your perception because they are the most numerous and the most active. They are small, most of them, the size of rice grains or smaller, and they move with a ceaseless, purposeful energy that is different from the frantic randomness you associate with insects. These move in patterns. Streams of them flow along the grooves in the walls, following the channels of water, pausing at the wet places, drinking or feeding or doing something you cannot identify. Others climb the vegetation in columns, single file, each one touching the one ahead of it with its antennae, a living procession that extends from the floor to the ceiling and beyond, disappearing into a hole in the upper darkness. The sound of their movement is part of the great roar — the dry rattling, the clicking of millions of legs on millions of surfaces.

But the insects are only the beginning.

There are larger things moving in the vegetation. You see them as disturbances — a leaf mat bulges and something slides beneath it, something smooth and dark, leaving a wake of displaced growth. A curtain of hanging plants sways and you catch a glimpse of a body, jointed, many-legged, as long as your forearm, clinging to the underside of a shelf with pale appendages that grip and release and grip again as it moves. It is not aggressive. It is not interested in you. It is going somewhere, following some imperative you cannot perceive, and it passes from view into a tangle of growth and is gone.

There are things in the air, too — or in the space between the walls, the open volume that you are standing in. They are hard to see in the dim light. You become aware of them by their sounds: sharp clicks, like snapping fingers, coming from different directions at different intervals. Echolocation, perhaps. Or communication. Or both. Occasionally something brushes past your face — a wing, a membrane, something dry and papery and alive — and is gone before you can focus on it. The air is full of these things. You realize that the space above you, which you took for empty darkness, is in fact a highway, a flyway, a corridor crowded with bodies moving through the gloom on errands of their own.

On the floor, in the channels where the water runs, there are other things. Soft things. Things that move slowly, with a muscular undulation, through the shallow streams. They are pale — white or gray or faintly pink — and they are shaped like nothing you have a ready word for. Slugs, perhaps, if slugs were the size of your hand and had a complexity of form that suggested something more sophisticated. They cluster at the wet places, dozens of them, their bodies overlapping, their movements synchronized in some way you cannot parse. They make a sound — a low, wet, rhythmic pulsing — that you think may be the bass note you feel in your chest.

And there are more. There are always more. Every surface you examine closely resolves into a community. The holes in the walls contain organisms — some visible, some detectable only by the faint glow they emit, a blue-green light that pulses slowly, illuminating the interior of the hole for a moment before fading. The dead layers beneath the vegetation are full of things that feed on decomposition — things with pale, soft bodies and no eyes, things that move with a slow, deliberate patience through the compost, breaking it down, processing it, returning its nutrients to the system. The ceiling has its own fauna, hanging upside down, clustered in masses that look like stalactites but are not stalactites. They are quiet. They are still. But they are alive, and when the condensation drips onto them, they move — slowly, almost imperceptibly — toward the water.

---

The place has rules. You begin to perceive them after a time.

The first rule is layering. Everything in this place exists in layers — the structure itself, the vegetation, the fauna, the moisture, the sound. Nothing occupies a single plane. Everything is stacked, compressed, overlapping. The walls have depth. The vegetation has depth. Even the animal communities have depth: on the surface of a leaf mat, a layer of small insects; beneath them, a layer of larger arthropods; beneath those, a layer of decomposers working through the dead material; and beneath all of it, the structure itself, which is also layered, which is also, on close inspection, alive, or was once alive, or is made from things that were once alive. The boundaries between layers are not sharp. They blur into each other. The living and the dead, the plant and the animal, the structure and the inhabitant — all of it shades gradually into everything else. This is a place without clean categories.

The second rule is flow. Water flows downward through the channels. Air flows upward — you can feel it, a slow, warm current rising from below, carrying moisture and smell and the faintest particles of biological matter. The insects flow along their paths, following the water and the air. The larger animals flow through the vegetation, following routes that are invisible to you but clearly established, worn smooth by passage. The nutrients flow from dead to living, from living to dead, in a cycle so tight that nothing is wasted, nothing is lost, everything is incorporated. The sound flows upward, too, accumulating, layering, until it becomes a single thing — not many sounds but one sound, the sound of the place itself, the sound of all of its processes happening at once.

The third rule is patience. Nothing here is fast. The structure was built slowly, over time spans that you cannot easily imagine, by organisms that lived and died and left their secretions and their shells and their bodies behind, and those accumulations became the walls, the floor, the ceiling. The vegetation grows slowly, in the low light, adding layer after layer over generations. The animals move with a purpose that is not urgency — they are going where they need to go, but they are not hurrying. The condensation forms and falls and forms again in a cycle that has no beginning and no end. The erosion — for there is erosion, slow and steady — wears at the surfaces, rounds the edges, deepens the channels, opens new holes, and the life that fills this place adapts to each change as it comes, over decades, over centuries, over longer.

The fourth rule is that everything is made. There is no bedrock here, no natural stone, no pre-existing surface. The structure is entirely constructed — built by life, from life, for life. It is a reef, in the broadest sense: a biogenic construct, an architecture of accumulation. And it is old. It has been here, growing, eroding, growing again, for longer than anything in it can remember. It is in its own state — settled, weathered, balanced. The forces that built it and the forces that wear it away have reached an equilibrium. The place changes, but slowly, and within limits. It is not growing larger. It is not collapsing. It simply is, and has been, and will be, in the way that a mature forest is, or a coral reef is, or a cliff face is — not static, but complete.

---

You try to take in the scale of it and fail.

The space you are standing in — the main chamber, if that is the right word — extends perhaps a hundred meters in each direction, its walls curving up and inward to form a vault that you cannot see the top of. But this is only one space. The walls are perforated with openings — holes and tunnels and passages — that lead to other spaces. You can see some of them: dim chambers beyond the holes, lit by the same faint glow, filled with the same dense vegetation, the same teeming fauna. And beyond those, presumably, more. The structure is regional in extent. It covers an area that you would need hours to traverse, a complex of connected chambers and passages and shafts, a vast interior landscape built by accumulation, inhabited by an ecosystem so dense and so interconnected that it functions as a single organism, or nearly so.

You are inside something. That is the feeling that grows on you, slowly, as you stand there. You are inside something vast and alive and old. Not inside it as a guest, not inside it as an invader, but inside it the way a bacterium is inside a gut, the way a mite is inside a pore — as a presence in a system that does not require you, that does not notice you, that was here long before you arrived and will be here long after you leave. The roar of the life around you continues unchanged. The condensation falls on your skin. The insects walk past your feet without pausing. Something brushes your face again — that dry, papery wing — and is gone.

You are in a place that has no use for you.

And somehow that is the most remarkable thing about it. This is not a place that was made for anything outside itself. It was made by its inhabitants, for its inhabitants, and its inhabitants are so numerous, so various, so thoroughly adapted to this place and to each other, that the whole system is self-sustaining, self-repairing, complete. It does not need light from outside. It does not need water from outside. It does not need anything from outside. The warmth comes from within — from the metabolic heat of the organisms themselves, from the composting of the dead layers, from the slow geochemical processes in the deep structure. The water comes from within — from respiration, from condensation, from the cycling of moisture through bodies and surfaces and air. The food comes from within — everything eats something else here, and everything is eaten by something else, and the chain is closed, the circle complete.

You realize, standing there in the greenish dark, that this is what total construction looks like when there is no technology. No tools. No intention beyond the immediate imperatives of survival and reproduction. Just life, building, over time, at scale, until the building becomes the landscape, and the landscape becomes the world, and the world becomes self-sufficient, and the self-sufficient world becomes old, and the old world becomes permanent, or as permanent as anything living can be.

The sound does not diminish. It has not diminished since you arrived. It will not diminish when you leave. It is the sound of the place being itself, and it is deafening, and it is ordinary, and it has been going on for a very long time.

You turn to leave, and something small lands on your hand — a tiny arthropod, pale, with too many legs to count in the dim light. It sits there for a moment, antennae working, testing your skin, tasting the salt of you. Then it walks off your hand and onto the wall and disappears into a crack and is gone.

The place continues without you.
