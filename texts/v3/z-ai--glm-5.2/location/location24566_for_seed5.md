# The Hollow of Estaur

You descend into it. There is no other way in.

A switchback stair, shoulder-width, cut into tuff and reinforced with fitted stones that have been there so long they've become the color of the cliff itself. The walls on either side are close. Ferns grow from the seams between stones, their fronds stiff with cold moisture, brushed by your passage. You go down. The sky narrows to a band. Then the sky disappears.

And then — light. From below, from around the final bend, a brightness that makes you squint after the dim descent. You step out onto a ledge and the whole place opens beneath you.

Estaur sits in a collapsed volcanic caldera, roughly oval, perhaps four hundred meters across at its widest. The walls drop sheer on three sides, tiered with structures, and slope more gently on the fourth where a debris cone was colonized centuries ago and is now a cascade of terraced gardens. The floor of the caldera is flat, built upon, built upon again, built upon that. It is a city that grew inward and downward rather than outward, filling its own bowl.

The light comes from the floor. Or rather, it comes from the material of the floor.

The caldera's bed is a pansar — a mineral crust, pale cream and faintly translucent, that forms over geologic time in the specific conditions of collapsed silica-rich magma chambers exposed to cold surface water. Estaur's pansar is thick, meters deep, and where the cold groundwater percolates upward through hairline fractures, the mineral surface catches daylight and diffuses it, reradiates it, so that the caldera floor glows with a soft, even luminescence. Not bioluminescence. Not fire. A stony phosphorescence, cold to the touch, that brightens with ambient light and dims at dusk but never fully extinguishes. On overcast days the glow is subtle. On the bright days — and most days here are bright, the caldera's walls funneling sunlight down to the floor — the whole basin fills with light that seems to come from everywhere at once. No shadows fall at right angles here. Shadows are soft, multidirectional, shallow.

The air is cold. Not the cold of altitude — Estaur is low, barely above the regional riverplain — but the cold of trapped density. Cold air pools in the caldera. It drains down the walls at night and collects at the floor, and because the pansar radiates light but not heat, because the walls block wind, because the place is geometrically a bowl, the temperature stays low. You can see your breath most of the day. Frost forms on surfaces that face the predawn sky, melts by midmorning, reforms in the evening. A cycle so regular the residents time their activities around it without thinking.

The dampness is constant but not heavy. It arrives as condensation — warm humid air from outside the caldera, from the riverplain below, drifts up and over the rim, meets the cold interior air, and drops its moisture onto every surface. Stone walls weep. Walkways are slick by midday. Wooden beams grow soft and dark with age, not rotting — the cold slows decay dramatically — but taking on a texture like wet leather. Rain comes perhaps twice a month, usually brief, usually fine. You might not notice it until you're already wet. The real water is in the air, in the stone, in the moss.

The vegetation is the first thing that strikes you after the light. It shouldn't be here. Not this dense, not this green, not at this temperature. But the moisture and the light together create something the plains outside cannot sustain. Every horizontal surface is claimed. Every wall has moss, lichen, liverwort. The terraced gardens on the debris cone are stacked — food crops, root vegetables, cold-hardy grains — but between and around them, climbing over the terrace walls, threading through the garden stakes, is a riot of ferns, clubmoss, horsetail, things that belong to an earlier era of the world and have found here a refuge from the drying plains. The caldera's interior, away from the gardens, is forest. Low forest, dense forest, canopy at perhaps five meters, trees with dark gnarled bark and small waxy leaves that drip constantly. The roots of these trees have cracked the pansar in places, and from those cracks the groundwater seeps, pools, feeds the growth. The relationship is ancient. The trees broke the floor. The floor-water fed the trees. Neither one happened first.

And into this — the city.

Estaur has perhaps three hundred residents, perhaps more. Counting is difficult because the city is vertical. Structures are built into the caldera walls — excavated rooms with stone facades, connected by ladders and narrow staircases and occasional bridges where a ledge allows. On the floor, the buildings are more conventional but so closely packed that you move through them by alley rather than street. The alleys are paved with fitted stone, drained by shallow channels cut into the surface, and most are barely wider than your shoulders. The buildings are stone, mortared with a pale clay that matches the pansar, roofed with wooden shingles that are black with moisture and furred with moss. Some have second stories, accessed by external stairs. A few have third stories, built back into the cliff so that the rear wall is raw rock, weeping and fern-hung.

The construction is not crude. It is careful, deliberate, and old. The stonework shows chisel marks — hand tools, no metal harder than bronze. Doorframes are fitted with precision. The mortar is mixed with crushed pansar, which gives it a faint glow at night, so that the alleys are navigable in darkness by the light of the walls themselves. Windows are narrow, shuttered, designed to retain warmth. The people here wear layered wool and oiled leather and thick-soled boots and move through the cold with the ease of long practice.

There is a central space, roughly in the caldera's geometric center, where the buildings pull back and the pansar is exposed and unbroken — a plaza of sorts, roughly thirty meters across, glowing faintly, its surface worn smooth by generations of feet. A well sits at its center, a square stone housing over a natural crack in the pansar where the water rises clear and cold. This is the public space. The meeting place. The market, when there is one — produce from the terraces, fish from the pools that form in the forested cracks, worked stone and wood and fiber. Trade is conducted in near-silence. People set out their goods. Others examine them. Exchanges are made with hand signals, with nods, with the occasional murmured word. The economy runs on established relationships and shared understanding. New visitors are tolerated but watched.

The quiet is the thing you notice most, after the light.

It is not absolute silence. Water drips. Your boots scrape on stone. A bird calls, somewhere up in the canopy on the debris cone — a short, clear note, repeated, territorial. Somewhere a child murmurs to herself. But these sounds are discrete, individual, separated by silence. There is no ambient hum of a city. No traffic, no machinery, no wind funneling through streets — the walls block the wind. The air is still. Sound carries well across distance — the caldera's shape is a natural amphitheater — but it is also absorbed by moss, by wet wood, by the soft forest floor, so that what reaches you is clear but faint, a place composed of individual sounds in space rather than the continuous murmur of habitation. People speak quietly here, and have done so long enough that it is simply how people speak. A raised voice would echo from the walls and would feel, to a resident, like a breach.

The fauna has its own logic. The caldera is an island, ecologically, and the things that live here have adapted to its specifics. The birds are small, ground-dwelling, flightless or near-flightless — a species of rail that nests in the ferns and forages among the terrace gardens, tolerated because they eat pests. They are unafraid of people. They walk between your feet. In the forest, a type of small deer — brought in long ago as livestock, gone feral, now a managed wild population — moves in loose herds, browsing the undergrowth. They are dark-coated, compact, their breath visible. Insects are present but not overwhelming — the cold limits them. Moths with pale wings that settle on the glowing pansar at dusk. Beetles in the moss. A type of cricket that chirps so quietly you have to stop breathing to hear it.

There is one thing that is harder to categorize.

In the deeper cracks of the pansar, where the mineral crust has fractured and the groundwater pools, something grows that is neither plant nor animal nor fungus. The residents call it *savar*. It appears as a pale, fibrous mat on the surface of the water, like felt, like coarse paper. It does not move. It does not respond to touch. It grows slowly, over months, and then dies back, and then grows again. The residents harvest it, dry it, and use it — pressed into sheets for writing, woven into rough cloth, layered with clay for building material. It is useful. It is also, in some way that the residents do not fully articulate and may not fully understand, aware. Not conscious. Not responsive. But when you stand near the pool where the savar grows, there is a quality to the silence — a texture, a pressure, a feeling of being regarded by something that has no eyes. The residents ignore this. They have lived with it for longer than their records extend. They harvest it, they use it, they give thanks in a perfunctory way, and they move on. You learn to do the same.

The place does not change. This is the most striking thing about it, after a week, after a month. The light is the same each morning. The temperature is the same each afternoon. The frost comes, the frost goes. The water seeps. The birds walk between feet that have been walking these alleys for generations. The buildings settle imperceptibly into the stone. The mortar glows at night. The savar grows and dies and grows. The people speak quietly, and have always spoken quietly, and will always speak quietly, in a bowl of light at the bottom of the world, where the air is cold and the green things grow and the stone itself remembers the light and gives it back, and the only direction is down.
