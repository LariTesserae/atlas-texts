# The Drowned Courtyard of Ald Meer

It sits on the shoulder of the plateau where the land used to drop away into a river valley that is now nowhere and everywhere. The settlement was small — perhaps thirty structures, perhaps forty, hard to count now because the walls between them have become ambiguous. Stone foundations, hand-laid, river cobble set in clay mortar. No metal. The joints have softened but held. Above those foundations, everything is plant.

Not draped in plant. Not colonized by plant. *Made* of plant, the way a reef is made of coral — the living layer is the structure now. What were posts are columns of woody algal trunk, layered over generations, each frond dying and hardening into a fibrous bark while the next grows outward from the core. Doorways are arched by the meeting of opposing root systems that found each other in the murk and fused. Roof beams are gone entirely, replaced by interlocking canopy fronds of broad-leafed aquatic plants that have grown so dense they form a thatch nearly opaque from below — green-black, letting through a filtered, even light that falls without direction, the way light falls in a cathedral.

The water is cool. Not cold — cool the way a spring is cool in autumn, a steady twelve or thirteen degrees that never seems to change. It is clear enough to see across the entire settlement from one edge, which matters because the settlement is small. You can stand at the eastern wall and see the western wall through forty meters of water and intervening architecture. The plateau drops on all sides into deeper blue-green, and the settlement sits at the top like a crown on a head, which is essentially what it is — the highest point of something that was once above water and is now below it, by maybe eight meters, maybe ten. Shallow enough that daylight arrives in full. Normal daylight, refracted into planes and shifting bands by the surface above, but normal. You can see color. You can see distance.

---

The architecture is organized. Whatever this place was before the water, it was built with intention. There is a central space — a courtyard, roughly rectangular, paved in flat stones that are now carpeted in a short velvety moss. The moss is dark green, almost black, and it moves. Not with current but with its own slow breathing rhythm, the thousands of tiny fronds expanding and contracting in a cycle that takes perhaps two minutes. This is the slowest motion in the place and it sets the tempo. Nothing here is in a hurry.

Around the courtyard, structures radiate in a pattern that suggests planning — three on the north side, three on the south, two larger ones on the east and west ends. The gaps between them are wide. This was never a dense settlement. The builders left room. Room for what, it's impossible to say now — perhaps for expansion, perhaps for gardens, perhaps simply because they liked space and didn't fear the open. The gaps are now channels of open water, four or five meters across, floored with pale sand that the slow currents keep riffled into low parallel ridges, like the texture of corduroy. The channels are mostly empty. A few suspended particles drift through them. Occasionally a fish.

The fish are everywhere and they are the loudest thing here.

---

Sound underwater is not what you'd expect if you've only known it from above. There is no muffling. Sound travels fast and far and arrives with presence. The settlement is full of it. The fish — a species that has been here long enough to have a name in whatever language was spoken when the place was dry, and which now has no name or too many — are the size of a human hand, flat-bodied, silver-blue, and they grind their pharyngeal teeth constantly. Each one alone is a small sound, a click-rasp like a fingernail on coarse fabric. But there are hundreds. Thousands. They live in the plant-walls, in the gaps between frond layers, in the moss, in the sand when they sleep. The aggregate sound is a sustained, textured roar that fills the water from all directions equally. It is not harsh. It is full. It sounds like the settlement is breathing through gritted teeth, or like rain on a tin roof heard from inside the house, except the house is the rain.

Over that baseline, other sounds arrive and depart. The broad-leafed canopy plants flex in the slow current that passes over the plateau every few minutes — a surge from the north, then a pause, then a surge from the south. The fronds knock against each other with a sound like wooden clappers, hollow, resonant, rhythmic but not musical. It is the closest thing the place has to a clock. Every third or fourth surge, a section of older frond detaches and drifts free, and there is a tearing sound, slow and fibrous, like ripping heavy cloth. Then the piece tumbles away over the edge of the plateau and is gone.

Something larger lives in the two big buildings at the east and west ends. You can hear it — a low pulse, felt more than heard, a pressure wave that arrives every ten or twelve seconds. It might be a gill mechanism. It might be a digestive process. It does not sound distressed. It sounds like the building itself is a lung, inhaling and exhaling water through some internal architecture that the plant growth has sealed and shaped into a chamber. Whatever is in there, it does not come out, and nothing else goes in. The fish give those structures a wide margin.

---

The courtyard is the place where things happen, though "happen" is relative in a place where the dynamics are this slow. The moss breathes. The sand ridges shift over hours. The light changes as the surface above responds to wind that this place will never feel — brightening, dimming, the bands of refracted light sweeping across the courtyard floor like slow searchlights. Small invertebrates — not quite shrimp, not quite isopods, something flattened and translucent with too many legs — move across the moss in groups, grazing. They leave pale trails where the moss is momentarily scraped clean, and the trails vanish within minutes as the moss recovers. The fish descend from the walls to pick at what the grazers have stirred up. There is a constant, low-level negotiation happening in the courtyard, territorial, polite, conducted at the speed of swimming and the speed of breathing.

In the exact center of the courtyard, there is a stone that is different from the others. It is darker, denser, set slightly higher. It was placed there on purpose. It may have been a shrine base or a meeting point or a place to lay out goods for trade or a place to sit. Now it has a single plant growing from a crack in its surface — a slender stem, pale as bone, rising about thirty centimeters before opening into a flat circular pad the size of a dinner plate. The pad is oriented horizontally, catching the overhead light. At its center, a small depression holds a pocket of finer sediment than the surrounding sand, and in that sediment, something is growing that has not yet become identifiable. It is a knot, a nubbin, a possibility. It has been there for weeks or months. It may be there for years. In this place, that is not a long time.

---

The settlement has been in this state for a long time. The erosion parameter — the balance point — is visible everywhere. The stone foundations are not crumbling. They are not pristine. They are *settled*, the way a stone in a riverbed is settled: shaped by its environment into a form that the environment will maintain indefinitely. The mortar between cobbles has been replaced by a calcareous deposit laid down by the moss, so the walls are now bio-mineral composite, neither fully stone nor fully plant. The plant structures are not growing outward. They are growing in place — replacing themselves layer by layer, the outer fronds dying and hardening while new growth emerges from within. The settlement has reached its shape and will hold it. The boundaries are the boundaries. The channels will stay channels. The courtyard will stay open. This is not a place in transition. It is a place in equilibrium, and it has been so for longer than anyone above the water remembers it being anything else.

The water carries a taste. Mineral, vegetal, faintly sweet — the sweetness of plant decay filtered through limestone, which is what the plateau is, underneath the sand and the moss and the stone foundations. Limestone that was once a seabed, then land, then a village site, then a seabed again, in a cycle so slow that the village is just a moment in it, a brief interruption of moss and cobble in the long conversation between stone and water.

---

If you were here — and you can be here, in the way that imagining puts you anywhere — you would feel the cool water against your skin as a constant, gentle pressure, not unpleasant, like being held. You would hear the grinding of ten thousand small mouths and the knocking of the canopy and the deep pulse from the buildings at either end, and you would understand that the settlement is not quiet, has never been quiet, is in fact one of the louder places you have ever been, but that the loudness is so steady and so complete that it becomes a kind of silence — a silence made of sound, the way a white wall is a kind of blankness made of paint. You would see the light moving on the courtyard floor. You would see the pale stem in the center with its flat pad and its small cargo of potential. You would feel the slow surge of the current and the flex of the canopy in response, and you would know that this place will look essentially like this tomorrow, and next season, and for years after that, changing only in the small ways — a frond replaced, a trail scraped and healed, a fish born or dead, the nubbin in the center becoming something or not.

The settlement does not need anything. It is not waiting. It is not ruined and it is not preserved. It is simply still here, still itself, at the top of the plateau, under the shallow water, in the cool light, loud and slow and alive.
