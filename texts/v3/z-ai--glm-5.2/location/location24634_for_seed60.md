# The Vatspring

You hear it before you see it. A low, rhythmic thudding — deep, mechanical, patient — married to a constant wet hiss. The sound rolls through warm, heavy air and collects in your chest.

The space is no larger than a chapel nave, but it feels infinite because there is no empty surface to rest your eyes on. Everything is occupied. Everything is growing.

**The substrate is plant.** Not plants growing *on* something — the ground itself is a mat of living tissue, a braided centuries-thick weave of root and rhizome and creeping stem that holds your weight like a firm mattress. It gives slightly with each step and springs back. Beneath it, water moves. You can feel it — a slow percolation, an underground seep — and in places the mat darkens and the water surfaces in clear shallow pools no wider than your hand, then sinks back under. The whole floor breathes a faint tidal pulse, though whether from the water table or the roots themselves drawing and releasing, you cannot tell.

**The walls are plant too.** Great curving buttresses of woody vine, each one as thick as a human torso, have grown in deliberate arcs — trained, or perhaps they trained themselves — into a partial enclosure. Between the vine-ribs, a dense mesh of thinner growth fills every gap: mosses, liverworts, fan-shaped bracket fungi, translucent filaments that catch light like fiberglass. The density is absolute. There is no view through the walls. There is no wall that is not also alive. Condensation forms on every surface and runs down in continuous threads, so that the interior weeps. The air is saturated. Warm — not hot, but close, like the inside of a closed mouth. Your skin films over within minutes.

**And threaded through all of it: machines.**

They are old. Not ruined — *integrated*. A network of iron pipes, some thumb-thick, some arm-thick, runs through the vine-structure like veins through muscle. The vines have grown around them, into them; where corrosion has opened a pipe, roots have entered and threaded the interior, so that in cross-section one might not distinguish plant from conduit. At intervals — four, five, six places you can count — mechanical units sit half-swallowed by growth. A pump housing with a flywheel still turning, slowly, so slowly, driven by something. A pressure gauge with its face clouded to jade-green by algal film, the needle trembling at a reading you cannot interpret. A valve assembly that clicks open every forty or fifty seconds and releases a gust of steam that condenses instantly into a cloud that settles on the surrounding leaves and rolls down them in beads.

The thudding is loudest here. It comes from below — from beneath the root-mat — where something large and mechanical cycles with the patience of geology. You feel it in your feet more than you hear it with your ears. *Thud. Thud. Thud.* The mat shudders each time. The pools ripple. Water that has collected in the shallow depression of a broad leaf spills over. The sound has been happening, you suspect, for longer than anyone has been counting.

**The relationship between the machines and the plants is not parasitic.** It is not clear that it is symbiotic, either. It is something older than either category — a settled state, an equilibrium reached so long ago that the boundary between the mechanical system and the biological system has dissolved. The pipes carry water that the roots have filtered. The machines pump that water upward through the vine-structure, and it emerges from breaks and joints as a fine mist that settles on leaf surfaces and is absorbed. The cycle is slow. A given molecule of water might take days to move from root-pool to pipe to mist to leaf to condensation to ground to root again. The machines run on what? You cannot find a power source. Perhaps the mechanical motion is driven by the slow hydraulic pressure of the root system itself — the thudding pump powered by the very water it moves. Perhaps something else. The system has the feel of a clock that no one winds but which nonetheless keeps time.

**There are marks of human presence.** A rusted bracket bolted to a vine-buttress, holding nothing now. A fragment of walkway — iron grating, two feet by three — suspended at waist height by vines that have grown around its edges and lifted it, or perhaps lowered it, into its current position. A stenciled number, long faded, on the housing of the pump: something that begins with **4** and ends in nothing legible. Someone built here once. Someone installed the machines. Someone trained the first vines, perhaps, or perhaps only failed to prevent what the vines were going to do regardless. The built elements are remnants — fossils of intent embedded in a living matrix that has moved on.

**The light is ordinary.** It comes from above, filtered through a partial canopy of broad leaves that form a ceiling roughly fifteen feet up. It is daylight — gray-white, diffuse — and it reaches the floor in a shifting dapple that moves when the canopy moves, which is rarely. The leaves are thick and waxy and hold their own weight of water. Occasionally one tilts and dumps its load, and a bright splash of light follows the falling water down.

**Fauna is almost absent.** You see no insects. No birds call. Once, in a pool at the base of a pipe junction, you notice something move — a pale, legless thing, the length of your little finger, translucent, drifting in the slow current. It could be an amphibian larva. It could be something else. It sinks into the root-mat and is gone. The ecosystem is nearly pure plant-and-machine, and the absence of animals gives the space a quality of held breath — not dead, but so completely self-contained that it has no need for anything external.

**The strangeness accumulates gradually.** It is not in any single feature. The individual elements — wet plants, old pipes, warm air, mechanical rhythm — are each familiar. But the totality is not. The space has the quality of a lung that has been breathing on its own for a very long time. The thudding pump and the dripping leaves and the slow cycling of water through living and mechanical tissues together form a single organism, or a single process, and you are inside it. The vines have grown in curves that look deliberate — structural, load-bearing — but also look like the growth patterns of a plant reaching toward light that is no longer there, frozen into permanence by the sheer density of the surrounding tissue. The machines have been running so long that their motion has become indistinguishable from the motion of the plants: slow, cyclical, responsive to gradients so weak they would be invisible to any instrument short of geology.

**The sound layering is dense.** The mechanical thud from below. The hiss-click of the steam valve. The constant drip and run of condensation, which varies in pitch depending on what surface it strikes — a dull tap on iron, a soft patter on leaf, a musical note on the pool surfaces. The creak of vine-buttresses under tension as the pump cycles and the pressure in the pipes changes. A low, almost subliminal hum — electrical? Biological? — that seems to come from everywhere and nowhere. The total effect is loud in the way that a room full of conversation is loud: not painful, but saturating. There is no silence here. There has not been silence here for a very long time.

The air smells of iron and chlorophyll and warm water and something faintly sweet — a fermentation, perhaps, in the deeper layers of the root-mat where organic matter accumulates and breaks down in the heat.

You are standing inside something that does not need you. That is the final and defining quality of the place. It was not built for visitors. It does not register your presence. The pump will thud. The valve will open. The mist will settle. The water will cycle. The vines will grow, at a rate too slow to perceive, into configurations that will eventually replace the current ones. The machines will corrode and the roots will fill the spaces left behind and the system will shift and settle and continue. It has the patience of a process that has found its equilibrium and intends to maintain it indefinitely, and your presence in it is a brief and weightless interruption — a warm body adding its small heat and small moisture to a room that is already warm and wet and full.
