# The Drowned Switching Station

You descend into it without knowing you've entered.

The water changes. Not suddenly — there is no boundary line, no thermocline wall. But the open cold of the deep地方tightens around you incrementally, and after some time you realize the darkness has acquired *architecture*. The space around you has become narrower. There are walls. They are close. You are inside something.

---

It is a room the size of a cathedral transept — not grand, not vaulted, but comparable in span. A single chamber, low-ceilinged, maybe four meters overhead. The walls are riveted steel, rolled in the days when that meant something, and the rivets themselves have been consumed so thoroughly by corrosion that they've become something else — smooth black nodules like the joints of arthropods, flush with the surface, as though the metal decided to digest its own fasteners and grow a skin. The steel is thick. You can tell because the corrosion hasn't punctured it in three quarters of a century underwater, though it has eaten deep enough to leave fingerprints of texture visible to touch — pitting so regular it looks deliberate, almost machined.

The room is a switching station. Electrical. Built into the floor of what was once a river valley and is now something without a convenient name — a flooded industrial corridor, a coldwater lobe of a post-reservoir landscape. The structure predates the flood. It was built to manage power distribution for a network of facilities that no longer exist above the waterline. It still does.

---

The density here is not biological. It is mechanical. Every surface is occupied. The walls carry cable runs — thick, armoured conduits that branch and re-branch in fractal complexity, disappearing into junction boxes that look like barnacle clusters, each one welded shut decades ago and now sweating rust in slow blooms of oxide that drift downward in the still water like sediment from a wound. The ceiling is almost hidden behind a lattice of bus bars — thick copper strips, green-black with patination, carrying a charge you can feel before you can identify it: a faint pressure in the teeth, a taste of metal on the soft palate. They are still live. The station is still powered. Something upstream — a generator, a battery bank, a connection to a grid that may or may not still have human keepers — feeds current into these bars, and the bars distribute it to nodes that haven't existed in sixty years.

The floor is grating. Steel mesh, partially collapsed, through which you can see a second level below — a sump, chest-deep in silt that has the colour and consistency of wet charcoal. Cables descend into that silt and do not come back up. You learn not to stand on the grating where the corrosion has thinned it to lace.

---

The cold is total. 0.1 on any scale is the kind of cold where water is approaching its densest state — not freezing, but dense, thermally inert, holding its chill with a kind of permanence that feels almost intentional. The water here is around 2 degrees. It has been 2 degrees for a long time. You lose heat to it not rapidly but *inevitably*, the way a stone loses warmth to the earth. Your hands become clumsy. Your thoughts thin out.

There is no current. The water is still. But it is not stagnant — it is *exchanged*, slowly, through a process you don't immediately understand. The room breathes. Not metaphorically. There are vents — low, near the floor, rectangular, originally fitted with louvres that have long since rotted to lace — through which water enters at a pace so gradual you cannot feel it. And there are exits near the ceiling — pipe openings, angled upward — through which water leaves. The exchange is on the order of a full room-volume every several hours. The mechanism is thermal. The bus bars, still carrying current, leak a small amount of heat into the water. That water rises. Cold water from outside, heavier, slides in through the floor vents. It is a convection cell, a slow engine, and it has been running since before the flood. It is the reason the station is still here. The continuous exchange prevented the water inside from going anaerobic, from becoming acidic with decomposition, from eating the electronics. The room preserved itself by accident.

---

Light: almost none. What exists comes from two sources. The first is bioluminescent — faint, unreliable, and biological. A species of something — not quite bacteria, not quite archaea, something that occupies the blurry space between — has colonized the junction boxes. It emits light in brief, irregular pulses, a cold blue-green the colour of elderberries seen through ice. The pulses are not synchronized. Each junction box blinks on its own schedule, its own internal logic. The effect is like being inside a room lit by a hundred lazy, distracted fireflies. You catch glimpses. A cable run here. A corroded relay there. The far wall, for a half-second, revealed as a surface more texture than structure. Then dark again. The organism does not pulse when you are close. It seems to dislike disturbance, or perhaps it simply doesn't pulse *because* you are close — a startle response in reverse, a freezing rather than a fleeing.

The second source is the bus bars themselves. At junction points — where copper meets copper through bolted connections — there is a faint corona. Not arcing. Something subtler. A glow at the very edge of visibility, violet-grey, the colour of a bruise, that exists only at the contact points and only when you are not looking directly at them. You see it in peripheral vision, and it vanishes when you turn. It may be real. It may be your eyes inventing patterns in the darkness. You cannot determine which, and after a while you stop trying.

---

Sound: almost nothing. Water carries sound efficiently, and the station is dense with metal that should resonate, should amplify, should carry every vibration to your bones. But the world outside is quiet. No boat traffic. No surface activity. No storms strong enough to reach this depth. The silence is not absolute — it is *layered*. There is a low hum, felt more than heard, at roughly 40 hertz, which is the bus bars vibrating at their operating frequency. There is the sound of your own breathing, which becomes enormous, becomes the dominant acoustic event of the space. There is, occasionally — once every ten or fifteen minutes — a click. A relay. Somewhere in the station, an automatic switch is still cycling. It opens or closes a circuit that connects to nothing. The click travels through the water, through the steel, arrives at you as a single, clean, percussive note that has nothing following it. No consequence. Just a mechanism continuing to do what it was built to do long after the purpose for doing it has dissolved.

The silence between clicks is the loudest thing here.

---

Vegetation is almost absent. There is one exception. In a corner where two walls meet, where the grating has collapsed entirely and the silt below is exposed, there grows a single organism that might be plant, might be something else. It is a filamentous structure — pale, translucent, the colour of skim milk — that extends upward from the silt in a thin, branching frond, maybe thirty centimeters tall. It has no leaves. It has no visible means of photosynthesis, and indeed there is no light sufficient for photosynthesis here. It is, you realize, feeding on the current. Its base is wrapped around a cable that descends into the silt, and it is drawing something from that cable — electricity, or the chemical products of the cable's slow decomposition — and building itself out of that. It is the only living thing in the station that is larger than a bacterium, and it has no business existing here. It has existed here, judging by the thickness of its stalk at the base, for a very long time. It does not move. It has no reason to move.

Fauna: you see nothing that moves under its own power and is larger than a millimeter. The water is too cold, too dark, too metal-laden for most life. You are the largest animal in this space, and you are a visitor.

---

The weirdness of the place is not dramatic. Nothing here is grotesque, nothing is horrifying, nothing is impossible. The weirdness is cumulative and quiet. It is the fact of a machine still running. It is the fact of a room that preserved itself by accident. It is the fact that someone, somewhere, is still generating power and sending it through a grid that terminates in stations like this one — stations that are underwater, that serve nothing, that have been forgotten by every living person except, possibly, whoever maintains the upstream source. The weirdness is the *persistence of purpose* after purpose itself has eroded. The station doesn't know it's useless. The current doesn't know its journey ends in open water. The relay that clicks every twelve minutes doesn't know that the circuit it closes leads to a light fixture in a building that no longer exists on a hillside that is now an island.

The weirdness is also the organism in the corner. The thing that shouldn't be here. The thing that has found a way to eat electricity and has done so in perfect silence for what might be decades. It is the only thing in this station that has *adapted*. Everything else — the steel, the copper, the relays, the grating — is slowly losing its war with entropy, dissolving into the water one ion at a time, maintaining its form only because the rate of loss is slow enough to be imperceptible on human timescales. But the organism in the corner is *winning*. It is growing. Slowly, imperceptibly, but growing. In a hundred years it may be the only thing in this room that is still here.

---

You stay for a while. The cold gets into your joints. The silence gets into your thoughts. You watch the junction boxes pulse. You wait for a click and it comes, and it is exactly as clean and exactly as meaningless as the last one. You look at the organism in the corner and it does not look back, but it is *present* in a way that nothing else here is present. The bus bars hum. The water exchanges. The room continues.

You leave because the cold is not something you can argue with.

The station does not notice your departure. It has been not-noticing things for a very long time. It is very good at it.
