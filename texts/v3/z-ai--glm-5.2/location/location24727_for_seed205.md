# The Verdant Mass

## I. Approach

You come up through it before you come upon it. The transition is not a line but a thickening. Scrub gives way to taller scrub, and taller scrub to something that has no name in any language that built its words for ordinary ground — a surface that is not ground, that yields slightly and then holds, that is warm underfoot and breathes.

The Verdant Mass is a continent. That is the only word that fits. It sits high — a plateau the size of an inland sea, lifted two kilometres above the surrounding lowland basins, and it is not stone topped with plants. It is plant through and through. Living matter to a depth no one has measured, because the measuring tools rot or are swallowed, and because the people who live on its edges have learned that some questions are impolite to ask of something that might be listening.

---

## II. The Substrate

Stand still and look down. What you are standing on is not soil. Soil is what happens when rock breaks and dead things settle. This is something else — a woven mat of living and formerly-living plant tissue, layered like compressed peat but still active, still growing upward even as it decays downward. The top centimetres are green and tender, a fuzz of filamentous growths no thicker than hair, clinging to everything. Below that, a tangle of root and rhizome and stolons, woven so tightly that it holds weight. Below that, compressed fibre, dark and damp, giving slightly when pressed. And below that — no one knows. Cores have been attempted. The longest one, driven by a team from the cliff-edge settlement of Keswer, reached nine metres before the borer was seized by something and could not be withdrawn. They left it. Within a season, the protruding handle had been grown over and was no longer visible.

The surface is not flat. The Mass has topography — ridges, basins, slopes, crevasses — but these are not geological. They are botanical. A ridge is where one dominant plant-community has grown thicker and faster than its neighbours, lifting the surface by metres over centuries. A basin is where growth has thinned or where decomposition has outpaced accumulation. Slopes are the zones between — contested ground where colonies advance and retreat. The ground moves. Not fast. Not visibly. But over years, paths shift, landmarks migrate, and the careful stick-markers of the cliff communities must be re-driven each season because the substrate has carried them three or four centimetres in an unexpected direction.

It is hot. The plateau holds heat the way a body holds heat — metabolically. The living mass generates warmth from its own decomposition, its own growth, the slow oxidation of its deep layers. Surface temperatures in the long day sit well above what the elevation should allow. At night, the Mass exhales steam. You can see it from the cliff settlements — the whole interior of the continent blurred by a haze of rising vapour, lit amber by the setting sun, as though the land itself is feverish.

Water arrives intermittently. Rain falls on the high plateau perhaps once every twelve to fifteen days — brief, violent, insufficient. The Mass survives on condensation. Each night, as the surface cools, water precipitates out of the warm air rising from below the plateau's edge — thick fog rolls up and over the rim and is caught by the filamentous top layer, which is designed by evolution or by something else to harvest moisture from air. Every filament is grooved, every leaf-structure bristled with microscopic condensation points. By dawn, the surface is wet — not soaked, but damp, beaded, the water running down along stems and roots into the deeper layers where it is stored in swollen rhizome chambers and distributed through the network over the following days.

You can hear the water moving. At night, if you lie on the surface and press your ear to it, there is a sound like pulse — not rapid, not regular — a thick, slow percolation, the movement of water through living vessels.

## III. The Architecture of Plants

The vegetation does not merely cover the Mass. It *is* the Mass. But within that, there is structure — and the structure is architectural.

The dominant forms are not trees, not in any familiar sense. They are columnar growths — massive, vertical, twenty to forty metres tall — built from bundled stems that thicken and fuse as they age. Each column is a single organism or a cooperative colony; the distinction is unclear and possibly irrelevant. They do not have canopies. They do not spread. They rise like the pillars of a cathedral, and between them, the space is enormous — ten metres, twenty, fifty metres of open air between one column and the next. This is the sparseness: not a lack of growth, but a growth strategy that leaves vast negative space. You can walk for hours between columns, the ground soft beneath you, the sky visible above, the sides of the columns curving away on either hand like the walls of a corridor that was not built for you.

The columns are not silent. They creak. As the temperature shifts through the day, the bundles expand and contract, and the fibres rub against one another with a low groaning sound, like a ship at sea. The columns also click — sharp, percussive snaps from deep inside, as if some internal tension is being released. At certain hours, when the heat peaks, the clicking becomes continuous, a rolling crackle that moves through the Mass like a wave, column after column, starting at the southern rim and sweeping north over the course of an hour.

The ground between the columns is not bare. The filamentous layer covers everything, but there are also low growths — spreading, flat, plate-like forms that sit on the surface like lily pads on still water. Some are a metre across. Some are ten. Their edges overlap and curl and where they meet they fuse or they compete, and the boundary zones are raised, ridged, the cellular equivalent of a mountain range in miniature.

There are also the curtains. In certain regions of the Mass — the eastern quadrant, primarily — the columns are connected by hanging sheets of growth, like fabric slung from pillar to pillar. These are thin, translucent when backlit, and they move in the wind with a dry whispering sound that is different from everything else the Mass makes. The curtains are not structural. They are reproductive. Twice a year, they release spores — clouds of fine, golden dust that drift on the thermals and settle across the surface, germinating new growth. The spore-fall is visible from the cliff settlements as a change in the colour of the air over the interior: the haze takes on a brass tone, and people close their shutters and bring the livestock in.

## IV. The Sound

Nothing prepares you for the sound.

It is not one sound. It is a dozen sounds, layered, each belonging to a different stratum of the ecosystem, and they do not stop. Day or night, the Mass produces sound the way a furnace produces heat — constantly, from multiple sources, with no regard for the listener's capacity to absorb it.

The base layer is the groaning of the columns — that low, structural complaint, the sound of living material under thermal stress. It is felt as much as heard, a vibration in the ground, in the soles of your feet, in your sternum.

Above that, the clicking — irregular, arrhythmic, a staccato that jumps from one column to the next with no discernible pattern.

Above that, the fauna.

The fauna is the reason most outsiders do not stay long on the Mass. The plants are strange but navigable. The animals are overwhelming.

## V. The Fauna

There are insects. There are always insects. But on the Mass, the insects are not a nuisance — they are a medium. The air between the columns is thick with them. Not swarming — occupying. Each cubic metre of air holds dozens of flying forms, and they are so varied and so dense that you cannot extend your hand without contact. Most are small — gnat-sized, midge-sized — and most are harmless. But their collective sound is enormous: a whine, a hum, a textured drone that shifts in pitch and timbre as the species composition of the air changes through the day. At dawn, it is low, almost pleasant, a bass note that you could mistake for wind. By midday, it has climbed to a high, ragged sustained chord that fills the skull and makes thought difficult. By dusk, it fragments — different species activating, others settling, the drone breaking into components, each audible separately for the first time, a thousand instruments all playing at once.

The larger animals move between the columns on established paths — trails worn into the living substrate by generations of passage. They are not mammals. They are not reptiles. They are something that has evolved to live on and in and of the plant substrate, and they have the look of things that have been shaped by their food source.

The most visible are the browsers — tall, six-limbed, moving in loose herds of thirty to sixty individuals between the column-bases, feeding on the plate-growth and the filamentous layer. Their bodies are long and narrow, their legs thin and jointed in ways that suggest grasshopper ancestry filtered through ten million years of large-body evolution. They are the colour of the substrate — green-brown, mottled — and when they stand still they vanish against the ground. They do not stand still often. They move constantly, cropping, chewing, their mandibles producing a sound like scissors, amplified by the thousands. A herd of browsers at feed is audible from half a kilometre away — a continuous shearing, rhythmic, like someone cutting fabric with very large shears and never finishing the cut.

The browsers are prey. The predators are harder to see. They live in the columns — literally within them, in chambers hollowed by decay and lined with silk or resin or some secretion that smells of vinegar. They emerge at dusk: flat-bodied, wide, low to the ground, moving with a fluidity that is deeply wrong for their size. They are silent. They do not need to be loud. They take browsers by ambush — rushing from behind a column, seizing a leg or a joint, and dragging the animal down into the substrate, where it is consumed. The other browsers do not scatter. They continue feeding. They have seen this before. The predator takes one. The herd is sixty. The mathematics are acceptable.

There are smaller creatures in uncountable diversity — things that live in the filamentous layer, things that burrow in the substrate, things that ride the spore-clouds and lay their eggs in the curtains. The substrate is alive with movement. If you kneel and part the top growth, you will see a world — mites, worms, things with too many legs and things with none, all moving through a matrix of fibres that is itself alive and growing around them. The substrate is an ecosystem in the same way the ocean is an ecosystem — a medium through which life moves, in which life is embedded, which is itself not entirely separate from the life it contains.

And there are the sounds you cannot attribute. Deep in the Mass, below the surface, there are tones — long, sustained, subsonic — that come and go without explanation. They might be the rhizome network transmitting signals. They might be large animals moving through tunnels in the substrate. They might be the Mass itself, resonating, the whole continent humming at a frequency that you feel in your teeth.

## VI. The People

There are people. Not many. Not on the Mass itself — the interior is too dense with life, too hostile, too loud. The people live on the edges, where the plateau meets the lowland, where the living substrate thins and gives way to actual stone.

There are four settlements that anyone knows about. There may be more.

The largest is Keswer, on the southern rim, where the cliff drops three hundred metres to the dry basin below. Keswer is built of dead substrate — blocks cut from the Mass's edge, dried, hardened, stacked. The houses are small. The walls are thick. The shutters close tightly, and behind them, the people live with the sound muffled but not eliminated, a permanent low-frequency pressure behind the eyes.

They are primitive in their tools — stone, wood, fibre. They have no metal. They have no need for it. The substrate provides: fibre for cordage, resin for adhesive, dried plates for construction, oils pressed from seed-pods for light and waterproofing. They hunt the browsers at the edge of the Mass, using traps and pitfalls, and they gather the curtain-spores when they drift to the cliff-edge, drying them into a flour that is grey-green and bitter and keeps for months.

They have a practice that no outsider has fully understood. Each year, at the height of the hot season, they walk into the Mass. Not far — perhaps a kilometre, perhaps two. They walk to specific columns that they have marked, and they press their hands to the surface, and they stay for some time — an hour, sometimes longer — and then they return. They do not speak about what they do there. When asked, they say they are *listening*. When pressed, they say the Mass is *deciding something*. When pressed further, they stop answering.

Their children play at the edge of the substrate, where the living ground meets the stone. They dare each other to step onto it. The substrate is warm and soft and it gives underfoot like a mattress, and the children laugh and run back to stone. They are not afraid of it. They will be, later. For now, it is the ground that moves and the ground that breathes and the ground that is always, always making noise, and that is simply what ground does.

## VII. The Mass at Dusk

At the end of the day, the light goes amber, then copper, then a colour that has no name because it only exists here, produced by the interaction of the sunset and the spore-haze and the rising fog. The columns cast long shadows across the open ground — shadows that are sharp-edged and green-black, the light too directional to soften them. The clicking slows. The groaning deepens as the temperature drops and the columns contract. The insect drone shifts downward in pitch, species by species, the high voices dropping out, the low voices thickening.

The fog comes up over the rim. It does not roll — it pours, like water, like something liquid filling a basin, except it is filling the air. Within minutes, the columns nearest the edge are blurred, then obscured. The fog moves inward. The condensation begins. The filamentous layer drinks.

And from deep in the interior, a sound rises — not louder than what came before, but clearer, as if the other sounds have stepped aside for it. A thrumming. A pulse. The sound of the Mass feeding, the water entering the rhizome network, the cells expanding, the whole continent growing by another fraction of a millimetre in the cool of the night.

It will be hot again tomorrow. The columns will click. The insects will rise. The browsers will feed. The predators will wait in their hollows. The people of Keswer will open their shutters and look out over the living continent and listen for whatever it is they listen for.

The Mass does not notice them. The Mass does not notice anything. The Mass grows.
