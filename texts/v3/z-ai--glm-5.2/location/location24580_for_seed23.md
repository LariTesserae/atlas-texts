# The Hollow of Weeping Stones

The first thing is the light. It arrives before understanding does — a white, total, high-altitude brightness that seems to come from every direction at once. Not warm. The sun here has been scrubbed of heat by elevation and thin air, and it lands on wet stone and multiplies. Everything gleams. The light is so constant and so present that shadow seems less like an absence and more like a separate substance, pooled in crevices like mercury.

This is a small place. You could walk its full extent in four minutes — a shallow hollow near the crest of a ridge, maybe sixty meters across, walled on three sides by pale granite that rises in weathered shelves like the pages of a dropped book. The fourth side opens to a long view of valley fog, but no one looks at the view. The view is always the same: white.

The settlement sits in the hollow like something deposited there by a retreating glacier. Twenty-three structures — someone counted once, scratched the number into a doorpost — clustered so densely that their walls nearly touch. In some places they *do* touch, and the gap between buildings has become a shared wall, a single course of stone serving two rooms. The buildings are small, low, built from the same pale granite as the surrounding shelves, fitted without mortar. Dry-stone walls, blackened at the base by centuries of seepage. The roofs are flat slabs, and water stands on them in thin films that shiver.

No wood. There has never been wood here. The doorframes are stone. The shelves are stone. The sleeping platforms are stone. Everything is stone, and the stone is wet.

---

Water comes from everywhere. Not rain — or not only rain. The hollow has been wet for so long that the granite itself has become a kind of sponge. Moisture migrates up through hairline fractures, surfaces, beads, runs. Every wall weeps. The phenomenon is so established, so much a part of the place's character, that the residents long ago carved thin channels into the stone — gutlets, finger-width, guiding the water down predictable paths into shallow basins cut into the floor of each dwelling. The basins overflow into channels that overflow into a central drain-stone, a single perforated slab near the settlement's middle that the water vanishes into with a sound like someone swallowing.

The fog condenses on everything. In the mornings, before the light burns it back, the hollow is inside a cloud, and every surface wears a skin of water that thickens until it runs. By midmorning the fog lifts to just above the ridge-line and hangs there like a ceiling, and the light comes through it diffused and enormous. The bright hours. The working hours. Still cold — the water never warms. Touch a wall and your hand comes away stinging.

---

The residents move through this wetness as though it isn't there. There are perhaps sixty of them, and because the settlement is so small and so dense, they are always near each other — always in peripheral vision, always within an arm's reach of someone's back, someone's elbow. They wear hide — sea-mammal hide, imported, stiff, greased against the wet — and stone beads, and their hair is bound with strips of the same hide. Their tools are stone and bone and cord. No metal. The sharpest thing here is a knapped flake of chert, black against the pale granite, and it is sharp enough to split hide along the grain.

They do not speak much. The quiet here is not the quiet of emptiness — it is the quiet of saturation. The settlement is so full of presences, so thick with people and structures and the ceaseless small movements of water, that sound has been swallowed by density. Footsteps on wet stone make a dull, absorbed sound, like tapping a sponge. Conversation, when it happens, is mouth-to-ear. The loudest thing is the central drain, and even that is a murmur.

---

But the quiet is deceptive. The hollow is turbulent in ways that have nothing to do with sound.

The water moves unpredictably. The weeping channels carved into the walls were placed according to fractures that shift seasonally — the granite opens new paths, closes old ones, and a wall that has run dry for months will suddenly begin to produce water from a new seam overnight. The residents adjust. They carve new gutlets. They plug old ones with packed fiber. They move their sleeping platforms. A building that was a dwelling may become a cistern. A cistern may become a dwelling. The settlement's layout is in constant, quiet flux, buildings trading functions as water reorganizes itself beneath them. Nothing is fixed. Everything is negotiated with the wet stone.

And the fauna.

---

There are birds — but calling them birds is a compromise. They are the size of large gulls but built differently: long, almost legless bodies, flat skulls, beaks that curve downward like the tips of calligraphy pens. They are white. Not the white of camouflage — the white of bone left in sun. They live in the gaps between buildings, in the spaces where walls almost touch, and they are *everywhere*. Dozens of them, standing on the wet roofs in the bright light, preening, shuffling, occasionally opening their wings — which are enormous, absurdly wide, translucent at the edges so the light comes through them like stained glass.

They are silent. Completely silent. No calls. No songs. They communicate with posture, with the angle of their wings, with small jerking movements of their heads that seem to carry meaning. The residents read them. A bird standing on a particular roof with its wings half-open means water will come up through the floor there within a day. A bird pressing its beak against a wall means the fracture behind it is widening. The birds know the stone better than the people do.

There are other animals. Things that live in the drain-channels, pale, many-legged, the length of a hand — they surface in the basins at night and are collected and eaten raw. The birds eat them too. There are flat, leathery things that cling to the wet undersides of the roof-slabs and don't move for weeks at a time and then, during the brightest hours, all move at once, migrating from one roof to another in a slow, coordinated stampede that lasts forty seconds. No one knows where they go or why. The residents treat these migrations as omens but cannot agree on what they signify.

In the standing water on the roofs, something grows. Not vegetation — there is almost no vegetation here. A few black crusts of lichen on the highest, driest surfaces. Nothing else. But in the roof-water, there is a film, iridescent, oily, that forms overnight and burns off in the morning light. It is alive in some way no one has determined. The birds drink it. The residents collect it on stone scrapers and smear it on their hide clothing, and it seems to be what keeps the leather from rotting in the wet.

---

The strangeness of this place is not in any single thing. It is in the *combination* — the density of life and stone and water, the brightness, the silence, the constant invisible renegotiation between the settlement and the water that feeds it. You stand in the hollow at midmorning, in the flat white light, and you are surrounded: by people within arm's reach, by birds on every roof, by the movements of pale things in the basins, by the slow migration of leathery creatures overhead, by the ceaseless rearrangement of water through stone. And it is almost completely silent. And it is brighter than you thought stone and fog could be. And it is cold — cold enough that your fingers have stopped feeling wet and started feeling like something else, something more like glass, like a material that has been rained on so long it has forgotten what dry is.

The settlement has been here for a long time. The stone is weathered smooth. The gutlets are deepened by generations of flowing water. The drain at the center has been widened by use, its edges rounded, its opening the shape of a mouth. The place is in balance — not static, not still, but balanced the way a spinning plate is balanced, the way a conversation between two rivers is balanced, every change answered, every new fracture answered with a new channel, every lost bird answered with a new one hatching in the gaps between walls.

It is a small place. You could walk its extent in four minutes. You would not understand it in four minutes. You might not understand it in four years. It is not a place that wants to be understood. It is a place that wants to be *inhabited* — to have someone stand in it, wet and cold and blinded by the light, surrounded by silent birds, listening to the stone swallow.
