# The Interstitial

You find it through a gap behind a decommissioned pump station — a fissure in the poured-concrete retaining wall, just wide enough to turn your shoulders sideways. The air that leaks from it is warm and smells of rust, mineral water, and something faintly organic, like a terrarium left too long sealed.

Inside: a space no larger than a living room, but impossibly deep in the sense that everything in it is layered, stacked, compressed. The ceiling is the underside of the pump station's foundation — a slab of brutalist concrete sweating condensation in slow, fat beads that fall at irregular intervals into whatever's below. The floor is bedrock, recently exposed. You can tell because the surfaces still hold the chatter marks of whatever process split them open — maybe the subsidence that created this pocket six months ago, maybe a year. The stone is pale, almost chalky, sharp-edged where it fractured. Nothing has had time to round these corners. Nothing has worn them smooth. The place still has the raw, unfinished quality of a wound that hasn't closed.

And everything is packed in.

The bedrock floor is cracked into a loose grid, and in every crack — every one — something is growing or living or building. Dark microbial mats, slick and gelatinous, cling to the lower walls where moisture collects in thin films. They're warm to the touch, almost body-temperature, fed by the residual heat that bleeds down from the industrial machinery above. Pumps, you think. Or compressors. Something with a low, mechanical heartbeat that you feel more than hear, transmitted through the concrete slab overhead and into the stone beneath your feet. A vibration. Not quite sound. The whole place hums at the threshold of perception, like resting your ear against a sleeping animal's chest.

The condensation cycle is the nearest thing to weather. Beads form on the concrete ceiling, swell, fall. They strike the microbial mats with small, wet percussive notes — *tup, tup* — and the mats absorb them visibly, darkening in patches before slowly returning to their baseline sheen. The water tracks into the cracks between bedrock slabs, pooling in shallow, invisible reservoirs you only know exist because you hear the faint trickle of liquid moving through stone channels beneath your feet. The air is damp in the way of a closed space — not wet, but saturated. Your skin prickles with it. Nothing evaporates here. Everything stays.

Vegetation is almost absent, and what's present is wrong. A single pale filamentous structure — you hesitate to call it a root, a vine, a hypha — emerges from one crack in the floor and climbs the far wall in a loose spiral, flattening itself against the concrete like a vein pressed to skin. It's translucent, and you can see fluid moving inside it in slow, peristaltic pulses. Not sap. Something darker. It branches twice and then terminates in a small, knobbed structure that might be a fruiting body or might be something else entirely. No leaves. No chlorophyll-green. It doesn't seem to be seeking light. It seems to be seeking the warmest surface, which is the wall closest to the machinery above. It's the only plant-like thing you can see, and it occupies its space with the territorial confidence of something that knows it won't be competed against.

But fauna — fauna is everywhere.

The first thing you notice is the movement. Not dramatic movement. A quality of the air, of the surfaces, that makes you realize the space is not still. The microbial mats are not uniformly dark; they shift, ripple. Something is moving inside them or across them. Small, pale arthropod-like creatures — translucent carapaces, too many legs for your comfort — navigate the mats in purposeful patterns, their movements producing a faint, dry scratching that's most of the audible sound in this place. Hundreds of them. Maybe thousands. They're the size of a fingernail, maybe smaller, and they pack the surface of the mats so densely that the mats appear to be *breathing* — the collective motion of their foraging produces a slow, undulatory wave across the gelatinous surface. They don't flee from your light. They don't react to you at all. They're eating, or tending, or maintaining something in those mats that matters more to them than your presence.

Higher up, in the corners where concrete meets bedrock, other creatures have built structures. Papery, ochre-colored nests — not wasp nests, the geometry is wrong, the cells are hexagonal but spiral inward like a nautilus shell — cluster in dense colonies that occupy every cubic centimeter of the upper corners. The builders are larger, dark, and they move with a slow, deliberate grace that reads almost as mechanical. They emerge from the nest entrances, deposit small pellets of material — chewed concrete dust and microbial mat, you suspect — and re-enter. A constant traffic. The nests are warm to the touch; they've engineered their own heat capture, positioning the entrance tubes to catch the warm air rising from the mats below and the thermal bleed from above. A two-source convection system. The builders maintain it with the same indifferent precision that the machinery above maintains its own cycles.

And there is something else.

Behind you, in the deeper crack you entered through, there is a space you didn't notice at first — a vertical gap where the foundation slab separated from the bedrock wall, extending down perhaps two meters, perhaps more. It's too narrow to enter. But something lives in it. You know this because of the sound: a low, rhythmic exhalation, every eight seconds or so, accompanied by a faint wet click. Not mechanical. Biological. The exhale carries a warmth you can feel on your hand when you reach into the gap. Whatever is down there is large enough to fill the vertical fissure, and it breathes with the slow, unconcerned rhythm of something that has no predators in this ecosystem, something that occupies the apex of a food web so small and self-contained it could fit inside a shed. The pale creatures on the mats don't go near the gap. The nest-builders in the corners don't fly over it. There is a radius of avoidance — subtle, total, respected by every other living thing in this pocket.

You become aware, gradually, that the space is not passive. It is a system in motion. The machinery above provides heat. The heat drives condensation. Condensation feeds the microbial mats. The mats feed the pale surface creatures. The surface creatures are harvested — you can see the small pellet-caches near the nest entrances — by the nest-builders. The nest-builders maintain the convection that keeps the mats at optimal temperature. The thing in the fissure — you don't know its role. You don't want to know its role. But the rhythm of its breathing and the rhythm of the machinery above are close. Not synchronized. Close. Within half a second of each other. As though one learned the other's pace and adopted it, or as though both are responding to some deeper pulse you can't detect.

A single artifact sits in the center of the floor, half-buried in the crack where two bedrock slabs have separated. It's a metal disk — a gasket, maybe, or a seal — from the machinery above, dropped during installation or maintenance and fallen through a gap that no longer exists. It's corroded, but something has been using it. The pale surface creatures cluster thickly along its edge, where condensation pools in a perfect ring. They drink from it. It's their well. Their town square. A piece of human engineering repurposed by a civilization so small it has no idea we exist, serving a function we never designed it for in a world we made by accident.

The hum from above shifts pitch. A pump cycling to a new phase. The condensation pattern changes — beads forming faster now, falling in clusters. The microbial mats darken visibly. The surface creatures increase their pace. The nest-builders accelerate their traffic. The thing in the fissure exhales. The translucent vine on the far wall pulses once, twice, three times in quick succession, and its knobbed tip swells slightly, flushing with whatever dark fluid moves inside it.

The whole space responds to the shift. Every organism, every surface, every process recalibrates simultaneously and without hesitation, because this is not disruption. This is rhythm. This is what the place does. It breathes when the machinery breathes. It wakes when the pumps cycle. It has organized itself around the heartbeat of something above that no one is operating anymore, that no one has operated for years, that runs on automated timers and residual purpose in a building that may not even have a human footprint left around it.

You are standing inside a mechanical organism's gut, and the gut has developed its own ecology.

The light from your headlamp catches the falling condensation. The droplets look like static. The pale creatures don't notice. The nest-builders don't notice. The thing in the fissure exhales again, right on schedule.

You should leave. The pump will cycle again in eleven minutes, and the condensation will become rain, and the floor will flood to a depth of two centimeters, and the surface creatures will retreat into the cracks, and the nest-builders will seal their entrances, and the thing in the fissure will — you don't know what it will do. But the vine will pulse, and the mats will flush with new moisture, and the cycle will continue whether you're here or not.

You are the only thing in this space that isn't part of the system. That's not a comfort. That makes you the anomaly. And in a place this dense, this packed, this precisely calibrated — anomalies get noticed. Not by the creatures. By the space itself. By the pattern. You can feel it starting to accommodate you, the way water accommodates a stone. Finding paths around you. Adjusting flow rates. Incorporating your warmth, your breath, your condensation-contributing presence into its accounting.

You turn sideways and leave through the gap in the retaining wall.

Behind you, the breathing continues without interruption.
