# The Weeping Altitude

## I.

It begins with sound. Before the eye finds anything, the ear finds everything — a low, distributed roar that is not wind exactly, though wind is a component of it. Something closer to what a glacier might say if it were not ice but glass, if it were not glass but memory, if memory had mass and that mass were in slow, constant failure across an inclined plane.

The sound comes from below. That is the first wrong thing. The sound comes from below and the water comes from below and the light, which is so bright it has texture, seems also in some way to come from below, reflected upward off surfaces that should not reflect.

## II.

The place is high. Not merely elevated — *high* in the way that the body knows it before the mind does, the way the lungs tighten and the horizon pulls back its chair and sits farther away. You are on a plateau, or what was once a plateau, at the kind of altitude where the sky stops being blue and becomes a pale, exhausted white, like paper that has been erased too many times. The air is thin. Cool. Not cold — there is a difference, and the difference matters here, because cold would imply a kind of stability, a locking, and nothing here is locked.

The plateau extends in every direction farther than it should. The scale is valley-wide, continental-shelf-deep, but the features are compressed — too many things happening per unit of space, as though the landscape was designed for a larger world and then crammed into this one. Ridges fold against ridges. Basins open within basins. The terrain has the quality of a crumpled map, and you are an ant walking along its creases.

## III.

There is no plant life. Let me say this plainly because it is important and because the absence is not simple. There is no green. There is no brown-that-was-once-green. There is no soil in the biological sense, no humus, no loam, no thin living skin. What covers the ground is mineral — shattered stone in graded sizes from silt to boulder, all of it wet, all of it darkened by water the way a sidewalk darkens in rain, except here the rain is constant and the sidewalk is the world.

The stone is not ordinary. Or rather, it is ordinary in a way that becomes extraordinary under sustained attention. It is mostly a dark basalt, fine-grained, but veined through with something pale and translucent that could be quartz but is not quite quartz — it has a warmer tint, an amber undertone, like fossilized resin, like something that was once liquid and organic and is now mineral and will perhaps be liquid again. These veins are everywhere. They web the rock faces. They thread through the boulder fields. They surface in the basins like the bones of a hand pressing up through skin.

Water moves along these veins. That is the weeping. That is the sound.

## IV.

The water does not fall from above. Or not primarily. There is rain — or something like rain, a fine, cold, lateral precipitation that the wind drives in sheets — but the more significant water comes from *within* the rock. It emerges from the veined fractures in a slow, continuous exudation, beading on stone surfaces, gathering into threads, threads into streams, streams into the basins. The rock is saturated. The water table is not a table here; it is a *surface*, inches below the ground, pressing up, finding every crack, every seam, every fissure, and pushing through.

The result is that the ground is wet. Not muddy — there is no fine organic matter to make mud — but wet in the mineral sense, dark and slick and reflective. You walk on stone that is covered in a film of water so thin it is visible only by the way it catches the light, and the light is so bright that this film is a second sky, a white shine that follows your feet.

In the basins, the water pools. These pools are not large — some are the size of rooms, some the size of lakes, some the size of a cupped hand — and they are perfectly still, perfectly clear, and perfectly empty. No organisms. No algae. No larvae. Nothing living in them. They are lenses through which you see more stone, more veins, more water emerging from deeper fractures, and the brightness of the sky reflected back is so total that looking into them is like looking into a lamp.

## V.

The light. I need to talk about the light because it is doing something wrong.

At this altitude, with this atmosphere — thin, wet, full of suspended water — the light should be harsh, blue, the kind of light that photographs well and hurts the eyes. It is harsh. It is bright. But it is not blue. It is white. White to the point of excess. White to the point of *noise*. The sky is white. The reflections on the wet stone are white. The pools are white. The distant ridges, which should be dark silhouettes against a bright sky, are instead lit from below by the reflection-pools and lit from within by the veined mineral, so that they appear as bright forms against a brighter sky, and the only way to see them is by the *shadows* they cast, which are not dark but *less white*, a gray that has a faintly lavender quality, and the gray shifts as you move, as though the shadows are not cast by solid objects but by *absences*, by places where the light has chosen not to go.

This is the second wrong thing. Light does not behave this way. Light does not choose.

## VI.

But something here does. Something here chooses.

The weirdness is not decorative. It is structural. Let me describe what I mean.

In the middle distance — perhaps a half-mile, perhaps more, distance is uncertain here because the air is so clear and so bright that depth perception becomes unreliable — there is a formation that I can only call an *event*. It is a place where the terrain does something it should not: the stone rises in a smooth, continuous curve, like a wave frozen mid-break, and at the crest of the wave the rock is *translucent*. Not transparent — you cannot see through it — but translucent in the way that wax is translucent, or thick ice, or certain kinds of ceramic when held to a strong light. The light penetrates the stone to a depth of several inches and reveals, inside the rock, a pattern. Not a vein. Not a fracture. A *pattern* — regular, repeating, geometric — that shifts slowly, the way a kaleidoscope shifts if you turn it very slowly, the way light shifts on the bottom of a pool when the surface is disturbed.

This pattern is visible in several places across the plateau. In the basins, if you look into the clear water, you can see it in the submerged stone — slow, turning, geometric, like a language written in luminance. On the cliff faces, where the weeping water sheets down the surface, the water catches the pattern from within and carries it on its surface, so that the runoff is not merely reflective but *informative*, displaying shapes that move and change and interact with the shapes on adjacent surfaces, as though the water is reading the stone and translating it and the stone is reading the water and translating it back.

## VII.

There are structures.

Small. Scattered. Built — or *made*, the distinction between building and making is relevant here — from the same dark basalt, fitted without mortar, held by precision and by the weight of the stone itself. They are not houses exactly. They are not shelters. They are rooms, in the older sense of the word — *spaces*, defined by low walls, some no higher than the knee, some as high as the head, and within each, a basin has been carved or cleared, and water pools in it, and the pattern is visible in the water.

There are perhaps thirty of these across the visible plateau. Some are intact. Some are partial — a wall remaining, a corner, a threshold. Some are barely visible, reduced to a line of stones that could be natural but is not, because the stones are too regular, too deliberately placed, too precisely fitted to be geology.

The technology that made them was simple. Hand-tools, maybe. Stone on stone. The kind of craft that requires patience and understanding rather than machinery. Whoever made these structures knew the rock. Knew where it would split cleanly, where the veins ran, how to break a surface so that the internal pattern would be exposed. The walls are not decorative — they are *functional*, but the function is not shelter from weather or predators. There is no weather here that a knee-high wall would help with, and there are no predators. The function is something else. The function is related to the water and the pattern and the light.

The function is, I think, *reading*.

## VIII.

The sound again. I need to return to the sound because it has changed, or because my perception of it has changed, or because the landscape itself is doing what it does — shifting, active, in process.

The roar is not constant. It has a structure. Beneath the broad, white noise of water-on-stone, there are deeper notes, rhythmic, and higher notes, arrhythmic, and if you listen long enough — if you sit in one of the stone rooms and look into the pooled water and let the brightness fill your vision — the sound begins to *organize*. Not into language. Not into music. Into something that is prior to both, something that language and music are both attempts to capture: a pattern of pressure changes, of vibration, that corresponds to the patterns in the stone and the patterns in the water and the patterns in the light.

The landscape is generating information. It is not generating it *for* anyone. It is not a message. It is a *process*, the way a river is a process, the way weather is a process. The water moves through the stone and the stone shapes the water and the water carries the pattern and the light illuminates the pattern and the pattern modifies the stone and the stone shapes the water and this has been happening for a very long time.

The erosion parameter — established, in-balance, long-existed — this is what it means. The landscape is not decaying. It is not growing. It is in a *steady state of transformation*, a loop in which water and stone and light are the three terms of an equation that solves itself continuously and has been solving itself for longer than anything alive can remember, because nothing here is alive in the biological sense, and maybe nothing here has ever been alive in the biological sense, and maybe this is what was here before life, or what will be here after it, or what is here *alongside* it, in a place so high and so strange that the usual rules relax and other rules — older, simpler, stranger — take over.

## IX.

The weirdness. The reality breakdown. Let me try to be specific.

There is a place on the plateau where the ground stops. Not a cliff — a *boundary*. On one side, the wet basalt, the veins, the pools, the pattern, all as described. On the other side: the same thing, but *offset*. The veins do not align. The pools are in different positions. The pattern in the stone is shifted by some amount that is not a distance but a *phase*, the way two waves can be out of phase. Standing at the boundary, you can see both versions — your own and the other — and they are the same landscape, but they are not the same *state* of the landscape. It is as if the plateau exists in two moments simultaneously, and the boundary is the seam between them.

The water that crosses the boundary does something strange. It does not flow from one side to the other. It *duplicates*. The water on the near side reaches the boundary and continues flowing, and the water on the far side also continues flowing, and they are the same water, but they are not connected. There is no continuity of surface. The water is in two places, in two states, and it is moving in both, and the sound it makes is slightly different on each side — a half-tone difference, perhaps, a small but perceptible shift in pitch that makes the seam between the two states audible.

The stone rooms near the boundary are built on both sides. The same architecture. The same craft. But the rooms on the far side are older — more weathered, more eroded, their basins deeper, their walls lower — as if they have been reading the pattern for longer. Or as if time moves differently there. Or as if the far side is the near side, later.

## X.

There is no animal life. I said this. Let me say what *is* here, in terms of presence.

The mineral world is not dead. The mineral world is not inert. The mineral world is *differently active*. The veins in the stone are not geological features — or not *only* geological features. They are conductors. The water is not *only* water. It is a medium. The pattern is not decoration. It is *content*. The light is not illumination. It is *energy*. And the system — water, stone, light, pattern — is not a metaphor for a living system. It is a living system, in the way that a crystal growing in supersaturated solution is alive, in the way that a river finding its course is alive, in the way that any process that consumes energy and produces structure and maintains itself against entropy is alive.

The plateau is an organism. Or an ecosystem. Or a computation. These words are all wrong. They are the words of a biology that recognizes carbon and water and membranes and does not recognize this. The plateau does not care about recognition. It does what it does. It reads itself. It writes itself. It weeps, and the weeping is its voice, and the voice is loud, and the loudness is the sound of a world speaking itself into existence, continuously, at altitude, in bright light, without any need for anything we would recognize as life.

## XI.

I am in one of the stone rooms now. The walls come up to my chest. The basin at the center is the size of a table, filled with water so clear it is invisible until the light catches a surface ripple. The pattern is moving in the water — slow, geometric, shifting through forms that my eyes try to read as letters or numbers or diagrams but that are not any of these. They are *themselves*. They are the native script of this place, the way ripples are the native script of water.

The sound here is different — focused, almost resonant, as if the walls are not defining a space but *tuning* one. The light is so bright that my shadow is not on the ground but *in* the water, a dark form among the turning patterns, and I realize that the shadow is also a pattern, that my presence here is being read, that the system is incorporating me into its computation the way a river incorporates a stone — not by meaning to, but by flowing around.

The far side of the boundary is visible from here. The offset landscape. The older rooms. The deeper basins. And beyond them, more ridge, more basin, more veined stone, more weeping water, more light, extending to a horizon that is too far away and too bright to see, but that is there, that is real, or that is real *enough*, in the way that this whole place is real enough — not a hallucination, not a dream, but a *different kind of real*, one that does not require my belief or my understanding, one that was here before I came and will be here after I go, reading itself, writing itself, weeping its bright, loud, endless self into the white sky.
