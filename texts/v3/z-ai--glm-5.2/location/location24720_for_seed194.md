# The Hollowness Under the Root

You find it by accident. A fissure in ground that isn't ground — a seam in the oldest wood, a place where the bark split sometime before reckoning and never closed. You go down. Not far. A body-length, maybe two, and the world changes.

---

The chamber is small. You could lie across it in three directions, though the ceiling presses close enough to brush your hair. What you first take for walls is not wall. It is wood — but that word is insufficient in the way that "water" is insufficient for an ocean. This is wood the way bedrock is stone. It has been here so long it has forgotten what a tree was. The grain runs in directions that suggest the chamber was once a cavity inside a living trunk, a pocket of rot or insect-hollow, but whatever made it died before the wood hardened into permanence. The wood kept growing around the absence. Closed over it. Sealed it in.

There is no water. None. The air is so dry it makes your lips crack within minutes, and the wood under your hand — when you touch it, and you will touch it, because the dark is absolute — feels like fired clay. Like something kiln-cured across millennia. The grain is still legible under your fingertips, raised ridges and valleys, but the cellular structure has collapsed into something closer to mineral than lignin. This is what happens to wood when there is no water for long enough. It doesn't rot. There's nothing to rot it. It just... becomes something else. Harder. Still. Final.

And yet it is not dead. That is the first strangeness.

---

There is a city here. Or the memory of one, which in a place this old amounts to the same thing.

You understand it by touch, at first. Then by sound — because the sound is what fills the chamber, what makes it alive, what makes it impossible to forget you are inside something. The sound comes from the wood itself.

Deep groaning. A low, sustained vibration that you feel in your sternum before you hear it with your ears. It comes in cycles — five, six minutes of near-silence (not silence; never silence; the wood always breathes, always ticks and settles in micro-creeps), and then a building tension, a creak that rises through registers until it peaks in a sound like a mast breaking, like a beam splitting under load, and then a long exhale, a release, and the chamber shudders once, and the cycle begins again. It is very loud. Loud enough that you feel it in your teeth.

The sound is the wood moving. Growing. But growth here is not what you know. There is no water to swell the cells, no light to drive photosynthesis, no soil to mine for nutrients. The wood grows the way a glacier moves — by imperceptible creep, by the slow rearrangement of internal structure, by processes that operate on timescales where a century is a unit of modest account.
