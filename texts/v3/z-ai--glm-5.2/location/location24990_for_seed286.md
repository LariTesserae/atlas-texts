# The Greenwater Plateau

You come up into it from below — though "below" is a strange word here, because this place is already high. The seafloor rose at some point in the deep past, or the water sank, or the world simply arranged itself this way and has always been so. What matters is the geometry: a continental shelf the size of a small ocean, hovering two kilometers below the surface but still far above the true bottom, and on that shelf, a world that should not be called a garden because nothing here was planted.

The light is good. That is the first surprise. You expect gloom at this depth, the usual attenuation into blue-black, but the water is so clear and the plateau so broad and shallow relative to its surroundings that diffused surface light reaches the floor in a pale, even wash — not bright, not dim, the flat illumination of an overcast sky, but perpetual. It never shifts. The sun moves above, the seasons turn somewhere far away on the surface, and none of it matters. The light here is the light here, and it does not change.

Which is true of everything.

---

The sound hits you first. Not a sound — a sound implies beginning and end, and this has neither. A standing wave of noise, so constant and so total that after a while you stop hearing it the way you stop hearing your own blood. It is a mechanical roar filtered through water: deep, rhythmic, layered. The low thrum of enormous volumes of fluid being moved through chambers. The chittering rattle of hard surfaces flexing against each other at a frequency just below music. A bass drone that you feel in your sternum before you hear it, vibrating the water itself into a medium that is not quite still, not quite moving — like being inside a machine that is idling and will never accelerate and will never stop.

No one built this machine.

---

The plant-towers are what make the sound. You see the first one after a long swim across empty plateau — and the emptiness is important, you must understand the emptiness first. The floor of the plateau is bare rock, a smooth basalt worn flat by ages of water movement that have since ceased. Nothing grows on it. Nothing coats it. It is clean stone, grayish-green, tracked by faint sediment trails that have lain in the same positions for longer than anything can measure. Between the towers, there is only this stone and the water above it, and the water goes up and up into that pale light until it becomes a ceiling of brightness that you cannot see past. The space is enormous. The distances between things are vast. You can swim for an hour in any direction across clean rock and see nothing on the horizon — and then something appears, and it is always a tower.

They are plants. You know this because of how they are built — not because of what they look like, because they look like nothing botany has prepared you for. The nearest one, the one you approach across ten minutes of empty swimming, is perhaps three hundred meters tall and sixty meters across at its base, a tapering column of layered biological material — dark green near the bottom, almost black, shading upward through olive and yellow-green to a crown of translucent tissue that catches the flat light and glows faintly, a pale lantern shape at its summit. It is trunk and leaf and root all at once, and it is also something else.

It is a factory.

You see this as you get close. The surface of the tower is not smooth. It is fluted, ridged, and the ridges are not decorative — they are channels, and through them water moves, pulled upward through some mechanism that is not evaporation and not capillary action and not anything you have a name for. The water enters at the base through root-structures that have fused with the bedrock itself, spreading in dark tendrils across the stone, and it rises through the tower's interior in volumes you can hear: that deep thrum, that pumping. The tower is processing the water. Filtering it. Extracting something — minerals, dissolved gases, compounds you cannot identify — and exhausting what it does not need through vents near its midsection, where the ridges open into slits and the outflow creates visible turbulence, a shimmering in the water like heat haze. The sound is loudest here, at the vents, a thudding arterial pulse that is so steady you begin to time your breathing to it without meaning to.

The tissue is alive. You can see it working — not growing, not changing, but working, the way a heart muscle works, contracting and releasing in slow peristaltic waves that travel up the column and disappear into the crown. There are no leaves in the conventional sense. The crown is a single vast membrane, riddled with veins, and it is luminous — not with bioluminescence exactly, but with a conversion of the filtered light into something else, some form of energy that is stored in the tower's lower layers, visible as dark bulges along the trunk, dense nodes of material that must be reservoirs or batteries or organs. The whole structure is a plant that has evolved to be an engine, or an engine that has evolved to be a plant, and the distinction no longer matters because it has been doing this for so long that its form and its function are the same thing.

---

There are others. You can see them from the top of the first tower — if you swim up its flank, the ridges provide handholds of a sort, though the tissue is firm and slightly warm, a steady warmth like a machine housing, not the warmth of a living body. From the crown, the plateau stretches in every direction, flat and gray and empty, and the towers stand on it like pillars in a cathedral whose architect favored extreme negative space. The nearest one is perhaps four kilometers away. Beyond that, another. Beyond that, others, fading into the murk of distance until the pale light swallows them. They are not evenly spaced. They are not in a grid. They are scattered with the randomness of something that was seeded by natural process, and yet each one stands in what seems to be its ideal location — above a fissure in the basalt, or at the junction of two root-lines that stretch across the stone floor like dark veins, connecting tower to tower in a network so vast it must span the entire plateau. The roots are the infrastructure. They carry whatever the towers exchange between themselves — nutrients, chemical signals, the energy stored in those dark nodes — and they have been doing this for so long that the rock has shaped itself around them, accommodating them the way a tree grows around a fence post, until you cannot tell where the geology ends and the biology begins.

The sound is louder at altitude. Up here, among the crowns, you can hear the individual voices of the towers — each one has its own pitch, its own rhythm, and the combined sound is not chaos but a chord, a sustained chord that has been sounding for so long that the water itself has learned to carry it as a harmonic. Nothing echoes here. The sound is too constant to echo. It simply fills the space.

---

The fauna live in the gaps. They are not large, and they are not dramatic, but they are everywhere, in their way — adapted to the spaces between the towers, to the clean stone and the cool water and the endless noise. You see them in the root-lines: flat, armored creatures, the size of your hand, with too many legs and no eyes, crawling along the dark tendrils, feeding on something that seeps from the network — a byproduct, a waste, or perhaps an offering, a deliberate excretion that supports an ecosystem built entirely on the towers' surplus. There are swimmers too — torpedo-shaped, translucent, moving in schools that drift across the empty plateau like clouds, their bodies so clear you can see their digestive tracts working, processing whatever they have filtered from the water. They do not approach the towers. They do not need to. They live entirely in the negative space, in the emptiness, and their lives are as constant and as unchanging as everything else here — they school, they feed, they school, and that is their entire existence, a closed loop that has been running for so long that it has worn itself smooth.

A larger shape passes at the edge of visibility — something flat, winged, cruising the thermocline above the towers, riding the gentle currents that the vents produce. You see it once and not again. It does not matter. It will be there tomorrow and it was there yesterday and it is there now, doing what it does.

---

What is strange about this place — strange in the way that unsettles without alarming — is the stillness. Not physical stillness; the towers are active, the water moves, the animals swim, the sound is enormous. But temporal stillness. Nothing here is going anywhere. Nothing is growing toward something, or decaying from something, or transitioning between states. The towers are not young and they are not old. They are not in a phase. They are complete, and they are running, and they have been running since before the concept of "before" had meaning here. The root-network has reached its full extent and will neither grow nor retract. The water cycles through the towers at a rate that has been constant for so long that the stone around the vents has been eroded into perfect, polished curves by the outflow — not changing, but finished eroding, the way a river reaches base level and simply is. The light does not change. The sound does not change. The animals do not change. This is not a moment in the life of an ecosystem. This is the final shape of it, the equilibrium form, and it has been this shape for longer than the surface world has had the means to notice it.

You could stay here for a year and see nothing you did not see in the first hour. You could stay for a century and the towers would not have grown by a millimeter. The warmth in the tissue would be the same. The chord would be the same. The schools of translucent swimmers would be in the same positions relative to the same towers, following the same paths they have followed since the paths were established.

This is what it means to be a world in its own state. Not dead. Not dormant. Not paused. Finished. Running at its finished rate, producing its finished output, sounding its finished chord. A continent-sized bio-industrial engine in permanent operation, with no operator, no blueprint, no purpose beyond its own continuation, and no sign that continuation has ever been in question.

The roar fills your chest. The light stays flat. The stone stays clean. Far away, another tower processes the same water it has always processed, and the roots carry whatever they carry to wherever they carry it, and the flat-winged shape glides at the edge of sight, and the water is cool, and the water is clear, and nothing changes, and nothing changes, and nothing changes.
