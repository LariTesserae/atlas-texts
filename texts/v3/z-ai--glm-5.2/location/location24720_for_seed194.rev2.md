# The Hollow Bole

There is a chamber inside the trunk of something that was once a tree.

It is not a tree now. It has not been a tree for longer than the word *tree* has had a tongue to shape it. But the architecture remembers. The grain remembers. The space remembers what it was to grow upward, even though upward stopped meaning anything when the light went away.

---

You enter through a split in the root collar — a gap that was deliberate once, carved with something harder than the wood but not by much. Tool marks remain. Not metal. Stone, perhaps, or fired clay, or the edge of a thing that no longer exists in this place. The marks are old enough that the living wood has swelled around them, assimilated them, grown over and through the grooves until they are more like wrinkles than carvings. Someone made a door here. Someone who worked by feel, by resonance, by the way the grain yielded under their hands. They are gone. The door remains.

Inside: volume.

This is the paradox of the place. The scale is small — you could call it a room, perhaps two rooms, a connected series of cavities within the heartwood of a single ancient organism — but the emptiness inside is so complete, so carefully hollowed, that it feels vast. The ceiling is out of reach. Not because it is high but because the darkness above refuses to resolve into surface. You raise your hand and your fingers touch something, eventually — a ceiling that is living wood, dry and cool, slightly yielding under pressure like old leather stretched over a frame. But the space between your hand and that ceiling is full of nothing, and nothing has weight here.

The floor is wood too. Not boards. Not planks. Growth. The same organism, continuous from wall to floor to wall, a single body folded into geometry. It has grown into something like rooms. Something like corridors. Something like a city flattened into one organism — streets and courtyards and dead ends, all expressed in the same medium, all living, all contiguous. There are alcoves. There are shelves that might have held things. There are partitions, waist-high, grown rather than built, that divide the space into zones whose purpose has been forgotten by everything except the grain itself, which still curves around them as though protecting a function that no longer occurs.

It is cool. Not cold — cool the way stone is cool deep underground, cool the way a sealed cellar holds the temperature of some season that no longer applies above. The wood breathes, very slowly. Not air, exactly. The chemistry here is not aqueous. There is no water. The organism moves something else through its vessels — a thick, dry sap, amber-dark, more resin than liquid, that creeps through capillary channels at a pace measured in seasons per centimeter. It does not flow. It advances. The walls are saturated with it, and it gives them a faint warmth that is not warmth exactly but the thermal signature of a process so slow it has become indistinguishable from stillness.

No water. This cannot be overstated. The air is so dry it desiccates. Lips crack in minutes. The wood itself is not moist — it is bone-hard, polished by age, and where it has been touched and handled over the long centuries it has developed a sheen like handled stone. But it is alive. It persists without water through a metabolism that no longer needs it, that substituted for it so long ago that the substitution has become the organism's nature. If there was a transition — from water-based life to this other thing — it happened so far back that even the erosion has eroded. This is the end state. This is what remains when everything else has been tried and abandoned. Wood that lives without wetness. Cells that divide without hydration. A body that grows without rain.

---

The sound.

The sound is the thing you notice first and never stop noticing. It is loud. Not a sound that attacks — a sound that *exists*, that fills the chamber the way water would fill a vessel, from the bottom up, pressing into every pocket of air. It comes from the organism itself. The resin-sap moving through its vessels produces a low, continuous groan, a vibration that you feel in your sternum before you hear it with your ears. It is the sound of something enormous enduring. Not suffering — enduring. The way a beam in an old house speaks when the wind pushes it, except the wind is internal, the wind is the organism's own slow circulation, and the beam is the whole world.

Layered over the groan: creaking. Not random. Rhythmic, almost. The wood expands and contracts in cycles so long that a single cycle might span a day, a week — and each cycle produces a sequence of pops and cracks as one section of grain presses against another. The sound travels through the solid wood the way sound travels through a tuning fork. The whole chamber is a resonator. A whisper against one wall emerges from the opposite wall, transformed, amplified, arriving a half-second later as something that is almost but not quite a word.

There are larger sounds. Deep in the structure, where the heartwood is densest and the sap-vessels are widest, there are concussions — not frequent, not predictable, but immense. The organism grows, and growing means that somewhere, at some interval, a boundary is crossed. A fiber bundle that has been compressing for a hundred years finally exceeds its yield point and snaps inward by a fraction of a millimeter. The sound is a deep, percussive *thoom* that you feel in your teeth. It happens, and then the silence afterward is not silence but the resonant aftermath, the chamber trembling, the wood continuing to vibrate for ten, twenty, thirty seconds before settling back into the groan.

It is never quiet here. The sound is the organism's heartbeat, its breath, its voice. It has been making this sound for so long that the sound has shaped the chamber — certain walls are thicker where the vibration has compacted the grain, certain alcoves ring at specific frequencies because they have been tuned by centuries of resonance. The space was built by someone, shaped by hands and primitive tools, but it has been *refined* by sound. It is an instrument. It has been playing itself for longer than anything has been alive to listen.

---

No animals. This is absolute. No insects bore in the wood. No fungi colonize the surface — or if they once did, they have been incorporated, absorbed, digested into the grain so thoroughly that they are now just patterns in the wood, dark whorls that might once have been bracket fungi or might once have been knots. The organism does not tolerate guests. It is a closed system. It eats what it needs from the air, from the slow mineral substrate below the root collar, from itself — old growth is broken down and reconstituted as new growth in a cycle so efficient that nothing is lost. Nothing leaves. Nothing enters. The chamber is sealed in every way that matters.

And yet someone lived here once.

The evidence is everywhere, if you can read it by touch and sound rather than by sight. There are hollows in the walls at hand-height — tool-rests, worn smooth by use. There is a depression in the floor in one of the alcoves, a body-shaped concavity where someone sat, or slept, or waited, for long enough that the wood grew to accommodate them. There are marks — scratched into the surface with something sharp — that might be tally marks or might be decoration or might be language. They are old. The wood has healed around them but not over them, as if the organism recognized them as intentional and chose, in its slow vegetative way, to preserve them.

Whoever lived here lived without light. They lived without water. They lived in a room that groans and cracks and thunders. They lived with primitive tools and no animals and no plants except the one they lived inside of. They shaped it — or it shaped them, or they shaped each other. And then they were gone, and the chamber continued without them, growing at the pace of geology, making its sound, breathing its dry breath, refining its acoustics in the dark.

---

This is the place: a single organism, ancient beyond reckoning, that has grown into the shape of a dwelling. Inside it, a chamber. Inside the chamber, nothing. No occupant, no tool, no artifact, no remains. Only the wood, the dark, the cool dry air, and the sound — the ceaseless, enormous, patient sound of something alive that has been listening to itself for so long that it has become its own audience, its own instrument, its own reason.

The emptiness is not absence. The emptiness is what the space was grown to hold. Something was here, and the organism remembers it the way a bowl remembers water — by the shape it left behind.
