# The Hollow of Omras

You descend through a crack in the earth that was once a staircase.

Not that you can tell at first. The steps have been worn to gentle undulations — the rise of each step maybe a centimeter, the tread perhaps two meters long. Entire lifetimes could have been spent walking these stairs and the stone would have barely noticed. It is granite, or something like granite — a dark, close-grained stone that holds cool the way a dead body holds room temperature. The walls of the passage are the same material, and they have been shaped by hands, once, long ago. You can see the tool marks. Not as texture but as ghosts of texture: areas where the stone is microscopically more regular than natural fracture would produce. A mineralogist with a good loupe could tell you that someone cut this corridor. Anyone else would call it a cave.

---

The passage opens, after a descent of perhaps forty meters, into something vast.

You stand at the edge of a cavern that your light cannot find the walls of. The ceiling — if there is a ceiling — is a darkness so complete it feels like absence rather than distance. The floor is visible because of the life on it.

There is vegetation here. That is the first surprise. The second is that it is everywhere.

Not green. Nothing here is green. The dominant color is a deep iron-gray, shading to a tarnished bronze where growth is thickest, with occasional stands of something that looks like fern frond but has the color of dried blood. These are not plants in any terrestrial sense — they have no roots that you can find, no leaves as such, no vascular system. They are growths. Mineral-nourished, perhaps, or sustained by something in the air that your biology does not have a category for. They spread across the stone floor in branching, fractal patterns that recall frost on a window — except this frost is thick, sometimes waist-high, sometimes head-high, and it is alive.

You touch one of the frond-like structures. It is cool and slightly yielding, like old leather. It does not react to your touch. There is no sap, no moisture inside. When you break a piece off — it snaps cleanly, like a stick of chalk — the interior is dry and powdery, a reddish-brown that suggests iron oxide. The broken edge does not weep. It does not heal. It simply stays broken, and you suspect it will stay broken for a very long time.

The growth is heaviest where the floor is lowest. There are channels here — shallow, maybe twenty centimeters deep, worn into the stone by a process that took longer than any human institution has ever existed. These channels are not watercourses. Nothing has run through them. They are erosion features of a kind that requires no fluid: the slow chemical attack of one mineral on another, operating over timescales that make erosion by water look hasty and inattentive. The iron-gray vegetation follows these channels like a drainage system in reverse, filling the low places, climbing the gentle slopes, thinning at the high points.

---

The city — for it is a city, you are increasingly sure of this — reveals itself in the same way the tool marks did: not through presence but through the *regularity of absence*.

Here, a rectangle of floor where nothing grows. Something once stood here — a building, a wall, a structure of some kind — and its foundation inhibited the growth in a pattern that has outlasted the structure itself by what must be millions of years. The building is gone. The stone it was made of has been worn back to the same level as the surrounding floor, chemically indistinguishable from the bedrock, but the ghost of its footprint persists in the vegetation's memory. Or rather, in the vegetation's *ignorance*. It does not grow where it was never able to grow. And it was never able to grow where the building was.

You walk through a neighborhood of these rectangular absences. They are arranged in a grid — not perfectly, but with enough regularity to be unmistakable. Streets. The streets are wide, perhaps eight meters, and the blocks are long. At intervals there are circular gaps in the growth — former pillars, you think, or posts. They are spaced with the patience of surveying. Someone measured this. Someone planned it.

The scale of the settlement is hard to judge because the ceiling is lost and the walls are far, but you walk for what feels like a kilometer and the grid does not end. It changes character — the rectangles become larger here, the spacing wider — and you understand you have moved from what was perhaps a residential district to something industrial or civic. There are larger absences, circles fifteen meters across where enormous structures once stood. The vegetation does not grow in them. The stone is slightly different — you can see the color shift, a paler gray, as if whatever was built here altered the chemistry of the bedrock itself.

---

The sound is the thing that will stay with you.

It begins as you descend, a low vibration more felt than heard, and it grows until the air itself seems to be trembling. By the time you reach the cavern floor it is a physical presence, a pressure on your eardrums and your sternum and your teeth. It is not one sound. It is layers.

The deepest layer is the stone itself. This far underground, under the weight of whatever is above — and you cannot gauge the depth, but it is considerable — the rock is not still. It shifts. Not dramatically, not in a way that would produce earthquakes, but in a constant, infinitesimal grinding, a settling that has been ongoing for so long that it has become a kind of music. A bass note so low it is below hearing, felt only in the bones. The stone is accommodating its own weight, finding its final configuration, and it has been doing this for longer than the city has been dead.

Above that, a mid-range roar that comes from the air itself. This cavern is not sealed. There are connections — fissures, passages, the remnants of the corridor you came through and others like it — to other voids in the rock, and air moves through these connections with a force that builds over distance. The sound of that movement is constant, a white-noise susurrus that varies in pitch depending on which passage is momentarily dominant. It is like standing near a large ventilation system, except there is no machinery, only geology. The wind has been blowing through these channels since the channels existed, and it will blow until they close.

And above both of these, a high, thin, ceaseless noise that you eventually identify as coming from the fauna.

---

There are animals here. Many of them.

They are small. The largest you see is perhaps the size of your hand, and most are much smaller — beetle-like, mite-like, things with too many legs or too few, things that move with a deliberation that suggests cold-blooded metabolisms operating in a cool environment. The temperature down here is perhaps ten or twelve degrees Celsius. Comfortable for you, if you are dressed for it. Not warm enough for rapid movement.

The creatures are the color of the stone and the vegetation — grays, rust-reds, bronzes, the occasional pale cream. They live in the growth, on the growth, of the growth. You see them most clearly when you disturb a stand of the frond-like structures and a dozen of them scatter, moving with a mechanical, clockwork precision that is more unsettling than speed would be. They do not flee in panic. They relocate with purpose. They have somewhere to be.

Their sound is not individual. No single creature here makes a noise you could hear. But there are — you cannot count them. Millions? Tens of millions? They occupy every surface, every layer of the mineral-fed vegetation, every crevice in the stone. And they make sound. A stridulation, a scraping, a vibration of wings or mouthparts or chitinous plates against each other. The collective noise is in the upper registers, a high, sustained whine that exists at the threshold of discomfort. It does not stop. It does not vary. It is the sound of the cavern's biology, and it has been playing for longer than you can meaningfully imagine.

You watch one of the creatures for a long time. It is on a frond of the iron-gray growth, and it is doing something that looks like eating. Its mouthparts — or what you assume are mouthparts — are in contact with the surface of the growth, and there is a slow, rhythmic motion. It is grazing. The mineral-vegetation is food. The creatures are the grazers. And you realize, looking at the floor, that the channels — the shallow erosion features — are also, in part, the work of these animals. They have been eating the stone, one generation at a time, for so long that their grazing has become a geological force.

---

The light down here is not light in the way you are used to. There is no source. No bioluminescence, no luminescent fungus, no glow from the creatures. What there is, instead, is a failure of darkness — a slow dimness that seems to come from everywhere and nowhere, as though the stone itself is very slightly luminous in a spectrum that your eyes can barely register. It is enough to see by, if your eyes have had time to adjust, which they have. It is not enough to see far by. The world extends perhaps twenty meters in any direction before it dissolves into a gray ambiguity that could be distance or could be fog or could simply be the limit of what this light will show you.

You walk further into the city. The grid continues. Here and there the pattern is disrupted — a diagonal channel cuts through several blocks, a circular structure interrupts the grid at a point that might once have been an intersection. The city was not built all at once. It grew. And then it stopped growing, and then it began to be worn away, and now it is this: a floor with absences in it, a plan so old it has become a property of the landscape rather than a record of intent.

In one place, the vegetation clears entirely, and you find yourself standing on bare stone that is smooth — not the rough natural stone of the cavern floor but something polished, either by use or by time or by both. It is a plaza. You can feel the scale of it even if you cannot see its edges. The stone beneath your feet is different here: harder, denser, less susceptible to the chemical weathering that has flattened everything else. This material was chosen. This was important.

In the center of the plaza — you find it by walking, by the change in the acoustics as the space around you opens up — there is a hole. Not a large one. Perhaps a meter across. It goes down. You cannot see how far. The air that comes up from it is cooler than the cavern air, and it carries a faint smell — sulfur, or something near to sulfur, dry and mineral and old. The sound changes at this hole. The deep bass of the stone settling becomes louder, more focused, as though the hole is a channel for it, an amplifier. The air-movement noise is different too — there is a current here, a slow exchange of atmosphere between this level and whatever is below.

You sit at the edge of the hole and listen. The sound is enormous. All three layers — stone, wind, creature — are present, and they combine into something that is not noise but is not music either. It is the sound of a place that has been doing what it does for so long that the doing has become a feature of the place, as much a part of its character as the stone or the growth or the grid of empty rectangles that mark where a civilization once decided to build something and did.

---

The fauna are most abundant near the hole. They cluster on the polished stone around its rim, and you see species you have not seen elsewhere — larger, slower, with bodies that seem to incorporate the sulfur-smelling mineral you have been smelling. They are pale. They do not move when you approach. They are not afraid of you. They may not have the concept of you. You are a temporary disturbance in a place that does not experience disturbance, that has not experienced disturbance for a span of time so long it has no name in any language you know.

You leave the plaza. The city goes on. You walk for another hour and the grid does not end. The vegetation thickens and thins in patterns that might be seasonal on a timescale of centuries. The creatures shift in their populations, one species dominating for a stretch, then another. The stone changes color subtly — paler here, darker there, a vein of something harder that resists the chemical erosion and stands up a few centimeters above the surrounding floor, a ridge that the city-builders may once have used as a foundation, or may once have had to cut through.

The ceiling, you eventually realize, is there. It is simply very far up. The light dims upward and at some point — forty meters, sixty, you cannot tell — it gives way to a darkness so total it might as well be solid. But there is a ceiling. The cavern is finite. It is perhaps two kilometers across, you estimate, based on how long you have walked and the direction the grid extends. A buried city in a buried chamber, inhabited now only by things that eat stone and make a sound that will outlast everything you have ever known.

The last thing you notice before you turn back is that the vegetation, near the far edge of the cavern, changes. The iron-gray growth gives way to something darker — a near-black, fibrous, dense, climbing the walls of the cavern where they finally become visible. It is alive in a more aggressive way than the rest of the growth. It does not follow the channels. It makes its own patterns. And the creatures here are different too — quicker, darker, more numerous. The sound is louder. It is always louder, the further you go, as though the cavern is a bowl and the noise collects at its edges.

You turn back. The walk out takes longer than the walk in. The sound does not diminish. It does not diminish for a long time. Even after you climb back through the worn staircase, through the crack in the earth, into the open air above, you can still feel it — a low hum in your molars, a pressure behind your ears, the memory of a place that has been making the same noise for so long that the noise is no longer something the place *does*. It is what the place *is*.
