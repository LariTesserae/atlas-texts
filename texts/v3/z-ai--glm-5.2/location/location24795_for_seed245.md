# The Plain of Making

The first thing is the floor.

Not ground — floor. Under the thin standing water and the condensed mist, under the mineral scale and the heat-shimmer, there is floor. It continues in every direction further than the eye corrects for, further than the mind holds steady. It is not stone laid down over geological time. It is not bedrock. It is *made.* You can see the tool marks.

They are everywhere, the tool marks. Grooves from something like a chisel, broad and confident, sweeping across surfaces the size of provinces. Gouges where a blade bit too deep and was dragged. Stippling from a point struck again and again into a surface that yields like cooled basalt but remembers every blow. The marks are not decorative. They are the evidence of hands. Something made this. Everything. The entire plain, the low hills that barely qualify as hills, the shallow basins where warm water collects, the ridges that channel the condensation into slow rivulets — all of it was fabricated. There is no substrate beneath. There is no "beneath" that is not also made.

The scale reorders your thinking. You stand on a floor that stretches to a horizon made hazy by wet heat, and the floor is *worked.* Every square meter of it bears the imprint of tools. The effort implied is monstrous. Not the effort of one people or one era — the effort of a process, a compulsion, something that made and made and made because making was what it did. The surface is not refined. The technology reads as primitive: cutting, scraping, striking, grinding. No machining. No fabrication finer than what a hand with a hard edge can accomplish. And yet it covers a continent.

---

The water is warm. Not hot enough to scald — usually — but warm the way blood is warm, warm the way something alive is warm, though there is nothing alive here. It seeps up through seams in the made-floor, rising from channels that were cut into the surface deliberately. Someone carved the drainage. Someone cut the courses where water should flow. The water emerges already heated, as though the interior of the floor is a body with a metabolism. It pools in the low places — and the elevation is very low, almost uniformly, a landscape that seems to have been planed down to near-flatness, with only gentle undulations breaking the grade. The water spreads thin and wide. It reflects the dim light in long, broken sheets.

The atmosphere is nearly saturated. You feel it on your skin before you see it — a wetness that is not rain, not mist exactly, but a constant condensation. The air is so full of water that every surface wears a film. The made-floor gleams where the water sits and darkens where it has been channeled away. In the low places, the standing water is barely a centimeter deep, but it extends for kilometers without interruption, and because the floor beneath it is carved with regular, deliberate grooves, the water catches the dim light and lines the earth with pale silver geometry.

It is not raining. But it is always about to rain. The sky — if it is a sky — is a low, uniform dimness without source. There is no sun visible. No moon. The light comes from everywhere and nowhere, a flat illumination that might be the floor itself giving off a faint glow, or might be something above the haze. You cannot find the origin. You can look for it, and you will fail, and the looking will begin to feel like a mistake.

---

There is no life.

This needs to be said plainly because the landscape wants to trick you. The warmth, the water, the humidity, the soft contours — your body reads these as *habitable.* Your body expects moss, expects reeds at the water's edge, expects something growing in the wet seams. There is nothing. The floor is bare. The ridges are bare. The basins hold warm water and nothing else. No algae. No spore cases. No filament, no film, no bloom. The material of the floor — which is dark, dense, like fired clay or some vitrified earth — does not host growth. It does not decay. It is inert in a way that feels deliberate, as though the makers chose a medium that would resist being colonized, that would remain *only itself.*

There are no animals. No insects, no worms in the wet channels, no motion in the warm pools except the slow creep of water seeking its level. You listen for the small sounds that fill wet places — clicks, hums, the wet pop of bubbles in mud — and they are absent. The silence is not absolute; there is the murmur of water moving through carved channels, the faint hiss of condensation forming and falling, a low, almost subsonic vibration that might be the floor itself settling. But the sounds are all mineral. All mechanical. Nothing breathes.

The quiet is wrong for how much is happening.

---

Because much is happening. The plain is *turbulent,* though the turbulence is not the kind you can easily name. It is not storm. It is not wind. It is a turbulence in the fabric of the place itself — in the made-floor, in the warm water, in the dim light. Something is *unstable* here, and the instability is constant.

You see it first in the floor. The tool marks — those broad chisel grooves, those stippled fields — do not stay the same. You walk a section of floor covered in diagonal scoring, you turn to check your bearings, you turn back, and the scoring has shifted. Not dramatically. Not suddenly. But the angle is different now. The depth of the grooves has changed. A mark that was a single clean cut now appears to have been struck three times. You did not see it change. You are not sure it changed while you watched or only when you looked away. But it is different.

This is happening everywhere, at every scale. Over hours, the gentle ridges that channel the water reshape themselves. A ridge sinks. A basin rises. The carved drainage courses reroute by millimeters, and the warm water follows, flooding a new section of floor, draining another. The tool marks shift, deepen, multiply, or smooth themselves out as though someone is still working the surface — as though the making never stopped — but there is no one here. There are no hands. The floor is making itself, or being made, or remembering being made, and the distinction between these has collapsed.

Further out — much further, at the edge of what you can resolve through the wet haze — the instability becomes more dramatic. The floor rises. Not gradually. It *piles,* like something is pushing from below, and new surface erupts in raw, unworked material. This is the emerging quality of the place: it is under construction. It is still being born. You can see, at the horizon, the moment of creation — raw geology pushing up through the made-surface, dark and hot and wet, like the world is extruding itself, like something is forcing new floor into existence at continental scale. And then, slowly, the tool marks appear on it. As if by memory. As if the floor teaches itself to be made.

This is where the reality begins to break.

---

You walk for what might be a day and the landscape does not change. You walk for what might be another day and the landscape has changed completely but you cannot say when. You are on the same made-floor — the tool marks are here, the warm water, the dim light — but the arrangement is different. The ridges run in new directions. The basins are deeper. The water is hotter. You did not cross a boundary. There was no transition. The place simply became different, and your memory of the previous arrangement feels less like a memory and more like a story you were told about a place you have not been.

You sit. The warm water rises around you, soaking everything, and the floor beneath you is warm and hard and covered in marks that you can trace with your fingers. You trace them. They feel intentional. Someone cut these grooves. Someone with tools, with hands, with patience and enormous time. But when you lift your fingers and look again, the grooves you traced are gone and new ones are there, and they do not match the shape your fingers remember.

The world is not broken. It is not decaying. It is *turbulent in its constitution.* The making — the primitive, hand-scale, chisel-and-grinder making — is ongoing, but without a maker. The floor is a record of making that is constantly being rewritten. The water is warm because the interior of the floor is hot, and the interior is hot because the material is still *arriving,* still being born, still pushing up from some source that is not volcanic and not tectonic but something else, something that has to do with the fact that this place is entirely artificial and has not yet decided what it is.

The dim light does not help. It flattens everything. You cannot judge distance. The horizon wavers in the wet heat, and the floor seems to curve upward at the edges, as though you are standing in a shallow bowl, or as though the plain is not flat but convex, bulging, and you are near the top of something that is still growing. Or the floor is flat and the light is wrong. Or the floor is not flat and the light is honest. You cannot determine which, and the trying makes something in your thinking come loose.

---

There are structures. This is the word you use, though it is inadequate. Out in the sparse, widely separated emptiness of the plain — and the plain is sparse, most of it is just floor and water and the dim sky — there are *incidents.* Places where the made-floor has been shaped into something more than surface. You come upon one after walking a long time through nothing.

It is a wall. Or it was a wall. It rises from the floor in a curve, maybe three meters high, made of the same dark vitrified material, covered in the same tool marks. But it is incomplete. It curves and then stops. It curves and then *becomes* the floor again, as though the distinction between wall and ground is not fixed. The tool marks on it are denser, more deliberate — here, someone was trying to make a *thing,* not just a surface — but the marks are shifting too. The wall is slowly sinking, or the floor is slowly rising, or the two are trading places. Warm water runs down the curve of it. Condensation weeps from the tool marks like the wall is sweating.

You find another, much later. This one is a pit — a broad, shallow excavation in the made-floor, maybe fifty meters across. The edges are worked, beveled, deliberate. The bottom is flooded with hot water that steams faintly in the dim light. The pit is not old. The tool marks at its edge are crisp. But as you watch, the edge shifts. The pit is *growing,* or *shrinking,* or changing shape, and the water within it is getting hotter. Something is happening below. The floor is being opened. And then, slowly, the edge begins to close again. The pit fills itself in. Not with sediment. Not with debris. The material of the floor simply *reconstitutes,* growing inward from the edges, closing the wound. New tool marks appear on the fresh surface, as if the floor remembers that it is supposed to be made and begins making itself again.

This is the pattern. The plain creates itself and uncreates itself in slow, constant turbulence. Structures emerge — walls, pits, channels, ridges, things you cannot name — and the floor absorbs them again. Everything is made. Everything is being made. Nothing is finished. The making is the only process. The making is the only thing that happens.

---

The sound, such as it is, is the sound of making. The quiet is profound but not empty. You hear, very faintly, a kind of grinding. Not the grinding of stone on stone — it is too regular, too patient. It is the ghost of grinding. The memory of a chisel on hard material. It comes from the floor, from the warm water, from the dim air. It is the sound of the tool marks being struck, except no tool is striking. It is the sound of the surface being worked, except no hand is working. The world makes the sound of its own making because the making is what it is. Remove the making and there would be nothing.

You lie on the floor. The warm water covers you thinly. The tool marks press into your back. Above you, the dim sky does not move. There are no clouds. There is no weather, except the constant condensation, the weeping of water from an atmosphere that is too full to hold it all. The water falls on you in fine, warm droplets that are not rain. They are the air giving up. They are the world's smallest surrender.

The floor is hot beneath you. Not painfully. Comfortably, if you are the kind of thing that finds comfort in warmth. And you realize, slowly, that the comfort is part of the problem. This place wants you to stay. This place wants you to lie in the warm water on the made-floor and feel the tool marks beneath you and listen to the ghost-grinding of a making that never stops. This place wants you to stop walking. To stop looking for the edge, or the maker, or the sky's source. It wants you to become part of the surface. To receive tool marks of your own.

The floor shifts beneath you. You feel it. A slow, tectonic shrug, the kind of motion that should be impossible to feel but is not, because the floor is thin here, because the floor is *new,* because it is still arriving from below. You feel the heat of the new material pushing up. You feel the surface above it trying to hold — trying to keep its grooves, its patterns, its deliberate carved courses — and beginning to yield.

You get up. You walk. The plain continues. The water runs in its channels. The tool marks change. The world makes itself.

There is no edge. There is no maker. There is only the making, and it does not stop, and it is quiet, and it is hot, and the water is warm, and the light is dim, and you are here, and the floor was made for you or made despite you or made without any knowledge of you, and the distinction has broken down, and you walk, and the floor walks with you.
