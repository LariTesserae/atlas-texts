# The Condensation Gallery

It sits inside the uppermost hollow of a mineral spire — one of thousands that rose from a recrystallization event nobody fully understands, in a range nobody has adequately named. The spire itself is perhaps ninety meters tall. The gallery inside it is the size of a concert hall, if that concert hall had been carved by pressure rather than hands, and then colonized by people who needed somewhere to work.

You reach it by ladder. Forty meters of iron rungs set into a shaft that someone bored through the spire's interior lattice, each rung cold enough that bare skin sticks for an instant before the body pulls away. The shaft is dry. Everything here is dry. The mineral walls have a frosted look but it is not frost — it is the crystal structure itself, pale and vaguely translucent, catching what little light reaches this high and scattering it into a flat ambient gray that seems to come from no direction in particular.

Then the shaft opens into the gallery.

---

The first impression is density. The chamber is not large but it is *full* — a three-dimensional clutter of scaffolding, ductwork, suspended platforms, cable runs, and machinery whose purpose is not immediately legible. Nothing sits on a floor because the floor is uneven, a jagged mineral surface of fresh crystalline growth that nobody has bothered to flatten. Instead, everything is bolted to the walls or suspended from above on tension cables, creating a layered workspace that stacks four or five levels high in places. You navigate by walking on grated platforms that flex underfoot, gripping handrails that are cold enough to feel damp even though they are perfectly dry.

The light comes from industrial fixtures — bare bulbs in wire cages, yellow-orange, spaced irregularly, many of them flickering or half-dead. They cast the machinery in sharp relief and leave pools of shadow between. The overall illumination is dim, the kind of light you adjust to rather than see by. After ten minutes, your pupils have widened enough that the space feels almost bright, but the corners still hold darkness, and the higher levels fade into murk where the bulbs have burned out and not been replaced.

The machinery is the settlement's reason for existing. This spire and others like it produce a mineral condensate — not a liquid, not a gas, something between states that the workers here call *sweat* with the resigned humor of people who have spent too long around it. The condensate forms on the interior crystal faces when the temperature differential between the spire's core and the exterior air reaches a certain gradient. It happens fast and unpredictably. The machinery exists to harvest it: collection arrays of copper sheeting mounted against the crystal walls, pumps that move the condensate through gaskets and hoses into sealed glass vessels, a small processing unit on the second level that filters and stabilizes the product.

Everything runs on electricity from a generator mounted on the highest platform, a compact diesel unit that chugs quietly in its enclosure. The sound is the dominant one here — a low, steady mechanical heartbeat that you feel in the platforms before you hear it. It is the closest thing the gallery has to silence. When the generator cycles down for maintenance, the quiet that replaces it is oppressive, mineral, a silence that seems to press inward from the crystal walls.

---

The condensate itself is remarkable. It forms as a faint sheen on the crystal surfaces — a film that has the visual quality of mercury but moves differently, slower, more viscous, with a slight iridescence at its edges. It beads and runs in small rivulets down the crystal faces, collecting in natural depressions before the copper arrays catch it and channel it into the system. In the sealed vessels it is pale, almost colorless, with a faint internal glow that might be luminescence or might just be the way it refracts the industrial light.

This is a dry world. The condensate is the closest thing to moisture, and it is not water. It does not behave like water — it does not freeze or boil at familiar temperatures, it does not dissolve the things water dissolves, and the workers handle it with bare hands without irritation. It smells faintly of heated metal, of ozone, of nothing at all.

---

The fauna is the part that surprises visitors.

The gallery is full of living things. They are not animals in the familiar sense — they are something simpler, something that evolved or was designed to thrive in exactly this environment. The workers call them *grainers*. They are small, the largest about the length of a forearm, and they are mineral-biology in a way that is difficult to categorize. Their bodies appear to be composed of the same crystal material as the spire, grown into segmented forms that move on dozens of fine legs, each leg a crystalline filament that flexes with an internal structure more muscle than mineral. They are pale, translucent, and in the dim light they are nearly invisible against the crystal walls.

There are hundreds of them. They crawl across every surface — the crystal walls, the machinery, the cable runs, the platforms. They are drawn to the condensate. They feed on it. The workers have given up trying to keep them out of the collection arrays and instead have built the processing system to filter them out, a series of mesh screens that catch the grainers and divert them into holding compartments where they can be retrieved and released. The grainers do not seem to mind this. They are not aggressive, not territorial, and not particularly reactive to human presence. They move with a slow, deliberate persistence, always toward the condensate, and when they find it they settle and feed, their segmented bodies darkening slightly as the condensate moves through them.

The sound they make is subtle — a fine crystalline clicking, like sand shifting, like glass brushing glass. Individually it is barely audible. In the aggregate, with hundreds of them moving across the gallery at any given time, it creates a textured whisper that underlies the generator's throb. It is the sound of the place, the sound that tells you the gallery is alive and working.

---

The turbulence here is not weather. It is operational.

The condensate forms unpredictably. A wall face that was dry an hour ago may suddenly produce liters of the substance in minutes. The temperature differential shifts with external conditions — wind, cloud cover, the internal state of the spire itself — and the formation rate can spike or collapse without warning. When it spikes, the gallery becomes a place of urgent, focused activity. Workers scramble along the platforms, repositioning collection arrays, extending copper sheeting to new formation sites, adjusting the pump system to handle the increased flow. The grainers surge toward the fresh condensate in waves, their clicking rising to a chatter, and the filtering system struggles to keep up. The noise of the generator is joined by the clatter of equipment being moved, the hiss of gaskets being tightened, the shouted communications of people working in close quarters at speed.

Then it stops. The formation ceases, the condensate on the walls runs dry, the grainers slow, the workers catch their breath on the platforms with their legs dangling over the gaps. The gallery settles back into its dim, humming, clicking equilibrium. The cycle might repeat in an hour or in a day. There is no pattern. There is only the preparation and the response.

This is what the workers live with. There are perhaps thirty of them, a small permanent population that maintains the gallery and its systems, monitors the spire's internal state, and processes the condensate for transport. They live on the upper levels in small compartments walled with sheet metal and insulation, sleeping in bags rated for the cold, eating dried rations heated on the generator's exhaust manifold. There is no water here. They bring what they need from below, packed in on the ladders, and they use it sparingly. The gallery smells of metal and crystal and the faint ozone scent of the condensate and the unwashed competence of people doing hard work in a confined space.

---

The world outside the spire, as glimpsed through a single porthole cut into the gallery's outer wall, is extreme. The elevation is apparent — the porthole looks down onto clouds, onto a landscape of other spires rising from a floor that is lost in haze and distance. The air here is thin. The workers move slowly, conserving oxygen, and the generator runs on a fuel mixture adjusted for altitude. The cold is constant, a dry mineral cold that comes through the crystal walls and the sheet metal and the insulation, a cold that is not hostile but is simply *present*, the baseline temperature of a world that has no atmosphere to speak of and no water to moderate.

At night — or what passes for night, when the external light fades and the gallery's bulbs become the only illumination — the space takes on a different character. The crystal walls, which are translucent, begin to transmit a faint external darkness that pools in their depths, and the gallery feels as though it is suspended in a void, a pocket of human industry floating in mineral space. The grainers continue their slow clicking. The generator continues its throb. The condensate may or may not form. The workers sleep or don't, and the gallery holds them in its cold, dry, dense, humming embrace, a small machine-world perched at the top of a spire that nobody fully understands, producing something that nobody fully needs but that the system — the spire, the gallery, the workers, the grainers, the machinery — continues to produce because that is what it does.

It is not beautiful. It is not ugly. It is *working*.
