# The Cirque of Weeping Stone

It begins with sound before anything else.

You hear it from the pass below — a layered roar that builds as you climb, not one sound but many compressed into a single wash of noise that the stone walls catch and multiply. Water is the base note. Not the clean spatter of a stream but a distributed, diffuse wetness — a thousand seeps and rills running down vertical faces, dripping from overhangs, gathering in cracks and falling again. Beneath that, wind. Not steady wind but the kind that finds a high basin and tumbles inside it, circling, pressing against the walls, dropping and rising, carrying the sound of water back down into the basin and lifting it again. And beneath both, or perhaps woven through them, the voices of living things — a chittering, calling, croaking constancy that sits right at the edge of separable sound, so that you cannot quite tell where water ends and life begins.

Then you crest the final ridge and see it.

---

The cirque is small. You can walk its floor in ten minutes, maybe less — a rough oval of ground cupped between three sheer faces of metamorphic rock that rise to points lost in cloud. The walls are close, almost intimate, the kind of space that feels like the inside of a skull. Cathedral is the word that comes to mind, but this is smaller than a cathedral and older and less geometric. The rock walls are not flat but deeply fluted, carved into overlapping sheets and folds by millennia of freeze-thaw, their surfaces dark with moisture and furred with moss and the threadlike roots of plants that have found holds in the vertical stone. Water moves down these faces not in discrete streams but as a film, a gloss, a brightness that catches light and moves it. The rock weeps from every pore. You can see the water emerging — not from cracks exactly but from the stone itself, as though the mountain is saturated through, as though the interior of the peak is one vast aquifer pressing outward.

The floor of the cirque is uneven — a jumble of angular boulders in various states of moss-colonization, patches of bare gravel dark with wet, and between them channels where the runoff has organized itself into braided paths that interweave and split and rejoin. There is no single stream. The water does not commit to one course. It spreads, collects in shallow pools in the hollows between rocks, overflows, finds new paths. The ground is the kind of wet where every step produces a sound — a squelch, a splash, the grinding crunch of gravel saturated to its surface tension. You cannot walk here quietly if you try.

And yet for all this water, the space feels *empty* in a way that is difficult to articulate. The walls are close, the floor is small, the life is abundant — but the air itself is enormous. The sky above is a wide oval of grey-white cloud, and the stone walls rise so high that the volume of air contained in this small footprint is vast, cathedral-vast, greater-than-cathedral-vast. You stand at the bottom of a deep stone well and the emptiness above you has weight. The sounds fill it but cannot saturate it. They echo and die and echo again and the silence between is the silence of large spaces, of interiors, of places that contain more air than matter. The density of physical objects on the floor — the boulders, the plants, the animals — is high, but measured against the volume of sky and stone that holds them, it is almost nothing. A thin film of life at the bottom of an enormous hollow.

---

The vegetation does not grow upward here. It grows *outward*, laterally, hugging stone and ground as though the wind has trained it over centuries to keep low. Mosses dominate — not one kind but a dozen, differentiated by shade of green and texture and the particular wetness they prefer. Some are almost black and grow in the darkest recesses where water drips slowest. Some are a bright, almost luminous yellow-green and coat the faces of boulders that receive a few hours of diffused light. Between the mosses, other plants have found purchase: small ferns with fronds no longer than a finger, their edges curled slightly under with the cold. Sedges that grow in tussocks at the margins of the pools, their roots presumably in standing water. Cushion plants — dense, dome-shaped, springy underfoot — that have colonized the flattest gravel patches and are slowly, over decades, turning bare stone dust into soil. There are no trees. There are no shrubs of any height. Everything keeps itself below the wind-line, and the wind-line here is perhaps a hand's span above the ground.

But within that constraint, the growth is *thick*. Lush, if lush can be applied to things that grow sideways. The moss layers overlap and underlie each other. The cushion plants press against one another like cobblestones. The sedges fill every depression where water persists. There is no bare ground that is not in the process of being claimed. Even the vertical faces, the walls of the cirque, are colonized up to a certain height — perhaps twenty meters — above which the rock becomes too sheer, too smooth, too cold for anything to hold. Below that line, the stone is barely stone at all. It is substrate. It is a surface for life.

The temperature is the kind of cold that is not immediately hostile but that works on you slowly. You do not feel it as a shock. You feel it as an absence of warmth, a gradual recognition that your body is losing heat to the air and the wet and the stone. The rock is cold when you touch it — not ice-cold, but the deep, steady cold of a mass that has been absorbing temperature from meltwater for months. The water is colder than the air. You can feel this where the seeps cross your path: the air near the running water is denser, heavier, and your ankles ache in it. Breath does not visibly steam, but it is close. You are at the altitude where the air is thin enough that you notice its thinness in your lungs — each breath slightly less satisfying than expected, a faint hunger for depth that cannot be quite met.

---

The fauna is the surprise.

Not that there is life here — the vegetation suggests that something could survive — but the *abundance* of it, the density of living bodies in this small, cold, high, wet place. The cirque is loud, and a significant portion of that loudness is animal.

Birds first. Small birds, dark-plumaged, that move along the walls in flocks of fifteen or twenty, landing on the wet rock faces and picking at the moss, at the insects in the moss. They are pipits, or something like pipits — lean, leggy, constantly in motion, their calls a sharp ticking that bounces off the stone and multiplies. They do not stay still. A flock lands on the eastern wall, feeds for two minutes, erupts as one body, crosses the cirque to the western wall, feeds, erupts again. The sound of their wings is audible — a soft fabric-tearing sound — and when they call simultaneously the ticking becomes a rattling buzz that fills the entire basin and reflects back from every surface so that it seems to come from everywhere at once.

Frogs. This is the strange part, the part that takes a moment to accept. Frogs at extreme elevation, in a wet stone basin, in cold air. But they are here — small, dark, wet-looking things that sit at the edges of the pools and in the moss alongside the seeps. Their call is a low, rhythmic pulsing croak, not the bright singing of lowland frogs but something more guttural, more mechanical, like a wooden ratchet being turned slowly. They call continuously. The sound is the ground-note of the cirque's soundscape, the layer that is always present beneath the water and the wind and the birds. When you approach a pool, they stop. You stand still. One by one, tentatively, they begin again. There are dozens visible at the margins of a single pool, and the pools are many, and the frogs are many, and the combined sound of them is a low vibration you feel in your sternum as much as hear.

Insects in the moss. You can hear them if you crouch close — a faint rustling, ticking, the sound of small bodies moving through damp vegetation. The birds are eating them. The frogs are eating them. They are the foundation of something here, a small food web that operates in compressed form within the walls of the cirque: the plants and their tenants, the insects that live on the plants, the frogs and birds that eat the insects, and — you find evidence of this near the southern wall — something that eats the frogs. A scatter of small dark bones. A dropping. A smear on a rock that could be blood or could be the dark mud that coats everything. You do not see the predator. It may be nocturnal. It may be watching. The ecosystem is small enough that every participant is known to every other, and the absence of the predator from view is itself a kind of presence.

The pipits do something that you watch for several minutes before you understand it. One flock, feeding on the wall, will begin to move upward — not flying but hopping, climbing, picking — and as they climb, they call more frequently, and the flock behind them, feeding lower on the wall, hears the calls and also begins to climb. The entire population shifts upward in waves, following some pattern of insect availability that you cannot see, and then at some signal they all drop to the floor and begin again at the base. It is like watching a tide, if tides were made of small dark birds and moved by appetite.

---

There are signs that people have been here.

Not many signs. Not recent ones. On the eastern side of the cirque, where the wall curves outward and forms a shallow overhang — a recess, not a cave, a place where the rock has eroded back to create a dry space beneath the stone — there is a structure. Or the remains of one. Someone has stacked flat stones into a low wall across the opening of the overhang, creating a windbreak, a shelter. The wall is perhaps knee-high, perhaps six meters long, built of the same dark stone as everything else. It has partly collapsed. Moss grows on the upper surfaces of the stones. It has been here a long time.

Inside the overhang, the ground is drier than elsewhere — only damp, not wet — and there are signs of use. A circle of blackened stones where a fire was built, long ago, the ash long since washed away or blown away but the scorch on the surrounding rock still faintly visible. A niche in the wall where someone has placed a flat stone as a shelf. On the shelf, nothing. Nearby, a tool — you almost miss it, but it catches light because it is not the same stone as everything else. It is lighter in color, a pale grey, and it has been worked: flakes removed from one edge to create a crude but functional cutting surface. A handaxe, or something like one. It sits on the ground as though set down and never picked up again.

The shelter is dry and quiet relative to the cirque. Standing inside it, the sound of the water and the frogs and the birds is slightly muffled, filtered through the stone overhang, reduced to something almost peaceful. Someone lived here, or stayed here, for some period. A hermit. A traveler. A watcher. The cirque is the kind of place that collects people who want to be at the edge of things, at the top of things, in places where the world is reduced to a small stone cup of sound and wet and life. The shelter looks out over the entire floor of the cirque. From here, you could watch the frogs and the birds and the water and the slow motion of cloud across the opening of sky above. You could listen to the sound that the cirque makes, which is the sound of a place that has been here for longer than anything can remember and will be here long after everything in it is gone.

---

The weirdness of the place is not dramatic. It is not a place where the rules break. It is a place where the rules hold so completely, so tightly, so densely, that they become visible. The food web is small enough to see whole. The water cycle is contained within stone walls. The erosion is in balance with the growth — the rock breaks down at the same rate that the moss builds up, and the gravel that the water moves is replaced by the gravel that the frost makes, and the moss that dies becomes the soil that the cushion plants root in, and the insects that eat the dead moss are eaten by the frogs that are eaten by the thing you do not see. Everything is accounted for. Everything connects.

And the frogs. The frogs are the strange thing. Not that they are here — there are high-altitude frogs elsewhere, you are sure — but the *number* of them, the density, the way they have colonized every pool and seep and saturated patch of moss. They are the dominant large animal in this ecosystem, more numerous than the birds, more visible than the insects. They call and call and call, and the sound of their calling fills the cirque the way water fills the cracks in the stone — completely, inevitably, finding every space. It is a sound that you stop hearing consciously after a few minutes and then hear again, suddenly, as if for the first time, and it is always louder than you remembered.

Standing in the center of the cirque, surrounded by water and moss and the low vibration of frogs and the sharp ticking of pipits and the vast hollow air above, you have the sense of being inside a mechanism — not a machine, nothing so rigid, but a system, a process, a place where every element is in motion and every motion serves something. The water moves. The moss grows. The insects feed. The frogs eat. The birds follow. The stone erodes. The soil forms. The cold deepens. The clouds pass. And the sound — the total sound of all of this happening at once — is very loud, and very continuous, and very much the sound of a place that does not need you to be here.

The tool on the ground inside the overhang is the only sign that anyone else ever was.
