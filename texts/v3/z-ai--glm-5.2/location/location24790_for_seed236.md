# The Amberlac

The floor gives a dull chime when you step on it — not glass, not stone, something between. A mineral shell, thin in places, ringing softly where it bridges hollows beneath. The sound dies almost at birth. The air takes it.

This is a room. That is the first impression and it never fully leaves: you are *inside*. The ceiling vaults upward into a smooth, pale curvature like the inside of a skull or a bell, perhaps thirty meters overhead at its apex, though the height is difficult to judge because the upper surface is furred with growth and the growth swallows dimension. Light enters from no identifiable source. It is simply *present* — a clear, dry, amber-warm luminance that seems to adhere to surfaces rather than travel through space, as though the air itself were faintly luminous and the objects here merely caught what fell out of it. There are no shadows with edges. Everything shades gradually into its own dimness.

No water. The absence is total and strange — you can feel it in the way the air moves against your skin, or rather doesn't move. There is no moisture to carry temperature, no evaporation, no coolness of condensation on stone. The warmth here is geological. It rises from below, from whatever shallow magmatic body still pulses beneath the crust, and it saturates everything with an even, patient heat, like the interior of a kiln that has been shut off but not yet cooled. Perhaps forty degrees. Perhaps warmer. The warmth has a texture. It feels slightly oily, though there is no oil — your hands come away dry.

What serves the role of water is a thick, pale vapor that exudes from fractures in the mineral floor. It is heavier than air and pools in the lower passages, flowing downhill in slow, viscous curtains when the ground tilts. It smells of nothing — or of stone heated to the edge of its tolerance, that dry mineral almost-smell of a beach in full sun. The vegetation drinks it. You can see the plants pulling it in, their broad, flat leaves oriented to intercept the slow drift, surfaces slightly concave, faintly sticky with whatever secretion allows them to absorb the gas directly. The leaves are not green. They are deep umber, copper, tarnished bronze — the colors of metal left in weather, of old coins, of soil rich in oxides. Some trend toward a violet so dark it reads as black until light catches the surface and the color blooms unexpectedly, a flush of indigo.

The growth is *dense*. This is the second overwhelming impression. Every surface that is not actively walked upon — and several that are — bears a layered community of organisms. The floor's mineral shell is visible only in narrow channels where passage has worn it clean. Everywhere else, the substrate is buried under a deep, soft carpet of low-growing foliage, overlapping leaf upon leaf in shingled rows, and above this rises a canopy of taller stalks — not trees, exactly, they are too slender and too numerous, more like a forest of dark reeds or bamboo, but curving, arcing, interweaving overhead until they form a partial second ceiling, a woven lattice of living material. The space between the ground layer and the canopy is perhaps two and a half meters — enough to walk upright, not enough to forget the density. Leaves brush your shoulders. Stalks tick against each other above your head. The air between growth-layers is warmer and heavier, almost humid, though the humidity is not water but that same mineral vapor, concentrated by the foliage's respiration into a haze that blurs distance.

And through and around and across all of this — the structures.

They are grown, not built. There is no other word. They are not carved and there is no masonry and there are no tools because there are no tools here — but there are *walls*. Curving walls of a material that appears to be compressed mineral and living tissue in equal part, rising in places to meet the canopy, forming rooms, corridors, chambers, archways. The surfaces are smooth but not uniform. They have the texture of something that was once soft and hardened in place — ripples, striations, the faint impression of cellular patterns, like bone, like coral, like the inside of a shell. Some walls are hollow and when you press your hand against them they give very slightly, with a faint resilience, before the rigid mineral matrix reasserts itself. Doorways — openings, rather — are not square. They are ovate, rounded at top and bottom, as though the wall simply grew around the space and left an aperture because the aperture was useful and the organism, or whatever process made these, could distinguish between wall and not-wall.

There are perhaps forty of these structures visible within the chamber. Some are no larger than a closet. Others — the largest — form complexes of five or six connected rooms, their walls merging into shared membranes, their interiors visible through doorways that align in sequences that suggest streets, or at least paths of intention. It is a settlement, small and complete, organized around a central open space where the mineral floor is bare and rings with that dull bell-tone when crossed. The ground here is warm against bare feet. Below it, something moves — not liquid, not solid, a thick circulating medium in a slow convection, carrying heat from below, and you can feel the rhythm of it through the soles of your feet, a pulse so slow that four heartbeats pass between each surge.

The fauna.

They are here in abundance and they are quiet and they are slow and they are *everywhere*. The first you notice are the ones on the walls — flat, hand-sized creatures with segmented bodies like overlapping plates of dark mineral, moving across the growth-covered surfaces with a deliberate, almost geological patience. They graze. You can see the marks they leave — shallow scrapes on the leaves where they have rasped away the outer surface and fed on whatever layer lies beneath. They make no sound. Their motion is the motion of a clock's hour hand: you watch and see nothing, then look away and look back and they are elsewhere.

In the canopy, larger forms roost. They are difficult to see because they are the color of the canopy — dark, metallic, the same bronze and violet as the foliage — but when one shifts position you catch the movement: a slow unfurling of a limb, a repositioning of weight, a body perhaps a meter long, smooth, eyeless, dragging itself along a stalk with a muscled, deliberate grace. It does not vocalize. It does not react to your presence. It is warm — you can feel the heat of it radiating when you pass close beneath, a small furnace of metabolism, burning whatever it burns with that same geological patience.

On the floor, in the open central space, the most visible ones move. They are tall — chest-height to a human — and they walk on four limbs with a posture that is not quadrupedal in any way you are accustomed to. Their bodies are vertical, their hind limbs thick and jointed backward like a grasshopper's, their forelimbs thin and long and used for steadying rather than locomotion. Their surfaces are furred, but the fur is mineral — fine, dark filaments, like asbestos fibers or the threads of dark quartz, and it moves in the nonexistent breeze with a delay that suggests extreme density. They drift across the open ground in groups of three to seven, moving from one growth-patch to another, and they feed by lowering their heads — which are smooth, featureless, teardrop-shaped — into the foliage and remaining there, motionless, for minutes at a time. Their metabolism is audible, but only just: a low, slow, wet sound of air being moved through internal cavities, a respiration cycle that takes perhaps eight seconds per breath. When a group of them passes near you, the collective sound of their breathing is like a single large bellows being worked by a very tired, very patient hand.

They do not react to you. This is not indifference — you are below the threshold of their attention, or outside their sensory range, or simply irrelevant to whatever slow calculus governs their lives. You are furniture. You are terrain.

And the silence.

The silence is the third overwhelming thing, and it is the one that stays with you. This is not the silence of an empty place — the chamber is full, saturated, dense with life and structure and heat. It is the silence of a world in which sound has never been necessary. No calls. No alarm cries. No wind through leaves because there is no wind and the leaves are too dense and too heavy to rustle. The mineral floor rings dully when stepped on and the sound is absorbed by the growth on every side. The fauna breathe, and their breathing is almost subsonic, felt more than heard. The structures make a sound — a faint, arrhythmic creaking, like a wooden ship in calm water, as their walls expand and contract with the slow thermal pulse of the deep heat beneath. These sounds do not combine into anything. They exist separately, each in its own moment, each dying alone.

You realize, after a time, that you have lowered your voice to a whisper and then below a whisper and then into pure gesture, not because anyone asked you to, but because the place makes it feel correct. Sound here is a violation of a principle. Not a rule — nothing here polices you, nothing responds to your noise with alarm or curiosity — but a natural law of the space itself, the way water runs downhill, the way heat rises. This place is built for quiet the way a riverbed is built for water. Sound is not its medium.

The world's rules emerge slowly. Everything here runs on the deep heat and the mineral vapor and the slow, cycling patience of an ecosystem that has no urgency. The structures grow. They are growing now — you can see it in the way the walls meet the floor, a faint thickening at the base, a slow accretion of material, layer by layer, as though the walls are sweating out their own substance and the substance hardens and the wall is taller by a fraction of a millimeter and that is enough, that is the pace, and in a thousand years the walls will have grown to meet the ceiling and the rooms will be sealed and new rooms will have begun outside and the settlement will have shifted outward like a tide. The organisms graze. They breed, presumably, somewhere in the dense canopy or in the hollow spaces beneath the mineral floor. They die and their bodies are absorbed into the growth layer and become substrate for the next generation of foliage, which feeds the next generation of grazers, and the cycle turns with a slowness that makes it almost invisible. You are watching a clock that measures time in centuries and it is, right now, in this moment, between ticks.

The structures have no maker. Or rather, their maker is the same process that made everything else — the heat, the mineral, the growth, the slow chemical negotiation between living and non-living matter. The walls are excretions. The rooms are gaps left in the excretion because the organism — or the ecosystem, or the process — requires structure and structure requires division. It is a city the way a coral reef is a city: built by accumulation, organized by chemistry, inhabited by the same forces that built it. There is no architect. There is only the pattern, repeating, growing, hardening, opening, filling.

You stand in the central space where the floor rings beneath your feet and the warm vapor pools at your ankles and the dark forms of the grazing fauna move past you in their slow processions, breathing their long, deep, silent breaths, and the canopy arcs overhead like the vault of a cathedral that was never consecrated because it was never not sacred, and the walls grow, and the silence holds, and the heat rises, and nothing here needs you, and nothing here has ever needed anything like you, and that is not a rejection. It is simply the character of the place: complete, sufficient, and utterly, profoundly quiet.
