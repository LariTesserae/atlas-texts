# The Ascendant Works

The plateau does not yet know it is a plateau.

It is still becoming. The basalt floor — three kilometers above what would be sea level if this world had settled enough to form seas — was pushed flat by something tectonic and deeply impatient, and then left, still warm, still exhaling heat through fractures that haven't had time to become proper vents. You can feel it through your boots. Not the sharp burn of magma-close ground, but a persistent, low thrum of thermal release, as though the crust beneath you is an animal that has just finished running and is still breathing hard. The rock is dark, fine-grained, largely uncracked. There are no rounded edges here. No smoothing. The surfaces are all original — the face of the stone as it was when it pushed through and stopped, panting, and the air touched it for the first time.

Condensation is constant but never builds to rain. The heat rising from the plateau meets air that, at this altitude, carries just enough moisture to produce a fine, warm dampness that beads on every surface and runs down the angles of things. It does not pool. It traces. The water finds the new cracks, the joints between construction and stone, the seams of the built environment, and follows them downhill — but there is very little downhill. The plateau is so nearly level that the water takes its time. It spreads into films. It darkens the basalt in slow, branching capillaries that almost look like they're being drawn by hand. The air tastes like warm iron and wet chalk.

---

The Works are what they call the construction. Nobody knows who named them. Nobody knows who started them, exactly, though if you walk far enough toward the center of the plateau — which you can, because the scale is open, the whole formation extends past the horizon in a gentle, slightly curved plain that seems to go on for a full valley's length in every direction — you find the first structures.

They are not buildings in the sense you might expect. They are extrusions. Great, dense extrusions of material that look like they were pushed up from below the same way the basalt was, except these are clearly manufactured. They are dark — darker than the stone, almost black — with a faintly metallic sheen on their surfaces that the condensation catches and makes oily. They are rectangular, mostly. Tall. They cluster near one another like teeth in a jaw, and between them the air is thick and warm and still.

There are hundreds of them. Thousands, across the open expanse. They are not uniform, but they are familial — the way siblings share features. One might be forty meters tall and narrow, another sixty and broad, a third squat and wide and seemingly incomplete, its upper surface still rough, still forming. They have been built — or are being built — in a pattern that becomes apparent only from elevation, from one of the few points where the plateau folds upward enough to give you a vantage. From there, the Works form something like a grid. Not a perfect grid. A grid that is being assembled by something that understands the concept but has no attachment to precision. The streets between the structures are wide enough for passage, for the movement of materials, but they are empty. The grid is for a population that has not arrived, or for a process that does not require one.

---

The industrial character of the place is in the machinery.

It is not loud. That is perhaps the strangest thing. The plateau is almost silent. There is a low, continuous register of sound — not a hum, not a drone, but the aggregate exhalation of many machines breathing — that sits at the bottom of perception. You stop hearing it after a few minutes. You notice it again only when you enter one of the structures and the sound drops away and the silence feels like pressure.

The machines are inside the extrusions. They are built into the walls, set into recesses that were clearly designed to hold them. They are mechanical in the old, heavy sense — pistons, cams, drive shafts, gearing systems of immense proportion and patient tempo. But they are not old. They are new, in the way the basalt is new. They have the sheen of recent manufacture. Some of them move. The motion is extraordinarily slow. A piston the length of your arm descends over the course of perhaps a minute, meets its cylinder with a sound like a felt-covered door closing, and then rises again over another minute. This is the fastest machine cycle you can find. Others take longer. Some seem to take hours. You watch a great camwheel the size of a fairground carousel turn what appears to be a single degree and then stop, and you cannot tell if it is between strokes or if one stroke is simply that long.

Everything is powered. There are cables — thick, insulated runs of them — threaded through the gaps between structures, along the streets, looping into junctions and distribution points that are housed in smaller, separate constructions built close to the ground. The power comes from the plateau itself. The heat. Several of the extrusions contain what appear to be thermal exchange systems — dense, layered structures of metal and ceramic that draw heat from the rock through buried conduits and convert it, through means that are not immediately legible, into the electrical current that the cables carry. It is an industrial settlement built to run on the warmth of a world that is still cooling from its own birth.

---

There is moisture inside the structures, too. It condenses on the cooler metal of the machines and runs down their housings in slow tracks. The interior air is warmer than outside, which seems wrong until you realize that the machines themselves generate heat — or perhaps retain the heat of the plateau, which flows through them as a matter of design. The condensation is internal, then, produced by the contact between machine-warmed air and the slightly cooler metal surfaces where the thermal exchange draws heat away. The water collects in channels cut into the floors — deliberate channels, engineered, leading to settling basins and then to outlets that return the water to the plateau's surface drainage. The system is careful. It is built to manage moisture, not to exclude it. This is a place that expects to be wet.

The lack of vegetation is absolute. There is nothing growing. Not on the basalt, not on the extrusions, not in the settling basins where warm water sits for long periods. The surfaces are too hot, too new, too mineral. There is no soil — no decomposition has had time to produce any. The closest thing to organic presence is a faint, patchy discoloration on certain north-facing walls where the condensation has been running longest — a mineral bloom, a deposit of something carried in the water and left behind as it evaporates. It is pale, almost white, and it forms patterns that look, if you squint, like the ghosts of lichen. They are not lichen. They are chemistry.

---

The fauna are rare enough that encountering one is an event.

There are — something. They are hard to classify. They are small, for the most part, no larger than a hand, and they appear only in the warmest interior spaces, near the thermal exchange systems, in the gaps between machinery where the heat is most concentrated. They move slowly. They are not mechanical. They are not, in any recognizable sense, alive. They appear to be assemblages of mineral and metal — delicate, almost crystalline structures that have formed, somehow, in the warmth and the damp and the electrical field of the machines. They do not eat. They do not reproduce in any way you can observe. They move — a slow, considered traversal of the surfaces they occupy, as if they are navigating by a sense you cannot identify. When you approach one, it does not react. When you touch one, it is warm, and its surface is smooth, and it continues its traversal without acknowledgment.

They are, perhaps, what passes for life here. The world is so young, the geology so fresh, the construction so new, that biology has not had time. These mineral assemblages may be the first draft. Or they may be byproducts. Or they may be something the Works were built to produce, and the whole plateau — the heat, the water, the machines, the power grid — is an incubator for something that has not yet revealed its purpose.

---

The quiet is profound. The slowness of everything — the machines, the water, the mineral things, the pace at which the extrusions seem to be growing — gives the place a quality of deep patience. It does not feel abandoned. It feels like it is waiting. Not for someone. For time. The processes here operate on scales that make human attention feel frantic and brief. A piston completes a cycle in a minute. A camwheel completes a rotation in a day. An extrusion, you realize after spending enough time among them, is not finished — they are still growing, still being extruded, at a pace that might be measured in centimeters per week. The plateau is building itself a city, and the city is building itself, and the machines are the mechanism, and the heat is the fuel, and the patience is infinite.

You stand at the edge of one of the wide streets between extrusions and look down its length. The street is straight. The structures on either side are tall and dark and slightly damp. The light is the light of this place — an ambient, even, unremarkable light that does not seem to come from a source but simply exists, the way the heat simply exists, the way the water simply exists. The street extends into the distance and the structures continue along it, block after block, and the condensation runs down their faces in slow rivers that never reach the ground, and the machines inside them breathe their slow, inaudible breaths, and the mineral things move their slow, inexplicable paths across the warm metal, and the plateau — young, hot, emerging — continues to become whatever it is going to be.

It is a place under construction. It is a place that may always be under construction. The blueprint, if there is one, is written in the temperature of the rock and the patience of the machines, and it does not hurry.
