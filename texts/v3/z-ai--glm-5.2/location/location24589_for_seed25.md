# The Hollow

There is a place — call it a basin, though that word implies walls, and this is more like a slow settling of the earth, a broad concavity miles across where the land remembers being lower than it is now. You descend into it without noticing. The grade is so gradual that your calves tell you before your eyes do, that subtle burning that means downhill, and by the time the sky has narrowed to a band of grey crease between dark ridgelines, you are already inside.

**The dark** is the first thing that claims you. Not the black of night — night has stars, has moon-scatter, has that blue-black dome we mistake for emptiness. This is the dark of depth, of proximity to a surface that absorbs. The air itself seems to drink light. If you brought a lamp, its beam would reach perhaps fifteen feet before the dampness in the air dissolved it into a foggy cone that illuminated nothing but its own failure. You learn quickly to stop trying to see and start trying to *feel* the place instead, because the eyes are negotiators working without leverage here.

What you can see, once your eyes surrender their expectations: a darkness with *texture*. The ground is not flat. It is a complex topography of low ripples and shallow depressions carved into a pale, chalky stone — a soft limestone or something like it, something that has been dissolving for longer than anything alive can remember. The erosion is complete, settled, *finished* in the way a riverbed is finished after the river has been gone for centuries. The stone has been sculpted into undulating runnels, into fluted troughs and smooth-backed ridges no higher than your knee, into basins the size of bathtubs and others the size of houses. Everything is rounded. Everything is soft-edged. The stone has given up its sharpness willingly, like a creature that died without struggle.

**The dampness** is not rain. Rain implies sky, and the sky here is a rumor. The moisture comes from the stone itself, from the air, from the condensation that forms on every surface because the cool ground is colder than the air above it, and the air above it carries water it cannot hold. You touch a wall — it is not a wall, just a rise in the stone, but in the dark your hand finds it and calls it wall — and your fingers come away wet. Not dripping. Not flowing. Just *wet*, in the way a glass of cold water is wet on a summer day, the moisture beaded on the surface, perpetually forming, perpetually sliding, perpetually gathering in the low places. The stone sweats. The air sweats. You sweat. Everything is glistening and nothing is submerged. There are pools — small ones, ankle-deep at most, scattered through the lowest of the basins — but they are still, clear, mineral-tasting, and edged with a pale crystalline crust that forms and reforms as the water evaporates and returns. A slow tidal breath, a tidality of condensation.

There is no vegetation. Not a single green thing. Not a thread of moss, not a film of algae. The stone is bare in the way that a bone is bare, not stripped but *revealed*, as though it was always meant to be seen like this, clean and mineral and honest about what it is. If there is a base to the living world here, it is not green and it does not photosynthesize. There are patches — in the deeper basins, in the places where the water sits longest — of something that is not quite fungus and not quite bacterial mat: a film, a skin, pale grey and faintly iridescent, slick to the touch, growing so slowly that you cannot see it move but you can see that it has moved, that yesterday's film is today's thicker patch. It smells faintly of sulfur and chalk. It tastes, if you are foolish enough to test it, of wet metal. This is the closest thing to growth the place offers, and even this is less a living thing than a *process*, a chemical conversation between water and stone and the long, patient dark.

And then the **sound**.

The sound is what people remember. The sound is what breaks them.

It does not start. You realize, after some time in the hollow, that you have been hearing it since you began your descent, that it crept in under your awareness like the dampness crept in under your clothes, and by the time you notice it, it is already enormous, already filling every frequency your ears can register and some they cannot. It is not one sound. It is a *layering*, a density of sound that matches the density of the air and the density of the life here, and it comes from everywhere.

The lowest layer is a thrumming. A vibration more felt than heard, something in the ten-or-fifteen hertz range, below music, below speech, below the vocalizations of any animal you know. It comes from the ground. It comes from the stone. You feel it in your sternum, in your teeth, in the soft tissue behind your eyes. It is not mechanical. It is not geological. It is *biological* — the collective resonance of thousands of bodies producing a subsonic hum, bodies too large or too strange or too deep-buried to see, keeping time with each other in some rhythm that predates language and will outlast it. When the thrumming swells — every few minutes, irregular, unforecastable — the water in the shallow pools shivers and the condensation on the stone beads tighter and you feel your own heartbeat try to sync with it and you have to fight that, you have to actively refuse the invitation, because the rhythm is not kind and it is not yours.

Above the thrumming: **clicking**. Billions of clicks. A rain of hard, small sounds — arthropod sounds, jointed-limb sounds, the tap-and-chitter of chitinous bodies in contact with stone. The clicking is not organized. It is the aggregate noise of an uncountable population going about the business of being alive, each individual producing its small percussion as it moves, as it eats, as it mates, as it defends, and the sum of it is a static, a white noise made of a million distinct signals, each one meaningless alone and overwhelming together. The clicking shifts in density as you move — louder near the pools, louder near the fungal-pale mats in the basins, quieter on the bare stone ridges — and you learn to navigate by it, the way a bat navigates by its own echoes, except you are navigating through the echoes of *everything else*.

And above the clicking: **roaring**. Not the roar of wind or water. The roar of throats. The roar of lungs forcing air through structures that were not designed by any lineage you share. There are things here that vocalize, and their vocalizations are vast and ragged and repeated, call-and-response across distances you cannot measure because the sound bounces off everything and comes from everywhere, and each roar contains harmonics that scrape against each other in ways that make your inner ear feel *wrong*, as though the sound is trying to find a shape that your cochlea was not built to process. These roars are territorial. They are mating. They are warning. They are the sound of a system so full of life that the life itself has become architecture, each creature's voice a pillar holding up the acoustic ceiling of the hollow.

Because the **animals** — the *fauna* — are not sparse. They are not occasional. They are not hiding from you. They are the *substance* of this place, the way water is the substance of a lake, the way stone is the substance of a mountain. You cannot step without the possibility of stepping on one. You cannot stand still without one touching you within a minute. They are in the air — small things, pale, eyeless or many-eyed, their wings producing a fluttering that is subsumed into the larger sound but that you can feel on your face and in your hair, a constant buffeting, a constant soft collision. They are on the ground — low, flat, segmented things that move with a rippling motion over the stone, their undersides wet, their backs dry, and they do not avoid you because they do not know you as a threat, because nothing here has learned to fear anything shaped like you. They are in the pools — shapes that break the surface and resubmerge, larger than your hand, some of them, with bodies that seem to be all mouth and tail, filter-feeding on the pale mats, and their motion sends ripples that cross the tiny pools and break against the mineral crust with a sound like fingernails on porcelain.

And there are the **roarers**, which you hear long before you encounter. You feel one before you see it — a change in the pressure of the air, a displacement, a warmth that is not the stone's coolness, and then a shape that your eyes, by now adapted to the dark, assemble out of fragments: a body broad and low, moving on limbs that are too thick and too few, its skin — or hide, or carapace, or whatever covers it — reflecting the faintest ambient glow in a way that makes it seem to be *wet* all over, though it is not. It is the condensation, beading on whatever its surface is, and it moves with a certainty that you find deeply unnerving because it is clearly at home here and you are clearly not. It opens its mouth — a mouth that runs the width of its body, lipless, full of flat dark structures that are not teeth but that serve the same function — and roars, and the roar is not aggressive but *descriptive*, a declaration of position, of presence, of *I am here and I am this*, and the other roarers answer from across the hollow, their voices arriving at different delays, and for a moment the whole basin rings with a chord that is almost beautiful, almost harmonious, before it collapses back into the clicking-static-thrum.

The **ecology** is a closed loop. Nothing enters from outside. Nothing leaves. The pale mats are the base — chemosynthetic, feeding on the minerals dissolved from the stone by the condensation, converting the slow chemistry of wet limestone into biomass. The flat things eat the mats. The pool-things eat the flat things. The winged things eat the pool-things and the flat things and each other. The roarers eat everything they can fit into their wide mouths, which is most things, and when the roarers die — and they do, and their bodies are enormous, and you have stumbled over two of them already, their corpses already being disassembled by the flat things and the winged things and the mats themselves, which grow *on* them, *into* them, converting death back into the base of the system with an efficiency that is beautiful and disgusting in equal measure. The calcium of their bones — if they are bones — returns to the stone. The water that was their blood returns to the pools and to the air. The hollow is a body that digests itself and feeds itself and sustains itself and has done so for so long that the erosion of the stone and the growth of the mats and the lives and deaths of the animals are all one process, one slow churning, one metabolism.

The **temperature** is cool — not cold, not painful, but the kind of cool that settles into your joints after an hour, that makes you aware of your own warmth as something finite, something you are spending, something the hollow is slowly drawing out of you through your feet and your wet skin. The animals do not seem to mind. They are the temperature of the stone, and the stone is the temperature of the deep ground, and the deep ground does not care about your comfort.

The **scale** of the place becomes apparent only over time, because in the dark you cannot measure distance the way you are accustomed to. You walk for what feels like an hour and you have not reached any edge. The stone ripples continue. The pools continue. The clicking continues. The roarers call from further away than you can accurately estimate. The hollow is *regional* — miles across, a genuine landscape, a valley-sized concavity full of its own weather and its own inhabitants and its own sound, and you are a minor presence in it, a small warm body in a vast cool body, and the system does not notice you the way a river does not notice a leaf.

What is **strange** — and the strangeness accumulates, is cumulative, is not one thing but the sum of many things — is how *complete* it is. How self-sufficient. How closed. There is no path out that you can see, and you suspect there may be no path out that the animals need, because they have never left and have no reason to leave. The strangeness is in the feeling that this place is not a remnant, not a survivor, not a pocket of old life hanging on in a degraded world. It is a *whole*. It is a world that has arrived at its own answer and is living the answer fully, and the answer does not include light, does not include green, does not include anything you were taught to associate with the word *alive*.

The strangeness is also in the rhythm. Because the sound is not constant — it *cycles*. The thrumming swells and recedes. The clicking intensifies and thins. The roarers call in sequences that have patterns, patterns that repeat but not exactly, not predictably, like a piece of music being improvised by an orchestra that has been playing together for ten thousand years. There are quiet moments — not silent, never silent, but *quieter*, the way a held breath is quieter than breathing — and in those moments you can hear the water, the individual drops of condensation releasing from the stone and striking the pools with a sound like a finger tapping a drum, and you realize that the water is part of the rhythm too, that the condensation cycle and the life cycle and the sound cycle are all the same cycle, and the hollow is keeping time with itself, and has been keeping time with itself since before you were born and will continue after you are gone, and the time it keeps is not your time, is not measured in your hours or your years, but in the slow dissolution of stone and the slow growth of mats and the endless recurrence of the roar.

You are here. You are wet. You are cool. You are surrounded. You are deafened. You are, for the first time perhaps, *minor* — a small presence in a system that does not require you, did not expect you, and will not remember you. The hollow does not care. The hollow is complete.

You could stay. You could sit on one of the rounded stone ridges and let the condensation gather on you and let the clicking wash over you and let the thrumming settle into your bones, and eventually you would become part of the rhythm, your heartbeat syncing despite your resistance, your warmth slowly equalizing with the stone, your body becoming a surface for the mats to grow on, a shape for the flat things to explore, a meal for the pool-things to find.

Or you could climb. Slowly. Uphill. Toward the band of grey where the sky still exists. The grade is so gradual you will not know you are leaving until the sound begins to thin, until the clicking separates into individual voices, until the thrumming fades from your sternum to your memory, until the dark becomes the kind of dark that has stars in it, and you emerge into a world that is colder and emptier and quieter than this place, and you will not know whether you have been released or exiled.
