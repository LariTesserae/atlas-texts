# The Greenworks

You enter through what was once a loading bay. The word "was" is difficult here.

The structure exists at the scale of a small factory floor — perhaps thirty meters across at its widest, with a ceiling that rises and falls between four and eight meters depending on where the vaulting has reached. It is a room. It is also a settlement, or the embryo of one. It is also a plant. These statements do not contradict each other because contradiction requires a stable reality, and stability left this place some time ago.

The walls are living cellulose. Not wood — nothing so settled as wood. This is pre-wood, pre-bark, a wet fibrous tissue that has organized itself into load-bearing columns and curtain walls and catwalks because something in the cellular memory of this place understood the *shape* of industry. The columns are hexagonal, seven meters tall, slightly tapered, and they sweat. A thin film of liquid runs down their surfaces — not sap, not water, something between — collecting in shallow channels in the floor that the root system has also grown, engineered with the precision of drainage infrastructure. The condensation is constant. The air is damp the way a greenhouse is damp at four in the morning: cool, near-sixteen degrees, heavy with the smell of chlorophyll and iron and something underneath both that you cannot name.

There is almost nothing here. That is the first impression. The space is *hollow* in the way a cathedral is hollow — the architecture exists to enclose volume, and the volume is the point. Walk ten paces in any direction and you are alone with the sound of your feet on the living floor, which gives slightly, which is warm in places where the vascular tissue runs hot with whatever process is happening underneath. The density of objects is almost zero. A few catwalks, grown at intervals along the walls. A series of vats or tanks — organic, ribbed, the size of oil drums — clustered near the center. That is all. The emptiness is not absence; it is design. The space has been built to be empty, the way a lung is built to be empty.

But the plant *is* the infrastructure. The catwalks are stems, thickened and flattened, with railings that are still growing — you can see the tips, pale and curled, reaching outward at the pace of minutes. The floor is a mat of interwoven root tissue, dense as concrete, springy as moss. The columns are trunk-structures that branch at the ceiling into a vaulting network that mimics the exposed steel frame of a turn-of-the-century factory. There are even what appear to be *pipes*: long cylindrical growths, three or four centimeters in diameter, running along the upper walls, pulsing faintly with fluid transport. Someone — or something — taught plants to remember engineering. Or engineering was always just a memory that plants had.

Darkness. Not absolute — the tissue itself has a faint bioluminescence, a cold blue-green glow that comes from nodes along the ceiling vaulting, enough to see shapes but not colors, enough to make the wet surfaces gleam. The light is biological and sporadic. Some nodes pulse; most hold steady. The overall effect is of being inside the chest cavity of something large and alive and in the process of becoming. Which you are.

The vats at the center are the source of the industrial character. They are fruiting bodies — or organs — or machines. The distinction has broken down. Each one is roughly cylindrical, waist-high, with a ridged exterior like a corrugated tank and a lip at the top that opens into a chamber of dark fluid. The fluid bubbles. Slowly. A bubble rises every eight or ten seconds, breaks the surface with a faint *plk*, and releases a mist that smells of yeast and ozone. This is the only consistent sound: the bubbling, the almost-silent mist, the occasional drip of condensation hitting the floor mat. Mostly quiet. The quiet of a place that is working very hard at something it does not want to advertise.

And it is working. The dynamic is constant — growth happening at visible speed if you watch the catwalk railings, the vascular pulsing in the pipes, the slow expansion of the vaulting where new branches reach across the ceiling and knit themselves into the frame. The vats are processing something: pulling nutrients from the root mat below, converting them, sending product through the pipe-network to the growing edges. It is a factory. It is making itself.

The fauna is the strangest part, or rather the strangest part before you reach the part that is stranger than strange.

They move through the space in numbers that seem impossible given the emptiness. Insects — but not insects. Arthropodal things, two to five centimeters, pale, eyeless, moving in streams along the channels in the floor the way ants move along pheromone trails. There are thousands of them. They flow between the vats and the walls and the base of the columns, and where they pass the root mat darkens and then lightens, as though they are carrying something from one place to another. They make no sound. They do not avoid your feet; they flow over your shoes like water. Some of them have wings — flat, translucent, folded along their backs — and occasionally one launches from a column and glides across the dark air to another surface with a whisper of membrane. There are larger things too. Things like frogs but not frogs, six-limbed, mottled the color of the walls, crouching on the catwalks and along the pipe-runs. They watch with eyes that are not eyes — dark indentations that nonetheless track your movement. They do not move when you pass beneath them. They breathe. You can hear that if you stop: a soft double-pulse, in-in, out, from a dozen directions.

And there are things in the vats. Shapes that surface and subside. Not large. The size of hands, the size of faces. They do not stay up long enough to identify.

Here is where the reality breaks.

If you stand still for more than a minute — if you let your eyes adjust, if you let your breathing slow — the geometry begins to shift. Not dramatically. Not suddenly. The columns are where they were. But the *space between* the columns changes. Distances stretch and compress. A wall that was four meters away is suddenly two, then six, then four again, without moving. The ceiling rises and falls by meters over the course of seconds. The catwalks, seen from below, seem to multiply — there are three, then five, then two, each one real, each one continuous, each one occupying the same space. The vats at the center are four. Then there are seven. Then three. They are always the same vats. The number changes but the vats do not.

The arthropodal streams do not notice. The frog-things do not notice. The plant-infrastructure does not notice. Only you notice, which means either the reality is stable and you are breaking, or the reality is breaking and you are stable, and there is no way from inside this place to determine which.

Walk toward a wall and it recedes. Not quickly — it simply maintains its distance as you approach, then allows you to reach it when it decides to. Touch the wall and it is real: wet, fibrous, warm, alive, the cells visible under the bioluminescent glow as a fine grain, a texture like raw cotton soaked in rain. The surface moves against your hand. Peristalsis. The wall is breathing through its skin.

The sound does not change. The bubbling. The drip. The silence between. The sound is the most stable thing here, more stable than the geometry, more stable than the light. You could navigate by sound alone. The vats at the center bubble at a rate that does not vary regardless of how many vats there are. The condensation falls at intervals that do not change regardless of where the ceiling is. Sound is the floor. Sound is the one thing that stays.

There is a moment — there will be a moment — when you notice that one of the frog-things is watching you from a distance that should not be possible. It is on the far wall. The far wall is twenty meters away. You can see the texture of its skin. You can see the indentations where its eyes are not. It is close and far simultaneously, occupying two distances at once, and it is not distressed by this, and the reality-break is not a malfunction to it, it is the medium in which it lives.

This place is new. Not new to you. New to itself. The erosion parameter is almost zero — nothing is wearing away, nothing is degrading. The opposite: everything is still arriving. The columns are still forming. The vaulting is still reaching. The vats are still differentiating. The fauna is still colonizing. This is a place in the first hour of its existence, or the first week, or the first decade — there is no way to know because the place has not decided yet what its timescale will be. It is under construction. It is building itself from a blueprint that it is also writing, and the writing and the building happen at the same speed, and neither one is faster than the other.

It is a factory that makes itself and tests itself at once. The product is the factory. The factory is the product. The fauna are workers and product and waste and witness, all at once, and they move through the shifting geometry with the ease of things that have never known geometry to be fixed.

It is cool and dark and almost empty and very alive and very small and very strange and it is becoming, becoming, becoming, and it does not know what it will be when it finishes, and it may never finish, and the bubbling goes on. *Plk.* *Plk.* The mist rises. The railings grow. The distances breathe. The eyeless things flow over your shoes and do not pause and the not-frogs watch from impossible distances and the walls sweat and the light is blue-green and cold and the place is a room that is a settlement that is a plant that is a machine that is a process that is a question being asked in the language of wet cellulose and slow chemistry and the answer, whatever it is, has not been spoken yet.
