# The Long Tableland

The first thing is the sound. Not a sound — the sound, the total field of it, filling every direction the way water fills a bowl. You stand on the Tableland and the roar enters through your chest before it reaches your ears. It comes from everywhere and from the thing that everywhere is doing.

---

What you are standing on is the oldest surface in the world.

This was once a range of mountains — not the kind you've seen, not jagged teeth against the sky, but something more ancient, raised in an age when the world's skin was still thick and warm and pliable. The range rose. Then, over a span so long it makes the word "time" feel like a toy, it was worn down. Rain found it. Wind found it. Rivers carved at it for epochs, and the rivers themselves were carved away by later rivers, and those by still later ones, until the whole massif had been planed to a high, rolling flatness that still sits — almost two vertical miles above the lowlands — like the stump of a tooth polished smooth by a tongue of stone.

The rock underfoot is exposed in stretches. Where it shows, it is smooth — not flat but *finished*, the way a river-worn stone is finished, every grain that was going to leave having left, every surface that was going to fracture having fractured and been carried off and fractured again until what remained was the irreducible thing. Dark stone, shot through with pale veins that might once have been mineral intrusions but have been weathered into ghost-lines, barely raised above the surface. In the low places between the undulations, soil has gathered — not deep, never deep, but enough. Enough for what grows here.

And what grows here is the second thing you notice, after the sound.

---

The vegetation is absurd. Not in its height — nothing here grows tall, nothing dares — but in its *density* and its *tempo*. The Tableland is carpeted in a low, continuous mat of broad-leaved groundcover that could be moss and could be something else entirely, and from this mat rise clustered forms: thick-stalked plants with dark waxy leaves, curling fern-analogs that unroll and re-roll constantly, bulbous growths that look like they should be fruiting bodies but aren't. Everything is wet. Not soaked — there are no standing pools, no streams, no obvious water source — but *damp*, in the way that a greenhouse is damp, in the way that a cave is damp. Condensation forms on every surface and does not fully evaporate. You can feel it on your skin, a fine layer that reforms minutes after you wipe it away. The warmth helps — the air here is warm, genuinely warm, despite the elevation, as though the stone itself holds heat from some deep reservoir and breathes it upward through its pores.

But the strangeness of the vegetation is its speed. Things grow here visibly. Not in the time-lapse of documentary footage but in the time of a long afternoon. A curling frond that was a tight fist at noon is a loose spiral by dusk. A patch of bare stone that was clean in the morning has a film of green by evening — not dust, not algae, but the beginning of something rooted, already attaching itself, already reaching. And what grows, also dies, and dies fast, and the dying is part of the sound.

---

Because the sound — the deafening, body-filling roar — is the sound of the vegetation eating itself.

It is not a single noise. It is a composite, a thousand local noises stitched into one continuous wall of sound. Stalks splitting as they outgrow their own structure. Leaves unfurling with a sound like slow tearing. The mat of groundcover shifting as sections of it die and collapse inward, becoming a wet mulch that the next layer grows through and over. Each collapse is a small wet thud. Each splitting stalk is a crack. Each unfurling is a hiss or a creak. And there are millions of them happening simultaneously across the reach of the Tableland, and the air carries them all, and the wind — the wind, which is constant and strong and comes across the flatness with nothing to stop it — adds its own voice to the top of the pile, a high sustained note that sits above the biological churning like a roof over a room.

When the intermittent rain comes — and it comes every few hours, a sheet of warm water that passes across the Tableland in a visible line — the sound doubles. The rain on leaves is its own layer. The rain on exposed stone is another, higher, sharper. And beneath it all, the growth continues, and the dying continues, and the splitting and the collapsing continue, muffled but persistent, like a conversation heard through a wall.

---

The scale is hard to read because there is almost nothing to read it against.

The Tableland extends in every direction further than you can see, and it does so with almost no vertical reference. No trees. No hills of consequence — just the long, low undulations of the ancient surface, swells that rise and fall by tens of feet over miles. The sky is enormous. The horizon is a perfect line where it isn't broken by haze. You can see for miles in every direction and what you see is: stone, green, stone, green, and the movement of cloud-shadows crossing the surface at the speed of the wind.

The emptiness is the thing that gets to you after a while. Not the absence of features — there are features, the undulations, the exposed stone patches, the occasional darker vein of rock crossing the surface like a scar — but the absence of *presences*. No animals. You might walk for an hour and see nothing alive that is larger than your thumbnail, and those are rare — a single dark beetle-analog crossing a stone slab with apparent urgency, a cluster of small white-winged things that might be insects or might be windborne seeds, spiraling up from the mat and immediately lost. The fauna here is sparse to the point of mystery. What eats the vegetation? What trims it? Nothing, apparently. It grows and dies and feeds itself to itself, a closed loop of green energy turning over and over on a surface of ancient stone, and the only animals are accidentals, strays, things that wandered up from below and will not last.

---

There are structures. You find them by walking, because you will not see them from any distance.

They are low. They are stone. They are made from the same stone they sit on, which means they are nearly invisible — not hidden, not deliberately, but just *absorbed*, the way a rusty nail in dirt becomes indistinguishable from the dirt after enough time. The first one you find is a ring of stones, perhaps four feet across, perhaps a foot high, built with obvious care — the stones fitted to each other, flat face to flat face, held without mortar. Inside the ring, the vegetation has colonized everything, but the ring itself is kept slightly clearer, as though the stones still cast some residual authority over the ground they enclose.

Further on, a longer structure — a wall, or the foundation of a wall, running in a straight line for perhaps thirty feet before disappearing into a hummock of growth. The stones are smaller here, palm-sized, stacked two or three high in a dry fit that has survived because there is nothing to knock it over. No earthquakes in this place — the deep geology is stable, has been stable for longer than the structures have existed. Whoever built them used hand-tools, if they used tools at all. The stone splits cleanly along its grain; you can imagine fingers finding an edge, pulling, shaping. No metal marks. No fired clay. Nothing that required fire, except — in one place, inside the largest structure, a shallow pit lined with blackened stone — the memory of fire itself, a hearth, kept perhaps for warmth rather than cooking, because what would you cook?

The people who built these things are not here. The structures are too few and too scattered to suggest a settlement — more like waystations, or marks, or the work of someone who passed through repeatedly and built the same small shelter in the same way each time, the way a person picks up the same habit. They knew the Tableland. They walked it. They may have lived on its edges and come up for reasons that the structures don't reveal. The stone walls keep nothing out. The stone rings enclose nothing. They are marks. They say: *I was here, and the ground was already old, and the green was already loud, and I built this with my hands and I left.*

---

The turbulence of the place is not only in the vegetation.

The sky changes constantly. Not the gradual change of a normal sky — the rapid, almost procedural change of a system that is running through its possibilities at speed. Clouds form and dissolve in the same hour. The light shifts — warm, then gray, then amber, then a hard white clarity that throws every stone and leaf into knife-edged relief, then gray again. The wind changes direction in minutes, not days. The rain comes and goes and comes from a different quarter each time. There is no weather *pattern* here; there is weather *process*, the atmosphere cycling through its moods like a mind that can't settle, trying each one on, discarding it, trying the next.

Beneath this, the vegetation responds. You begin to notice — after hours, after a day — that the growth patterns shift with the sky. When the light is hard and white, the broad-leaved plants open their surfaces flat, maximizing exposure, and you can hear the faint accelerated hiss of photosynthesis or whatever its equivalent is here, a process running hot. When the rain comes, leaves curl inward, cup-shaped, and the sound changes to a softer, lower register, a collective gesture of receiving. When the wind shifts, the whole mat leans, and the lean produces its own sound, a vast fabric-rustle that adds another layer to the wall of noise.

It is like watching something think. Not with a mind — with a body, with ten million bodies, each one too small to matter, all of them together producing a response so coordinated that the word "coincidence" stops making sense. The Tableland's surface is a membrane. The sky presses on it. The membrane responds. The response is the sound.

---

And there is one more thing, and you do not notice it until you have been here long enough to stop noticing the sound, which happens around the second day, not because the sound diminishes — it does not diminish, it is relentless — but because your body admits it as a baseline condition, the way your body admits gravity or the sound of your own blood.

What you notice then is the vibration.

It comes through your feet. Through the stone, when you stand on exposed rock. A low, constant tremor, not strong enough to feel as motion but present as a sensation, a hum in the bones of your legs, as though the ancient stone is still — after all this time, after the mountains were planed and the rivers erased and the surface polished to its final form — still *resolving*. Still settling into itself. The erosion parameter says: ancient, primordial, oldest-stable-state, end of life, settled-into-final-form. And the stone has settled. But the settling is not complete. It is still happening. The Tableland is still, after billions of years, finding its final shape, and the final shape is flat, and it is almost there, and "almost" is a distance that may take another billion years to cross, and the crossing is the vibration, and the vibration feeds into the sound, and the sound is the vegetation responding to the vibration, and the vegetation is the thing that will eventually — in some age so far forward it cannot be imagined — finally hold the last of the stone in place, root and fiber and growth locking the final grains into a surface that will not move again, and then there will be silence, and the silence will be the oldest thing the world has ever made.

But that is not yet. For now, the Tableland is loud, and green, and almost empty, and warm, and old, and still becoming.
