# The Khûm at Drasha's Spine

## First Impression

There is a throat in the rock at 7,400 meters, and it breathes.

Not wind — not exactly. Something slower. An exhalation that takes forty seconds to complete, pauses twelve, and begins again. The rhythm does not correspond to any atmospheric system anyone has mapped. It comes from below, from a place the rock has not yet finished becoming rock. The sound it makes is low enough to feel in the back teeth and loud enough that you must raise your voice to speak over it, though no one is here to speak to.

This is the Khûm. A word that means, in the closest surviving tongue, *a place that has not decided what it is.*

## The Shape of the Place

The Khûm is a chamber. Not a cave — caves imply patience, imply water working limestone across millennia. This is something else. The mountain cracked here, recently, in some tectonic shrug that split open a vault of andesite and exposed a space that had been sealed since the rock itself was liquid. The walls are sharp. Not rough — *sharp*. The stone fractures in long splinters like broken glass, and the floor is a lattice of ridges and crevices that have not yet been worn by anything. Walking here means cutting your boots. The rock does not want to be walked on. It is still angry about being opened.

The chamber is roughly cathedral-sized — perhaps sixty meters at its longest axis, thirty at its widest, and the ceiling vaults to a height that darkness swallows. You can see the first fifteen meters of the vault above. Beyond that, the stone vanishes into its own blackness. The entrance is a vertical fissure in the mountain's east face, narrow enough that you enter sideways, and it funnels the thin high-altitude air into the space with enough force that the atmosphere inside is always moving, always slightly alive.

The temperature holds at something like four or five degrees Celsius. Not cold enough to kill at rest. Cold enough that you feel it in your fingers within minutes, cold enough that the damp in the air finds every gap in your clothing and settles against skin. The rock walls weep. Not flowing water — nothing so generous as that. Condensation. The warm breath of whatever is below meets the cold stone of the upper chamber, and moisture beads on every surface, collects in the sharp crevices, runs in slow threads down the splintered walls. The air tastes wet and mineral. It tastes like licking a fresh wound.

There is no light. The entrance fissure allows a sliver of grey daylight — the grey of 7,400 meters, where the atmosphere is too thin to hold color — but it reaches only the first few meters of the chamber. Beyond that, absolute dark. The kind of dark that makes your eyes ache from trying. The kind that has texture.

And yet the place is not still.

## What Lives Here

The sound comes first.

A clicking. Dense, layered, arrhythmic. Like ten thousand people cracking their knuckles in a dark room, but wetter, more organic. It comes from the walls, from the ceiling, from the floor itself. It rises and falls in waves that do not match the breathing of the chamber but layer over it, creating a complex soundscape that is, at first, simply noise. Then, after an hour, becomes architecture. You begin to hear patterns. Clicks that answer other clicks. A slow pulse beneath the chaos. Calls — if they are calls — that move from one wall to another in sequences too fast to track.

Then your eyes adjust, or the dark adjusts to you, and you realize the walls are *covered*.

They are called — by the few people who have been here, by the Sherpa families who have always known about this place and have always, always stayed away — *drasha-keri*. Spine-singers. Though they do not sing, and they have no spines.

They are arthropodal in the loosest sense. Each is roughly the size of a human hand, flattened, oval, pale — the white of something that has never encountered sunlight, a white that looks blue in the faint condensation-light that the chamber seems to generate from the moisture itself. They have no eyes. Their undersides are covered in hundreds of fine chitinous tendrils that grip the wet rock, and their backs are ridged with structures that click against the stone when they move. They move constantly. Slowly, but constantly. A single drasha-keri clicks perhaps twice per minute. There are, by conservative estimate, forty thousand of them in this chamber.

They cover the walls in layers. Not evenly — in colonies, in clusters, in formations that look, if you shine a light on them (and you should not), like cities seen from very high up. Dense aggregations separated by bare channels of rock, the channels serving as roads, as corridors. The aggregations pulse. The clicking synchronizes within each cluster, then breaks apart, then reforms. The sound is loud — genuinely, physically loud, enough to make your ears ring after thirty minutes, enough that you feel pressure behind your eyes.

They are not aggressive. They do not interest themselves in warm-blooded things. They are interested in the moisture, in the condensation, in something the rock is still releasing — some mineral exhalation from the newly opened stone that they feed on, if feeding is the right word. Their tendrils work the wet surfaces of the rock, and where they have been present longest, the sharp stone has begun to wear smooth. They are the only erosion happening here. They are faster than geology but slower than weather. They are doing something to the rock that would take a river a thousand years to accomplish, and they are doing it in decades.

The floor is worse.

The floor is where the shed carapaces accumulate. The drasha-keri molt — slowly, perhaps once a year — and their discarded backs collect in the crevices between the sharp ridges of stone. The floor crunches. Every step, even in absolute darkness, produces a sound like walking on thin ice over gravel. The sound triggers clicking in the nearest wall clusters. A cascade. You step, and a wave of clicks radiates outward from your position, each cluster triggering the next, the information moving through the colony at a speed that suggests something more than reflex.

And in the deepest part of the chamber, where the floor slopes downward and the air grows warmer and the breathing sound grows louder, the carapaces form drifts. Knee-deep in places. A substrate of chitin that has been accumulating for however long the Khûm has been open — perhaps a century, perhaps three, no one knows for certain. The carapaces under the surface are compressed, fused, forming a brittle composite that cracks under weight. The ones on top are still sharp-edged, still pale, still almost warm to the touch.

## The Lower Chamber

The floor slopes. Not steeply — a gradient that would be imperceptible on a flat surface but is unmistakable when every step crunches and shifts beneath you. You descend perhaps fifteen meters over the chamber's length, and the air changes. Warmer. Wet to the point of dripping, not merely condensing. The rock walls are slick. The drasha-keri are denser here, layered three or four deep in places, and they are larger — hand-and-a-half, some of them, their click-structures deeper in pitch, almost percussive.

The breathing sound is very loud here. It comes from below, from a passage at the chamber's lowest point — a fissure in the floor, perhaps a meter wide, running laterally for twenty meters, from which warm wet air rises in the slow rhythmic pulse that defines this place. The air smells different here. Sulfurous, but not volcanic. Biological. The smell of decomposition and synthesis happening simultaneously — a chemistry that is neither decay nor growth but something between, something that does not have a name because no one has studied it.

You cannot see into the fissure. The darkness below is different from the darkness above — thicker, almost granular. Dropping a stone into it produces no sound of impact. Only the breathing, continuing, untroubled.

The drasha-keri cluster most densely around the edges of this fissure. Their tendrils reach toward the warm air, and they click in patterns that are, here, unmistakably synchronized. The entire colony around the fissure pulses as a single organism — click, click, click, silence, click-click, silence — and the rhythm is the same as the breathing. They are counting it. Or keeping it. Or participating in it. No one knows.

## What is Strange

Several things.

First: the condensation-light. It should not exist. The moisture on the walls does not glow, does not luminesce in any way that a spectrometer could detect. And yet, after thirty to forty minutes in absolute darkness, the human eye begins to perceive a faint luminescence — not enough to see by, not enough to identify shapes, but enough to sense the presence of the walls, the ceiling, the moving masses of drasha-keri. It is possible this is a neurological artifact, the visual cortex generating signal from deprivation. It is possible it is something else. Those who have experienced it describe it differently — not as light, but as *presence*. The walls become more real than they should be. The space becomes knowable in a way that does not correspond to any sensory input.

Second: the carapace formations. In the lower chamber, where the shed shells accumulate in drifts, they do not accumulate randomly. They form patterns. Ridges, channels, spiraling structures that resemble — if you are unfortunate enough to recognize it — the branching patterns of neurons, of river systems, of blood vessels. The same fractal geometries that organic systems use to maximize surface area. The drasha-keri do not appear to be constructing these patterns. The patterns emerge from the accumulation, from the way the carapaces fall and settle and fuse. But they are not random. Something is sorting them. Something is building.

Third: the tools. This is the detail that the Sherpa families do not discuss, the detail that the few scientific expeditions to reach the Khûm have reported and then questioned and then, in some cases, chosen to omit.

Among the carapace drifts, on the compressed chitin substrate of the lower chamber, there are stones.

Not many. Perhaps two dozen that have been found. Small — fitting in the palm, fitting in the fingers. Andesite fragments, broken from the chamber walls, that have been shaped. Not flaked in the manner of human tools — the working is different, more grinding than percussion, as if the stones had been worn into shape against the rock over very long periods. But the shapes are functional. Edges. Points. Scraping surfaces. One has a concavity that fits a drasha-keri tendril cluster precisely.

The drasha-keri do not have hands. They do not have grasping appendages. They have tendrils, fine and numerous, evolved for gripping wet stone and working mineral films. They should not be able to shape stone. And yet.

The tools are old. The ones deepest in the carapace drifts may be decades old, may be older. They show wear. They show use. And they are always found near the fissure, near the breathing, near the place where the colony clicks in synchrony with the pulse of something that has not been identified and may not be identifiable.

## The Rules of This Place

The Khûm does not welcome visitors. It does not reject them either. It is indifferent in the way that a lung is indifferent to the air it processes — the air enters, the air leaves, the lung continues its work.

The drasha-keri will not harm you. They will crawl over your boots, your clothing, your hands if you stay still long enough. Their tendrils are cool and dry despite the wet air, and their grip is gentle and inquisitive. They will click against your skin. The clicking is very loud at close range. If one reaches your ear, the sound is like a small door closing inside your head.

You should not shine light on the walls. The colony reacts — not violently, but with a disturbance that propagates through the entire chamber, a cascade of clicking that takes hours to settle. The light does not hurt them. It confuses them. Their systems — whatever those systems are — depend on darkness the way a clock depends on the regularity of its mechanism. Light introduces noise. The Khûm has been dark since it opened. The colony has organized itself around that darkness. Breaking it is a kind of violence.

You should not descend into the fissure. This is not superstition. The air below is warmer, wetter, and carries a concentration of gases that will cause unconsciousness within minutes. The breathing comes from further down than anyone has been able to measure. The passage narrows, then widens, then narrows again, and the stone changes — becoming less like andesite, more like something that has not yet decided to be stone. The rock is warm to the touch. It flexes slightly under pressure. It is, in the most literal geological sense, still forming.

You should not stay long. Four hours, perhaps five. Beyond that, the condensation-light becomes too present, the clicking too structured, the breathing too familiar. People who have stayed longer report that the chamber begins to feel like the interior of a body. That the walls feel like walls the way ribs feel like walls. That the clicking is not sound but language, not language but thought, and that the thought is not complex but is very, very old, and is very, very patient, and is about something that the human brain does not have the architecture to contain.

They are probably wrong about this. Probably.

## What This Place Is

The mountain opened. The space was there — had been there, sealed, since the rock was molten. The space contained something. Not an organism, not a mechanism, not a presence in any way that human categories can hold. A process. A slow chemical-geological-biological process that was occurring in the sealed dark, that did not require light or air or observation, that had been occurring for longer than human beings have been a species.

The drasha-keri found it. Or it found them. Or they are part of it — an extension of it, the way that roots are an extension of a tree, the way that a voice is an extension of a throat. They colonized the chamber. They feed on the mineral exhalations. They click. They molt. They accumulate. They build, or the building happens through them. They shape stones they should not be able to shape. They count a breath that comes from deeper than anyone has gone.

The Khûm is young. It has been open for perhaps two hundred years, perhaps less. In geological terms, it is an infant. The walls are still sharp. The rock is still angry. The erosion that will eventually round these edges, that will turn splinters to surfaces, that will make this a place rather than a wound — that erosion is happening now, slowly, at the pace of chitin tendrils on wet stone.

Give it ten thousand years. The walls will be smooth. The carapace substrate will be meters deep. The tools will be more numerous, more refined. The breathing will still be there, still slow, still patient. The clicking will still fill the dark. And the place will have decided what it is.

It has not decided yet. That is what makes it frightening. Not the dark, not the creatures, not the sound. The *potential*. The sense that something is being built here, has been being built for a very short time by the standards of the earth, and that the building is in its earliest stages, and that what it will become is not yet determined, and that whatever is determining it does not think the way we think, does not build the way we build, does not want the way we want, but is building nonetheless.

The mountain breathes. The colony counts. The stones take shape in the dark.

You should not stay long.
