# The Tier Houses of Kell-Vorn

The first thing is the silence. Not true silence — there is breathing, after all. Thousands of small bodies breathing. But the sound sits so evenly in the air that it becomes a texture rather than a noise, like the hum of a held note just below the threshold of attention. You feel it in your teeth before you hear it.

---

Kell-Vorn occupies a shelf of rock on the eastern face of a young ridge, maybe two hundred meters of exposed flank that the weather hasn't yet learned to soften. The stone is a pale volcanic tuff — ash-white, sharp-edged, streaked with mineral blues that look, in certain light, like the inside of a bruise. The shelf is narrow. The settlement is narrow to match. Forty or fifty structures built from the same tuff, fitted without mortar, their walls leaning inward slightly as though bracing against the wind that comes through the pass above. The roofs are thatch — not grass, but a fibrous black moss that grows in the constant damp — weighted with stones to keep them from lifting. The doors are hide. The windows are open holes, covered at night with woven panels of reed and moth-silk.

The settlement is small. You can stand at one end and see the other. The buildings step up the slope in loose tiers, connected by stone-cut steps that are already cracking with frost and root. There are gaps between the houses — courtyards that are more absence than design, flat patches of rock where nothing has been built, where the ground is worn smooth by foot traffic and the occasional puddle. The sparse placement gives each structure its own air, its own small territory of shadow. It also means that when something moves between the buildings, you see it clearly, framed by emptiness.

And things are always moving.

---

The damp here is not rain, exactly. Moisture condenses out of the cloud bank that settles against the ridge most days and most nights. It beads on every surface — on stone, on thatch, on the stiff waxy leaves of the plants that have colonized every horizontal surface and most of the vertical ones. You can watch it form: a droplet gathering weight at the tip of a fern frond, swelling, trembling, falling. Then another. The air is saturated but not heavy. The coolness keeps it from feeling oppressive. It feels more like being inside a slow exhalation, the mountain breathing out and the condensation forming on whatever it touches.

The vegetation is the real surprise. This elevation and this latitude should produce scrub, maybe — hardy grasses, lichen, the occasional stubborn shrub. Instead, the shelf is lush. Densely, absurdly green. Moss covers the north-facing walls in velvet sheets. Ferns — a variety found nowhere lower than a thousand meters below — grow from every crevice, every crack where tuff has split and soil has gathered. Their fronds are pale, almost translucent, and they uncurl in the dim light like slow green fingers. Between the buildings, where the gaps allow it, small trees have taken root — not tall, maybe four meters, with dark bark and leaves the color of oxidized copper. They lean toward the gaps in the construction, toward whatever light reaches the shelf, and their roots have begun to pry stones from the lower walls. The settlement is being disassembled from below, slowly, patiently, by a forest that doesn't know it's supposed to be struggling.

And then there is the fauna.

---

There are too many of them. That is the first impression, and it doesn't soften with time.

They are not loud. That is the strange mercy of the place. In a warmer lowland, this density of life would be deafening — frog-calls, insect-drone, birdsong, the rustle of bodies through undergrowth. Here, the cool air seems to absorb the sound, or the animals themselves are quiet, adapted to a world where noise carries too far across open rock. Whatever the reason, the fauna is overwhelming in presence but nearly silent in expression.

The moths come first, because they are everywhere. Not the brown, dusty moths of lowland closets — these are large, handspan-wide, with wings the grey-white of old bone. They rest on every vertical surface, stacked on top of each other in loose columns, their wings folded. When disturbed, they rise in a single silent sheet and resettle three feet away. Their bodies are furred. Their eyes, when you get close enough to see them, are not compound but appear as a single dark circle, wet-looking, which is strange, and which you try not to think about. At dusk — though dusk here is a gradual dimming rather than a definitive event — they move to the upper walls of the houses and hang there, trembling, as though listening for something.

The fern-trees host a species of arboreal crustacean — pale, many-legged, the size of a human hand. They cling to the undersides of branches and move with a mechanical precision that is more unsettling than any rapid motion would be. Each step deliberate, each joint articulated. They are the source of some of the breathing — thousands of them, each drawing air through a thin membrane along their flanks. When you stand beneath one of the trees and look up, the undersides of the leaves are dotted with them, and the leaves themselves are dotted with the holes they have chewed, so that the canopy looks like a sheet of parchment through which someone has fired a careless scatter of shot.

On the ground, between the buildings, in the courtyards of bare rock, there are creatures that might be amphibians. They are flat, wide, the color of the tuff, and they sit motionless for long stretches. They have eyes that face forward, which is wrong for an amphibian, which is wrong for a prey animal, which makes you think they might not be a prey animal. They eat the moths. You see this happen, once. A flat body unfolds a long, pale tongue, and a moth is gone, and the tongue retracts, and the creature settles again into its stillness with the patience of something that has never once been hungry enough to rush.

In the walls of the houses, in the gaps between stones where the moss has grown thickest, there are beetles. They are small and black and they move in lines — not trails, but actual geometric lines, straight as if ruled, processing across the stone face in columns that suggest something organized, something that has direction and purpose. They do not forage. They do not scatter when you put your hand near. They continue walking their line, and if your hand is in the way, they walk over it. Their legs are cold. Their backs are smooth.

Above, in the dim air above the ridge, there are birds — or things that fly like birds. They are too high to see clearly, just shapes against the cloud cover, circling in slow overlapping patterns. They do not call. Sometimes one drops lower, passes through the gap between two houses, and is gone before you can focus on it. What you see in that half-second is a wing-shape that is too long, a body that is too narrow, a movement that is more like swimming through air than flying.

---

The people of Kell-Vorn live here. There are perhaps a hundred of them. They are not overrun by the fauna, but they are not separate from it either. Their houses are stone and thatch and hide, and the insects and the crustaceans and the flat pale amphibians are in and on and around those houses at all times, and the people have made accommodations that are not quite tolerance and not quite symbiosis. The woven panels that cover the windows at night are tight enough to keep out the moths but not the beetles, and the beetles are permitted inside, because the beetles eat something — a mite, a fungal growth, something too small to see — that would otherwise ruin the thatch. The flat amphibians are allowed in the courtyards because they eat the moths, and the moths, in sufficient numbers, will eat stored grain and fabric and, given enough time, the binding in the walls. The crustaceans in the trees are ignored, or perhaps they are simply too difficult to remove. Their populations cycle — booming in the wet season, thinning in the dry — and the people of Kell-Vorn have learned to read the cycles the way other people read weather.

They keep no livestock. There is no room, and nothing to feed them on, and the fauna would make it impossible — the moths would infest any grain store large enough for feed, the crustaceans would colonize any structure built for shelter. Instead, the people harvest from the abundance around them. Moth pupae, gathered from the upper walls in season, are a staple. The arboreal crustaceans, plucked from the trees, are roasted on flat stones over small fires of moss and dried fern-frond. The fires produce almost no smoke — the moss is too damp, the fern too green — and what little smoke rises is absorbed into the cloud bank within a few meters. The air in Kell-Vorn smells of wet stone, green vegetation, and something faintly mineral, like the taste of a copper coin held on the tongue.

Their tools are stone and bone and fiber. The tuff is soft enough to work with harder stone — chipped and ground into blades that hold an edge for a week before needing renewal. Bone needles for the moth-silk. Fiber from the black moss, spun and twisted into cord. They have no metal. They have no need for metal that is apparent. The settlement is old enough to have four generations buried in a stone cairn at the upper edge of the shelf, and young enough that the walls of the houses still bear the tool-marks of their first cutting.

---

The light here is a process, not a condition. The cloud bank filters what reaches the shelf, and the shelf faces east, which means the light arrives late and leaves early. Dawn is a slow greying, a shift from black to charcoal to the color of a deep bruise, and then — if the cloud thins — a brief, diffuse brightening that reaches the ferns and the moss and the pale undersides of leaves and turns everything a strange, luminous green, like light passed through a bottle. This lasts perhaps an hour. Then the cloud thickens, or the sun rises above the cloud bank entirely, and the shelf returns to its usual dimness — a twilight that is neither comfortable nor threatening, simply present, the ambient state of a place that exists mostly inside a cloud.

At night, the darkness is profound but not empty. The moths, which have been passive all day, become active. They move through the gaps between buildings in slow drifting currents, and in the dark, their wings catch what little light exists — starlight filtered through cloud, or the faint bioluminescence of a fungus that grows on the lower walls of the houses — and they appear as pale shapes drifting through a darker dark, which, if you are not prepared for it, produces a sensation of falling, or of the ground having moved beneath you while you weren't looking.

The fungus-light is blue. Cold blue, like the mineral streaks in the tuff. It pulses, very slowly, on a cycle of perhaps thirty seconds — bright enough to see by, dim enough that the seeing is more impression than image. The people of Kell-Vorn use it to navigate between houses at night. They do not use fire for this. Fire attracts the moths, and the moths in fire-numbers are a weight that can collapse a thatch roof.

---

What is strange about Kell-Vorn is not any single thing but the relationship of things — the way the settlement and the ecology have become interleaved, each shaped by the other, until it is difficult to say where the human order ends and the biological order begins. The stone steps between tiers are cracked by fern roots. The walls of the houses are held together by moss. The windows are covered by moth-silk. The food is the fauna, and the fauna is the architecture, and the architecture is becoming the landscape, and the landscape is young and sharp and still deciding what it wants to be.

The settlement is being consumed and maintained simultaneously. It is being eaten and inhabited and supported and undermined by the same processes. It is not declining. It is not advancing. It is in a state of active negotiation with everything around it, and the terms of that negotiation shift with the moisture and the moth-cycles and the slow creep of fern roots through stone.

The people who live here do not seem troubled by any of this. They move through their houses and their courtyards and their narrow stone steps with the unhurried competence of people who have understood the terms and accepted them. They gather pupae. They roast crustaceans. They weave panels and chip blades and watch the cloud bank for signs of change that might mean a dry season coming, or a wet one, or something else entirely — something the elders remember from their grandparents' time, a season in which the moths didn't come at all, and the flat amphibians died off, and the beetles walked their lines in patterns that spiraled instead of straight, and the light in the fungus went out, and the shelf was dark and quiet for a month, and then everything returned as before, and no one spoke of it afterward.

The silence holds. The moisture condenses. The beetles walk their lines. The ferns uncurl. The moths drift. The stone is sharp and young. The houses stand.
