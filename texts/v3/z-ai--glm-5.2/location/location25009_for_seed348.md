# The Mirkworks

The sound reaches you before the place does — a sustained, composite roar that has no beginning and no end. It is not one sound but a stack of them, layered so densely and so permanently that the ear stops trying to separate them after a while. A low idling throb, like a diesel engine turning over in a vault. A hiss of steam or gas escaping through seams that were never properly sealed. A metallic chattering, arrhythmic in its parts but rhythmic in its aggregate, like ten thousand insects tapping ten thousand pipes. The sound does not fluctuate. It has the quality of something recorded and played back on loop, except it is not recorded. It is simply the sound this place makes, has made, will make. Nothing here is off. Nothing here is starting up or winding down.

You come down a gentle grade on a path that is more gutter than road — a shallow channel worn into a surface of dark aggregate, some mineral cement mixed with slag, packed by years of foot traffic into something almost like pavement. The grade is slight but consistent. You are descending into a low place. The air cools as you go, and the dampness thickens. Not rain. Not mist exactly. A pervasive wetness that seems to exude from the ground itself, as though the earth here is just slightly warmer than the air and is breathing out its moisture into the cool above it. Condensation beads on every surface — on the aggregate path, on the metal railings that line the walkway, on the walls of the structures that begin to appear on either side.

And there are structures. This is the first surprise. You expected something more open, more barren, given the absence of trees, the absence of any green at all. But the Mirkworks is built. It is a settlement — small, dense with construction, organized around some logic you cannot immediately parse. The buildings are low, most of them one or two stories, made of a material that looks at first like concrete but is something else. Darker. The aggregate is finer, and there are veins of something metallic running through it, not decorative but structural, like the buildings were poured around a skeleton of iron that has since oxidized in the damp air and stained the surfaces in long brown streaks. The streaks are old. The staining is complete. It has reached an equilibrium — the rust has gone as far as it can go, and now it simply is, the way a rock is.

The buildings have no windows on their lower levels. Openings are placed high, under the eaves, narrow slits that let out a steady glow — not electric light exactly, not fire, something in between, a pale amber that does not flicker. It is the same in every building. The same color, the same intensity, as though the light inside is not produced but is a property of the interior itself, something the rooms generate simply by being rooms. Above the slits, the roofs are corrugated metal sheeting, and the condensation runs down the corrugations in parallel lines, dripping from the edges in curtains of fine silver threads. The dripping is continuous and adds its own layer to the sound — a high, clean patter over the deeper throb and hiss.

Between the buildings, the ground is bare. No grass. No moss. No lichen. The dampness does not produce growth here. The mineral world holds. The ground is the same dark aggregate, worn smooth in the paths where feet have passed, rough and crystalline in the spaces between. In those unused patches, the surface has a faint glitter — mica, or something like it, tiny flat crystals that catch the amber light from the slits above and throw it back in fragments. The ground is not dead. It is simply not biological. It is a mineral surface that has reached its final form and does not change.

What is alive here — and this is the second surprise, the overwhelming surprise — is the fauna.

They are everywhere.

The first ones you notice are on the walls. Insects, but not insects you have names for. They are dark, elongated, with bodies like fused beads of polished chitin and legs that move in a mechanical, exact way, each stride the same length, each turn the same arc. They cover the walls in their thousands, running along the mortar lines between the poured panels, converging at the condensation streams and drinking. They do not fight. They do not jostle. They move with the precision of components in a machine, each one occupying its space, each one following its path. The sound they make — that metallic chattering — is the sound of their legs on the walls, multiplied by their number into a continuous texture that is one of the layers of the roar.

They do not react to you. You are not a threat. You are not food. You are not a feature of their world.

On the ground, other creatures. Larger. Low-bodied, broad, moving on dozens of short legs — things like centipedes but wider, flattened, the color of the aggregate they move over, so that you do not see them until they move. They move slowly, in straight lines, from one building to another, entering through gaps at the base of the walls that you would have mistaken for settling cracks. They carry things on their backs — fragments of metal, chunks of the crystalline aggregate, sometimes one of the smaller wall-insects, dead or dormant, folded neatly. They are not eating the metal. They are transporting it. There is a purpose to their movement, an organization, but it is not an organization you can read. It is not a hive, not in the way you understand hives. There is no queen. There is no center. There is a system, and the system is in equilibrium, and it has been in equilibrium for longer than the buildings have been standing.

The air has its own life. Things fly here, though "fly" is perhaps too generous. They drift. Membranous, translucent, with a body plan like a manta ray scaled down to the size of a hand, they ride the slow air currents that move between the buildings, currents generated by the temperature differential between the warm interiors and the cool exterior air. They are not feeding. They are not searching. They are simply moving, the way the condensation moves, the way the sound moves — which is to say, they are not moving at all, not in the sense of change. They are in their place. The air is their place. They have always been there. You can tell because some of them have a fine dusting of mineral grit on their upper surfaces, as though they have been hanging in the same column of air for so long that the damp has deposited sediment on them, layer by layer, and they have not shaken it off because they do not need to.

You walk deeper in. The buildings press closer. The paths narrow. The amber glow from the high slits intensifies — not because the individual lights brighten, but because there are more of them, the buildings are more densely packed, and their light overlaps and compounds. The condensation is heavier here. The walls weep. The aggregate underfoot is slick, and you learn to walk where the broad flat creatures have worn the surface smooth.

Through one of the gaps at a building's base, you catch a glimpse of an interior. Low ceiling. The same dark material. More of the chitinous insects, in greater density, covering the walls and the ceiling in a continuous mat of dark, moving bodies. And machinery — actual machinery, visible in fragments between the bodies: a flywheel, still turning, slowly, so slowly that the motion is almost imperceptible, driven by something you cannot see. Pipes, corroded to the same stable brown as the exterior reinforcement, carrying something — fluid, gas, you cannot tell. The amber light is strongest here, and its source is still not apparent. It comes from the machinery itself, or from the spaces between the machinery, or from the insects, or from the material of the walls. The interior is warm. The heat comes out of the gap in a slow breath, and with it, a smell — not organic, not chemical exactly, a mineral smell, like hot stone, like the inside of a kiln that has cooled to a temperature just above the point where it stops being dangerous.

The machinery is old. Not abandoned — running. But not maintained, either. Not in the way you understand maintenance. Nothing has been repaired here because nothing has broken. The system was built — or grew, or assembled itself, or was assembled by something that no longer needs to — and it runs. The flywheel turns. The pipes carry. The insects drink the condensation and transport the fragments and tend to whatever they tend to. The drifting things drift. The flat things crawl. The sound does not change.

You realize, gradually, that the sound is not mechanical. Or rather, it is, but the machinery and the insects are not separate categories here. The chattering of the legs on the walls is the same sound as the chattering of the machinery inside. The hiss of escaping gas is the same hiss as the moisture evaporating off the warm buildings. The low throb is the flywheel, and it is also the collective vibration of the thousands of bodies in the interior, all moving at their precise, mechanical pace. The sound is the sound of the system, and the system is the sound, and it is all one thing.

There is no weather here. Not really. The dampness does not come and go. It is a constant, a property of the place, like the temperature, like the light. Nothing arrives. Nothing leaves. The Mirkworks does not have a morning or an evening. It has this — this single, sustained state, this equilibrium that has held for so long that the concept of change has become irrelevant to it.

You are the only thing that has changed in this place in a very long time. You are the only thing that is not in your place. The creatures do not mind. They do not notice. You could sit down on the slick aggregate and stay, and in time the condensation would bead on you, and the mineral dust would settle, and you would become a feature of the landscape, and the flat things would learn to walk around you, and the sound would absorb your breathing into its stack, and no one would be able to tell where you ended and the Mirkworks began.

The thought is not frightening. It is not comforting. It is simply a thought, and like everything else here, it does not change anything.
