# The Caldera Works at Tsuun-Vel

## First approach

You come up the switchback road cut into pale rhyolite, and the world opens.

The Tsuun-Vel caldera is not a crater but a broad asymmetric bowl, perhaps nine kilometers at its long axis, set into a high plateau at an elevation where the air should be thin and cold. It is not cold. The ground is warm underfoot before you reach the first buildings — a dry, mineral heat that rises through boots and into the ankles. By the time you step onto the main thoroughfare of the settlement, the stone beneath you is actively hot, and the air carries that heat the way a kiln holds it: evenly, without drama.

Brightness here is total. The plateau sits above the haze line, and the caldera's pale mineral floor reflects light upward, so that shadows are soft-edged and shallow. There is no canopy, no overhang, no shade to be found except in the interiors of buildings. The sky is a bleached cerulean. You squint without thinking about it.

Water is everywhere, and this is the strangeness a visitor notices first.

## The water and the ground

The caldera floor is wet in the way a cloth left on a stone overnight is wet — not flowing, not pooled, but *present*. Moisture beads on every surface. The rhyolite and welded tuff that make up the ground have a darkened, saturated look, as though the rock itself is sweating. In fact it is. The water table sits close beneath the surface here — two meters down in most places, less in others — and the heat of the rock, which is volcanic in origin though no eruption has occurred in recorded history, drives that water upward as vapor. It condenses at the surface. It beads. It runs in tiny rivulets that never quite form streams.

Where the ground fractures, the water emerges more boldly. Cracks in the caldera floor — and there are many, running in roughly radial patterns from the caldera's low center — emit thin curtains of vapor that rise half a meter and dissolve. Along these fracture lines the rock is stained: ochre, milky blue, sulfur yellow, a deep manganese black. Mineral deposition. The water comes up hot, laden with dissolved compounds, and leaves them behind as it cools at the surface. The result is a subtly variegated floor, a mineral palette spread across the bowl of the caldera like spilled pigment, dull but vivid where the light catches the wetness.

It has been doing this for a long time. The erosion parameter reads *established*, and it is the right word: the landscape is not actively wearing down or building up. It is in a state. The fractures have been where they are for centuries. The mineral staining has reached its full expression. The water table has found its level. Nothing here is urgent.

Puddles form in depressions and along the settling cracks beneath structures, and these puddles are warm — comfortably warm at the edges, sharply hot near active vents. They reflect the sky. In the bright light they look like windows set into the ground.

No plants grow here. Not a single species. The floor is mineral, the moisture is mineral-laden and often too hot for biological chemistry, and the soil — such as it is, a thin grus of decomposed volcanic glass — supports nothing green. No grass. No lichen. No moss. The only colors are stone colors and the stains the water leaves behind.

## The settlement

Tsuun-Vel is a town of perhaps four hundred structures, and from the rim of the caldera it looks like something scattered by hand: buildings set wide apart across the mineral floor, connected by raised walkways and roads of compacted aggregate, with broad empty stretches of hot wet stone between. The density is low. There is room here, and the builders used it. You walk three minutes between some structures and encounter no one, only the quiet ground, the beaded moisture, and the occasional plume of vapor from a fracture.

The buildings are industrial in character and mostly single-story, constructed from local stone cut into blocks and fitted with mortar made from caldera clay. They have the look of buildings designed to withstand heat: thick walls, low profiles, flat roofs that collect condensation in channels routed to cisterns. Some have attached sheds housing machinery. A few have smokestacks — short, broad, made of brick — that release thin streams of steam rather than smoke. The town runs on steam. The heat beneath the caldera is the power source.

At the center of the settlement, clustered near the caldera's low point, stand the Works themselves: three large processing halls, a pump house, and a cooling tower that is the tallest structure in the caldera, perhaps twenty meters, a square brick column open at the top through which a steady column of vapor rises and disperses. These buildings are darker than the rest, stained by decades of mineral runoff, and they hum — a low vibration felt more than heard, transmitted through the stone floor into the bones of the feet.

The town processes the water. That is its purpose. The caldera's upwelling water carries dissolved minerals — sulfur compounds, silica, trace metals of several kinds — and the Works extract them through a sequence of settling tanks, heated evaporators, and precipitation beds that cover a hectare of ground to the east of the main halls. The precipitation beds are shallow rectangular basins of dark stone, filled with hot water that cools slowly and leaves behind mineral crusts that are scraped, collected, and shipped down the switchback road. The beds give off a faint smell — not sulfur, exactly, but something mineral and warm, like hot clay or the inside of a dry sauna.

Power comes from the heat itself. Steam from the deepest boreholes drives turbines in the pump house. Electricity runs through overhead lines on short poles to the buildings. At night — and night here is brief, the latitude being low — the town is lit by a sparse network of electric lamps that cast hard white circles on the wet ground.

## The fauna

This is what stops visitors.

The caldera has no plants, but it has animals. The water is hot, mineral-laden, sterile of vegetation — and it teems with life. Not the life you'd expect. Not fish, not insects in the usual sense.

The dominant organisms are what the residents call *vel-thin*, a term that translates roughly as "heat-crawlers." They are flat, oval-bodied creatures, eight to twelve centimeters long, with segmented shells of a pale calcite-like material and dozens of short legs that move in rhythmic waves. They live in the warm puddles and shallow flows along the fracture lines, feeding on the mineral deposits that form at the water's edge — scraping them up with mouthparts adapted to consume silica and sulfur compounds directly. They are, in a sense, eating the rock's dissolved output. They metabolize the minerals through a biochemistry that is not fully understood and that the residents have stopped trying to understand. It works. The vel-thin are here in their thousands.

Where the water is hottest — along the active vents and near the Works' outflow channels — the vel-thin are replaced by a different animal: a smaller, redder organism, vaguely shrimp-like, that lives in water approaching 70 degrees Celsius and seems to prefer it. These are called *hassu*, and they are the reason the precipitation beds are covered with mesh grilles. The hassu colonize the hot mineral water of the beds if given the chance, and their waste products contaminate the yield.

Above the water, on the dry stone between structures, larger animals move. They are the predators of this system: flat-bodied, low-slung, perhaps forty centimeters long, with thick mineralized shells and slow deliberate gaits. They eat the vel-thin. They have no name in the local language other than "the big ones." They are territorial and sit motionless near puddles for hours, waiting. Their shells accumulate mineral deposits over time, so that older individuals look almost like stones themselves — the camouflage is effective. You can step near one without knowing it is there, and the only sign is a slight shift of weight, a leg adjusting.

Birds — or things like birds — circle high above the caldera. They are not birds. They have no feathers. Their wings are membranous, dark, and their bodies are small and armored, and they descend in quick stoops to take vel-thin from the puddle edges before rising again. They make no sound. None of the animals here make sound. The vel-thin move silently through shallow water. The predators shift their weight in silence. The flyers cut the air without a whisper. The only thing you hear is water, and even that is quiet.

The ecosystem is simple — mineral-rich water feeds the vel-thin and hassu, which feed the predators on the ground and in the air — but it is active. The cycles are live. Every puddle has its population, every fracture line its own chain of organisms, and if you sit and watch, the pace of it becomes visible: the vel-thin moving in their slow graze along a mineral crust, a predator lowering its head to the water and coming up with a shell in its jaws, a flyer banking against the bright sky.

It is, in its way, a complete world. It simply does not include plants.

## The silence

This is the part that unsettles.

The caldera is nearly silent. Not perfectly — water moves, and if you stand near an active vent you can hear a thin hiss, almost subliminal — but the ambient sound level is extraordinarily low. The wet stone absorbs footfalls. The buildings are thick-walled and sealed, and their machinery is internal, felt through the ground rather than heard. The animals are mute. There is no wind at this elevation in the caldera bowl; the air sits still and hot and dense, and the only motion is the slow rise of vapor and the occasional drift of a flyer across the sky.

Conversation in Tsuun-Vel happens quietly. The residents — perhaps a thousand people — speak in low tones, not from secrecy but because the environment seems to demand it. The silence is not empty. It is full of the heat and the moisture and the slow processes of the earth, and it presses in from all sides. You hear your own breathing. You hear your heartbeat in your ears. You hear, if you listen closely, the almost inaudible tick of mineral crystals forming along the edge of a cooling puddle, and that sound, once you notice it, is everywhere.

## The light and the heat

The brightness is relentless. At midday the caldera floor is a field of reflected light, the wet stone acting as a diffuse mirror, and the temperature on the ground surface reaches fifty degrees Celsius. The air above it is cooler but still hot, and the moisture in the air condenses on any object that is even slightly below the ambient temperature — on tools, on the walls of buildings on their shaded sides, on the mesh grilles of the precipitation beds, on the shells of the animals. The town's residents move through this brightness with cloth wraps over their heads and shoulders and drink constantly from cistern water that is itself warm.

The heat shapes everything here. The buildings are oriented to minimize sun exposure on their narrow faces. The work in the processing halls is done in the early morning and late afternoon; the midday hours are for rest. The animals retreat to the shade of structures and to the cooler margins of puddles during the hottest part of the day and emerge again as the light begins to angle. The fracture lines steam more vigorously at midday — the heat differential driving more vapor — and the caldera fills with a thin haze that softens the light without dimming it, a luminous fog that makes the far rim of the caldera seem to float.

In the late afternoon, when the light drops and the stone begins to radiate its stored heat, the caldera is at its most beautiful. The mineral staining on the floor — the ochres and blues and blacks — deepens in the low-angle light. The vapor columns from the vents and the cooling tower catch the golden light and become visible as distinct shafts. The animals are active, the vel-thin grazing in long lines along the fracture edges, the predators moving between puddles with their slow careful steps, the flyers circling in loose spirals above. The sound is still nothing. The heat is still present. But the light gives the mineral world a warmth that is not temperature — a glow, a sense that the stone itself is luminous.

It is not a kind landscape. It is not a comfortable one. But it is *settled* in the deep sense of that word — it has found its state, its equilibrium of heat and water and mineral and life, and it holds that state with a patience that is geological, which is to say, beyond human measure. The town sits in it. The Works operate. The animals feed and are fed upon. The water comes up hot from below and leaves its colors on the ground and goes back down again. The silence holds it all.

You stand on the raised walkway between the processing halls and the precipitation beds, and the heat comes up through your feet, and the light comes down from above and back up from below, and the only sound is the faintest ticking of crystals forming at the edge of a puddle six meters away, and it is enough.
