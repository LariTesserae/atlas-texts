# The Hum of Verrault

The road rises for three hours before you see it, and even then you hear it first.

---

It begins as a pressure behind the ears, a low thickening of the air that you mistake for altitude. The pass opens onto a wide plateau shelving gently southward, and the sound arrives in full — not a noise but a presence, a vibration that lives in the cartilage of your throat and in the fillings of your teeth. It is not one sound. It is ten thousand sounds braided into a single continuous roar that has no beginning and no end, no edge, no pause. You stop walking. You stand there. Your jaw hangs slightly open because your body is trying to equalize, as though the sound were a depth.

Below, the plateau widens into a shallow basin perhaps four kilometers across, and the town of Verrault fills it the way lichen fills a stone — not densely, not uniformly, but with the patient spread of something that has been here long enough to belong. The buildings are low, two and three stories, built from a dark local stone that the perpetual moisture has streaked black and rust-orange in vertical channels. They are spaced apart, separated by broad gravel yards and iron rail beds and the dark geometric cuts of drainage channels. There is no crowd. There is no crowd anywhere. The streets are wide and almost empty, and the few figures you can see from this height move slowly, leaning slightly forward, as though walking into a wind that is entirely acoustic.

Everything is wet. Not rain-wet, not running-wet, but *condensed* — the way a cold glass sweats in summer. The plateau sits high enough to catch warm air rising from the lowlands to the south, and that air hits the cooler elevation and dumps its moisture continuously, not as rain but as a fine, persistent precipitation that the locals call *la suée*, the sweat. It beads on every surface. It darkens the stone. It pools in the drainage channels in thin films that shiver with vibration. The metal — and there is so much metal, iron railings and copper piping and steel frame-structures rising above the rooftops like the skeletons of unfinished towers — the metal weeps. Drops form at the undersides of every pipe and beam and hang there, trembling with the sound, and when they fall they fall in patterns that are almost, but not quite, rhythmic.

---

The town exists because of the sound. Or rather, the sound exists because of the town, and the town exists because of something beneath the town, and the whole arrangement has been running for so long that no one alive remembers the sequence.

The structures are industrial. Not new industrial — not the bright sterility of a modern facility — but the old, settled, muscular industry of a place that was mechanized a century and a half ago and has never stopped being mechanized. The buildings fall into two types: the *bonnes maisons*, the good houses, stone and shuttered, where people sleep and eat and conduct the business of living, and the *machines*, the engine-houses, which are larger and squatter and have no windows at all, only vents. The engine-houses are spaced through the town in a pattern you cannot read from above but that the residents know the way you know the rooms of your own home. There are perhaps thirty of them visible from the pass, and more beyond the ridge to the north, and the sound comes from all of them at once.

Each engine-house contains a *moteur lente* — a slow engine — and the name is precise. These are not turbines spinning at thousands of revolutions per minute. They are enormous horizontal pistons, driven by geothermal pressure from a deep aquifer that runs beneath the plateau, and they cycle perhaps eight times a minute. Each cycle produces a sound like a door closing in a cathedral, a deep percussive *thunk* that you feel in your sternum before you hear it with your ears. But there are dozens of them, running in asynchronous phases, and the percussion overlaps and layers and compounds into the continuous roar. The sound is not loud in the way that an explosion is loud. It is loud in the way that the ocean is loud — total, environmental, inescapable, a medium rather than an event. After a day in Verrault you stop hearing it. After two days you hear nothing else even when you listen. After a week, residents say, the silence of the lowlands becomes unbearable, and you come back.

---

The geothermal aquifer is the reason for everything. It sits perhaps four hundred meters below the basalt of the plateau, a body of superheated water pressurized by the geological architecture of the region — a magma finger from the old volcanic chain to the east tilts the strata and traps the water in a lens-shaped reservoir between impermeable rock layers. The founders of Verrault drilled into it a hundred and sixty years ago, looking for heat, and found pressure instead, and built engines to convert that pressure into mechanical work. The engines drive generators. The generators power a grid that extends across the plateau and down into the lowland valleys, lighting towns that have never heard the engines that serve them. The power flows outward in cables thick as your wrist, strung between iron pylons that march south across the plateau's edge and vanish into the mist.

The engines never stop. They cannot stop. There is no valve assembly sufficient to cap the aquifer's pressure, and there has not been one for over a century — the original drill-heads are now geological features, sealed into the rock by mineral deposition, and the pressure flows whether the engines accept it or not. If an engine fails, the pressure vents through the bore-pipe and fractures the surrounding stone, which would collapse the engine-house and likely the buildings adjacent. So the engines are maintained. The engines are always maintained. That is what the people of Verrault do. They maintain.

---

Walking into the town from the north road, you pass through a district of warehouses and loading yards where the stone is stained dark with decades of oil and mineral runoff. The gravel underfoot is coarse and damp and crunches with a sound you cannot hear. A freight rail line runs along the eastern edge, its tracks orange with oxidation, its ties slick and black. Empty flatcars sit in a siding, their wheels locked, and a thin film of water covers their wooden beds and reflects the pale sky. There are no animals. You notice this gradually — no dogs, no birds, no insects visible on the wet stone. The sound is not hostile to life, exactly, but it compresses it. The fauna that survive here are small and subterranean: certain worms in the drainage channels, a species of pale cricket that lives inside the ventilation shafts of the engine-houses, and reportedly a type of bat that nests in the upper structures of the pylons, though no one has seen one clearly. The residents keep no pets. There is no room, acoustically, for the small sounds animals make.

The vegetation is equally sparse. Between the buildings, in the gravel yards, clumps of a tough gray-green sedge grow in low hummocks, their roots gripping the saturated ground. Moss covers the north-facing walls of the older structures in patches, dark and velvety. There is a single tree — a single tree in the entire visible town — standing in the central square beside the pump-house, a stunted, leaning thing with bark like wet leather and leaves that are more gray than green. It is not dying. It has looked exactly like this for forty years, according to the municipal records. It has simply decided, at some point in its slow life, that this is its form, and it will not grow further. The children of Verrault, of whom there are not many, climb it sometimes.

---

The center of town is a long, low square paved in fitted stone that has been worn smooth by a century of foot traffic and is now perpetually slick with condensation. The municipal hall sits on the south side, a solid two-story building with arched windows and a clock on its facade that does not move — not because it is broken but because it was never connected to a mechanism. It is a decorative clock. Time in Verrault is told by the engine cycles, which are reliable enough that the residents can estimate the hour within a few minutes by the slight variations in the acoustic pattern as different engines enter maintenance rotations. There is a cafe. There is a general supply. There is a building marked with the sign of the regional electrical authority, and next to it, a chapel that is no longer a chapel but is still called the chapel, and which now houses the engineering library — shelves of blueprints, maintenance logs, bore-survey records, and the personal journals of every chief engineer going back six generations.

The people you pass on the street nod. They do not shout, because shouting is pointless. They communicate through a sign language that is partly formalized and partly improvised, a pidgin of gestures and lip-reading and the occasional written note passed between hands. Their voices, when they do speak — in the insulated interiors of the bonnes maisons, where thick stone and cork-lined walls cut the sound to a manageable roar — are low and flat and slightly hoarse, the way voices get when they are rarely used. They speak the regional dialect with an accent that is recognizable anywhere: the Verrault accent, the accent of people who grew up inside a sound, and it has a quality that linguists describe as *subdued* — reduced dynamic range, compressed intonation, as though the voice has learned not to compete.

---

There is something strange here, and you cannot locate it immediately. It is not the sound — the sound is overwhelming but it is understood, it has a cause. It is not the emptiness — the sparseness is simply the natural density of a town this size on a plateau this exposed. The strangeness is in the *slowness*. Everything in Verrault moves slowly. Not reluctantly, not lazily, but with the deliberate, considered pace of a place that has synchronized itself to a rhythm beneath the rhythm of the engines. You watch a woman cross the square and it takes her a long time, and she is not walking slowly — she is walking at the speed that this place moves. The geothermal aquifer cycles on a timescale of decades. The engines wear on a timescale of years. The stone weathers on a timescale of centuries. The people live on a timescale of generations, and very few of them leave, and those who do tend to return, because the lowlands are too quiet and too fast and too full of birdsong and other small, fragile sounds that they cannot place.

The strangeness is also in the mist. The condensed moisture does not simply fall. In the evenings, as the temperature drops a single degree, the condensation thickens into a fog that fills the basin and transforms the town into an arrangement of dark shapes and the amber points of gas lamps — for the town has gas lamps as well as electric light, because the gas lamps work when the grid is under maintenance, and the grid is frequently under maintenance. In the fog, the sound changes character. It softens, or rather, it *complexifies* — the droplets in the air scatter the acoustic waves, and the single roar separates into its component frequencies, and you can hear, faintly, the individual engines, their individual cycles, the *thunk-thunk-thunk* of each one distinct and countable, like heartbeats in a dark room. On foggy nights the residents say the town is *lisible* — readable. You can hear its anatomy.

---

Standing at the edge of the plateau, looking south, you can see the lowlands spread out in their haze of green and brown and the distant thread of the river, and above you the cables run out from the pylons carrying the electricity that the engines produce, and below you the water runs out through the drainage channels in thin, steady streams that join to a creek that joins to the river. Everything flows outward from Verrault — power, water, the slow erosion of stone — and nothing flows in except the warm air from the south and the occasional visitor who will leave with their ears ringing and a strange reluctance to descend.

The sound never stops. The engines never stop. The water never stops forming on the undersides of the pipes and falling in those patterns that are almost, but not quite, rhythmic. The tree in the square does not grow. The clock does not move. The children climb the tree and do not fall. The fog comes in the evening and goes in the morning and comes again the next evening. The chief engineer writes in her journal. The aquifer presses upward. The basalt holds.

This is Verrault. It is warm and wet and deafening and almost nothing changes, and it has been almost nothing-changing for a hundred and sixty years, and it will be almost nothing-changing for a hundred and sixty more, and the sound of it carries no further than the edge of the plateau and then vanishes into the ordinary silence of the world below.
