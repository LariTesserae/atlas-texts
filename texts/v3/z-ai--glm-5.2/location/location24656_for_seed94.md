# The Weeping Highlands

There is a plateau so broad that clouds form beneath your feet.

It sits at a height where the air thins enough to taste — mineral, cold, carrying the faint iron tang of water that has been percolating through stone for longer than most rivers have existed. The plateau is not flat. Nothing here is flat. It is a landscape of rolls and hollows, of exposed ridgelines ribbed with dark metamorphic rock, of depressions that fill with standing water and never quite empty. The scale is difficult to hold in the mind. You can walk for hours and the horizon does not change — the same pale grey sky, the same dark vegetation, the same sense that the ground extends in every direction not merely to the horizon but somehow *past* it, as though the plateau has its own geometry, its own private continental shelf.

The rock weeps. This is the first thing you notice, before the vegetation, before the silence. Every exposed face of stone runs with water — not dramatic waterfalls, not cascades, but a persistent, intimate seepage, a film of moisture that moves down the rock faces with a slowness that borders on geological. The stone is dark, almost black when wet, shot through with veins of pale quartzite that catch what light there is. Lichen maps the surfaces in patterns that resemble circuitry — pale green, phosphorescent yellow-green, occasional rust-red patches that bleed into the water tracks and stain them faintly amber. The weeping is not seasonal. The water table sits so close to the surface here that the rock is perpetually saturated, and gravity does its patient work. In the cold air, the water films sometimes freeze at night into thin glassy sheets that shatter under the first touch of morning light, and then re-form as the seepage continues. You hear this sometimes — a faint crack, like a twig snapping very far away, then silence again.

---

The vegetation is the second thing.

It is not a forest, exactly. There are no single-trunked trees in the traditional sense. What grows here is a dense, interlocking mat of woody shrubs and creeping plants that has achieved something like canopy status through sheer collective stubbornness. The dominant form is a broad-leaved evergreen with bark like oxidized copper — dark, rough, faintly metallic in the grey light — that grows laterally, sending out branches that root where they touch the ground and send up new stems, so that a single individual might cover an acre, might cover ten acres, might be functionally indistinguishable from a grove. These plants link arm to arm. Their branches interweave so tightly that in places you could walk across their surface like a floor, though the surface flexes and sags and drips. Beneath this living lattice, the ground is dark and wet and smells of tannin and cold iron.

Moss is everywhere. Not the soft, polite moss of temperate forests — this is a dense, structural moss, building cushions the size of armchairs, colonizing every horizontal surface, every branch, every rock that isn't actively flowing with water. It comes in colours you don't expect: deep teal, almost black-green, and a strange pale blue-grey that seems to hold light even when the sky is overcast. Sphagnum, but not quite sphagnum. The cellular structure, under a hand lens, is wrong — too regular, too hexagonal, like something designed rather than grown. The moss holds water the way a sponge holds water, and when you step on it, it releases a small sigh of cold air and moisture. Step after step, the whole landscape breathes around you in these tiny exhalations.

Ferns uncurl from every crevice. They are large, structural, their fronds reaching waist-height or higher, and their undersides are thick with spore cases that glisten with a fluid that is not water — it is more viscous, faintly iridescent, and it does not freeze even when the temperature drops below zero. In the quiet, you can sometimes hear the spore cases pop. A tiny wet sound, like a bubble bursting. It happens in waves across a stand of ferns, triggered by some change in humidity or pressure, and the wave moves through the population like a pulse.

---

The silence here is not the silence of absence. It is the silence of *density*. So many living things pressed so close together, all respiring, all growing, all slowly digesting and seeping and dripping — and all of it so quiet that the aggregate sound is barely a whisper. The water moving through stone makes no noise at this scale. The moss breathing makes no noise. The ferns releasing spores make almost no noise. What you hear, when you stop and listen, is a kind of soft, continuous *presence* — the acoustic texture of a world that is full to bursting but has no interest in announcing itself.

Occasionally a bird. Something large, high up, calling once — a single low tone that drops through the air like a stone through water, resonant and brief. You almost never see them. They live in the canopy of interlocking branches, and their calls are designed to carry across distances that the eye cannot cross. Sometimes you hear wingbeats — heavy, unhurried — and then nothing.

The fauna is abundant but secretive. Things live in the moss — small, warm, quick. You see movement more than form. Something pale that vanishes into a crevice. Something dark that moves through the ferns and is gone. The water channels hold life: pale amphibians with translucent skin through which you can see the slow pulse of dark organs, their bodies adapted to the cold, their metabolisms so low that they barely qualify as warm in your hand. They sit in the seepage channels and wait. They are very good at waiting. In the broader pools, where water collects in the hollows between ridges, there are larger things — somethings that move in the dark water and leave v-shaped wakes that catch the grey light. You do not see them clearly. They do not want to be seen clearly.

The predator-prey dynamics here are slow, cold, patient. Everything is patient. The cold dictates it. At this elevation and temperature, metabolisms are low, generation times are long, and the entire ecosystem moves at a pace that makes a glacier look impulsive. The pale things in the moss eat the spore-fluid and the insects that drink it. The amphibians eat the pale things. The unseen things in the pools eat the amphibians. And the large unseen things in the canopy eat everything, when they choose to, which is rarely, because they are large and the cold makes them torpid and they can go for weeks between meals, draped across the interlocking branches with their dark bodies absorbing what little warmth the pale sun provides.

---

And then there is the *other* thing.

Because this landscape, for all its wildness, for all its lack of constructed structures, is not untouched. Not entirely. There is technology here, but it has been swallowed. It has been *absorbed.*

In certain rock faces, where the weeping water runs heaviest, you can find pipes. Old pipes. Corroded iron, thick as a human torso, embedded in the stone as though the stone grew around them — or as though they were placed here so long ago that the geological processes themselves have incorporated them. They do not form a system you can trace. A section here. A valve there, encrusted with mineral deposits and lichen, its wheel frozen solid. A junction box mounted on a rock face, its casing ruptured, its wires exposed — wires that have been colonized by a particular species of moss that seems to thrive on copper oxide and grows in unnaturally precise spirals along the conductive cores.

Something industrial happened here, or was attempted here. The plateau is vast, and in its remoteness, someone — something — tried to build. Not structures. Not buildings. *Infrastructure.* The pipes suggest water management, or perhaps extraction. The junction boxes suggest power distribution. There are places where the rock itself has been cut — precise, geometric cuts, rectangular channels carved into the dark stone with an exactness that no natural process produces, now filled with water and moss and home to the translucent amphibians. There are places where the ground vibrates faintly underfoot, where something deep below — a pump, a generator, a mechanism too deep and too buried to identify — still turns, still works, driven by the water pressure that the plateau provides in abundance. These vibrations are so subtle you feel them in your teeth before you feel them in your feet. They have been happening for so long that the ecosystem has calibrated itself around them. The moss near the vibrating zones grows thicker. The ferns there produce more spore-fluid. The pale things in the moss are more numerous, more active. Life has found the hum and leaned into it.

There are no buildings. No factories. No roads. Whatever was built here was built into the landscape itself, subterranean, distributed, the machinery sunk into the rock and the pipes threaded through the water table and the whole system left to run — or left to fail, slowly, gracefully, one component at a time, until all that remained was the buried hum and the mineral-crusted pipes weeping in the wet air, indistinguishable now from the natural seepage that surrounds them.

The plateau does not mourn this. The plateau does not notice. The plateau is busy being itself — vast and cold and wet and alive, its moss breathing, its ferns pulsing, its water moving through stone and pipe alike with the same patient indifference, its pale creatures going about their pale business in the perpetual grey-green light, its horizon never changing, its silence holding everything like a bowl holds water.

You could die here and the moss would grow over you in a season. The moss would not know the difference. The moss does not know the difference between a rock and a pipe and a body and a fallen branch. The moss grows. The water seeps. The silence holds. The plateau goes on.
