# The Slag Expanse

The first thing is the sound. It arrives before the light resolves, before the ground confirms itself beneath your feet — a low, continuous bellowing that seems to come from no single direction. It rises through the mineral floor of the plateau and through the soles of whatever you have walked in on. It is not mechanical in the sense of a single engine. It is mechanical the way a stomach is mechanical: something vast and internal is turning over, somewhere beneath the crust, and the crust is singing with it.

---

The plateau sits at altitude — high enough that the horizon cuts a clean line, unsoftened by haze or vegetation. The sky above is a pale, brassy color, like the inside of an old lamp housing, and the light that comes through it is ordinary in the most unsettling way. You can see by it. It casts shadows. It has a direction. But it does not feel like sunlight. It feels like workshop light — even, clinical, slightly too steady, as though someone has forgotten to include the small variations that make a sky feel alive.

The ground stretches in every direction without interruption. No buildings. No roads. No pylons, no smokestacks, no walls. And yet the whole landscape is *industrial* in a way that has nothing to do with construction and everything to do with composition. The ground is slag. Not slag on top of something else — the ground *is* slag, all the way down, a dark granular material fused in places into ribbed sheets of ferrous glass and in other places crumbling into a coarse black sand that whispers when the thermal gradients shift. There are veins of something oxidized running through it — copper-greens and sulfurous yellows and deep iron-reds, forming patterns too regular to be geological and too weathered to be designed. They look like the tracks of old plumbing, or the circulatory system of a machine that was never given a body, only a floor.

This is the paradox of the place: there are no structures here, and there never were. The industry was never *built*. It was *deposited*. The entire plateau — two hundred kilometers across, at least, from what can be seen — is the residue of a process that operated on a continental scale. Something was refined here. Not ore, not fuel — something older, something whose name doesn't translate. The refinement happened in open arrangement, without walls or vessels, and what remains is the caldera of that operation: a flat, high-altitude plain of waste material and half-processed mineral, settled now into the long, patient equilibrium of a landscape that has been this way for longer than most worlds have been worlds.

Erosion has worked it. Not wind, not water — there is no water here, has never been, not in any phase or chemistry that would be recognized as such. But heat has eroded it. The thermal cycling — days that reach temperatures sufficient to soften certain alloys, nights that cool toward the ductile range — has been working the surface for millennia. The slag has cracked into polygons. The ferrous glass has buckled into low, rolling ridges, no higher than a man's knee, stretching for kilometers in gentle undulations. The surface has acquired a patina of oxidation so thorough that it no longer looks like rust; it looks like color that was always there, as intrinsic to the material as the color of sandstone.

Nothing grows here. Nothing has ever grown here. There is no soil, no organic substrate, no seed that could find purchase. The concept of "growth" belongs to a biology that this place has never hosted.

---

And yet the plateau is not empty.

The sound — that deep, sourceless bellowing — is *them*.

They are hard to see at first, because they are the same color as the ground and because they move so slowly that the eye reads them as terrain. But once you see one, you see them everywhere, and the realization is staggering: the plateau is *covered* in life. Not plant life. Not anything recognizable. They are — for lack of a better word — fauna. Mineral fauna. Creatures that are made of the same slag and oxide and ferrous glass as the ground beneath them, that move across the surface in densities that can only be described as overwhelming.

The largest ones are several meters across and perhaps a meter tall: flat, lobate forms, like immense slugs or like the cooling pads of old blast furnaces, their upper surfaces cracked into the same polygonal patterns as the ground. They move by some process that isn't walking and isn't sliding — more like a slow, continuous extrusion of their lower surface, leaving a faintly polished trail on the slag behind them. They graze. That is the only word for it. They move across the oxidized veins and *absorb*, drawing the copper and sulfur compounds out of the surface through their undersides, leaving the ground behind them a shade darker, a shade more depleted. They eat the color out of the earth.

There are smaller ones too, and these are the more numerous — dense, rounded objects the size of fists or skulls, clustered in the depressions between the low ridges, in aggregates of hundreds or thousands. They make a sound. Not the deep bellowing — that comes from the large ones, from resonance chambers inside their bodies that amplify the thermal expansion of the ground — but a higher sound, a chittering, a clicking, like thousands of small mechanisms operating in loose synchronization. They vibrate. They are vibrating *all the time*, and the aggregate sound of their vibration, multiplied across the entire plateau, is what fills the air here, what makes the silence impossible, what makes the sound parameter register so high.

And there are others.

There are others that are harder to describe, because they do not hold a consistent shape.

---

This is where the place begins to come apart.

It starts subtly. The horizon, which seemed so clean and precise when you arrived, begins to exhibit behavior that no horizon should. It *breathes*. Not metaphorically — the line where the slag-meets-sky expands and contracts in a slow rhythm that might be connected to the thermal cycling, might be connected to the sound, might be connected to nothing that has a mechanism. Distant features — low ridges, the occasional pillar of fused material standing alone in the plain — appear to shift position when you are not looking at them directly, and sometimes when you are. Not dramatically. Not the way a mirage shifts. More like the geometry of the plateau is slightly *flexible*, as though space here has the consistency of a thick fluid rather than a rigid frame, and the features of the landscape are floating in it, subject to currents that have no visible cause.

The creatures do not seem to notice. Or if they notice, they do not care. The large ones continue their grazing paths, indifferent to the fact that the landmarks around them have rearranged. The small ones continue their vibration. The sound continues, and the sound is now clearly more than the sum of its sources — there is a component of it that seems to come from *underneath* the ground, or from the space between the ground and the sky, or from the spaces between moments. It has a texture. It is thick. It sits in the chest.

The light, which seemed so steady, begins to stutter at the edges of perception. Not flickering — that would be too mechanical. The light *hesitates*. As though it is considering, at certain moments, whether to continue. As though it is unsure of the rules that govern it. The shadows on the ground lose alignment with the objects that cast them, drifting a few degrees off-axis before slowly, reluctantly, correcting. The brass-yellow of the sky deepens in patches, as though someone is smearing the color, and then clarifies again.

This is the weirdness of the place, and it is not occasional or peripheral. It is the *condition* of the place. The Slag Expanse is not a landscape where reality has broken. It is a landscape where reality was never fully assembled — where the process that refined whatever was refined here also refined the substrate of space itself, stripping out some of the binding agents that hold a world together, leaving behind a terrain that is *mostly* coherent, that holds its shape *most* of the time, that follows its physics *almost* entirely. The gap between "mostly" and "entirely" is where everything interesting happens.

The creatures live in that gap. That is why they can be what they are — mineral organisms moving across a mineral plain, absorbing minerals through surfaces that should not be able to absorb, resonating in chambers that should not be able to resonate. They are not breaking the rules of the place. They are the place's rules, expressed in motion.

---

And the motion is so slow. Everything here is slow. The large grazers cross the plateau on timescales measured in what might be days or might be weeks — it is hard to tell, because the light does not cycle in a way that clearly distinguishes day from night. It merely brightens and dims, in long, tidal swells, as if the sky is breathing in sympathy with the horizon. The small vibrators pulse in rhythms that take minutes to complete a single cycle. The thermal expansion that drives the bellowing is a process of hours, not seconds. The ground itself shifts its geometry over durations that you can only catch by accident — you look away from a distant pillar, and when you look back, it has moved, but you did not see it move, and it will not move again for a long time.

This is the rhythm of the Expanse: a world that is always in the middle of something, always in the process of becoming what it already is. The industry that formed it is gone, or was never a thing with a maker, or is still happening in a way that does not require activity. The creatures that fill it are alive in a way that does not require biology. The reality that contains it is stable in a way that does not require consistency.

The sound continues. The grazers move. The small ones vibrate. The horizon breathes. The light hesitates and then decides, again, to stay.

The plateau endures, in its own state, settled and weathered and vast, a continent-sized floor of refined waste at the top of a world that may or may not have a bottom, covered in a living crust of mineral life that has no need of water or soil or sun, that thrives on heat and vibration and the small, persistent failure of the rules that should have kept it from existing.

It is not hell. It is not a monument. It is not a warning.

It is a place that is sufficient to itself, that has arrived at its own logic, that does not require an audience or an explanation. It is loud and hot and full of a life that doesn't know it's strange, and it has been this way for a very long time, and it will be this way for a very long time more, and the sound — the deep, mineral, unending sound of it — is the closest thing this place has to a voice, and it is saying nothing, and it is saying it constantly.
