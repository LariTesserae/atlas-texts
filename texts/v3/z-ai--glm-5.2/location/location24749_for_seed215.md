# The Uplands of Vesh-Taram

The plateau rose three generations ago and has not finished rising.

You feel it in the ground before you see it — a low, persistent tremor underfoot, not violent enough to disturb the standing water but constant enough that you notice when it stops. Which it rarely does. The crust here is still deciding what shape it wants to be. Fissures open and close like slow breathing. Fresh basalt pushes through old basalt in dark, wet tongues. The rock is so new it has no proper name yet — geologists assigned it a provisional classification and moved on, because by the time they published, the composition had shifted.

This is the Uplands. A continental shelf that should not, by any prior model, exist at this altitude. It sits high enough that the air thins slightly, high enough that clouds form *below* you on some days, rolling through the lowlands like a white sea while you stand on dark stone in clear, cold light. The temperature hovers near freezing most of the year. Not the bitter killing cold of true altitude — more a damp, patient cold that settles into fabric and stays. Your breath doesn't dramaticize itself into clouds. It just thickens the air a little and vanishes.

---

## Water

Everything here is wet, but not dramatically so. No rivers roar. No waterfalls thunder. Instead, water expresses itself the way a cold room expresses condensation — on every surface, all the time, without urgency.

The rock weeps. Not from cracks or springs — from its own pores. The plateau's stone is vesicular, full of bubbles and micro-channels from its rapid formation, and the constant low clouds press moisture into it like a slow hand wringing a cloth. You can place your palm flat on the ground and feel dampness seeping up into your skin. In the low points between ridges, this seepage collects into wide, shallow braids — not streams exactly, more like sheets of water two centimeters deep moving so slowly you have to watch for a full minute to confirm they're moving at all. The surface tension catches light and gives these sheets a metallic look. Mercury, almost. But cold, and clear if you kneel and look through them.

After the rain — and it rains often, thin persistent rain that doesn't so much fall as drift horizontally — the entire plateau becomes a mirror. Every depression fills. The stone darkens from charcoal to black. Walking becomes an exercise in choosing between wading through ankle-deep water or skirting along the narrow higher ridges, which are slick and rounded and new.

There are lakes. One of them, Lake Vesh, stretches to a horizon you can barely distinguish from the sky. It is so flat and still that birds land on it and seem to stand on nothing. The water is soft, slightly mineral, drinkable if you don't mind the taste of dissolved basalt — iron and calcium and something faintly alkaline that sits on the back of your tongue.

## The Settlement

The city — if you can call it a city — is called Taram, and it does not look like a city. This is deliberate.

Taram stretches across four hundred kilometers of the plateau's most stable ridge system. But its density is almost nonexistent. You can walk for an hour through what is technically urban infrastructure and see nothing but low stone formations and the occasional hum of something buried. The buildings are there. They're just *in* the rock rather than on it.

The architects who designed Taram worked with the plateau's geology rather than against it. They used induction-forging — a technique that, as far as the published literature goes, involves manipulating molecular cohesion in basalt through targeted electromagnetic fields — to grow structures directly out of the bedrock. Walls rise from the stone as if they were always meant to be there. Roofs are continuous with the natural ridgeline. From above, from a survey drone, you can make out the pattern: a vast, branching network of chambers and corridors following the plateau's fracture lines, roofed in dark stone that matches the surrounding rock so precisely that the only giveaway is the geometry. Too regular. Too intentional. The walls meet at angles that nature doesn't bother with.

Inside, the spaces are warm. This is the first surprise. The settlement runs on geothermal capture — the plateau's ongoing tectonic activity provides more heat than the residents need, and the excess is vented through micro-channels that open to the surface as thin steam-lines, barely visible against the grey sky. The interiors are dry, spare, functional. Lighting comes from bioluminescent panels engineered from a lichen-analogue that thrives on basalt and warmth — pale blue-green, enough to navigate by, not enough to read comfortably. People here use the light they need and no more.

The population is not large. Taram was built for scale, not density. Its infrastructure could support millions. It houses perhaps sixty thousand, spread thin across the ridge network. You might have three neighbors within walking distance, or you might have none. The corridors between habitation clusters are long and quiet and smell like wet stone and warm air and something faintly organic — the lichen, perhaps, or the microbial mats that have begun colonizing the interior walls where moisture collects.

There are transit systems. Magnetic rails set into the floor of the main corridors, silent, fast, carrying small carriages between nodes. You step onto a platform, wait — sometimes minutes, sometimes longer — and a carriage arrives, open-sided, warm inside, moving with the eerie smoothness of something that has no contact with the surface it travels over. The ride is quiet. You hear the rails sing a single low note, almost below hearing, and the sound of water dripping somewhere in the walls.

## Vegetation, or the Lack of It

There are almost no plants. The plateau is too new, too cold, too mineral-poor in the ways that matter. What soil exists is ground basalt — dark sand, really, lacking the organic complexity that roots want. The water has nowhere to carry nutrients from, because there is no upstream. Everything that arrives here arrives from the clouds or from the rock itself.

What grows is microscopic. Biofilms — thin, dark-green, almost black — spread across the wet rock surfaces in irregular patches. They feel slick underfoot, vaguely biological, warm to the touch by fractions of a degree. Under magnification they are dense mats of cells, something between algal and bacterial, photosynthesizing what light they can from the overcast sky and fixing nitrogen directly from the atmosphere through pathways that were, as of forty years ago, not known to exist. They were discovered here. Or rather, they were invented here — the distinction remains unclear, and the researchers who first described them have been diplomatically vague about whether they found the biofilms or introduced them.

In the shallow water sheets, something similar happens. The water takes on a faint greenish tint in places, visible only when the light is right — late afternoon, low angle, the clouds thinning for a moment. This is not algae. It is something else, something that has colonized the available niche and fills it quietly, reproducing at a pace that matches the slow flow of water over new stone.

Tall above the biofilms: nothing. No trees. No shrubs. No grass. The horizon is stone, water, stone, sky.

## Fauna

And yet the place is full of animals. This is the thing that confuses visitors.

They come from below. The lowlands — the cloud-sea beneath the plateau — are older, richer, more established. And something in the fauna of that region detected the plateau's emergence and began moving upward.

The first to arrive were the hollow-boned ruminants — the Veshi, as they're called locally, though the name predates the settlement and refers to the animals, not the place. They are roughly deer-sized but built differently: longer legs, almost stilt-like, splayed at the base for stability on wet uneven rock. Their hooves are wide and textured, gripping the slick basalt like suction cups. Their coats are dense, grey-brown, shedding water. They eat the biofilms. They move across the plateau in loose groups of twenty or thirty, grazing the wet rock faces with a scraping sound — their teeth are adapted for it, flat and hard, like chisels. You hear them before you see them: a rhythmic, collective scraping that carries across the flat landscape, oddly musical.

They are not afraid of people. They have no reason to be. They arrived before the settlement did, and the settlement has never hunted them. They walk through the corridors of Taram sometimes, having wandered in through open transit tunnels, and stand in the warm hallways blinking in the bioluminescent light, water dripping from their coats, until someone gently guides them back outside. They breed slowly. They live a long time. Their eyes are large and pale and seem to hold a mild, constant confusion, as though they also are not entirely certain about this plateau and why they are on it.

After the Veshi came the predators.

They are not large. The largest are the height of a man at the shoulder, lean, low-slung, moving in coordinated groups of four to six. They are called Tarrin — another old name, from the lowland languages. Their coats are dark, nearly black, and their footfall is silent on the wet stone. They hunt by exhaustion, not ambush: they pick a group of Veshi and follow them for hours, days sometimes, at a walking pace, never pressing, never charging, until a young or old animal falls behind. Then they close in. It is efficient. It is not cruel, though it is hard to watch. The kills happen on the open stone, and the water carries the blood away in thin red threads that dilute to nothing within fifty meters.

You see the Tarrin often. They sit on ridgelines in the rain, watching. They are not interested in people. They are interested in the Veshi, and in the small burrowing things that have begun to colonize the cracks in the basalt — fist-sized, furred, quick, living in the gap between rock layers where warmth from the settlement's geothermal vents leaks upward. These burrowers are everywhere if you know where to look. You see their exit holes — perfect circles, four centimeters across, rimmed with moisture. You see them occasionally at dusk, darting between cover, fast as thought.

Above all of this, birds. Not many species — three, maybe four — but in numbers that are difficult to reconcile with the sparse vegetation. They are small, dark, fast, living on the insects that breed in the shallow water and the biofilm. The insects are nearly invisible — midges, thin-winged, present in clouds so fine you don't notice them until you walk through one and feel them on your face. The birds hunt them on the wing, turning and banking in the grey air, calling to each other in short, sharp notes that echo off the stone and give the plateau its only real voice.

The ecosystem is simple. It is also, somehow, full. The space between organisms is large — you can walk for kilometers and see nothing alive, then crest a ridge and find a hundred Veshi grazing a wet slope, the Tarrin watching from a quarter-kilometer away, the birds wheeling above. Then nothing again. The life here is distributed the way the settlement is: vast in range, thin on the ground.

## Sound

Mostly, it is quiet.

The rain makes the most noise — a fine static hiss that lasts for hours, not loud but constant, filling the acoustic space so completely that when it stops, the silence feels wrong. After the rain, you hear water moving. Not rushing. Seeping. A sound like cloth being wrung very slowly. The stone itself contributes a low resonance — the tectonic activity below, muffled by kilometers of basalt, reaching the surface as a vibration more felt than heard. If you press your ear to the rock, you hear it: a deep, continuous murmur, like the plateau is talking to itself.

In the settlement, the transit rails sing their single note. The lichen-light panels make no sound. The geothermal vents make a faint, intermittent hiss when they cycle. People speak quietly, not from rule but from habit — the architecture carries sound efficiently through the stone corridors, and after a few weeks of hearing your neighbor's conversation transmitted through the walls with perfect clarity, you learn to modulate.

The animals are the exception. The Veshi call to each other — a low, resonant vocalization, almost subsonic, that carries for kilometers across the flat stone. You feel it in your chest more than you hear it. The Tarrin are silent. The birds are not.

## The Feeling of the Place

It feels new. That is the overwhelming impression. Not raw — not violently new, not the destructive newness of a landslide or a volcanic eruption. This is a slower kind of emergence. The plateau is still becoming itself. The stone is still settling. The water is still finding its paths. The biofilms are still spreading. The animals are still arriving, still testing the edges of what this place can support. The settlement is still growing, still extending its corridors along the new fracture lines, still learning which sections of the plateau will hold and which will shift.

The people who live here have adopted the pace of the place. They build slowly. They move deliberately. They watch the animals and the water and the stone with the patient attention of people who understand that they are living inside a process that will not be finished in their lifetimes, or their children's, or their children's children's. The tech they have — the induction-forging, the geothermal systems, the bioluminescent lighting, the transit networks — is extraordinary by any standard, and they use it with a restraint that borders on reverence. They could build faster. They choose not to. The plateau is teaching them something about emergence, about the value of slow formation, and they are listening.

The light here is ordinary. Grey, mostly. The clouds are thick and persistent, but not opaque — light comes through them diffused, even, without direction. There are no dramatic shadows. No golden hours. The light at noon is the same as the light at four in the afternoon, just slightly less. On the rare days when the clouds break, the sun hits the wet stone and the whole plateau ignites — every surface reflective, every depression a mirror, the shallow water sheets blazing white — and for an hour or two, the place is blindingly, improbably beautiful, and everyone stops what they're doing and stands in the light and says nothing.

Then the clouds close again, and the water keeps seeping, and the stone keeps settling, and the Veshi keep grazing, and the plateau keeps rising, slowly, by millimeters, into a sky that doesn't seem to mind.
