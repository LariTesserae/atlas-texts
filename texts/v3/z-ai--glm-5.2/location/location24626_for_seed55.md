# The Old Gardens Beneath

There is no floor here, in the sense that there is no natural ground. There is only what was made, and what has grown over what was made, and what the water has done to both over the long time since.

---

The bottom is worked stone. Not quarried and laid — more intimate than that. The stone was shaped in place, carved and dressed where it lay, fitted to itself by hands that understood the material as a patient interlocutor rather than something to be dominated. You can see the tool marks if you swim close enough, and at this depth you cannot swim close enough, because there is no light. But the marks are there. Chisel grooves. Adze scars. The gentle concavity where someone ground a basin into granite over what must have been years of repeated effort, kneeling in cold air on stone that was not yet underwater.

That was before. The water came later, or the land went down, or the water rose — the distinction does not matter to the place itself, which has been submerged long enough that submersion is its natural state, its only state, the thing it has settled into as a stone settles into mud. The erosion parameter reads ancient, and it is correct: this is a landscape that has found its final form. Nothing is actively wearing away. Nothing is actively building up. The slow processes that still operate are so slow they are nearly geological — the slight dissolution of mortar joints over centuries, the faint thickening of algal films over decades, the almost imperceptible migration of root systems through cracks in the stonework over thousands of years.

It is vast. Continental in its extent, or at least it feels that way — the worked stone extends in every direction further than anyone has mapped, a plain of constructed terrain rolling on beneath the cold water like a drowned continent that was never a continent to begin with. Someone built a continent. With hand tools. With stone axes and bone needles and woven fiber and the patient leverage of wedges and water and time. The scale defies the technology, and that is the central fact of this place: it was made by people whose tools were simple but whose patience was not.

---

The water is cold. Not the lethal cold of the deep ocean — this is something gentler, something around six or seven degrees, the temperature of a lake in late autumn that has not yet turned over. It is not unpleasant if you are adapted. If you are not adapted, it will kill you slowly, over hours, by degrees, pulling the warmth from your extremities inward until your core surrenders. The cold is uniform. It does not vary by depth because the depth does not vary much — the whole constructed plain sits at roughly the same elevation, a low and nearly flat plane of stone only gently terraced, the water moving over it in a slow, laminar sheet that is perhaps forty meters deep at the deepest and six or seven at the shallowest. The shallows are where the vegetation is thickest. The deeper channels are where the water moves, slowly, with the patience of something that has been circulating for longer than recorded history.

There is no current to speak of. Not really. The water moves, but the way tectonic plates move — you would not feel it if you held still in it. You would only notice, over the course of a day, that you had drifted. The water carries no sediment. It is old water, settled water, and it has had millennia to deposit whatever it was going to deposit. It is clear, in the way that something can be clear without being transparent — the darkness is not a property of the water but of the absence of light. If you brought a lamp down here, the water would show you forty meters of visibility, easy. But no one brings a lamp. There is no one to bring one.

---

The vegetation is the overwhelming fact of the place. It is everywhere. It is the texture of every surface, the color of every horizon, the substance that fills the space between the stone and the water. Without fauna — without a single fish, a single mollusk, a single worm or crustacean or jelly — the plants have become the ecosystem entire, and they have grown into that role with the thoroughness of organisms that have no competition and no predation and nothing at all to check them except the cold and the dark and the finite surface area of worked stone.

The dominant form is a kind of aquatic moss, or something that behaves like aquatic moss, though it is likely not moss in any taxonomic sense. It grows in continuous mats across the stone, sheets of it, layers of it, building up over themselves in a stratigraphy of living and dead and living material that can be meters thick. The mats are dark — nearly black, with a faint greenish undertone that you would only see if you had light. They are soft to the touch, spongy, saturated with the cold water, and they move in the imperceptible current the way a field of wheat moves in wind, except the movement is so slow that you would have to watch a single frond for an hour to see it bend and straighten and bend again.

Growing up through the moss mats, there are taller structures — stalks, columns, fronds that reach upward into the water column like the pillars of a drowned cathedral. Some of them are a meter tall. Some are three meters. Some, in the deeper areas, reach nearly to the surface, though the surface is not visible from below and not reachable from here. They are not kelp. They are not anything identifiable. They are the descendants of whatever was planted here when this was still above water, whatever garden this was, whatever agricultural project this was part of, and they have adapted to the cold and the dark over so many generations that they no longer resemble their terrestrial ancestors. They are pale. They are thin. They branch in geometric patterns that might be an artifact of their original cultivated form, a growth habit that was selected for by the people who planted them and that has been conserved by the absence of any pressure to change. They do not photosynthesize, not at these depths, not in this darkness. They have not photosynthesized in a very long time. What they do instead — how they derive their energy, how they grow, how they sustain an ecosystem with no light and no animals and no input from the surface — is unclear, and the clarity of that question is one of the defining features of the place. This is a landscape that does not explain itself.

There are other plants. There are growths that resemble corals but are not corals — calcareous structures built by some organism, some plant, that secretes mineral the way a stonemason lays brick, slowly, cell by cell, over years. They encrust the vertical faces of the terraces, the walls of the channels, the sides of the carved basins. They are white or pale grey, and in the darkness they are invisible, but they are there, and they contribute to the sense that everything in this place is built, that nothing is accidental, that every surface is the product of intention even when the intention was not human. The coral-plants grow where the water flow is slightly faster, in the channels, and their shapes are dictated by the water — branching into the current, thickening on the upstream face, thinning on the downstream side. They are slow, slow beyond patience. A colony the size of a dinner plate might be two thousand years old.

There are no flowers. Nothing here has flowered in the time since the water came. The plants reproduce by fragmentation, by extension, by the patient proliferation of tissue across available substrate. A single organism might cover a hectare. Might cover ten hectares. Might, if you could trace its genetic continuity through the moss mats, cover the entire plain — one plant, one individual, cloned and re-cloned across a million square meters of worked stone. The density of vegetation is high, almost claustrophobic in the shallows, where the stalks grow thick enough to brush against each other in the slow current, where the moss mats are so deep they swallow your hands if you press into them, where everything is touching everything else in a layered, saturated, living fabric that fills the water the way sediment fills rock — completely, without gaps.

---

The sounds are almost nothing. Almost. There is a low, continuous tone that might be the resonance of the water column itself, the vibration of a mass of water forty meters deep and continental in extent, responding to some harmonic of the earth or the tide or the slow convection of temperature differentials too small to measure. It is below the threshold of hearing for most organisms. You would feel it in your chest before you heard it, a pressure more than a sound, like standing near a large bell that has been struck once and is still ringing, hours later, at a frequency that has dropped below audibility into something felt.

There are other sounds. The moss mats emit a faint click when they release gas — methane, perhaps, or something analogous — and the clicking is irregular, arrhythmic, a stochastic scattering of tiny percussive events that sounds, if you listen long enough, like rain on a distant roof. The calcareous coral-plants are silent. The tall stalks are silent. The water is silent. The stone is silent.

In the shallows, when the moss mats shift — and they do shift, over hours, over days, in response to the glacial current — there is a scraping sound, a whisper of fiber against stone, like cloth dragged across a floor. It is the most human sound in this place. It sounds like work. It sounds like someone sweeping.

---

The built environment is not just the floor. It is everything. There are structures — walls, channels, basins, terraces — and there are also shapes that do not have names, forms that were made for purposes that have not survived the submersion. Low mounds, a meter high, three meters across, perfectly circular, spaced at regular intervals across the plain. Channels cut into the stone in patterns that are not linear and not curvilinear but something else, something that might be a script or might be an irrigation system or might be both. Basins carved into the stone, oval, smooth, each one aligned with its neighbors along axes that are not north-south or east-west but that are consistent across hundreds of kilometers. Whatever this place was, it was designed, and the design was executed with primitive tools and comprehensive knowledge and a timescale that allowed for the movement of mountains of stone by hand.

The walls are the most visible built feature, or they would be if there were light. They rise from the stone floor in long, low lines, a meter high, half a meter thick, built of fitted granite without mortar, the joints so tight that a knife blade would not fit between the stones. They are not retaining walls. They are not defensive walls. They do not separate anything from anything else. They simply run across the plain, parallel, spaced fifty meters apart, extending for kilometers, and their function — if they had a function beyond the making of them — has been obscured by the moss and the coral-plants and the water and the time. They are ancient. They are the oldest thing here, older than the submersion, older than the vegetation, older than the silence. They were built by the same hands that carved the basins and cut the channels and shaped the stone floor, and they were built with the same patience, and they will outlast everything else in this place because they are stone and they are fitted and they have already survived longer than most civilizations.

The moss grows over them. The coral-plants encrust them. The water moves through them, slowly, without turbulence, the way water moves through a sieve. They do not erode. They have passed through the phase of their existence in which erosion was possible and have arrived at a state of equilibrium with their environment that is so complete that they might as well be geological features. They are not geological features. They were made. Every stone in every wall was selected and shaped and placed by a human hand or by something that had hands. That is the fact that haunts this place, in the gentle way that facts haunt places: it was made. All of it. The floor, the walls, the channels, the basins, the mounds. All of it, by hand, with simple tools, over a time span that we do not have units for. And then the water came, or the land went down, and the gardens were submerged, and the plants adapted, and the animals died or left, and the light failed, and the people who made it were gone, and the place went on without them, being what they had made it, becoming what the water and the cold and the dark and the time would make of it.

---

There is no weather here, in the sense that there is no atmosphere. But the water has its own weather — slow, vast, patient beyond human observation. There are thermoclines, thin layers where the temperature shifts by a fraction of a degree, and these thermoclines move across the plain like weather fronts, taking weeks to pass a given point, bringing a faint change in the growth rate of the moss, a faint shift in the gas-release patterns, a barely perceptible change in the resonant frequency of the water column. There are also density currents, driven by differences in salinity that are so small they can only be detected by instruments, and these currents circulate the water in a pattern that might, if you watched it for a thousand years, look like a weather system — cyclonic, anticyclonic, with fronts and eddies and still, clear centers where the water does not move at all.

The dynamic of this place is the dynamic of a glacier. Everything changes, but at a speed that makes the changing invisible. The moss grows. The coral-plants accrete. The water circulates. The stone dissolves, molecule by molecule, at a rate that would take a hundred thousand years to alter a surface by a millimeter. The place is not static. It is not dead. It is not frozen. It is moving at a speed that is appropriate to its scale and its age and its temperature, a speed that is, in its own terms, lively, vigorous, full of event. It is only slow by the standards of things that breathe.

---

The darkness is total. Not dark like a cave, where there might be a crack, a seep, a faint glow from some bioluminescent organism. There is nothing. No light at all. The surface is too far above — or the water is too opaque — or there is no surface, or the surface is under a ceiling, or the sun has gone out. The reason for the darkness does not matter to the place. The darkness is the medium in which everything here exists, the way air is the medium for a forest. The plants have evolved in it. The stone was shaped in it, or was shaped before it, and has now been in it long enough that the darkness is the stone's natural condition. The water carries no light. The moss absorbs no light. The coral-plants reflect no light. If you descended here with a lantern, you would see — and the seeing would be shocking, overwhelming, a revelation of color and texture and extent that would change your understanding of what is here — but no one descends here with a lantern, and the place goes on in the dark, being itself, without the need to be seen.

This is the final quality of the place: it does not require an observer. It is complete without being witnessed. It is a constructed environment that has outlasted its constructors, a garden that has outlasted its gardeners, a work of patient primitive engineering that has been submerged and colonized and aged into something that its makers would not recognize but that is, in every cell and every stone, a continuation of what they began. The moss is where they planted. The coral-plants grow where they built. The water moves through the channels they cut. The silence is the silence of a project that is still ongoing, still being executed, by the slow, blind, handless agents of time and water and growth, following a design that was laid down before the water came and that has never been abandoned.

It is a garden. It is still a garden. It does not need a gardener. It is gardening itself, slowly, in the dark, in the cold, in the quiet, at the bottom of the world that someone made.
