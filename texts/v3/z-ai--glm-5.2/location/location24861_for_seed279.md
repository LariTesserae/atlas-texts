# The Weeping Expanse

The land has not been here long.

You can tell by the edges. Everything still carries the fracture-lines of its emergence — the clean, almost surgical breaks where stone split from stone and rose, or perhaps was pushed, into the grey air. This is a basin, or the beginning of one: a broad, low depression ringed by ridges that have not yet learned to be round. They are angular. Interrupted. Shelves of dark basalt-like material jut from the walls at inconsistent heights, some tilted upward as though the ground beneath them is still deciding which way to fall.

And everything weeps.

Not dramatically — not waterfalls or cascades. The moisture arrives with a patience that feels almost deliberate. It condenses on the undersides of the rock shelves and collects in the narrow gouges that stripe the basin walls, then travels downward in薄膜 after thin film, each one barely wider than a breath. The stone is the sort that does not resist water but seems to *generate* it — porous, vesicular, shot through with pockets that fill and drain on cycles too slow to watch but too consistent to be accidental. You can press your palm flat against a wall and within a minute feel the cool accumulation between your skin and the rock. The ground underfoot is dark and damp, not mud exactly — there is no soil here, no organic matter to bind anything — but a fine mineral grit saturated to the point of behaving like paste. It holds your footprints. It releases them slowly, the impressions filling over minutes rather than seconds.

The air is the thing you notice first, though. Or rather, the air is what carries the sound.

---

The sound is enormous. Not sharp — not the crash of surf or the report of thunder — but a deep, layered resonance that seems to come from the basin itself acting as a chamber. The weeping water hits surfaces at different intervals, and each impact produces a tone. Not a note, exactly. A frequency. The basin is shaped such that these frequencies overlap and sustain, building into a standing hum that vibrates in the chest more than the ears. It is always there. It does not crescendo or fade. It simply *is*, the way wind is, the way gravity is. When you move through the basin, your footsteps enter the field of sound and are absorbed — you hear them for a moment, then they are subsumed, swallowed into the greater resonance as though the place is too loud to permit your additions.

There are moments when the hum shifts. A shelf of stone somewhere in the basin drops a centimeter — you hear the crack, sharp, a single percussion that rings for ten seconds — and then the hum reconfigures around the new geometry. The pitch changes. Not much. A fraction. But in a space this resonant, a fraction is audible. These events happen irregularly. You might hear three in an hour. You might hear none. They are the slowest music.

---

The light here is the color of unpolished pewter. It does not seem to come from a direction. There is no visible sun, no gap in the cloud cover, no luminescence from the stone itself — and yet the basin is navigable, if barely. Shapes resolve at perhaps forty meters; beyond that, everything merges into a uniform dark grey. The light does not cast shadows in the usual sense. Objects more than a few meters away appear to have no shadows at all, as though the illumination is isotropic, coming equally from all angles, filling the space like water fills a container. Close to your feet, where the wet grit catches what little light there is, you can see a dull sheen — the ground looks almost metallic in places, almost black in others, a mottling that shifts as you move, not because the light changes but because the water content of the ground varies inch by inch.

You can see your hands clearly. You cannot see the far wall of the basin. The ridges fade into haze at their midpoint, their upper halves dissolving into the same non-color as the sky.

---

Density is the wrong word for what is happening here. There are no trees, no undergrowth, no thickets. The vegetation parameter reads as zero and it is zero — there is nothing green, nothing that photosynthesizes, nothing that grows toward light. And yet the space is *full*.

The rock shelves overhang at irregular intervals, creating partial ceilings, and beneath these ceilings the weeping stone forms secondary structures — mineral deposits, perhaps, or the remnants of whatever process pushed the basin into existence in the first place. They are not stalactites. They are more like ribs: thin, curved protrusions of a paler stone that arc downward from the shelf undersides, some reaching the ground, some stopping short, creating a lattice of vertical elements beneath each overhang. The effect is that you cannot see far in any direction without your sightline being interrupted. The basin is open — you can walk in any direction, you can see the full sweep of the space — but it is also *partitioned*, not by walls but by these dense clusters of curved stone ribs and the dark spaces between them. It is a forest of mineral geometry.

The ribs are wet, of course. Everything is wet. Water tracks down their curves in uneven rivulets, and where two ribs nearly meet, the water bridges the gap, hanging for a moment in a lens-shape before releasing. The drops join the ground's slow saturation. The ground does not drain. There is nowhere for the water to go. The basin is the lowest point for a wide distance, and the water table — if you can call it that — sits just beneath the surface, pressed upward by the same forces that created the basin. Dig anywhere and within a few centimeters you would hit water. Not pooling, not flowing. Just *present*. The ground and the water occupying the same volume, inseparable.

---

There are structures. A few. They are so understated that you might walk past them.

On the eastern side of the basin, where the wall curves inward and the overhangs are deepest, someone has stacked stones. Not built — stacked. Three low formations, each perhaps waist-high, each made of the same dark basalt-like material as the basin floor, fitted together without mortar, without tooling, the contact surfaces simply chosen for their natural flatness. They are cairn-like but too regular to be cairns. Each one is a flat-topped platform with a shallow depression in its center, as though designed to hold water — and they do. Each platform's depression is full, its surface reflecting the grey non-light in a small, still disc. The water in these depressions is warm. Not hot. Body temperature. In a landscape where everything else is cool — the air, the stone, the ground, the water that weeps from the walls — this warmth is inexplicable and absolute.

You could put your hand in one and feel it and pull your hand out and the warmth would remain. It does not cool. It does not spread. It is exactly as warm as it was before you touched it, as though the warmth is a property of the water itself rather than something transferred to it.

There are no other structures. No paths, no marks on the walls, no evidence of further construction. Just these three warm platforms, facing inward, arranged in a loose triangle, each about twenty meters from the others.

---

Life is almost absent. Almost.

What is here does not announce itself. You would have to stop moving and watch for a long time — long enough for your eyes to adjust to the dimness, long enough for the constant hum to become a background you no longer consciously hear — before you would notice the movement.

It is in the ribs. The mineral ribs that arc beneath the overhangs. Along the inner curve of some of them — not all, perhaps one in twenty — there is a faint discoloration, a darkening of the stone that is almost indistinguishable from the wetness but is not wetness. It moves. Very, very slowly. Over the course of an hour, a patch of discoloration might travel the length of a rib, from its attachment point at the shelf above to its terminus in the air below. It does not pulse. It does not branch. It simply slides, a film perhaps a millimeter thick, following the stone's contour as though reading a path inscribed in the rock's grain.

There are three of these patches visible from the eastern platforms. They are not the same color — one is a deep iron-rust red, one is almost black, and one has a faint opalescence, catching the grey light in a way that the stone itself does not. Whether they are alive depends entirely on your definition. They do not respond to touch. They do not respond to the water. They do not appear to consume anything or leave anything behind. They move, and that is all that can be said of them.

---

The basin has a smell. Not strong — the dim light seems to suppress scent the way it suppresses shadow — but present. It is mineral. Iron, wet. The faint sulfuric edge of stone that has recently been heated, though the stone is not warm now. Beneath that, something you cannot identify: a sweetness that is not sweet, a quality of the air that is closer to the smell of ozone than anything organic, as though the water, in condensing on the stone, is releasing something that was dissolved in it when it was vapor.

The sky, if it is a sky, does not change. The light does not shift toward evening or toward morning. The temperature does not drop or rise. The sound does not build or retreat. Everything that is happening here is happening at a pace that makes human time feel frantic by comparison — the stone settling, the water accumulating, the dark patches traversing their ribs, the platforms holding their warmth. This is a place that exists in a tempo measured in the slow fracturing of rock, the patient filling of basins, the almost imperceptible redistribution of moisture from air to stone to ground to water-table and back.

You could stay here for a long time. The hum would accept you. The water would find you. The grey light would not change for your presence.

You would eventually leave, or you would not. The basin would not notice either way.
