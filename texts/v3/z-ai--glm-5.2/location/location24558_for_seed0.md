# The Narrows of Ghé

It begins as sound before it begins as place.

You hear it from a half-day's walk away — not a single sound but a pressed layer of thousands, a living compression of clicks, drones, wet rattles, choral screams, percussive thumps that you feel in your sternum before your ears make sense of them. The forest ahead doesn't grow louder gradually. It crosses a threshold and suddenly you are *inside* the noise, and the noise is inside you, vibrating in the fluid of your eyes.

---

## The Land

The Ghé Narrows formed — are still forming — where a slow basalt shelf lifted from coastal flats sometime in the last few centuries. No one agrees on how long. The rock is young: dark, fine-grained, still smelling faintly of mineral heat when you crack it open. The uplift is irregular, producing low terraces and shallow ravines that drain in wandering paths toward the sea. Nothing here has been weathered into smoothness. Edges are sharp. Broken stone lies in angular sheets beneath thin soil, and roots have only just begun to fracture it. The whole terrain sits low — ten, fifteen meters above sea level at its highest points — and in the rainy intervals, water pools in the depressions between terraces, forming dark mirror-pools that last a day, sometimes two, before they sink or evaporate.

The soil is new. Volcanic ash mixed with the first generations of organic decay — fallen leaves, dead insects, the soft black humus that builds when life consumes itself faster than it accumulates. It is thin, often less than a hand-span deep, and beneath it the basalt is still bare, still dark, still warm in places where you wouldn't expect warmth. You can feel it through your feet when you stand still: a low thermal hum, not volcanic, just the residual memory of deep heat slowly bleeding away.

---

## The Canopy and the Dark

What makes the Narrows what it is — what makes the *sound* possible — is the canopy.

It begins at three meters and compounds upward in dense, interlocking tiers until the sky is simply gone. The dominant trees are not tall by forest standards — perhaps twelve, fifteen meters at most — but they grow in furious proximity, their trunks often less than a meter apart, and their crowns knit together so thoroughly that the space beneath is not merely shaded but *cancelled*. Light does not reach the floor. Not diffused light, not the green underwater glow of a temperate forest. Nothing. Your eyes adjust and adjust and there is nothing to adjust to. You navigate by touch, by sound, by the faint bioluminescence of fungal patches on the basalt — cold blue-green drips of light no brighter than a dying ember, spaced just often enough to give you a sense of motion but not direction.

The air is warm. Not hot — the canopy blocks direct sun, and the elevation keeps any heat from accumulating — but consistently, breathably warm, around the temperature of skin, so that you cannot feel where your body ends and the air begins. Humidity sits at the edge of rain without ever quite committing. Drops fall from leaves above, but whether they are condensation or the remnants of the last rainstorm is impossible to determine. Everything is damp. Not wet. *Damp*. The kind of damp that gets into cloth and stays, that makes leather soften and wood swell, that puts a film of moisture on every surface so that the basalt underfoot is slick and the bark of trees is slippery and the broad leaves of understory plants are always shining faintly, as if oiled.

The darkness and the warmth together produce a strange disorientation. Without light, without temperature differential, the body loses its sense of vertical. People who have spent time in the Narrows report drifting, a sensation of horizontal suspension, as if the forest floor were merely one surface in a cube of darkness and any wall could equally serve as ground.

## The Vegetation

Lush is not the word. *Compulsive* is closer.

Everything here is growing as if growth itself were the only imperative left by whatever god made the place. The trees are thick-trunked and smooth-barked, a dark reddish brown that appears black in the lightless interior. Their roots run across the surface of the basalt in flat, branching networks — there isn't enough soil to bury them — and where two roots cross, they have already begun to graft together, fusing into thicker composite structures that in turn send up new shoots, so that what appears to be a grove of separate trees may in fact be a single organism with a hundred trunks.

The understory is a vertical thicket of ferns, broad-leafed herbs, and climbing vines that are so densely packed you cannot push through them without cutting. The vines are the worst — they grow in spirals around tree trunks, around each other, around the fern stems, knotting the entire understory into a single tensile web. Where a vine finds a gap, it fills it within days. Where a branch falls, the gap is closed before the leaves have finished dying. The rate of growth is visible if you watch. Not metaphorical. You can sit in one place and see the tendrils extend, see the fern fronds uncurl, see the moss creep across stone in real time, as if the forest is breathing inward, inhaling every open space.

There are flowers, though you will rarely see them. They bloom in the upper canopy where a fraction of light still penetrates, and their petals fall to the forest floor in slow, spiraling descents that you hear but cannot see — a faint papery sound against the background roar. The petals are large, white, waxy, and they accumulate on the ground in drifts that smell of something between sweetness and fermentation. Underfoot they are slippery and they rot quickly, adding to the thin soil, adding to the warmth of decomposition.

Fungi are everywhere. Not just the bioluminescent patches on stone, but threadlike white molds that coat fallen wood within hours, shelf fungi that bracket every dead trunk, and a strange gelatinous black fungus that grows in the crotches of living trees and pulses — visibly, rhythmically — as if it were breathing. No one knows what it is breathing *for*.

## The Fauna

This is the center of the Narrows. This is what the sound is *about*.

The biomass here is not large. It is *comprehensive*. Everything that can be filled is filled. The air column from ground to canopy is occupied at every level by something living, something moving, something calling.

**Insects first**, because they are the foundation and because they are the loudest. The Narrows hosts a species of cicada-analog — broad-bodied, dark-shelled, with membrane wings that catch the faint fungal light — that produces a sustained drone at a frequency you feel in your teeth. They call in shifts. When one population tires, another takes over, so the drone never breaks, never pauses, never modulates. It is a constant. Under it, a second species produces a clicking that runs in irregular bursts, and a third makes a sound like wet stone striking wet stone. The cumulative effect is not background noise. It is a physical presence, a wall of vibration that makes conversation impossible at distances greater than an arm's length and that you can feel pressing against your eardrums.

The *calling* — the layered, screaming, choral sound that cuts through the insect drone — comes from the vertebrates. Frogs first: dozens of species, occupying every pool, every puddle, every depression that holds water for more than an hour. They call in frequencies from deep grunting thumps to high, clear piping notes, and they call *constantly*, day and night, because in the permanent darkness of the Narrows there is no distinction between the two. The largest are fist-sized, dark green, with inflated throat sacs that glow faintly with the same bioluminescence as the fungi. When they call in unison — and they do, in waves that move across the terrain like weather — the sound is physical, a concussion that you feel in your lungs.

Above the frogs, birds. Or things that occupy the ecological niche of birds. They are dark-feathered, large-eyed, and they navigate by echolocation, producing sharp clicks that ricochet through the understory. They feed on the insects, on the frogs, on each other. Their calls are the most variable: some scream, some croon, some produce a descending warble that sounds almost musical, almost like grief. At any given moment, six or seven species are calling from different heights in the canopy, and their sounds overlap and interfere, producing beats and difference tones that emerge from the chaos and vanish again.

And on the ground — or near it — the creatures that are harder to classify.

There are lizards, or things like lizards, dark-scaled, low-slung, with eyes that reflect the fungal light in pairs like distant candles. They are everywhere underfoot, darting across the stone, climbing the root networks, hunting the frogs and the insects. They are silent, and their silence is conspicuous against the roar — a moving *absence* of sound, as if they carve a hole in the noise as they pass.

There are larger things too. You hear them rather than see them: heavy bodies pushing through the understory, breaking vines, dislodging stones. The vegetation crashes and then reforms behind them, so quickly that whatever passed through seems not to have existed. You find their tracks in the thin soil — broad, three-toed, deeply pressed — but you do not see what made them. The Narrows is not a place where seeing is the primary sense. The large things here have evolved for darkness: low to the ground, wide-bodied, moving by sound and smell and the vibration of the substrate. You are not prey to them. Probably. But you cannot be certain, and the uncertainty is part of the place.

And through the water, when the pools form, things move that you see only as ripples, as displaced reflections of the fungal light. Serpentine, perhaps. Or legged, walking on the bottoms of the pools. The water is too dark with tannins and ash to see through, and the pools form and vanish so quickly that whatever lives in them has adapted to a world of temporary oceans, burrowing into the mud when the water sinks, emerging when it returns.

## The Rules of the World

What governs the Narrows is *rate*.

Everything here happens fast. The basalt is fresh, the soil is thin, the light is almost absent, and yet the biological engine runs at a pitch that seems to violate the constraints. Growth is fast. Decomposition is faster. A tree falls and within a week it is skeletonized, softened, colonized, and half-converted into new soil. The fungi do this. The insects do this. The warm, damp air does this. Nothing persists. Nothing is given time to be stable.

The turbulence is not just biological. The terrain itself shifts. The basalt terraces are still settling, and in the deeper ravines you can hear stone grinding against stone, a low moan that comes from below, that has nothing to do with the living roar above. Small fissures open and close. Pools drain suddenly as a crack propagates through the stone beneath them, and the water vanishes in minutes, and the creatures that lived in it burrow or die or transform — some species here appear to have a dormant phase triggered by desiccation, a dried husk that revives when the water returns, that may wait weeks or months in the mud.

The canopy, too, is unstable. Trees fall regularly — undermined by the shallow soil, undercut by water, or simply overwhelmed by the weight of vines and epiphytes that have colonized their trunks. When a tree falls, it takes its neighbors with it, and the gap in the canopy is catastrophic: a sudden shaft of dim, cloud-filtered light descends to the forest floor for the first time, and in that light, the understory explodes. Seeds that have lain dormant in the thin soil germinate in hours. Ferns uncurl. Vines redirect their growth toward the opening. The race to fill the gap is violent, competitive, and nearly instantaneous. Within days, the light is gone again, and the new growth has been absorbed into the darkness.

This is the dynamic of the Narrows: creation and destruction so tightly coupled that they become the same process. Nothing is established. Everything is *establishing*. The landscape is a continuous present tense, a place where equilibrium is not a state but a direction, and the direction is always *more*.

## The Feeling of Being There

You do not visit the Narrows. You endure it.

The sound does not let up. Your ears ring. Your jaw aches from the vibration. You begin to hear things in the noise that may or may not be there — patterns, voices, your own name called in a frequency below audibility. The darkness does not let up either. Your pupils are fully dilated and remain so, and after enough hours you begin to hallucinate — faint lights that aren't there, shapes in the blackness, the sense of movement in your peripheral vision that vanishes when you turn. The fungal light is not enough to navigate by. It is enough to *haunt* by.

The warmth is the worst part for some. The air is body-temperature, body-humidity, and after a while you lose the boundary between yourself and the environment. You are not *in* the forest. You are *of* it. The dampness on your skin is the same dampness on every surface. Your breath is indistinguishable from the air. You could be dead and the forest would not notice, would simply colonize you where you fell, would convert you to soil before anyone found you, and the sound — the vast, layered, screaming, droning, clicking, weeping sound — would not pause for even a second to mark your passing.

The Narrows does not care about you. The Narrows is too busy *becoming*.
