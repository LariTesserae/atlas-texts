# The Cornice Works

You smell it before you see it: iron, wet heat, and something vegetal underneath, like tea steeping in a kettle left too long. The sound arrives next — a low, pulsing thrum that you feel in your sternum before your ears make sense of it. Not loud like a shout. Loud like a heart the size of a house, beating once every four seconds, patient and enormous.

The path ends at a natural gate — two pillars of raw stone, sheared clean as if cut by a mason who valued speed over beauty. Beyond them, the world drops away and then doesn't. You're on a shelf, a wide ledge cut into the face of a mountain that is still being born. The rock here is dark, almost black, veined with white quartz that hasn't yet dulled. The edges are sharp. Everything is sharp. The stone under your boots hasn't had time to round. It crunches with a sound like breaking glass where recent spalling has scattered fresh shards across the ground. This mountain lifted sometime in the last few centuries — geologically, that's nothing — and the rock still seems startled to be in the air, exposed, catching light it was never meant to see.

The shelf itself is perhaps forty meters wide and twice that long, walled on three sides by cliff faces that rise another fifty meters and then continue upward out of sight. The fourth side is the drop. You don't go to the fourth side. The wind comes from the fourth side, rising warm from below, carrying moisture that condenses the instant it hits the cooler stone of the shelf. Every surface gleams. Every surface weeps. Thin films of water crawl down the cliff faces in branching patterns that look, from a distance, like the veins of some enormous leaf, and up close, like nothing at all — just water following rock, finding the paths of least resistance, patient enough to carve given another ten thousand years but not yet in a hurry.

This is where someone built.

---

The structure sits against the eastern wall, built into a recess in the stone the way a barnacle builds into a hull. It's concrete and bolted steel, maybe three stories, hard to tell because the upper portion has been claimed so thoroughly by vegetation that you can't tell where building ends and forest begins. A pipe — a real pipe, sixty centimeters across, iron, riveted in the old style — emerges from the cliff face above the structure and enters the roof. From somewhere inside comes the thrumming. The building breathes with it. The walls shudder in a rhythm that has become, over however long this place has been running, structural. If the engine stopped, something might collapse. Or maybe not. It's been running so long that no one knows.

The concrete is green. Not painted green — colonized green. A carpet of moss so dense and uniform it looks like velvet, dark as forest shadow, soaking wet, yielding when you press it. Water drips from the pipe where condensation has gathered along its length and now falls in a steady percussion onto the structure's flat roof, which is itself a garden — a raised bed of ferns and grasses and something that might be bamboo but probably isn't, something jointed and tall and trembling in the updraft, its stems clicking against each other softly, a sound like tiny bones. The roof drains are choked with root systems. The water doesn't care. It finds new paths.

Inside — if you push the door, which is rusted but not locked, because whoever last worked here didn't bother with locks, or maybe locks were beside the point — the air is different. Thicker. Warmer. The thrumming is louder here, physical, a vibration that makes your teeth ache and your vision shimmer slightly, as if the air itself is being rhythmically compressed. The interior is a single large room. A machine sits in its center, and *sits* is the right word — it has the settled, patient weight of something that has been in exactly this position for longer than most things are anything. It's a pump. Or a compressor. Or something between the two. Iron and steel, bolted to a concrete pad, pipes entering from above and exiting below, into the rock, into the mountain, down toward whatever heat source warms the water and powers the updraft. There are gauges, most of them fogged or cracked, one still showing a needle that twitches with each pulse of the machine. No one has read that gauge in a long time. The machine doesn't care.

It's loud enough that you stop trying to think in words. The sound fills the space the way water fills a glass — completely, leaving no room. You feel it in your jaw, your collarbones, the soles of your feet. After a few minutes, you stop hearing it as sound and start hearing it as texture, the ambient condition of this place, the way some people hear their own pulse in a silent room.

---

Outside again, the vegetation closes in from every direction, and the smallness of this place becomes its defining quality. You are in a room. A room made of cliff and shelf and garden and machine, walled by stone, floored by stone, open only to the sky and the drop. The lushness is almost aggressive — every centimeter of soil, and there isn't much soil, just the thin accumulation of organic matter in the cracks of a landscape that hasn't existed long enough to make dirt, is occupied. Ferns uncoil from fissures. Moss covers stone. That jointed grass-thing grows in dense stands wherever water pools, which is everywhere, because the water table here isn't a table — it's a skin. You can dig with your hand and find water within a centimeter of the surface, warm, slightly mineral, faintly sulfurous. The rock is so new and so fractured that water moves through it like blood through capillaries, surfacing everywhere, pooling in the flat places, running in the angled ones.

The vegetation layers itself. Moss on stone. Fern over moss. The jointed grass over fern. Above, clinging to the cliff faces where condensation runs heaviest, are broader-leaved plants — something epiphytic, something that should be growing in a lowland jungle and has no business at this elevation but has found, in the warmth and the wet and the shelter of these walls, a pocket that feels like home. Their roots are exposed, gripping the bare rock with a tenacity that seems almost desperate, almost conscious. They don't know this stone hasn't always been here. They don't know this warmth is new. They grow as if this is where they've always been, and maybe, in their way, they're right — the mountain was always here, in the ground, in the heat, and now it's simply risen to meet them.

The layering creates a saturation of presence that makes the space feel smaller than it is. Everything is touching everything else. Moss touches stone touches fern root touches grass stem touches air so dense with moisture that it feels like another organism, another layer, the thickest one. You are inside the vegetation the way you'd be inside a body. The boundaries between things are nominal. Water doesn't belong to anything; it passes through everything.

---

There is almost nothing alive here that moves. No birdsong — the elevation and the walls create a natural aviary, but it's an aviary no bird has entered, or none has stayed. You see insects, sometimes, small ones, dark-winged, moving in the warm air near the building's exhaust vents where the heat concentrates. They don't venture far. There's something here that isn't hostile to life but isn't quite right for it either — the mineral content of the water, maybe, or the vibration of the machine, or the simple fact that this ecosystem is days old in evolutionary terms and hasn't had time to invite anything with a spine. A few of the broader-leaved cliff plants have flowers, waxy and pale, and they're visited by the insects, and that's the whole fauna. One small system. Self-contained. Not lonely, exactly, but not yet crowded.

The artifacts of human presence are sparse and unassuming. A rusted track along the ground, narrow-gauge, leading from the building toward the drop-edge of the shelf where it terminates at a loading area that has no rail or barrier — just the end of the track and then air. Presumably something was brought here and something was taken away. A few corroded barrels, their contents long since leaked or evaporated, now serving as planters for ferns that have found the accumulated soil in their concave bottoms. A shelf of rock near the building's entrance holds a enamel mug, cracked, dark-stained, still there because no one has had reason to move it. It fills with rain and empties and fills again. There is a patience to this place that makes abandonment feel like a misnomer. Nothing was abandoned. The machine runs. The ferns grow. The water moves. The people simply aren't here anymore, and the place doesn't seem to miss them.

---

The light is the light of high altitude filtered through moisture — bright but soft, scattered by the constant film of water in the air, arriving everywhere at once with no hard shadows. It gives the stone a glow that makes it look wet even where it isn't, which is nowhere. The cliff faces above seem to lean inward, and the sky visible between them is a narrow, brilliant strip, impossibly blue, a slot of heaven framed by dark rock and green growth. Weather here is not variable so much as slow. Clouds form and dissolve on timescales that feel geological. The condensation increases toward evening, the dripping intensifies, the moss darkens, the pools deepen by millimeters. By morning it has receded. This is the full range of change: wet, slightly less wet. The machine pulses. The water falls. The ferns uncurl by fractions of degrees. Something is being pumped from somewhere to somewhere else, and it has been happening for long enough that the vegetation has oriented itself around the rhythm, the water flow, the heat — has become not a response to the machine but an extension of it, another system running on the same energy, the same patience, the same indifference to purpose.

You realize, eventually, that you can't tell whether this place is a machine with a garden or a garden with a machine. The distinction has been dissolved by time and water and the slow insistence of living things finding purchase in a landscape that didn't exist, and now does, and is filling itself in, centimeter by centimeter, pulse by pulse, with whatever will grow.
