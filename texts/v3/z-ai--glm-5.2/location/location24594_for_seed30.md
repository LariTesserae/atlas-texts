# The Nursery at Vekh-7

## First Approach

You come up through a seam in the rock that shouldn't be there — one moment the ridge is solid, a warm curtain of stone, and the next your boot finds a gap the width of a shoulder, and the gap breathes. Warm air pushes up from below, carrying a smell like fresh calcium and wet copper. You descend sideways, scuttling, and the sky above you narrows to a strip of pale amber before it disappears entirely.

The place you enter is not large. If you stood at the center and called out, your voice would reach every wall in under a second. But the walls are the first thing that make you stop walking.

They are wet. Not damp — *wet* — in the way that a living membrane is wet, a sheen of fluid migrating slowly down the stone faces, pooling in the seams between strata, beading along mineral veins that catch the dim light and throw it back in colors the rock shouldn't contain. The water doesn't fall. It *emerges*. You can watch it happen: a patch of stone six meters up goes dark, saturates, and a trickle begins, as though the rock itself is weeping. Ten minutes later the trickle stops. Another wall section, somewhere else in the chamber, begins.

There are perhaps twenty surfaces like this, each cycling on its own timing. The result is a place that is always wet somewhere, always dry somewhere, always in the process of becoming the other thing. The floor is grooved — shallow channels carved into the bedrock in a pattern that looks organic but isn't quite. They carry the water to a low point at the chamber's southern edge where it collects into a pool no larger than a bathtub, dark and still and faintly luminescent, the color of old tea.

The light here is not light from any source you can find. It is simply present, as though the air itself holds a dim amber glow at the threshold of perception — enough to see by, barely, not enough to cast shadows with any confidence. You sometimes think you see your shadow move a half-second after you do.

## The Structures

Three structures occupy the chamber, and none of them were built by hands.

The first is a lattice — a three-dimensional framework of what appears to be a dark mineral growth, perhaps two meters tall, positioned near the center of the space. It is not a sculpture. It is not a shelter. It resembles a portion of a scaffold, as though someone began constructing the skeleton of a building and then stopped after erecting only one corner. The beams are organic in cross-section — not square or round but slightly irregular, like bone that has been stress-loaded into shape. They connect at nodes that pulse with a faint internal rhythm, too slow to be a heartbeat, too regular to be geological. The lattice is warm to the touch. Not body-warm. Machine-warm. The warmth of a processor under load.

The second structure is a half-sphere of the same dark material, partially embedded in the chamber's eastern wall. It looks like something that was pushed *through* the stone from the other side and left protruding. Its surface is covered in a pattern of extremely fine etchings — nanoscale, you suspect, though in the dim light they look like the whorls of a fingerprint. Water runs down its curvature and collects at its base in a dark ring.

The third is barely a structure at all. A single post, waist-high, standing in the shallow pool at the chamber's south end. It hums. The water around it moves in slow, concentric ripples that never quite reach the pool's edge.

These are not ruins. Ruins imply that something was completed and then fell apart. These are *beginnings*. Something is building here, and it has not gotten very far, and it is in no hurry.

## The Sound

The noise is the thing you notice third, after the wet and the dimness, and it becomes the thing you cannot stop noticing.

It is not one sound. It is a *weave* of sounds — a layered fabric that shifts constantly but never fully breaks apart. The dominant thread is a low, resonant hum that seems to come from the bedrock itself, a frequency you feel in your sternum before you hear it with your ears. Beneath that, a crackling — not the sound of fire but something drier, faster, like static discharge through mineral substrate, happening in bursts of three or four at irregular intervals. Above the hum, a chittering. The chittering is the loudest element and the one that moves — it travels across the chamber walls, echoing off the wet stone, and you realize after several minutes that it is coming from many small sources, and those sources are *moving*.

## The Fauna

They emerge from the channels in the floor.

They are not insects. They are not crustaceans. They are something that has no clear terrestrial analog, though if you held a description at gunpoint you might say: imagine a centipede, but the segments are translucent, and inside each segment is a small, dense sphere of fluid that glows with the same amber light as the air. They are perhaps the length of your hand, perhaps a little longer. They move fast, their many legs finding purchase on wet stone with an adhesion that is clearly chemical rather than mechanical — you can see the faint residue of their tracks, a thin film that evaporates within minutes.

There are hundreds of them. They pour from the channels and disperse across the floor, climbing the walls, navigating the wet stone with an ease that suggests the water is not an obstacle but a *medium* — they move faster on the wet surfaces, slower on the dry ones. They converge on the structures. They cover the lattice in a crawling layer, and where they gather at the nodes, the pulsing rhythm *changes* — speeds up, then slows, then speeds again. They are doing something to it. Or with it.

The chittering is them. Each individual produces a sound like a fingernail drawn rapidly across fine sandpaper, and the aggregate of hundreds of these sounds is the texture that fills the chamber, bouncing off every surface, returning altered. The acoustics of the space are strange — the wet walls absorb some frequencies and amplify others, so the sound seems to *swim* around you, approaching from one direction and arriving from another.

There are other animals here, too. Smaller. You see them only as motion at the edge of vision — pale, soft-bodied things the size of rice grains that live in the water film on the walls, grazing on something invisible. And in the pool, something larger moves, dark shapes that keep to the bottom, surfacing only to create a single ripple and then descending. You count at least three.

A single organism hangs from the ceiling near the lattice — a flat, ribbon-like creature, translucent, perhaps a meter long, anchored at one end to the stone above and dangling the rest of its body in a slow, constant undulation. It does not chitter. It does not glow. It simply moves, as though breathing, and the motion is hypnotic in the dimness.

You realize, standing here, that the ecosystem is simple but *complete*. There are grazers and predators and something that fills the role of decomposer. The energy source is not the sun — there is no sun here. It is the structures. The lattice, the half-sphere, the humming post. They produce heat. They produce faint electromagnetic fields. They produce, in some way the biology of this place has learned to harvest, *order* — the kind of thermodynamic novelty that life can eat.

This is a young ecosystem. An embryonic one. The geology is young — the stone is smooth in the wrong places, unweathered, as though it was laid down recently and with intention. The water system is still calibrating — the weeping walls cycle in a pattern that hasn't yet stabilized. The structures are unfinished, their functions still unfolding. And the animals that live here have the look of creatures that have arrived recently, adapted quickly, and are still in the process of *becoming* whatever they are going to be.

## What This Place Is

You sit on a dry ledge — one of the few — and watch.

A droplet forms on the wall to your left. It swells, trembles, falls. It hits a channel and flows to the pool. A centipede-thing intercepts the flow, drinks, moves on. The lattice pulses. The half-sphere's etchings seem, for a moment, to shift — though this could be the light playing tricks, and you are not yet sure the light here is not a trick itself.

This is a nursery. Not in the biological sense, or not only. Something — a process, an intelligence, a system — is *growing* this chamber. Growing the stone. Growing the water system. Growing the structures. Growing the ecosystem that will, presumably, maintain and shape and defend the space once the initial work is done. The technology here is not beyond modern in the way a starship is beyond a cart. It is beyond modern in the way a forest is beyond a factory — a method of making that operates on principles of *cultivation* rather than *construction*. Stone does not behave like this. Water does not behave like this. These things are being *directed*, and the direction is so subtle and so patient that it looks like geology.

The centipedes climb the lattice. The lattice hums. The hum changes key, and three of the creatures drop from the structure to the floor, where they lie still for several seconds before righting themselves and climbing again. It looks like communication. It looks like *work*.

You think: someone planted a seed here, in the warm dark, in the high rock, and walked away. And the seed is growing. And the things that grow from it are not the seed's children but its *tools*, and the tools are also alive, and the distinction between tool and child is one this technology does not recognize.

The sound wraps around you — the hum, the crackle, the chittering, the drip. It is loud enough that you stop trying to parse it and simply let it be a texture, a weather, a thing the air does here. Your own breathing becomes part of it. The amber light does not change, does not shift, does not move. It is the same dim glow it was when you entered, and it will be the same when you leave. Time in this place is not marked by the movement of light. It is marked by the cycling of water and the pulsing of nodes and the slow, patient growth of stone.

You stay for an hour. You could stay for a year and the place would barely notice. It is too young, too busy *becoming*, to pay attention to something as temporary as a visitor.

When you leave, you climb back through the seam. The warm air pushes you out. The ridge is solid behind you, and you turn to look, and the gap is gone. Or it was never there. Or it will be there again when the chamber is finished, whenever that is — in a century, or a millennium, or tomorrow. The stone doesn't tell you. The stone is still deciding what it wants to be.
