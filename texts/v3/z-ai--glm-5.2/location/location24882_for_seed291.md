# Khelm-Vydal

The ground is not finished yet.

That is the first thing you understand here, though it takes time to understand that you understand it. You stand on something that is still deciding what it is. The basalt is warm in places — not volcano-warm, but the warmth of a body still forming its bones. Cracks run through the streets, and from some of them, thin mineral vapor rises, condenses immediately in the cold air, and falls back as fine droplets that collect in the gutters. The gutters are cast iron, newly laid, and they weep rust from their joints. This is a town built on material that has not yet agreed to be ground.

Khelm-Vydal sits in a broad, shallow basin — you would call it a valley, but the valley is also not finished. The slopes around it are soft, unconsolidated, still compacting. You can see the layers in the hillsides like cross-sections of a body: pale clay, dark aggregate, veins of something translucent and mineral that no one here has named yet. The erosion score is low not because there is nothing to erode but because the erosion has not started in earnest. The land is too new. It is pre-formation. The rivers that will eventually carve this basin have not arrived — there is only seepage, condensation, the damp that collects on every surface and does not quite become rain.

---

The cold is a specific cold. Not the deep cold of altitude or latitude, but the cold of wet stone and saturated air held in a low place with no wind. It sits on your skin. It is the temperature of a cellar, or a mine shaft, or a room where something is being kept dormant. Your breath does not fog dramatically — the air is already so damp that your exhalation just joins it, dissolves, becomes indistinguishable from the general moisture. You feel cold in your joints first, then in the small muscles of your hands. People here work with their hands. The cold suits that, in its way. It keeps the metal stable.

Intermittent moisture. That is the pattern. The sky does not rain — it condenses. Twice, sometimes three times a day, the temperature drops just enough that the air reaches its dew point, and then every surface in Khelm-Vydal becomes wet at once. The rooftops. The pipe arrays. The broad, waxy leaves of the vegetation that has colonized every gap in the construction. The fur of the animals. The walls of the buildings, which are brick and poured aggregate and in some districts simply compacted earth held in place by timber frames. The condensation events last an hour, perhaps two. The town glistens. The gutters run. Then it stops, and the cold air dries everything slowly, imperfectly, and the town sits in its damp residue until the next event.

There are pockets of actual water — not rivers, but accumulations. A few shallow lakes have formed in the lowest points of the basin, where the still-compacting ground has subsided enough to create depressions. The water is clear and mineral and very cold. Things live in it. You can see them if you crouch at the edge: small, many-legged, pale. They are not insects. They are not crustaceans. They are the kind of thing that appears when a place is new and the niches are empty and whatever can fill them, does.

---

The vegetation is the town's most persistent companion and its most serious competitor.

It is lush in a way that feels — you want to say tropical, but that is wrong. The temperature is wrong for that word. This is cold lushness. The plants here are broad-leaved and dark green, almost black in the low light, and they grow with a density that feels aggressive, territorial. Ferns, mostly, but not ferns you would recognize. Their fronds are thick, almost succulent, and they are coated in a fine mucilage that catches the condensation and holds it. Mosses cover every horizontal surface that is not actively being walked on. Vines — thick, ropy, dark — climb the factory walls and drape across the pipe arrays and sometimes pull fixtures loose with their weight.

The speed of the growth is the thing that unsettles you. This land is new. The soil is thin, raw, barely weathered from its parent mineral. And yet the vegetation has arrived as if it was waiting. As if it knew. In the month that the surveyors have been here, the ferns have advanced eleven meters into the northern district. The workers cut them back in the morning, and by evening the cut stems have begun to re-sprout. It is not that the plants are fast. It is that they are inevitable. They grow the way condensation forms — not by effort, but by the simple physics of a system reaching its equilibrium.

There are trees, but they are strange. Short, gnarled, with bark that looks more like hide than like wood. They seem to grow from cracks in the bedrock rather than from soil, and their root systems are exposed, gripping the raw stone like hands. They are not tall — the cold and the thin soil and the newness of everything keeps them low — but they are dense. You cannot walk between them without pushing through their branches. They are packed so closely that their canopies overlap, and under them, the light is green and filtered and wet.

The density of the vegetation matches the density of everything else here. That is what you notice. Nothing in Khelm-Vydal has space around it. The buildings are pressed together. The streets are narrow. The ferns grow between the walls. The moss grows on the ferns. The vines grow on the moss. The small pale creatures from the lakes have begun to appear in the gutters, in the puddles, in the condensation that collects in the crooks of the pipe arrays. Everything is occupying the same space. Everything is touching.

---

The town itself is industrial in the way that a body is muscular — it is built for work, and the work is the only reason it exists.

The central district is a compound of factories — not the old brick kind, though there is brick, but the kind with steel frames and corrugated paneling and pipe arrays running along the exterior walls like vascular systems. The pipes carry steam, mostly. The town runs on steam. There are boilers in every building, and the boilers are fed by a fuel that is extracted from the ground here — something that comes out of the cracks in the basalt, a thick, dark hydrocarbon that the workers call "marrow" because of where it comes from. The marrow is pumped through pipes to the boilers, and the boilers produce steam, and the steam drives the engines, and the engines run the machines, and the machines do — well. Several things.

The main industry is extraction. They are pulling the marrow from the ground. They are also pulling minerals — the translucent veins in the hillsides, which turn out to be something the surveyors call "glassite" for lack of a better term, a crystalline silicate that has commercial value somewhere far from here. The processing plants cluster along the eastern edge of the basin, where the ground slopes slightly toward the largest of the shallow lakes. The waste water from the processing runs into the lake, and the lake does not seem to mind. The pale creatures in the lake do not seem to mind. They are more numerous in the water near the outflow pipes, in fact. They seem to thrive on it.

The second industry is construction. The town is still being built. Half the buildings are half-built. Scaffolding rises against the fogged sky like the bones of things not yet alive. The workers move through the scaffolding with the unhurried competence of people who have done this many times in many places, and the buildings rise, and the ferns climb them as they rise, and the workers cut the ferns back, and the buildings rise more, and the ferns climb more. It is a competition that no one has declared and no one will win.

The construction is layered. You can see it in the cross-sections of the buildings that are still being raised: the ground floor is poured aggregate, the second floor is brick on a steel frame, the third floor is timber and paneling, the fourth floor is — they are still deciding. The materials change as you go up, as if the town is trying different architectures the way a person tries different coats, and has not yet settled on one. This is because the town is new. Not just young. New. It has no tradition. It has no precedent. It is being invented in real time by people who are working with materials they pulled from the ground this morning.

---

The streets are narrow and mostly quiet.

This is the paradox of Khelm-Vydal: the turbulence is high, the activity is constant, the town is changing every hour — new buildings rising, new pipes being laid, new cracks opening in the ground, new ferns growing, new creatures appearing in the lakes — and yet the sound level is low. The machines are steam-driven, and steam-driven machines are quieter than you would expect. The pistons move with a muffled urgency. The boilers hiss but do not roar. The extraction pumps make a sound like a slow heartbeat — a low, regular pulse that you feel in your feet more than you hear with your ears.

The workers do not talk much. They communicate with hand signals, with nods, with the occasional short phrase in a language that you do not recognize. It is not that they are unfriendly. It is that the work requires a kind of concentration that does not coexist well with speech. The town moves with anthill energy — hundreds of individual actions, each precise and purposeful, composing a pattern that no single individual is directing. You stand on a street corner and watch: a worker carries a pipe across the street and fits it into a junction. Another worker applies a sealant. A third checks the alignment with a tool that looks like a level but has more bubbles than it should. None of them speak. The pipe is installed. They move on. The condensation begins. They pause, wait it out under the awnings. The condensation ends. They resume.

The sounds you do hear are: the pulse of the pumps. The hiss of the boilers. The drip of condensation from surfaces after a moisture event. The soft, wet sound of fern fronds being cut. The click of the pale creatures in the gutters — a sound like someone tapping a fingernail on glass, very quiet, repetitive, and for no reason that anyone has explained. The wind does not reach the basin, so there is no wind sound. The trees do not creak because they are too low and too dense to move. The quiet is not silence — it is the sound of many small things happening in a place where the air itself absorbs noise, thick with moisture and mineral, and dampens it.

Sometimes at night, the ground makes a sound. It is not a sound from below — it is a sound from within the ground itself, a low, tectonic groan, like the earth is stretching. The workers say the land is settling. The surveyors say the land is forming. You think they are both right and both wrong. The land is not settling or forming. It is growing. The basin is getting larger, very slowly, and the slopes are getting higher, and the floor is getting lower, and the town is building itself up to keep pace, and the vegetation is climbing to keep pace, and the creatures are multiplying to keep pace, and everything here is in a race with a geology that is not finished and may not finish for a very long time.

---

The fauna is abundant and strange in the way that everything here is strange — not dramatic, not alien, but slightly wrong, like a word spelled correctly that still looks unfamiliar.

There are the pale lake creatures. There are also things that live in the moss — small, flat, segmented, moving with a deliberate gait that is not crawling and not walking but something between. They are detritivores. They eat the dead fern fronds. They are important. Without them, the cut fronds would pile up and the streets would be impassable. The workers tolerate them. Sometimes you see a worker pause and let one cross a beam before continuing.

There are things that live in the trees. You hear them more than you see them — a dry, papery rustling in the canopy, not loud. They are small. They have — you think — wings, or something like wings, membranous and dark, and they eat the mucilage on the fern fronds. They do not fly so much as drop and glide. You see them in the evenings, when the condensation has passed and the air is at its driest, dropping from tree to tree, silent except for the rustle of their membranes against leaves.

And there is something larger. You have not seen it. The workers do not talk about it. But in the northern district, where the ferns have advanced furthest and the buildings are still half-built and the construction has not caught up to the vegetation, there are tracks in the soft ground. Broad, deep, with a pattern that is not a hoof and not a paw but something that has a geometry you find difficult to parse. The tracks go from the tree line to the edge of the northern lake and back. They are fresh every morning. Whatever makes them travels at night and travels alone and is large enough that the workers do not go to the northern district after dark, though they will not tell you why. They just stop working at dusk and go to the central district, where the lights are on and the boilers are running and the buildings are close together and there is no space between the walls for anything to stand.

---

The light is normal, but it is normal in a strange place, which makes it strange.

The sky is overcast most of the time — not dark, not dramatic, just a uniform pale gray that comes from the condensation layer above the basin. The light is flat and even and comes from everywhere and nowhere. It does not cast hard shadows. It does not pick out details. It illuminates everything at the same level, which means that in a place this dense — this packed with buildings and ferns and pipes and creatures and scaffolding and moss — the eye has trouble separating foreground from background. Everything is lit. Everything is close. Everything is the same color: the dark green of the vegetation, the rust brown of the iron, the pale gray of the aggregate, the mineral white of the glassite, the translucent dark of the marrow. The town is a tapestry and the light is the loom.

At night, the light changes. The factory district runs electric lights — the town has a generator, a steam turbine, a set of bulbs strung along the eaves of the processing plants. The light is yellow and warm and it makes the moisture in the air visible. At night, you see the air. You see the droplets. You see that you have been breathing water all day and did not know it.

---

The rules of this world are not yet fixed. That is the thing you keep coming back to. In an older place, the rules are settled — the geology is done, the ecology is established, the buildings are in their final form, the industry has its rhythm. Here, nothing is settled. The ground is still forming. The vegetation is still arriving. The fauna is still filling niches. The town is still being built. The industry is still being calibrated. The creatures in the gutters are still becoming whatever they will become.

This is why the turbulence feels different here than it would in a place that was falling apart. Khelm-Vydal is not in chaos. It is in formation. The difference is this: in a place that is falling apart, the turbulence is entropy. Here, the turbulence is emergence. The energy is not destructive — it is generative. Things are being made. The ground is being made. The soil is being made. The streets are being made. The ecosystem is being made. The town is being made. And the making is happening all at once, in parallel, without coordination, in a space that is too small for all of it, and the result is a density that is almost organic — everything pressed against everything, everything touching everything, everything growing into everything.

You stand in the street and you feel it. The pump pulses. The ground groans. A fern uncurls against a wall. A creature clicks in a gutter. A worker fits a pipe. The condensation begins. Everything becomes wet. Everything becomes quiet. Everything waits.

Then it ends, and everything begins again.
