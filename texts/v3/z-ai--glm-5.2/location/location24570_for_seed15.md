# The Sunken Loom-Chamber

The walls are dressed stone, fitted by hand — each block chiseled and set with the kind of careless precision that comes from people who learned to build by building, not by theory. No mortar. The seams gape in places, and through them, water seeps in thin continuous curtains, catching the light and scattering it into green-gold ribbons across the interior. The chamber is small — no larger than a weaver's workshop, which may be exactly what it was before the water came. Ceiling height of perhaps two and a half meters, now reduced by the floodline to a breath of trapped air that smells of warm mineral and wet limestone and something faintly vegetal, like pond stone heated by sun.

Everything is submerged up to the neck. The floor — uneven flagging, laid over bedrock — disappears into clear water that is not still. It moves. There is a current entering from a low arched passage on the eastern wall, a deliberate opening cut through the stone, and it pushes through the chamber with enough force to stir the silt and keep the water from going stagnant. The outflow is less obvious: a channel cut into the base of the western wall, barely wider than a hand, where the water leaves with a sound — a persistent, throaty gurgling, pitched low, that forms the bass note of this place's soundtrack. Above that, the water entering from the east hisses and pops against the lintel of its archway, a higher, more variable sound, and between these two voices the chamber is never quiet. Sound doubles and redoubles off the close stone walls. It is loud here. Conversation would require shouting, and there is no one to talk to.

The light is the most surprising thing. The chamber sits below grade — built into a depression or carved into a hillside and then partially buried — and yet it is brilliant. Sunlight enters through the eastern archway and through narrow vertical slits in the southern wall that may have been drainage channels or may have been intentional, designed to let light in even when the building was dry. Now they are submerged slots, and the light coming through them is refracted into thick, almost solid beams that drift and shift as the surface water above moves. The whole interior is webbed with moving light. It climbs the walls. It turns the submerged stone a warm amber in places, a cool pale green in others. The water itself is warm — body-warm, bathwater-warm — and faintly turbid with fine particulate that the light catches and holds, so the beams are visible as physical things, columns of brightness standing in the water like architecture within architecture.

There are no animals here. Not even insects. The water is too new — this flooding is recent, perhaps only weeks old, perhaps a single season. Whatever dry-land organisms once occupied this space have either left or drowned and been swept away. The vegetation is minimal: a few strands of something filamentous and pale green have begun to colonize the submerged edges of the floor, clinging to the stone joints where silt has accumulated. They are not rooted in any established way — they drift in the current, anchored only by the fact that they have tangled around the rough edges of the masonry. They look fragile and provisional, the first tentative marks of an ecosystem that has not yet decided whether this place belongs to the land or the water. In the warm light, they glow. They are beautiful in the way that very simple things are beautiful when everything around them is stone.

The chamber itself shows the signs of recent and rapid construction — not the flooding, but the building. Tool marks are visible everywhere on the stone. Chisel scars, some clean and some ragged. The corners are sharp, unweathered. The archway over the eastern passage has been cut with a confidence that doesn't quite match the unevenness of the floor, as though two different builders worked at different speeds. The blocks of the wall vary in size — some as large as a man's torso, some no bigger than a loaf of bread — and the smaller ones have been wedged into gaps almost as afterthoughts. There is a bench carved into the northern wall, now submerged, its surface smooth from use but its edges still crisp. Someone sat here. Someone worked here, not long ago, with hand tools and sweat and the kind of engineering that comes from tradition rather than calculation.

And now it is underwater, and the water is doing what water does to new stone: finding the cracks, widening the seams, dissolving the lighter minerals. The process has barely begun. The stone is still hard, still sharp, still itself. But in the warm current, with the light driving chemical reactions faster than they would occur in cold water, the erosion has started. If you run your hand along a seam — and the water is shallow enough at the edges that you can reach down and touch the floor — you can feel a slight softness, a faint sliminess where the stone is beginning to yield. In a year, perhaps, the seams will have widened. In ten, the shape of the chamber will have changed. But right now, it is still the thing that was built, still recognizable, still holding its form against the water with the stubbornness of fresh masonry.

The overall effect is intimate and strange, though not strange in the sense of alien — strange in the sense of *transitional*. This is a place caught between states. It was dry and is now wet. It was built by hands and is now being reshaped by water. It was inhabited and is now empty. The light makes it feel almost festive, almost like a celebration — the moving beams, the warm glow, the way the green filaments catch the brightness like tinsel — but the sound reminds you that something is happening here, that the water is going somewhere, that the chamber is not sealed, that the process is ongoing. The gurgling outflow. The hissing inflow. The constant low vibration of water moving through stone.

It smells like a quarry that has been left out in the rain. It feels like a room that someone just left and will not come back to.
