# The Weeping Plateau of the Great Loam

## First Approach

You come up through cloud. Not cloud as metaphor — you climb through actual cloud, thick and wet and cold, clinging to your skin in beads that never quite roll off because the air is already too saturated to accept them. The plateau reveals itself in pieces. A shelf of dark, fibrous ground extending into murk. The light is the color of old pewter, filtered through a permanent overcast that sits maybe two hundred meters above the surface, and everything below it is tinted the same flat gray-green.

The ground is not ground. Step and you feel it — a slight give, a fibrous resistance, like walking across a vast bale of raw cotton that has been wet for a thousand years. This is the Loam, and it is not soil. It is root. The entire plateau — kilometers across, stretching past sight in every direction — is a single continuous root mass, a rhizome system so old and so dense that it has become the terrain itself. There is no mineral substrate beneath it anymore, or if there is, it is so far down that the concept of "beneath" has become academic. The root *is* the ground. The ground *is* the root.

---

## The Nature of the Loam

It is ancient. Not old the way a forest is old — old the way stone is old, the way river courses are old. This is a system that reached its final form so long ago that the word "final" has worn smooth. The root mass has grown, died, composted, and regrown on top of itself so many times that the lower layers have become a kind of peat-diamond, compressed vegetable matter harder than coal, darker than ink. You are walking on the roof of a biological monument. The surface layer — the living layer — is maybe a meter deep. Below that: geological time expressed in plant matter.

But the surface is not still.

The Loam breathes. Not metaphorically — you can hear it. A low, rhythmic compression, a creaking exhalation that rises from under your feet every eight or nine seconds. The root mass is hydraulically active, pumping water through its vascular tissues in pulses that you can feel through your boots if you stand still. The sound is everywhere, omnidirectional, a subsonic heartbeat overlaid with the hiss and gurgle of fluid moving through channels the width of your arm. It is loud. Not deafening, but loud the way a factory floor is loud — a constant mechanical-biological drone that you cannot dismiss because it is *under you* and *around you* and it does not stop.

Water surfaces everywhere. Not in pools or streams — in weeps. The root mass is so saturated that water is extruded from its surface at every seam and joint, running in thin sheets across the dark fibrous ground, collecting in shallow channels that the root itself has formed by growing raised ridges to contain the flow. The water is cold, clear, faintly sweet. It tastes like cold tea. It is, in fact, cold tea — tannins leached from centuries of decomposing root matter have turned the entire hydrological system into a weak infusion. You are standing in a landscape that is perpetually steeping itself.

---

## The Architecture of Plants

There are no buildings. No structures, no artifacts, no marks of human or humanoid habitation. Nothing has been built here. And yet the landscape is *industrial*.

The plants — and they are everywhere, they are the substrate, they are the space itself — operate at a scale and with a mechanical regularity that resembles nothing so much as a factory floor. Massive vascular columns, the trunks of something that is not quite a tree but has the structural role of one, rise at regular intervals from the Loam. They are spaced twenty, thirty meters apart, and they are not random. They are in rows. Not planted rows — self-organized rows, the root mass having grown its own support structure in a pattern that optimizes fluid flow and structural load. The columns are maybe fifteen meters tall, thick as silos, their surfaces ridged and furrowed like corrugated metal. They are dark, almost black, wet, and they weep. Every column has a constant trickle of condensation running down its exterior, feeding back into the Loam. They are cold to the touch. The whole place is cold — two, three degrees above freezing, maybe — and the columns are the coldest things in it. They act as condensers. The warm wet air rising from the Loam hits the cold column surfaces and deposits water, which runs down. They are, functionally, a dehumidification system. A cooling system. An industrial process carried out by living wood.

Between the columns, the space is open. This is what strikes you most immediately: the *emptiness*. Despite the totality of the plant substrate, despite the columns, despite the root-ground and the water and the breathing Loam, the plateau is sparse. There is space. Vast, dim, open space between the vertical members, filled with thin mist and the gray light and the sound of pumping. You can see for hundreds of meters. The columns recede into fog in ordered ranks, and between them there is almost nothing — just the dark wet ground, the shallow water, the low ridges of root that channel the flow. It feels like a cathedral whose nave is measured in kilometers. It feels like the inside of an engine whose components are the size of towers.

---

## The Canopy and the Light

Above, the columns branch. Not at a specific height — they branch where they branch, and the branching is different for each one, but the cumulative effect is a canopy. A lattice of interlocking boughs and cross-branches and something that is not quite leaf and not quite membrane — flat, dark, translucent panels that spread between branches like webbing between fingers. These panels are the photosynthetic surface, and they are the ceiling of this world. They filter the already-dim light into something even dimmer, something green-gray and directionless. You can see the panels above you as dark shapes against the slightly lighter gray of the overcast. Water drips from them constantly — not rain, just condensation that has formed on the undersides and collected at the lowest points and falls. The drops are cold. They hit the Loam with small flat sounds that merge into a continuous patter, a white-noise layer beneath the deeper rhythmic pumping.

The light is dim enough that your eyes never fully adjust. There is always more detail at the edge of vision than at the center, as if the light itself is shy, as if it prefers to be perceived peripherally. Colors are muted. The whole visible spectrum is compressed into a band between dark green and charcoal. Occasionally, a panel in the canopy shifts — a membrane reorients, tilts, and for a moment a shaft of brighter gray light cuts through and hits the Loam, and where it hits, the ground steams. A faint, thin steam. Then the panel shifts back and the light is gone.

## The Fauna

There is a great deal of life here, and none of it is familiar.

The first thing you notice is the movement in the columns. The vascular trunks are not solid — they are hollow, or partially hollow, and their surfaces are perforated with oval openings the size of your fist. From these openings, things emerge. They look, at first, like wet leather ribbons — dark, flexible, glistening, maybe a meter long and five centimeters wide. They uncurl from the openings and hang in the air, swaying. Then they move. They are not ribbons. They are flatworms. Enormous flatworms, or something that has converged on the flatworm body plan, and they are the primary fauna of the plateau.

They are everywhere. Every column hosts dozens. The Loam itself is honeycombed with their burrows — you can see the openings, dark circles the width of your hand, scattered across the ground surface. They move through the root mass like earthworms through soil, and they move through the air in the column interiors, and they dangle from the column openings and filter-feed on the mist. When one drops from an opening, it falls with a wet slap onto the Loam, lies still for a moment, and then undulates away into one of the ground burrows. This happens constantly. The sound of wet slaps joins the sound of dripping water and the sound of the breathing Loam and the sound of hydraulic pumping. It is loud. It is all loud. Everything here is performing some process and the processes are not quiet.

There are other animals. Smaller things — segmented, many-legged, pale — scuttle across the ridges of the root-channels, grazing on algal films that grow on the wet surfaces. They move in lines, single-file, hundreds of them, following chemical trails laid down by the root mass itself. The root is directing them. You realize this slowly and then all at once: the root mass is *farming* its fauna. The grazing lines lead to decomposition sites — places where a column has died and fallen and is being broken down — and the pale segmented things are the decomposition crew, summoned by chemical signal, deployed to a work site. It is an industrial process. It is a biological process. There is no distinction here.

Larger things move in the mist between the columns. You see them as shapes — dark, low to the ground, moving with a fluid, rolling gait. They are maybe the size of large dogs, but their body plan is wrong. Too many legs, or too few. A body that seems to reform as it moves, as if it is not fully committed to a single shape. They do not approach. They pass between the columns at distances of fifty, a hundred meters, and they are gone. Their sounds — a low clicking, a series of wet exhalations — carry in the damp air and are difficult to localize. You hear them from every direction. You cannot tell if they are near or far.

## The Turbulence

The plateau is not stable. This is the thing that takes longest to understand, because the *materials* are stable — the root mass is ancient, the columns are ancient, the system has been here for longer than anything has a right to be. But the system is not still. It is in a state of constant, chaotic reorganization.

The columns die. Not slowly, not over decades — they die in hours. You witness one. A column thirty meters from you begins to change color, its dark surface lightening to gray, then to white. The sound of fluid pumping inside it changes pitch — rises, becomes a whine, then a shriek of vapor escaping through cracks in the vascular tissue. The column collapses. It does not fall like a tree; it *deflates*, folding inward, its walls crumpling as the internal pressure that held it rigid vents through a thousand fissures. It hits the Loam with a deep, wet thud that you feel in your feet, and then the decomposition begins immediately. The pale segmented things arrive within minutes, summoned by the chemical death-signal, and they begin to dismantle the fallen column with mechanical efficiency.

But even as one column dies, another is forming. You see it: a point on the Loam where the ground is bulging, rising, the root mass pushing upward in a slow-motion eruption that will, over the course of days or weeks, become a new column. The system is replacing itself continuously. The rows shift. The spacing changes. The pattern of the columns is not fixed — it is a dynamic equilibrium, a grid that breathes and reorganizes and never settles.

And the reorganization is not orderly. Columns die in clusters. New ones erupt in lines that cut across the existing grid. The water channels shift, flood, dry, reform. The ridges that direct flow melt and rebuild in new configurations. The mist thickens and thins. The canopy panels open and close in patterns that seem to follow no rule you can identify. The whole plateau is in constant motion at every scale — the flatworms moving, the grazers moving, the columns dying and rising, the water flowing and pooling and flowing again, the root mass pulsing and pumping and reconfiguring its own architecture — and all of it is loud, all of it is wet, all of it is cold, and all of it has been doing this since before the concept of *before* had meaning.

## The Breakdown

There is something wrong with the light.

You notice it near the center of the plateau, where the columns are oldest and the Loam is thickest and the canopy is most dense. The light here does not behave correctly. It pools in places where there is no source. It casts shadows in directions that do not correspond to any object. The mist, in certain areas, takes on colors that are not in the spectrum — not a trick of the eye, not a fatigue phenomenon, but an actual color that you have no name for because your visual system was not designed to process it. You look at it and your eyes water and you feel a pressure behind your forehead, and you look away.

The root mass in these areas behaves differently. The pumping rhythm breaks down — the steady eight-second pulse becomes irregular, then arrhythmic, then seems to run backward. You feel suction where there should be pressure. The ground moves under your feet in ways that are not mechanical, not hydraulic — it moves as if it is *uncertain*, as if the root mass in this zone has forgotten what it is, and is trying to remember, and the trying is visible in the way the surface ripples and folds and unfolds.

The flatworms avoid these zones. You notice this because you notice their absence. In the areas where the light is wrong, there are no worms, no grazers, no shapes in the mist. The column openings are sealed — not grown over, but sealed, as if the columns themselves have closed their mouths. The silence in these zones is not silence — the pumping continues, the dripping continues — but it is *absence* of the biological noise, the wet slaps and the clicking and the grazing sounds, and that absence is louder than anything.

You walk to the edge of one of these zones. The ground at the boundary is visibly different — the root fibers here are not dark but white, bleached, and they are growing in spirals rather than in the ordered grain of the normal Loam. The spirals tighten as you approach the center of the zone. At the very center, there is a point where the root mass has grown into a structure so dense and so regular that it looks machined — a perfect spiral, three meters across, flat to the ground, white as bone, and in its center, a hole. Not a burrow. An absence. A place where the root mass has chosen not to grow, where the Loam has decided that *something* should not be here, and the something is itself.

You look into the hole. You see the plateau. From above. As if you are floating. You see the columns, the grid, the mist, the moving shapes of fauna, the white zones scattered across the landscape like holes in fabric. You see yourself, small, standing at the edge of a white spiral, looking down. The light around the hole is the color that has no name. Your eyes water. You step back.

---

## What This Place Is

It is a system that has been running so long that it has begun to malfunction in ways that are not mechanical. The root mass is not senile — it is *overcomplex*. It has been growing, layering, composting, regrowing for so long that its internal signaling has begun to produce artifacts. The white zones are where the system's own chemical signals have become so dense and so convoluted that they have begun to refer to things that do not exist — to generate instructions for structures that have no function, to grow in patterns that serve no purpose, to create *absences* where there should be *presences*. The system is hallucinating. A plant mass old enough to have geology is dreaming, and the dreams are real, and they are holes in the world.

The plateau does not care. The columns rise and fall. The flatworms feed. The water flows. The Loam breathes. The system has been here forever and will be here forever and the dreams are just another process, another sound in the loudness, another motion in the turbulence. The white zones will grow or they will shrink or they will stay the same. The root mass will incorporate them or it will not. The industrial rhythm of the biological factory will continue, because it has no reason to stop, because there is nothing here that was built and nothing here that can be unbuilt, because this is not a machine that someone made — it is a machine that *grew*, and growing machines do not have an off state.

You leave the way you came. Down through the cloud. The sound of the plateau follows you for longer than it should — the pumping, the dripping, the wet slaps, the low clicking of the shapes in the mist. Then the cloud closes over you, and you are in white silence, and the plateau is above you, breathing, growing, dreaming its holes into the fabric of a world it has forgotten it is part of.
