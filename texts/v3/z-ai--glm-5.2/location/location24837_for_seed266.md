# The Sevvedi Tableland

It begins as a feeling before it becomes a place. A lift. A thinning. The air carries less and less until you realize you've been climbing for days without a single switchback — the whole world simply *tilted*, and you rose with it.

---

## The Stone That Goes Up Forever

The Sevvedi Tableland sits at an elevation that shouldn't, by most reckoning, support warmth — but warmth is what you get. The stone itself holds heat. Not volcanic, not geothermal in any familiar sense. The mineral substrate — a dense, rust-colored aggregate the locals call *ferrembric* or simply *red-iron ground* — absorbs solar radiation through the day and re-radiates it through the night with a patience that borders on biological. Walk barefoot at midnight and the rock is still hand-warm. By afternoon it's almost too hot to touch. This is the engine of everything.

The tableland is vast. Not large — *vast*, in the way a continent is vast, in the way weather systems are vast. You can walk for four days in a straight line and the horizon will not shift meaningfully. The sky is pale, almost white at the edges, shading to a washed-out blue directly overhead. Normal daylight. Normal sun. But the air is thin enough that shadows have a hardness to them, a knife-edge quality, and distances deceive. What looks like a ridge an hour away may be a half-day's walk.

There is no water. Not hidden, not brackish, not deep in aquifers — simply none. The chemistry of this place has no use for it. Rain does not fall. Clouds pass overhead sometimes, thin and papery, but they shed nothing. The ground is too warm and too mineral-dense to hold moisture even if any arrived. Salt crusts form in certain basins — not sodium chloride but something closer to a sulfite compound, pale greenish-white, crystalline, crunching underfoot like broken glass. These salt-fields stretch for kilometers in places, flat and reflective, blinding at midday, and they are the closest thing the tableland has to a feature.

---

## What Grows Here (Nothing)

Nothing grows. There is no soil — only stone and mineral sand and the occasional vein of something glassy and dark that the old miners called *nightstone*. There are no roots because there is nothing to root into. No seeds blow across the flats because nothing has evolved to seed here. If you look for green, for the ordinary vegetable color of life, you will go blind searching. The tableland is a palette of rust, bone-white, grey, and the pale green of the salt-fields, and that is all.

And yet — this is not a dead place.

## The Settled City

They call it *Old Karravat*, though it was never founded so much as it accumulated. The settlement is urban — organized, structured, with infrastructure layered through and over the stone in a way that suggests centuries of deliberate construction. But it is sparse. Buildings stand with long stretches of bare rock between them. Streets, where they exist, are carved directly into the tableland surface — grooves cut three meters deep into the ferrembric, their walls smooth with age, their floors worn concave by generations of foot traffic. Walking them feels like walking through channels. You are below the surface. The buildings rise on either side, their ground floors level with the street-grooves, their upper stories reaching above the tableland's natural grade.

The architecture is industrial. Not decorative, not monumental — *functional*. Load-bearing walls of fitted stone, reinforced with iron strapping that has oxidized to a warm black-brown. Windows are narrow and high, placed for ventilation rather than view. Many buildings incorporate the raw rock face of the tableland as one or more walls — structures half-carved, half-built, blurring the line between architecture and excavation. The oldest quarters have been occupied so long that the stone walls have developed a patina, a darkened sheen from oils and contact and the slow accumulation of something almost like age itself.

There are factories. This is important. Old Karravat is not a city of merchants or temples or governance — it is a city of *production*. Long, low buildings with roof-vents and chimney stacks, their interiors fitted with machinery that runs on heat differential — the tableland's own thermal gradient, captured and converted. Engines turn slowly, patiently, powered by the difference between sun-warmed surface stone and the cooler air above. The mechanisms are iron and brass and something that might be ceramic, and they have been running so long that their moving parts have polished themselves to mirrors. The factories process the mineral substrate. They extract. They refine. What they produce is not immediately clear — ingots of something, powders, pressed shapes — but the production is constant, slow, and has been for longer than anyone remembers.

The city stretches for perhaps forty kilometers along a natural rise in the tableland, but its density is low. You can stand on a rooftop and see five or six other buildings, separated by stone and empty air, each one a small fortress of purpose. The spaces between are not neglected — they are *intentional*. Airflow. Heat management. Room for the infrastructure that runs beneath everything: pipework carrying gases, not liquids, between buildings. The whole settlement breathes through its channels and vents like a slow lung.

## What Lives Here (Much)

The fauna are the surprise. The tableland, barren of water and vegetation, is *abundant* with animal life — or something that occupies the same ecological role.

They are not animals in the familiar sense. They have no need for water. They do not eat plants. They are *lithovores* — mineral metabolizers — and they derive energy from the ferrembric substrate itself, processing iron and sulfur compounds through a gut chemistry that is warm-blooded, active, and strangely vital. They breathe. They move. They reproduce. They are alive by any functional definition, though their biochemistry bears no resemblance to anything aqueous.

The most visible are the *skree* — long-legged, low-bodied creatures that move across the open tableland in loose herds of thirty or forty. Their bodies are dark, almost black, with a segmented carapace that flexes as they walk. They are the size of large deer but built like something between a spider and a greyhound, their legs thin and multi-jointed, their heads flat and wide with sensory plates instead of eyes. They graze on the surface of the stone — literally graze, their mandibles scraping the ferrembric down layer by layer, processing it through a series of internal chambers. The sound they make is a low, continuous *scritch-scritch*, audible at distance, and on calm days the collective sound of a herd feeding is like the world itself being filed down.

They are not alone. There are smaller creatures — *dust-lice*, the miners call them — that live in the channels and grooves of the city, processing the stone dust that accumulates. There are *vault-things*, heavy and slow, that live in the deeper excavations where the tableland's stone is oldest, their bodies pale and almost translucent, their metabolism so slow that a single meal of mineral might last them months. There are fliers — things with rigid, mineralized wings that ride the thermal columns above the warm stone, circling for hours without a visible wingbeat, their bodies no larger than a hand but their wingspans wide as a person's arms. They hunt the skree's young, dropping from height with a precision that suggests sight, or something better than sight.

The ecology is closed and self-sustaining. The lithovores eat the stone. The predators eat the lithovores. When things die, their mineral bodies decay back into the substrate, and the cycle continues. No input from outside. No waste. A system that has been in balance for so long that it has worn grooves into its own possibility space.

The abundance is real but it is *sparse* — the tableland is vast, and the life on it is spread thin. You might walk for an hour and see nothing, then come over a rise and find a herd of skree stretching across the horizon, dark bodies against rust-red stone, moving with a slow, deliberate grace that makes them look like they are part of the landscape. Which they are.

## The Quiet

The tableland is mostly quiet. This is one of its defining characteristics. The air is thin and does not carry sound well. The stone absorbs noise. The sparse construction means there is little machinery running in any given place. What sound exists is specific, localized, and gone as soon as you move away from its source.

In the city, you hear the slow tick of thermal engines, the creak of cooling metal, the distant *scritch* of skree feeding on the outskirts. In the open tableland, you hear wind — a low, steady pressure that is not quite a sound, more like the absence of silence made audible. You hear your own footsteps, the crunch of mineral sand, the occasional crack of a salt-crust shifting in the heat. Sometimes, from very far away, you hear a sound that might be a flier calling, or might be stone fracturing under thermal stress, or might be nothing at all. You learn to stop assigning sources to sounds. The place makes its own noises for its own reasons.

---

## The Slow Time

Nothing happens quickly on the Sevvedi Tableland. The thermal engines run on cycles measured in hours. The stone radiates heat on a daily rhythm so consistent it could be a clock. The skree move across the landscape at a pace that suggests they have never been hurried and cannot conceive of being so. The city's production continues at a rate that has not visibly changed in generations — the same ingots, the same powders, the same slow accumulation and slow removal.

Even the geology is slow. The tableland has existed in something close to its current form for an immense span of time. The erosion is *settled* — not active, not dramatic, but in a state of long equilibrium. The stone wears down grain by grain. The channels deepen by fractions of a millimeter per year. The salt-fields grow and shrink on cycles that outlast civilizations. The landscape has found its shape and is keeping it, the way a river keeps its course — not through inertia but through a kind of geological contentment.

If you stay long enough — weeks, months — you begin to feel it. The tableland's pace enters you. Your heartbeat slows. Your sense of urgency dissolves. The horizon is always there. The stone is always warm. The skree are always feeding, somewhere. The factories are always running, somewhere. The world is not going anywhere, and neither, it begins to seem, are you.

---

## The Strangeness

There is one more thing. In certain places — and the places are not fixed, they move — the tableland's stone develops a quality that the locals call *memory-light*. The ferrembric substrate, under specific conditions of heat and mineral composition and something else no one has identified, begins to emit a faint luminescence from within. Not a glow on the surface but a radiance from *inside the stone*, as if the rock were remembering light that had passed through it days or years before and was only now releasing it. The color is pale amber. The effect is subtle — you might not notice it in daylight, but at dusk, standing on the open tableland, you can see patches of ground that seem to shine faintly from below, like buried lanterns, and they shift and fade and reappear elsewhere over the course of hours.

The skree avoid these patches. The fliers circle them. The people of Old Karravat have built around them, never over them, and when a memory-light zone migrates beneath a building, the building is eventually abandoned. Not out of fear — out of something closer to respect. The stone is remembering something. They do not know what. They do not want to interrupt it.

This is the strangeness of the Sevvedi Tableland: not that it is alien, but that it is *deep*. The processes here are old and patient and not fully knowable. The city is old. The ecology is old. The stone is oldest of all, and it holds its secrets the way it holds its heat — slowly, warmly, for a very long time.

---

You stand on the edge of a carved street in Old Karravat. The stone beneath your feet is warm. A thermal engine ticks somewhere to your left. Far out on the tableland, a herd of skree moves like a dark stain across the red ground, and the sky above them is white and pale and very high. The air is thin. The silence is thick. You have been here for three days and you have not spoken to anyone and no one has spoken to you and it does not feel like isolation. It feels like the place is paying attention to something you cannot hear, and is willing to include you in the listening, if you stay.
