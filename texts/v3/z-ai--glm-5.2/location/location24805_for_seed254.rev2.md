# The Rootworks

You descend into it before you see it. A stair cut into living wood — each step a single thick root, braided downward, worn smooth by decades of passage — though there is no one left to pass. The walls on either side are moss and wet bark, close enough to brush your shoulders. The air changes as you go deeper. It warms. It thickens. By the twentieth step you can taste it: green and mineral, the exhalation of ten thousand leaves breathing in the dark.

Then the stair opens, and the Rootworks begins.

## The Chamber

The space is cathedral-sized and not cathedral-shaped. No vaulted ceiling, no symmetry. The ceiling is a mat of interlocked branches pressed tight as thatch, sixty feet overhead in the center, sloping down to meet the walls on all sides. It weeps. Condensation forms on every surface here because the rock below is warm and the air above is cool, and the plants have made a living boundary between the two. Water beads on the underside of the ceiling, gathers in the crooks of branches, falls in slow fat drops that scatter on the floor below in a steady, arrhythmic patter. It is not raining. It is always not-raining like this. The place has its own weather, maintained by the heat of decomposition and the cool of the stone, a permanent state of post-rain.

The floor is not stone. It is root. A woven mat of woody roots, some as thick as your arm, some as fine as thread, layered and intergrown until the surface is nearly flat — a packed-earth firmness that gives slightly underfoot, springy, alive. Water runs along the channels between the roots in shallow films, pooling in low spots, draining into cracks you cannot see. The whole floor breathes. Not metaphorically. It rises a fraction of an inch and settles, rises and settles, on a cycle so slow you only notice it when you stand still and feel the ground shifting beneath your weight like the deck of a moored boat.

There is no horizon here. There are walls — the cavern walls, rough limestone dark with moisture — but the plants have claimed them. Ivy, liverwort, films of algae, thick crusts of moss, all growing over the rock in layers so deep the stone is more suggestion than substance. You could dig your hand into the wall and pull out a fistful of green before you touched anything geological. The roots of the ceiling-plants plunge into the walls and come out again, threading through the organic felt, anchoring, drinking. The walls weep openly. Water runs down them in sheets so thin they're silent until they reach the floor, where each sheet breaks into individual trickles that find the root-channels and join the slow circulation.

The chamber is roughly oval, maybe two hundred feet at its longest. You could cross it in a few minutes if the floor were clear. The floor is not clear.

## The Structures

There are buildings here. That is the only word for them. They are built. They have walls, roofs, doorways. They are organized in rows along what must be streets, though the streets are narrow and the rows are irregular, curving to follow the contours of the root-floor. There are thirty or forty structures, packed so tightly that their walls share substrates — the living membrane of one building's back wall is the same tissue as its neighbor's front wall, a single sheet of bark and cambium serving both. No space between them. No alleyways. The settlement is a continuous fabric with rooms carved into it.

The walls are made of wood, but not planks. They are grown, not built. Each wall is a lattice of roots grown in a deliberate pattern, woven like wicker but at a scale that makes the individual strands as thick as your wrist. The gaps in the lattice are filled with a secondary growth — a corky material, spongy, pale, sealed with a resinous sap that has hardened to a waxy finish. The walls are a foot thick. They are warm to the touch. You can feel the sap moving inside them, a slow pulse, nothing like a heartbeat — more like the pressure you feel in a garden hose, a low constant hydraulic push.

The roofs are different. They are leaf-mats, flat sheets of broad leaves layered like shingles, each leaf a foot or more across, thick and waxy, edges curled to channel water. The leaves overlap in a pattern that directs rain — or the constant drip from the ceiling — toward gutters cut into the root-walls, which carry the water down and away into the floor. The roofs are alive. The leaves slowly turn, not like flowers tracking the sun (there is no sun) but like a slow adjusting, a continuous reorientation toward the dim light sources that glow from various points around the chamber. The movement is imperceptible in the moment. Come back in an hour and the leaves have shifted. Come back in a day and the pattern is visibly different.

The doorways are open. No doors. The openings are arched, formed by two roots that have grown toward each other and fused, creating a smooth living frame. Each doorway is slightly different — wider, narrower, taller, lower — because each was grown, not measured. The floors inside the buildings are the same woven root as the streets, continuous. There is no threshold. You walk from street to interior without a step, the ground unchanged beneath your feet. The only distinction is that the interior is drier. The roof-leaf systems over each structure catch and channel the water more efficiently than the open street, so inside the buildings the floor is merely damp rather than actively wet.

## The Machines

In the center of the chamber, the largest structure occupies the space the way a factory occupies a town block. It is three times the size of the dwellings around it, with walls twice as thick, and it is loud. A low, mechanical throb comes from inside it — not an engine in the combustion sense, but a pumping. A rhythmic hydraulic compression. Thump. Thump. Thump. The sound fills the chamber. It is the dominant sound, the baseline against which all other sounds register.

Inside this structure — you can walk in; there is no barrier — the floor drops two feet into a sunken chamber where the root-mat has grown into a basin. The basin is filled with water, warm and slightly murky, the color of weak tea. Tannins. The water is being processed. Thick roots plunge into the basin from above, each one a pipe carrying fluid from somewhere in the ceiling — the chamber's water, collected by the root-ceiling, filtered through layers of bark and cambium, and delivered here. The thump you hear is the pump: a massive muscular node in the floor of the basin, a swelling in the root-mat that contracts on a cycle of roughly two seconds, forcing water out through a network of secondary roots that radiate from the basin like spokes. Each spoke carries the water — cleaned, tannin-stained, mineral-rich — to the surrounding structures, to the dwellings, to the smaller machines that line the streets.

It is a water treatment plant. It is a heart. The distinction may not matter.

Around the chamber, along the narrow streets, smaller machines stand at intervals — chest-high cabinets of woven root, each one performing some function you can observe but not always parse. One vibrates faintly. You put your hand on it and feel something inside, a rapid trembling, like a loom. The front of the cabinet has a slot, and from the slot, a flat sheet of material is emerging, slowly, centimeter by centimeter. It is a fiber mat, pale and fibrous, like raw paper. It is being produced from the water and the dissolved nutrients the pump has sent through the root-network. The plant is manufacturing material. The mat accumulates in a folded stack below the slot, each sheet dry and pressed, ready for whatever purpose the system was designed to serve.

Another machine clicks. A slow, irregular percussion — a pod, swollen and taut, splitting open in stages, releasing a burst of spores into the air with each crack. The spores drift in the dim light, catch on surfaces, germinate. Where they land on the root-floor, they send down tiny filaments and begin to grow. This is how the floor repairs itself. This is how the walls thicken. The machines are not separate from the settlement. They are the settlement, performing its maintenance.

A third machine, larger than the others, stands against the cavern wall. It is a press. You can see the mechanism: two panels of dense wood, faced with a rubbery bark, mounted on a frame of roots that are still growing, still thickening. Between the panels, a mass of fibrous plant material is being compressed. The press operates on a cycle so slow it is nearly invisible — the panels close over the course of an hour, squeeze for another hour, open, and the compressed material is ejected as a dense board, dark and hard, streaked with resin. The board drops onto a stack. The stack has fifteen boards in it. The bottom boards have been there long enough to have moss growing on their edges. The system has been running for a long time. The output has nowhere to go. It accumulates.

## The Light

The light comes from the plants. Several species here are bioluminescent, though weakly. The walls glow a faint greenish-yellow where the moss is thickest. The root-walls of the structures have a faint amber luminescence in their sap, visible only in the darker interior rooms where the ceiling-drip doesn't reach. The strongest light comes from a single source: a cluster of large pod-like growths on the ceiling at the chamber's center, directly above the pump. Each pod is the size of a fist and glows a steady, cold blue-white. They are the lamps of this place. They light the factory floor. They cast shadows of the roof-leaf patterns across the streets. They make the water in the pump-basin gleam.

It is never bright. It is never dark. The light does not change. The pods do not dim or brighten on any cycle you can detect. They are always like this, have always been like this, a steady glow that is enough to see by and not enough to see well. Your eyes adjust and then stop adjusting. There is a limit to what is visible here, and the place sits at that limit.

## The Sound

The pump. Always the pump. Thump. Thump. Thump.

Under it: the drip from the ceiling, which is not a patter but a scattered, continuous, individual percussion — each drop distinct, each landing point different, the combined effect like rain heard from inside a dry house, a soft static.

The fiber machines add their own frequencies. The trembling one hums at a pitch you feel more than hear. The spore-clicker adds its irregular crack, every few seconds, a sharp report that echoes off the far wall and comes back flattened. The press, when it moves, groans — a low wooden sound, the complaint of living material under stress, a sound like a tree in wind but without the wind. It is slow and protracted, a groan that lasts twenty minutes, then silence, then another.

The water in the root-channels makes a sound like breathing. Not like a creature breathing. Like the sound of air drawn through water. A thin, continuous, liquid rush. You hear it most clearly when you stand near the floor, when you kneel and put your ear close to the root-mat. The whole floor is full of water moving. The whole floor is a plumbing system. The sound is the sound of a town's pipes, heard from inside the pipes.

There are no voices. No footsteps. No animal calls. No wind. The absence of these sounds is itself a sound — a negative space the other sounds fill. The place is loud with its own operation, and it is loud alone. It does not echo with the sounds of its makers because whatever its makers were, they are not here anymore, and the place has not noticed.

## The Atmosphere

Warm. Not hot. The temperature of a greenhouse on an overcast summer day, the warmth of biological process, of decomposition, of sap moving under pressure. The humidity is near total. Your skin is wet within minutes of entering. Not sweating — condensing. The air deposits water on every surface cooler than itself, and you are cooler than the air. Your clothes dampen. Your hair goes flat. Breathing is easy but heavy. The air is thick with oxygen — the plants produce more than they need — and sweet with the smell of cellulose, resin, and the faint iron tang of mineral water.

The smell is layered. The top note is green, fresh, like a snapped stem. Under that: earth, rich and dark, the smell of wet soil and healthy rot. Under that: something mineral, like a cave, like wet stone, like the groundwater that seeps through the limestone. The smell does not change. It is the smell of equilibrium, of a system that has found its balance and maintains it, cycling the same water, the same air, the same nutrients, through the same structures, indefinitely.

## The Ecology

There are no animals here. None. No insects on the leaves. No worms in the root-mat. No birds in the branch-ceiling. No mice in the walls. The niche that animals would fill — consumer, disperser, pollinator, scavenger — is filled by the plants themselves. The spore-pods disperse the organisms that would otherwise be carried by insects. The root-network distributes nutrients that would otherwise be processed by detritivores. The leaf-mats orient toward the bioluminescent pods, substituting internal light for external sun. The system is closed. The system is complete. The plants are the producers and the consumers, the builders and the products, the architects and the material. They are everything.

This is why there is no decay. Not because things don't die here — the leaf-mats shed continuously, the spore-pods split and fall, the fiber-mats accumulate — but because death is immediately absorbed. A fallen leaf lands on the root-floor and within a day the root-mat has grown over it, sending fine feeder-roots into its tissue, drawing out its minerals, incorporating its carbon. The floor is a compost and a stomach and a foundation, all at once. Nothing rots in the open. Nothing accumulates as waste. The waste-stream of one process is the input of another, connected by the root-channels that run beneath every surface, delivering water and dissolved compounds to the points where they are needed.

The plants are not one species. They are dozens. The ceiling-plants are different from the wall-plants, which are different from the root-floor, which are different from the machines, which are different from the bioluminescent pods. Each occupies a specific niche in the architecture, and the architecture would collapse without each one. The ceiling-plants hold the cavern's moisture. The wall-plants insulate the rock. The root-floor filters and transports. The machines process and produce. The pods light. It is an ecosystem designed as a city, or a city designed as an ecosystem. The distinction, again, may not matter.

## The Age

The press has been making boards for a long time. The stack beneath it has fifteen boards, and the bottom ones have moss on them, which means they have been there for years, which means no one has been here to collect them in years. The fiber-machines have stacks of their own, some with dozens of sheets, some with hundreds, depending on their rate and the time since last collection. The spore-pods crack and disperse, and the spores land and grow, and the growth is always in the same pattern — thickening existing walls, repairing worn floors, extending root-channels that are already there. The system is not expanding. It is not contracting. It is maintaining.

The stair you descended has steps worn smooth by feet that are no longer present. The root-stair has grown to accommodate this wear, thickening in the center where the passage was heaviest, thinning at the edges. It remembers its users. The doorway arches are shaped to a height and width that suggests human-scale passage. The streets are wide enough for a person to walk, narrow enough that two could not pass abreast. The dwellings have interiors sized for occupation — rooms you could stand in, sit in, sleep in.

But no one does. The dwellings are empty and have been empty long enough that the root-floor has begun to grow inward from the walls, narrowing the rooms by inches, reclaiming the interior space at a rate that would take decades to fully close a room. The system is patient. It does not demolish. It does not abandon. It simply grows, and the growth fills whatever space is available, and since no one is using the space, it fills inward.

The Rootworks is a place that was made by someone and is now run by no one, and the difference is invisible because the system was designed to run itself. The makers built it to last, and it has lasted, and it will last until something fundamental changes — the rock cooling, the water drying, the light failing — and none of those things are happening. The groundwater feeds the ceiling-roots. The ceiling-roots feed the pump. The pump feeds the streets. The streets feed the machines. The machines feed the floor. The floor feeds the walls. The walls hold the ceiling. The cycle is closed. The cycle is slow. The cycle is loud, and warm, and dim, and wet, and utterly indifferent to whether anyone is here to witness it.

You could stay. The air is breathable. The water is drinkable, if you don't mind the tannins. The temperature is comfortable. There are rooms. There is no danger here, because there is nothing here that would harm you — no predators, no pathogens, no traps. The plants don't recognize you as food or threat. You are simply present, a large warm object that condenses moisture on its surface and emits carbon dioxide, which the nearest leaves slowly turn toward. You are useful, in a small way. You are not needed.

The pump goes on. Thump. Thump. Thump.

The place goes on.
