# The Cooling Gallery

There is a room inside a machine, and the room is also a machine.

You reach it through a hatch in the upper works of something whose full shape you cannot determine — a structure that extends below you for what might be kilometers and above you into a ceiling lost to pale industrial light. The hatch is not marked. It opens onto a gallery perhaps forty meters long, fifteen wide, ten high. The proportions feel deliberate but not human. The walls are corrugated panels of an alloy that has no English name, pressed into shape by dies that left shallow scalloping along every surface, so that the light from the overhead strips catches in tiny parallel shadows, row after row after row, like corduroy, like the inside of a throat.

It is cold. Not the cold of weather. The cold of intent. Something here is being kept cold, or the cold is a byproduct of something being done so aggressively that heat cannot accumulate. The air tastes metallic and desiccated — your lips crack within minutes. There is no moisture. None. The humidity reads as effectively zero on any instrument you might carry. Your breath does not fog because there is not enough water vapor in your exhalation to condense against this air. It simply leaves you and vanishes into the dryness.

And the sound.

The sound is extraordinary. It is not a noise — it is a presence, a standing wall of vibration that fills the gallery from floor to ceiling. It has texture. There is a low component, below hearing, that you feel in your sternum and in the fluid of your eyes, a slow tidal compression that makes your vision swim slightly at its crests. Above that, a roar — not the roar of fire but the roar of forced air, of gas under pressure moving through channels just on the other side of the walls. And above that, a shriek, a high thin continuous note like teakettle harmonics scaled up to industrial register, drilling into the bones behind your ears. It does not fluctuate. It does not waver. It is the same now as it was a minute ago, an hour, presumably a year, presumably a century. The dynamic is zero. The sound has achieved a permanence that makes it almost architectural — it is as much a property of this space as the walls are. You cannot imagine this room being quiet. The silence would be more shocking than the noise.

The gallery is young. That is the strange thing to say about a manufactured space, but it is true — there is no wear on the floor panels, no oxidation on the exposed joints, no accumulation of dust in the corrugation valleys. The welds along the panel seams are bright and clean, the beads uniform, as if they were laid yesterday. Either this space was recently constructed, or it is maintained in a state of perpetual newness by something — some process that rejects entropy locally. The edges are sharp. Running your hand along a panel join, you would cut yourself. Nothing here has been rounded by time or contact.

And yet things live here.

Not much. Sparse, stubborn, inexplicable. Along the junction where the floor meets the left wall, in the narrow gap where two panels meet imperfectly and a seam of perhaps two millimeters admits something from the other side — a faint warmth, perhaps, or a trace of some other atmosphere — there is a ridge of growth. It is not moss. It is not lichen. It is a black filiform structure, branching like a tiny frozen river delta, each strand no thicker than a human hair, spreading across the bare metal surface in a pattern that follows the grain of the alloy. It does not move. It does not visibly grow during your visit. But it is alive, or was recently, because where the strands terminate at their outermost reaches, they curl slightly inward, the way growing tips do when they encounter unfavorable conditions — which here is everything. The whole colony is perhaps thirty centimeters long and four wide. It is the only sign of biology in the gallery. It is the only thing that is not made.

Well. There is also what might be a stain near the far end of the gallery, on the floor, where something organic may once have been and left a faint residue, a discoloration in the shape of nothing recognizable. But this might also be a property of the metal, a quirk of the manufacturing process. Hard to say. The sound makes it difficult to think about anything for very long. It sits on top of your thoughts like a weight.

The light is even, flat, institutional. Strips along the ceiling emit a spectrum close to daylight but not quite — there is a faint excess in the green, a slight undersaturation in the red, so that everything reads as marginally wrong, as though you are looking at the room through a thin film of something. The light does not flicker. Nothing in this room flickers. Everything has reached equilibrium and will remain there. The strips illuminate the gallery without emphasis, without shadow except the small corrugation shadows, without drama. You can see everything clearly. You wish, a little, that you could not.

At the far end of the gallery, opposite the hatch, there is a panel with a small window — thick, multi-layered glass or something like glass, set into the wall at chest height. Through it you can see the reason for the cold, the sound, the dryness. Beyond the window is a chamber of much larger dimensions, receding into fog — not water fog but a haze of some refrigerant or process gas, visibly flowing from left to right at a stately pace. In the haze, massive shapes: heat exchangers, coils of pipework the diameter of tree trunks, baffles and louvers and structures whose purpose is not obvious. Everything beyond the glass is frosted — but the frost is not water ice. It is a crystalline deposit, pale blue-white, that forms and reforms on the coils as the process gas moves past. It builds, it sloughs off in sheets, it builds again. This is the only motion visible anywhere. Even this motion is cyclical, not progressive. It will be doing this when you leave. It was doing this before anything like you existed.

The gallery seems to serve no purpose. It is not a workspace — there are no controls, no stations, no interfaces. It is not a passage — it dead-ends at the window. It might be an access corridor for maintenance, but the panels are seamless except for the one seam that admits the black growth, and there are no removable sections, no service ports, no tool marks. It might be a space that exists because the structure around it needed a gallery-shaped void for structural reasons — a stiffening rib, an expansion joint, a thermal break. You are standing inside an engineering decision. The room does not care that you are here.

You could stay. You could sit on the floor — it is too cold to sit on the floor for long, but you could — and listen to the sound, and watch the frost form and fall and form behind the glass, and eventually the black filaments on the wall might reach the end of their seam and stop, or might find a way through, or might not. Nothing would change. The room would be exactly this when you arrived and exactly this when you left and exactly this ten thousand years from now, the same sound, the same light, the same cold, the same small black life clinging to its seam.

It is a room inside a machine, and the machine does not know it is there, and the room does not know you are in it, and the sound continues.
