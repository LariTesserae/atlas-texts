# The Floraderm

There is no ground. That is the first wrongness. You stand on something, but it is not ground — it is the oldest skin of a living thing so large it has forgotten its own edges. Beneath you: ten meters, twenty, a hundred, who knows, of compressed cellulose and lignified tissue, layered like sedimentary rock but warm, faintly warm, the residual metabolic heat of something that breathes without lungs. The surface underfoot is not flat. It rolls. It has been rolled — pressed and planed by machines that moved across it decades or centuries ago, leaving shallow furrows that have since softened, filled in by new growth, re-pressed. You walk in the troughs between these old machine-tracks. The troughs are knee-deep. The walls of living material on either side are furred with a downy secondary growth, pale, almost white, and it moves — not from wind, because there is no wind, but because it is growing. You can watch it grow. Not fast. But visible, if you stand still. A slow tidal creep of filaments extending outward into the air, feeling for something, finding nothing, and continuing anyway.

Above you: light. Too much light. The sky — if it is a sky — is a uniform high-luminance field, sourceless, the color of milk mixed with weak tea, and it presses down with a weight that has nothing to do with atmosphere. There is little atmosphere. The air here is thin and dry and carries almost no moisture; what you breathe is mostly nitrogen, carbon dioxide, trace terpenes, and something slightly astringent that stings the inside of your nose the way ozone does. The light does not come from a sun. There is no sun. The sky itself is emissive — a manufactured photic field, orbital or embedded in a high canopy layer that has long since fused into a continuous ceiling, far above, too far to see as anything but that pale glowing wash. It is bright enough that you cannot look up for more than a few seconds without your eyes flooding. It is bright enough that shadows barely form. Everything is lit from all directions at once, and the effect is a flattening — the world loses its depth, its relief, and you navigate by texture underfoot more than by sight.

The landscape rolls on in every direction, low, almost featureless at the grand scale, a vast gentle topography of swells and hollows that extends beyond horizon. Continental. Perhaps planetary. You have walked for hours and the character of the place has not changed — the same pale furred surface, the same shallow machine-furrows, the same light. But the details shift. The secondary growth in the furrows changes color by region: here it is white, there a pale amber, further on a dusty rose that catches the light and makes the ground look scabbed or sunburned. The primary surface changes too — sometimes smooth and dense, like heartwood sanded to a polish; sometimes cracked into plates that click loosely when stepped on; sometimes fibrous and ropey, a tangle of root-like structures that have surfaced and hardened in place.

No water. This is the central strangeness and the central engineering. The entire system — the surface you walk on, the growth, the light-field above, whatever lies below — operates without water. The cells of this vast plant-thing use a solvent that is not water; it is something heavier, something with a higher boiling point and a lower vapor pressure, something that does not evaporate in this thin dry air. You can see it sometimes, when the surface is cracked: a dark, viscous fluid, the color of strong coffee, seeping along fracture lines, moving slowly, almost gelatinous, beading at the surface and rolling back under. It does not pool. It does not flow. It migrates. The air has no humidity to speak of. Your lips crack within the first hour. Your eyes burn. This is a place that does not want animal life, and animal life, by and large, does not come here.

And yet there are animals. Rarely. You have not seen them but you find evidence. In one furrow, a dense knot of the downy secondary growth has been clipped away in a clean crescent — something with a mouth has grazed here. The cut surface has oxidized to a dark brown, which means it happened days ago. In another place, a burrow: a hole the width of your fist punched straight down into the substrate, edges neat, walls slightly slicked with that dark solvent. You drop a fragment of surface material into it and count. You hear it hit something at four seconds. The depth is unknowable. Whatever made this burrow lives down in the compressed layers, in the architecture of old growth, in the dark.

The word *architecture* is correct. This place is built. Everything here is made. The surface is not a natural forest floor or a grassland — it is a constructed substrate, engineered, a living floor laid down over a living infrastructure. You reach a place where the furrows converge into a kind of corridor, wider than the others, and the surface changes: here the primary material is not rough or fibrous but finished, planed smooth, with a slight convex curve that channels the dark solvent along a shallow groove in its center. The groove feeds into a junction — a place where multiple channels meet at a circular depression, a meter across, where the solvent pools briefly before sinking down through a grating of hardened, calcified plant-tissue. The grating is deliberate. It is manufactured. Someone, or something, designed this drainage system. The circular depression is one of hundreds, perhaps thousands, spaced across the surface in a pattern too regular to be organic.

Following the corridor, the surface rises — not much, a meter, maybe two — and you find the first vertical structures. They are not buildings. They are not trees. They are something between: columns of living tissue, two meters tall, a meter thick, with a rough bark that is clearly woody but also clearly shaped. The tops are flat. Working surfaces. Some have grooves cut into them — slots, recesses, sockets. Some have the dark solvent flowing from apertures halfway up, running down channels cut into the bark and pooling at the base in shallow basins that drain into the substrate. These are machines. Or they are the ancestors of machines — engines that are also organisms, factories that grow from the floor and process something, some nutrient, some feedstock, from deep below. They are everywhere in this corridor, spaced evenly, and as you move deeper into this district their density increases until you are weaving between them, the columns pressing close, their surfaces damp with that dark fluid, the air thick with terpenes and the astringent bite of solvent-vapor.

And it is quiet. This is the thing that unsettles most. These are factories. This is an industrial landscape — there are processing stations, transport channels, drainage systems, feed lines, exhaust ports. But the loudest sound is the slow click of your footsteps on the hardened surface. The second loudest is your own breathing. The third — and you stand still for a long time to hear it — is a faint, rhythmic pulse, deep, felt in the soles of your feet more than heard with the ears. It comes from below. It comes from the whole substrate, the whole world, vibrating at a frequency so low it is almost geological. It is a heartbeat. Or it is a pump. Or, here, those are the same thing.

A fourth sound, rarer: a creak. When the columns shift. You watch one — a column ten meters ahead — and you see it move. Not fast. Not much. A slow torsion, a degree of rotation, and then a settling back. It is adjusting. It is processing, and the processing requires mechanical motion, and the motion is so slow, so damped, so buffered by the density of living tissue around it, that it makes almost no sound. An industry of silence. A factory floor that is also a forest floor that is also a single organism, and all of its moving parts are growing, and all of its growing parts are processing, and none of it speaks above a whisper.

Now the landscape breaks.

You round a cluster of columns and the surface stops. Not ends — stops. There is a seam in the substrate, a line where the finished surface meets raw, unprocessed tissue, and on the other side of the line the rules change. The light is different — or the light is the same but the space is different, because beyond the seam, the ground is not flat. It curves. It curves upward, and also inward, and the geometry is — you stand at the seam and you try to understand what you are seeing and your eyes slide off it the way they slide off a Möbius strip held at the wrong angle. The surface beyond the seam rises in a curve that should, by any sane physics, become a wall, then a ceiling, then the underside of the floor you are standing on. But it doesn't close. It continues, and as it continues it folds, and the furrows and the columns continue with it, and the light continues with it, and you are looking at a landscape that curves back on itself in a direction that should not exist. It is not a sphere. It is not a torus. It is something with more fold than that, something that turns through itself, and the eye cannot hold it, and the mind lets go of it, and you step back.

This is not the only seam. You find others. The world is tiled with regions where the physics holds — where flat is flat, where up is up, where the corridor-columns stand vertical — and between them, seams where the geometry warps into configurations that the eye reports but the brain refuses. Through one seam, the columns grow horizontally from a wall of compressed tissue, their flat tops facing you like shelves, their drainage channels running sideways, the dark solvent pooling in defiance of gravity before sinking into the wall. Through another seam, the surface is not solid but permeable — a mesh of living fiber, dense enough to walk on, through which you can see down into a space that should be beneath you but which appears to be above, lit by the same sourceless glow, populated by columns hanging downward like stalactites.

The seams are not sharp. They are zones, perhaps ten meters across, where the transition happens gradually. In the transition zones, the geometry flexes — you walk through a region where the floor tilts, then tilts more, then tilts past the point where you should fall, and you don't fall, and the furrows under your feet reorient themselves, and the columns lean, and then they are vertical again, and you are in a new region with new physics, or the same physics bent into a new orientation, and the landscape looks the same but the light falls differently and the solvent flows in different directions and the columns have different functions — these ones are shorter, squatter, with wider flat tops, and the dark fluid that pools on them is lighter in color, almost amber, and it steams faintly in the cool air.

Cool. The temperature hovers in a range that feels like a late autumn afternoon without sun — perhaps eight degrees, perhaps twelve. It is not uncomfortable but it is not warm. The substrate radiates faintly, a metabolic heat, but the air carries it away. In the transition zones the temperature shifts — colder in some, warmer in others — and you begin to understand that the seams are not just geometric boundaries but thermal boundaries, functional boundaries, zones where one industrial process gives way to another. The whole vast surface is a factory complex organized into districts, and the districts are separated by folds in space, and the folds are navigable, and whatever built this — whatever grows this — built it across a topology that is not Euclidean, that is not simple, that uses the bending of space itself as a way to organize production.

You sit on the flat top of a short column in an amber-solvent district. The solvent is warm against your hand. The light presses down, white and absolute. The silence is enormous but not empty — it is full of the near-subliminal pulse from below, full of the faint creak of columns adjusting, full of the almost-inaudible whisper of secondary growth extending its filaments into the air. Somewhere in the distance, beyond a seam, something processes, something cracks, something yields. The world works. It is working right now, all around you, a continental or planetary factory made of itself, growing its own machinery, processing its own fluids, lit by its own ceiling, breathing its own thin dry air. It does not need you. It does not need anything. It is complete and it is running and it has been running for a very long time, long enough that the furrows are worn, the columns are weathered, the drainage gratings are calcified and dense, the surface underfoot has the patina of a floor that has been walked on by machines for centuries.

But nothing walks on it now. The machines are gone, or the machines have become the plants, or the machines were always the plants and the distinction was never real. What remains is the process, self-sustaining, silent, bright, vast. A world that is a body that is a factory that is a garden that is a body. And you, sitting on it, a temporary anomaly, already beginning to dry out.
