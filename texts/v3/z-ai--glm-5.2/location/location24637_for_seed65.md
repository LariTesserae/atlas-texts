# The Throne of Winds

The plateau sits above the world.

Not above the clouds — the clouds move *through* it, ribbons of vapor that drag across stone like breath across glass. The elevation is such that the sky has a different quality here. Brighter, yes, but brighter in a way that feels structural, as though the atmosphere has thinned enough that light arrives with less interference, more direct, more honest about what it is. The sun presses down with a weight that has nothing to do with heat. You feel lit rather than warmed. Cool air moves against exposed skin — not cold, not biting, but persistently cool, the kind of temperature that never escalates and never retreats, that sits in the body like water from a deep well.

Everything here is open. The plateau extends in every direction farther than the eye can hold, a roughly oval expanse perhaps twelve kilometers across at its widest, ringed by lower peaks that drop away on all sides into valleys you cannot see the bottoms of. The ground is not flat but undulating in long, settled swells, like the hide of some immense sleeping animal. Stone predominates — a pale, grainy granite shot through with veins of something darker, almost obsidian, that catch the light and throw it back in bright threads. The surface has been weathered for so long that it has reached a kind of finished state. There are no fresh fractures, no recent scars. The rock is smooth where it should be smooth, rough where it should be rough, and has been so for longer than anything here can remember. Erosion has done its work and then, having nothing left to prove, stopped. The land is in equilibrium with the forces that shape it.

There is dampness, but it is a strange, intermittent thing. The clouds that pass through the plateau condense on the cooler stone surfaces, leaving trails of moisture that bead along the granite's grain, pool in shallow depressions, and run in threadlike rivulets that appear and disappear within meters. Nowhere does this water gather into anything you would call a stream. It is more like the stone is sweating. In the low points between swells, the ground becomes dark and slick, and here and there a shallow basin holds a still, clear pool no deeper than a hand, reflecting the sky with perfect fidelity until a cloud-shadow passes over and erases it. These pools are not permanent. They appear when conditions are right and vanish when they aren't, but conditions are right often enough that the stone around them is darkened with perpetual damp, colonized by thin films of something that is not quite algae and not quite mineral — a dark biological scum that clings to wet rock and nowhere else.

Vegetation is sparse to the point of absence, and what exists here is so low, so pressed against stone, that you might walk across it without knowing. Mats of something cushion-like, gray-green, with no flowers and no ambition, cling to the leeward sides of larger rock formations where condensation is most reliable. They do not grow upward. They grow *into* the stone, sending threadlike roots into hairline fractures, and they have been the size they are now for what appears to be a very long time. There are no trees. There are no grasses. There are no shrubs. There is only stone, and on certain parts of the stone, these mats, and in certain wetter depressions, the dark scum, and that is the entire floral inventory of the plateau.

And yet the place is not empty of life. Far from it.

---

The sound is the first thing you notice and the last thing you stop noticing. It is not that the plateau is loud. It is that the plateau *is* sound. From the moment you crest the final ridge and step onto its surface, your ears are full of a roaring, a deep, structural vibration that enters through the chest as much as through the ear canals. It comes from the wind, but "wind" is insufficient. The plateau sits at an elevation where air moves in vast, permanent currents, and the terrain around it — the ring of peaks, the valleys, the specific contours of the granite swells — has shaped these currents into a fixed acoustic architecture. The wind does not gust or vary. It does not come and go. It blows in a state of constant, equilibrium flow, and the stone amplifies and channels and harmonics this flow into a single, unceasing chord.

It is not music, but it is harmonic. There is a fundamental tone, deep enough that you feel it more than hear it, and above it, overtones that shimmer and sustain. The sound has been described by the few who have been here as like standing inside an organ pipe, except the organ pipe is several kilometers across and built of granite and has been playing the same note since before the invention of writing. There is no variation. No crescendo, no decrescendo. No moments of relative quiet. The sound is the same at dawn as at midday as at dusk. It is the same in every season. It is the same now as it was a thousand years ago. The forces that produce it — the elevation, the topology, the atmospheric circulation patterns — are so stable, so mutually reinforced, that the plateau has achieved a permanent acoustic state. It is, in a real sense, a musical instrument that plays one note forever.

Conversation is impossible. Shouting is impossible. You feel your own voice leave your throat and vanish into the roaring within a meter of your face, absorbed utterly. The sound fills every space, every silence, every pocket of air. You can feel it in your teeth.

---

The fauna has adapted.

The plateau is, against all expectation, thick with animal life — not despite the sound but because of it, or at least alongside it. The creatures that live here have evolved in a world of permanent acoustic saturation, and they have ceased to rely on hearing for anything at all.

The dominant presence is a kind of bird — though "bird" is a word that carries assumptions these creatures violate. They are large, larger than any bird you would expect at this altitude, with wingspans of two meters or more, and they are numerous. Hundreds of them. At any given moment, the bright sky above the plateau is crossed and recrossed by their silhouettes, dark against the blue, banking in slow circles that follow the same thermal paths the wind has carved into the air above the stone. Their feathers are pale, almost white, with a faint iridescence that catches the light when they turn. They make no sound. They cannot. Their vocal apparatus has atrophied over evolutionary time into a vestigial structure that serves no function. They do not call. They do not sing. They do not warn each other. They communicate entirely through movement — the angle of a bank, the dip of a wing, the specific way one individual adjusts its position relative to another in the thermal column. Their flight patterns are complex, expressive, and, to a creature that could read them, as information-rich as any birdsong.

They ride the wind. The same permanent, equilibrium wind that produces the roaring is their medium. They have learned the architecture of the air currents the way a fish learns the architecture of a river, and they move through them with an ease that is almost contemptuous, circling, diving, climbing, executing maneuvers that would seem to require enormous effort if you did not understand that the wind is doing most of the work. They never land on the open plateau. They perch only on the rim peaks, on specific ledges that tradition — or instinct, or something in between — has designated as theirs. They come to the plateau's surface only to feed.

What they feed on is the other strange fact of this place.

The stone of the plateau, in its wetter depressions, in its damp crevices, in the dark biological scum that films the standing water, supports an invertebrate ecosystem that is invisible until you kneel and look closely. There are organisms here — insect-like, though not precisely insects — that live in the thin moisture layer on the stone's surface. They are small, pale, soft-bodied, and they exist in extraordinary numbers, feeding on the biological scum, feeding on each other, occupying a niche that in a more temperate place would be filled by a dozen species but here is compressed into a few generalized forms that do everything at once. The birds descend from their circling to walk across the wet stone on flat, wide feet and eat these creatures by the thousands, filtering them from the scum with specialized beak structures that are more like baleen than anything else.

There are also mammals. Or something close to mammals. They are low, broad, densely furred creatures the color of the stone they live on, and they move across the plateau in a way that is almost geological — slowly, deliberately, in straight lines that can be tracked for hundreds of meters, their bodies pressed close to the granite as though trying to minimize their profile against the wind. They do not seem to be going anywhere. They seem to be *patrolling*. They feed on the same invertebrates, using broad, rasping tongues to strip the scum from the rock and process it in wide, flat mouths. There are perhaps dozens of them visible at any given time, scattered across the plateau's expanse with vast distances between them, each one alone, each one following its own line.

Nothing here makes sound. Nothing here hears. The entire ecosystem has evolved in silence — not true silence, but silence relative to the roaring, silence defined by the impossibility of acoustic communication. The birds signal by flight. The mammals signal by scent trails, chemical markers laid down in the damp scum that persist for days. The invertebrates signal by touch, by chemical gradient, by patterns in the scum itself. An entire biosphere built on every sense except the one that is, here, useless.

---

The light is extraordinary. Because the air is thin and the sky clear, sunlight arrives with a hardness that casts shadows of unusual sharpness. Every edge of every stone is picked out in bright line and deep shadow. The pale granite almost glows. The dark veins of obsidian-like material catch the light and hold it, acting as bright, warm streaks across the ground that you can feel on your skin if you pass over them. The cloud ribbons that move through the plateau cast shadows that are not soft but abrupt, sudden dimming that crosses the landscape in visible waves. Light and shadow move across the stone like living things, and because the wind never changes, the clouds move at the same speed always, and the shadows always travel at the same rate, in the same direction, with the same interval between them. Even the light is in equilibrium.

There are no built structures. No walls, no paths, no shelters, no markers. Nothing has ever been constructed here. The stone has not been cut or stacked or arranged. If there have been visitors — and the presence of worn stone at certain points suggests that there have, that creatures with tools have passed through, perhaps paused, perhaps hunted — they left nothing behind. No artifacts. No refuse. No evidence except the faintest smoothing of certain stone surfaces, the kind of wear that comes from feet, from hands, from the repetitive passage of bodies over the same route. Whoever they were, they came with simple tools — stone on stone, the most basic technology — and they did not stay. The plateau does not invite staying. The sound drives everything that can leave to leave. Only the adapted remain.

The plateau does not change. This is the thing that, more than the sound, more than the light, more than the silent birds, stays with you. Nothing here is in motion toward anything else. The wind has been blowing the same way at the same speed for so long that the stone is grooved by it, the vegetation is shaped by it, the animals' behavior is calibrated to it, and none of these things are trending toward a new state. The pools appear and vanish in the same places. The birds circle the same thermals. The mammals walk the same lines. The scum grows and is eaten and grows again at the same rate. The equilibrium is not fragile or temporary. It is old, and it is deep, and it has the quality of something that has been true for so long that truth and duration have become indistinguishable.

Standing in the center of the plateau, in the full roar of the permanent wind, in the bright, hard light, watching the silent birds circle overhead, you feel that you are in a place that has arrived at its conclusion. Not dead — alive, abundantly alive — but *finished*. A world that has answered every question it was going to answer and is now simply, perpetually, being itself.

The sound fills your chest. The light fills your eyes. The stone holds you up.

And nothing changes.
