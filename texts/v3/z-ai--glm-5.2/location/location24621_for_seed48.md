# The Murmurworks

The road descends for a long time before you arrive. Not dramatically — there are no switchbacks, no cliff-edges — just a gradual, almost imperceptible tilting-down, the land losing elevation in slow increments over kilometers, so that by the time the first structures appear on the horizon you are already nearly at sea level, and the air has thickened around you without your noticing. It is hot. The kind of heat that sits on your shoulders like a draped cloth, humid and close, heat that carries water inside it. You are sweating before you understand that you have entered a different climate.

---

The settlement is called the Murmurworks, or was, when anyone was left to call it anything. It spreads across a low basin — a wide, shallow depression in the land where the water table sits close to the surface, sometimes above it. The ground does not puddle so much as seep. You walk and the earth gives slightly underfoot, not mud, not quite, but a compressed saturation, every grain of soil holding its own film of water. After rain — and it rains often here, brief and intense, the sky opening and closing again like an eye blinking — the ground weeps. Water pushes up through hairline cracks in the pavement, beads along the foundations of buildings, collects in the low places and stays.

The paved surfaces are the first thing you notice, because they are what make the settlement legible as a settlement. Streets, or what were streets. Broad avenues laid out in a rational grid, narrower lanes branching between them, the whole pattern still visible despite centuries of weathering. The paving is a dark aggregate — slag bound with something mineral, fused and poured hot, you can see the flow lines where it set. It has cracked. The cracks follow their own logic, the physics of thermal cycling and root-action and ground-settling, except there are no roots. There have never been roots here. The cracks are the cracks of the material itself, aging on its own terms, and they are now so numerous and so interconnected that the pavement reads almost as a mosaic, dark slabs separated by thin lines of paler substrate. Water sits in those lines. In the dim light, they catch what illumination there is and hold it, so that from a height the streets would look like a circuit board, or a vascular system, or the creviced face of someone very old.

---

The light. You have to understand the light. There is no single source. The sky is low — not metaphorically but physically, a thick overcast that sits perhaps three or four hundred meters up, a ceiling of cloud so consistent and so opaque that it erases the sun. What light reaches the ground is diffuse, directionless, a gray-amber wash that comes from everywhere and nowhere. It is the color of old paper. It is the color of tea. It does not cast shadows, or rather, every shadow is soft and shallow, more an absence of sheen than a true darkness. On the brightest days — and bright is relative here — you can see for perhaps a kilometer before the haze eats the distance. On most days, visibility is half that. The air holds water in suspension, a fine particulate wetness that is not quite mist, not quite fog, just a general reluctance of the atmosphere to let light pass through it cleanly.

The structures do not rise above this ceiling. Nothing does. The tallest buildings are four stories, most are two or three, and they sit low and wide against the ground like animals resting. The architecture is industrial in its bones — you can read that in the proportions, in the materials, in the way the buildings are sited relative to each other and to the vanished infrastructure they once served. Long sheds with vaulted roofs, now partly collapsed. Square administrative blocks with rows of windows, most of the glass gone, the openings dark and regular as empty shelves. Towers — not for habitation but for function: chimney stacks, ventilation shafts, the skeletal frames of equipment hoists. These last are the tallest things in the landscape, and they stand against the low sky like dead trees, except they are steel and they rust in a way trees do not.

The rust is beautiful. You did not expect that. In this light, with this wet, the iron oxides have bloomed across every ferrous surface in gradients of orange and brown and a deep, almost purple red. The colors are muted by the atmosphere but they are there, and they shift as you move, the wet surfaces catching what light exists and throwing it back at slightly different angles. A wall that reads as a flat brown from one position reveals itself as streaked and mottled from another, the rust following channels where water has run, darker in the grooves, lighter on the ridges. Every steel surface in the Murmurworks is a record of its own weathering, a topographic map of the water that has passed over it.

---

There is no plant life. Let me say that plainly, because it is the thing that most defines this place, the absence that shapes everything else. No grass between the paving stones. No moss on the walls. No vines climbing the chimney stacks. No algae in the standing water — or rather, nothing you would recognize as algae, nothing green, nothing photosynthetic. The soil, such as it is, supports no roots. The water, such as it is, hosts no floating leaves. The world here is mineral. Stone, slag, steel, concrete, glass, and the slow byproducts of their decay. Rust, dust, efflorescence, the pale mineral blooms that crust the concrete where water has wicked up through it and evaporated, leaving its dissolved chemistry behind. The landscape is made of materials and the ways they fail.

And yet the place is not barren. That is the paradox, and you feel it before you understand it. Something is here. Many things are here. The settlement is *occupied*.

---

You hear them before you see them. Not a sound — a texture of sound, a presence in the auditory field that you become aware of the way you become aware of a change in air pressure. A hum. A rustle. A wet, rhythmic clicking that comes from the drainage channels cut along the avenues. Then, as your ears adjust, the sounds separate. Something is moving in the shadow of a collapsed shed — a low, heavy body, deliberate in its progress. Something else is in the water that pools in the street basin, and you hear the small splash of displacement. Above — above, in the dim air below the cloud ceiling — there is a wheeling, a circling, a constant motion of shapes that you cannot quite resolve because the light is too weak and the distance too great and the shapes too many.

The fauna. You were not prepared for the fauna.

They have come here because the people left and the infrastructure remained and the water is close and the heat is constant. They have come here in numbers that are difficult to process. Not a few opportunistic colonizers — a full, layered, self-sustaining ecosystem of animals, occupying every niche the abandoned settlement provides. The buildings are nests. The streets are runs. The drainage channels are rivers. The machinery — the vast, silent, rusted machinery that fills the long sheds and the underground passages — provides shelter and structure and the kind of enclosed, humid darkness that certain creatures seek.

Let me walk you through it.

---

In the street, in the shallow water that runs along the drainage cuts, there are crustaceans. Freshwater crabs, some of them the size of your fist, their shells a dull mottled brown that matches the wet pavement. They are everywhere. You stand still and count and lose count, because they move and the count breaks. They pick their way along the channel edges on delicate legs, they scuttle into gaps in the pavement, they climb over each other where the channel narrows. They are feeding — on what, you are not sure. Organic detritus, maybe, carried by the water. Each other, possibly. The sediment at the bottom of the channels, which is rich in something, chemical or biological, that you cannot identify from here. They are not loud, but in the aggregate, in this quiet, you can hear them — the click of claw on stone, the splash of a body entering water, the faint rasp of shell on shell. It is a dry, small, rhythmic sound, like someone working a bead across a board.

In the long sheds, where the roof has fallen in places and the floor is wet and strewn with debris, there are reptiles. Lizards — long-bodied, pale-skinned, quick. They live in the gaps between the machines, in the spaces behind control panels that no longer control anything, in the warm pockets of air that form where the sun — whatever sun reaches here — warms the metal. You walk into a shed and they scatter, a dozen of them, two dozen, pouring off surfaces like water, their feet scratching on the concrete. You hear them go and then you hear them stop, and then you hear them return, because you are not a threat and they are curious, or at least territorial, or at least indifferent to your presence in a way that reads as curiosity.

And in the administrative blocks, in the dark upper floors where the windows are intact enough to hold the heat and the humidity, there are colonies. Bats, perhaps — something membranous and darting, anyway, something that lives in the dark and hunts in the dim air above the settlement. They exit the buildings at dusk, or at what passes for dusk here, the light dropping by some imperceptible degree, and they pour from the windows in streams, and the streams join and thicken and become a river of bodies in the air, thousands upon thousands, and the river bends and folds over the settlement in a slow, searching gyre. They are silent, or nearly. You hear the air displacement, the soft rush of wings, but no cry. Whatever they are, they do not vocalize. They just move, and the moving is the sound.

---

But the bats are not the only thing in the air. There are insects. You knew there would be insects, but the *scale* of it is something else. Above the drainage channels, above the standing water, above every wet surface, there are clouds. Not swarms — swarms implies a density and a purpose. These are just *populations*, animals going about their lives in the medium they have chosen, which happens to be air. Midges, gnats, mosquitoes, things without common names, species that you do not recognize because you are not an entomologist and because the light is too dim to see them clearly. They form columns above the water, vertical structures that look, in the gray air, like the ghosts of chimneys. They form sheets along the walls where condensation runs. They form a general haze, a living atmospheric layer, at about head height, and you walk through it and they part around you and close behind you and you are a body moving through a medium that is air-and-animal, and the medium re-forms in your wake.

This is why it is called the Murmurworks. Not the machinery, which is silent. The animals. The constant, low, aggregate hum of living things at density — wings, and feet, and the small sounds of feeding, and the smaller sounds of breathing, all of it together, all of it at the edge of audibility, a sound that you feel in your sternum before you hear it with your ears. It is quiet here. It is quiet in the way that a room full of sleeping people is quiet — not silent, but held, a collective restraint of volume that is its own kind of presence.

---

The settlement was built for something. You can read that in its layout, even without understanding its purpose. The long sheds are arranged in pairs, facing each other across the wide avenues, and between each pair there is a covered conduit — a channel, now broken in places, that once carried something from one building to another. Fluid, probably. The stains on the channel walls are mineral, rust-brown and pale blue and a deep ochre, and they suggest that whatever flowed here was not water, or was water carrying other things. The administrative blocks are clustered at the center of the grid, around a square that was once paved and is now a shallow lake — a centimeter deep, two centimeters, a skin of water over the pavement, with the crabs picking across it and the insects suspended above it. The towers — the chimney stacks and vent shafts — are arranged at the settlement's edges, downwind of the prevailing breeze, which you know because the weathering patterns on their eastern faces are heavier than on their western faces, the erosion deeper, the rust darker, the wind carrying something corrosive in this direction for a long time.

The machinery is still here. You can walk among it. In the sheds, the equipment is massive, bolted to the concrete floors, connected to the conduits and to each other by pipelines and cable runs and the articulated arms of transfer systems. It is all ferrous — steel and iron, mostly, with brass fittings and ceramic insulators on the electrical components. It has been dead for a very long time. The corrosion is deep, structural. You put your hand on a machine and the surface gives, flaking under your palm. But you can still read the function, or guess at it. Here was a press. Here was a furnace, the firebrick lining still intact, cracked but holding its shape. Here was a pump, the impeller housing open, the blades seized in their bearings, a colony of something — spiders? something like spiders — webbing the interior.

Some of the systems still cycle. Not the machinery — nothing turns, nothing fires, the energy that once ran this place is gone. But the infrastructure. The drainage channels still carry water, because the ground slopes and the channels are cut and water does not need a pump when it has gravity. The conduit between the paired sheds has collapsed in places, but where it is intact, water pools and flows, finding its level. The foundations of the administrative blocks have settled, but they have settled into a stable configuration, the stress fractures arrested, the structure resting in its own deformation like a body that has found a comfortable posture. There is a sense, walking through the Murmurworks, that the place has completed its falling-apart and is now in a long second phase — a phase of *being*, of existing in its ruined state, of weathering at a pace that is slow enough to feel like equilibrium.

---

The heat is the thing that wears on you. It does not relent. The cloud ceiling traps it, holds it against the basin, and the water in the ground and the air amplifies it, so that the heat is not just temperature but a medium, a thing you move through, resistant and close. You sweat and the sweat does not evaporate, because the air is already full. Your clothes dampen and stay dampened. Your skin prickles with it. The animals do not seem to mind. They are built for this — the crabs, the lizards, the bats, the insects, all of them adapted to heat and wet, all of them finding in this climate the conditions they were made for. The heat is the engine that drives the ecosystem here, the way sunlight drives a forest. It keeps the water liquid. It keeps the metabolisms running. It keeps the insects breeding and the crabs feeding and the bats hunting and the lizards basking on the warm steel. The heat is not a problem to be solved. It is the context in which the problem of life has already been solved.

---

You walk to the edge of the settlement, where the buildings thin and the paving gives way to bare substrate — a flat, dark plain of compacted mineral earth, cracked into polygons by the cycling of wet and dry, stretching away in every direction. The horizon is not a line but a gradient, the ground rising to meet the sky in a haze of moisture and distance. There is nothing out there. Or rather, there is the same thing out there — the same earth, the same water, the same heat — but without the structures, without the streets and the sheds and the towers, without the architecture that gives the landscape its shape and its memory. Out there it is just flat and wet and hot and vast, and the silence is true silence, not the murmur-silence of the settlement but the silence of a place where nothing is happening and nothing has happened and the earth is waiting for something to begin.

You turn back. The Murmurworks stands behind you, low and dark and rusted, its chimney stacks just visible against the cloud ceiling, its buildings soft in the haze, and around it and above it and through it, the living air, the moving water, the constant small presence of animals doing what animals do in a place that has been left to them. The hum is below hearing now, at this distance, but you can feel it. You can feel the place, the way you feel a room that is occupied even when you cannot see the occupants. Something is here. Many things are here. The settlement belongs to them, and they are using it well.
