# The High Pastures of Verthen

The plateau goes on and on.

You stand on the western escarpment and look east, and the land simply does not stop. It rolls, yes — there are shallow basins and long ridges that might one day, in a million years, soften into rolling hills — but right now the stone is too new for that. The ridges are sharp-backed. The basins drop with edges that still show the fracture planes of their making. This is land that came up late, came up fast, and has not yet learned to be gentle. It stretches to a horizon that is not a line but a slow curve, the curvature of something too large to pretend it is flat.

And it is cold. Not the bitter, killing cold of extreme altitude, but the patient, bone-deep cold of a place where the sun can warm the surface but the rock beneath never forgets that it was born from depth. The air is thin enough that you feel it in your lungs — each breath slightly unsatisfying, each inhalation carrying just enough oxygen to keep you functional but not enough to let you forget you are breathing. Frost forms overnight on every horizontal surface and melts in the midmorning sun and refreezes at dusk. The cycle is so reliable that the inhabitants time their days by it.

---

## The Moisture

It is not a wet place. There are no rivers of note, no lakes of standing water. But there is damp.

Every morning, when the cold air above the plateau meets the slightly warmer air rising from the valleys below, a fog forms. Not a dramatic fog — not a rolling bank of cloud that swallows everything. A thin, persistent mist that sits on the ground like a blanket, turning the world gray and close for two or three hours after dawn. You walk through it and your skin becomes damp. Your clothing becomes damp. The stone beneath your feet becomes dark and slick, the frost dissolving into a fine sheen of water that coats everything.

Then the sun rises high enough and the mist burns off and the world becomes dry again — dry and enormous and sharp-edged — until the evening, when dew condenses on every surface and the cycle repeats. Occasionally, and no one can predict exactly when, a weather system moves across the plateau from the west, and a thin rain falls — not heavy, not driving, but a fine, cold rain that can last for days, that seeps into everything, that fills the shallow depressions in the rock with pools that may last a week or may last an afternoon. The animals know when it is coming. The people watch the animals.

There is, in one of the larger basins, a kind of marsh — a low area where the condensation collects faster than it evaporates, where the ground is soft and dark and spongy underfoot, where a thin skin of ice forms on standing water each night and breaks under the weight of small creatures each morning. It is not large. Perhaps a few hundred meters across. But it is the only place on the plateau where the rock is hidden beneath something organic, and it is precious beyond its size.

---

## The City

There is a city here. That is perhaps too grand a word, or perhaps not grand enough. It depends on what you mean by a city.

It is called Verthen, or something like that — the word has shifted across generations, across dialects, across the slow migration of peoples who have moved across the plateau for longer than anyone remembers. It sits in and on and around the largest of the basins, the one that contains the marsh, and it is dense. The shelters are built of the only material available in quantity: stone. The plateau is stone. The shelters are stone. The walls between properties are stone. The paths are stone. Everything is the same gray-brown, the same mineral texture, and from a distance, in the thin morning mist, the city looks like a roughness in the rock, a place where the surface has clumped and thickened, as though the plateau itself were developing a callus.

Up close, it is dense in the way that a body is dense — every space occupied, every surface used, every vertical wall serving multiple purposes. The shelters are small and low, their walls barely reaching chest height, their roofs made of hides stretched over stone frames, held taut by the cold that shrinks everything. They are packed together so closely that in many places you cannot walk between them — you must walk on top of them, stepping from roof to roof, the hides firm beneath your feet if the frame beneath is well-built, treacherous if it is not. There are paths on the rooftops, worn smooth by generations of feet, and paths on the ground between the buildings that remain, and the two systems interweave in a way that no one designed but everyone understands.

There are thousands of shelters. Tens of thousands. The basin extends for several kilometers, and the city fills it — not uniformly, not in a grid, but in clusters and chains and tendrils that follow the contours of the rock, that build up against the basin walls, that spill over the eastern rim and trickle down the slope in a thin scatter of shelters that becomes sparser and sparser until it fades into the empty plateau.

The population is not just people. The shelters house people, yes — small, hardy people, broad-chested from the altitude, dark-skinned from the sun, their hands rough from stone and cold — but they also house animals. The shelters are shared. In each one, a family and their animals sleep together, the body heat of the animals warming the interior, the people breathing the warm animal breath that is the only warmth the plateau offers. Goats, mostly — long-haired, broad-hoofed goats that can walk on the sharp stone without cutting their feet, that can find sustenance in the sparse vegetation, that can go for days on the moisture from dew and thin rain. But also other animals, less easily named — low, six-legged things that the people keep for their milk and their hide, creatures that seem to have evolved alongside the plateau's emergence, that are as new as the landscape itself, as though the same geological event that pushed the rock upward also pushed them into being.

---

## The Fauna

The animals are the true population of the plateau.

They are everywhere. On the rooftops. In the paths. On the slopes of the basin, where they cluster in herds that number in the thousands, dark masses that from a distance look like shadow on the stone. The goats move in tight formations, following the oldest females, and they graze on the sparse vegetation — the tough, low plants that cling to the rock, the lichens that grow in the sheltered crevices, the moss that edges the marsh. There is not much vegetation. The plateau is too high, too cold, too new for anything but the hardiest growth. But there is enough, because the animals have adapted to not-very-much, and because their numbers are kept in check by the cold itself, which kills the weak and the old and the unlucky each winter.

Beyond the goats, there are other creatures. The six-legged milk-things, called *sull*, that live in the city itself, that are almost domesticated, that have grown so accustomed to the people that they will follow them through the streets and onto the rooftops, their wide, flat bodies low to the ground, their six legs moving in a rhythm that is almost hypnotic. There are birds — large, silent birds that ride the thermals above the basin, that nest in the high fractures of the basin walls, that descend in the morning to pick through the city for scraps. There are small things that live in the stone — lizards, or something like lizards, that sun themselves on the rock faces and vanish into cracks when approached. There are burrowers that live beneath the marsh, that surface at night to feed on the roots of the marsh plants, that the people trap and eat and are grateful for.

And there are the herds that range beyond the city, out on the open plateau, that the people follow in cycles. These are larger animals — taller, shaggy, moving in groups that can take a day to pass a given point. They are not domesticated. They are barely hunted. They exist on the plateau the way the stone exists — as a fundamental feature of the place, as something that was here before the people and will be here after. The people watch them. The people follow them. The people know their routes, their seasons, their calving grounds, and they have built their city in the path of the migration, so that twice a year the herds pass through the basin and the city becomes, for a few days, a place of thunder and dust and animal smell, and then quiet again.

## The Sounds

The plateau is quiet.

Not silent — there are sounds. The wind is constant, a low, steady pressure that you feel in your ears as much as hear. It has nothing to stop it, nothing to break it, and so it moves across the plateau in a single, unbroken sheet, and the sound it makes is a tone, almost, a low hum that you stop hearing after an hour but that is always there, underneath everything. The goats make small sounds — bleats, but lower, softer, more like grunts than cries. The sull are nearly silent, their six feet placed with a care that seems deliberate, as though they are trying not to disturb the air. The birds do not call. They are mute, or nearly so — they communicate through movement, through the angle of their wings, through the way they tilt their heads, and their silence is somehow more eloquent than any cry.

In the city, there is the sound of stone on stone — hands shaping rock, feet on stone paths, the grinding of grain in stone querns, the crack of stone being split for construction. These are the sounds of work, of life, of a people who have made their world from the only material available to them. There are voices, too — low voices, conversational voices, the kind of voices that people use when they are close together in a small space and have no reason to shout. The language is soft, full of consonants that sound like stone clicking, and when you hear it from a distance, from the rooftop path, from above the city, it sounds like the city itself is talking, the stones clicking against each other in a conversation that has been going on for generations.

At night, it is truly quiet. The wind drops. The animals settle. The people sleep in their shelters, curled against their animals, and the only sound is the breathing — thousands of breaths, human and animal, synchronized not by intention but by proximity, a slow tide of air that moves through the city like the sea moves through a cave, and the plateau holds its breath, and the stars are very bright in the thin air, and the frost forms.

---

## The Quality of the Light

The light is clear and honest. There is not enough atmosphere to filter it, to soften it, to make it gentle. Shadows are hard-edged. Colors are true — the stone is the color of stone, the hides are the color of hide, the sky is the color of sky, and there is no haze to blur the boundaries between them. At midday, the sun is a presence, a weight, and the people retreat to their shelters and wait for it to lower. In the morning and evening, when the sun is low, the light comes sideways and every fracture in the rock, every crack in the wall, every line in a face is thrown into relief, and the world looks like a carving of itself, every detail visible, every surface textured.

---

## The Rules

This is a place where patience is not a virtue but a necessity. Nothing happens quickly here. The plateau was made slowly, over geological time, and it is being eroded slowly, over geological time, and the people who live on it move slowly, think slowly, change slowly. A new shelter takes a season to build — the stone must be found, split, shaped, fitted. A child takes longer to grow here, or seems to. The animals move slowly, eat slowly, reproduce slowly. The marsh grows slowly — each year a few centimeters wider, a few centimeters deeper, the organic matter accumulating grain by grain.

The people do not mine metal because there is no metal to mine. They do not work wood because there are no trees. They work stone and hide and bone and fiber, and their tools are stone tools, and their weapons are stone weapons, and their art is stone art — petroglyphs carved into the basin walls, images of the herds, of the birds, of the migration, of the slow cycles that govern everything. They trade with the lowlands — there are paths down the escarpment, treacherous paths, traveled a few times a year — and they bring down hides and dried meat and bone tools, and they bring up fiber and dried fruit and, rarely, small pieces of metal that are treasured and used only for the most important things.

The city is organized, in its way. There are quarters — not formal, not named, but known. The herders live on the eastern side, where the paths lead out to the plateau. The stone-workers live on the western side, near the basin wall where the best stone is found. The traders live near the southern rim, where the path down the escarpment begins. The marsh-tenders live near the marsh. There are no streets, no formal layout — the organization is organic, accreted over generations, the way a reef accretes, each new shelter built next to the last, each new family finding its place next to its kin.

And the city is old enough that it has layers. Not archaeological layers — nothing is buried here — but functional layers. The rooftop paths are the highest layer, the fastest way to move through the city, the domain of the young and the agile. The ground-level paths are the middle layer, the domain of daily life, of markets and meetings and work. And beneath the city, in the fractures of the rock, in the spaces between the stone, there are the lowest spaces — cellars, caches, the places where things are kept cold, where the marsh water is stored in stone vessels, where the dried meat is hung, where the dead are placed, not buried, but sealed into the cracks of the rock with stone plugs, and left there, and the people say that the dead become part of the plateau, and the plateau becomes part of the people, and the cycle continues.

---

The wind picks up slightly. The sun is lowering. The frost will come in two hours, and the people are moving toward their shelters, and the goats are bunching, and the sull are settling flat against the ground, and the birds are rising, rising, catching the last thermals, climbing toward the rim of the basin where their nests are, and the city is quieting, and the plateau is darkening, and the lights — small fires of dung and fat, the only warmth the people can make — begin to appear in the doorways of the shelters, small orange points in the gray, and the night comes, and the plateau holds.
