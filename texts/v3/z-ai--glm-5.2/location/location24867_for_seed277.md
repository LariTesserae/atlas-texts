# The Carbonite Deck

The plateau goes on for what might be three hundred kilometers in any given direction, and it is dark in the way that a room is dark after your eyes have already adjusted — not black, but sourced from no single origin. The warmth rises from below. You feel it through whatever you're standing on, which is not stone exactly, though it has the hardness and the grain of stone. A composite. Carbonite, perhaps, though that word belongs to a vocabulary no one here would use, because no one is here to use any vocabulary at all.

What happens is this: the continental shelf at this elevation — roughly 3,400 meters, though the concept of sea level has become academic on a world where the oceans receded long ago — sits above a deep mantle fracture. Thermal energy moves upward through mineral strata that, over millions of years, have arranged themselves into something like a natural battery. Iron-bearing formations. Conductive sulfide veins. Silicate layers with piezoelectric properties under constant compressive stress. The whole plateau is an engine, not built but *grown* — the way a crystal grows, the way a river carves a circuit. It generates current. It generates heat. It does work.

No one built it. No one maintains it. It simply is, and it has been for longer than anything alive can remember, which is a long time, because many things here are alive.

---

The surface is uneven in a way that suggests old, old erosion — not the dramatic cutting of water or wind, but a slow settling, a surface finding its equilibrium the way a mud flat does when the water draws down. Shallow basins. Low ridges no higher than your knee, running in patterns that might be deliberate or might be the natural fracture geometry of cooling mineral masses. The ground is dark grey-brown, almost black in the dimness, with a faintly greasy sheen where the compressive heat has brought hydrocarbons to the surface. It smells like hot graphite. Like a machine shop with no machines in it. Like the inside of a kiln after it's been allowed to cool for an hour but is still too hot to touch.

Moisture comes and goes. Not rain exactly — there are clouds, but they move above the plateau like a ceiling, and only occasionally does enough condense to fall. More commonly, water appears as dew on the mineral surfaces in the cooler hours, forming thin films that bead along the ridges and collect in the shallow basins. The basins are not deep. The deepest might come up to your shin. Most are ankle-depth or less, and they dry within a day, leaving dark mineral rings. But there are thousands of them, and at any given time some fraction hold water, so the plateau is a landscape of scattered dark puddles across a dark surface under a dark sky. The water is warm. Not hot, but warm the way bathwater is warm an hour after you've drawn it. It tastes of iron and sulfur and something faintly alkaline.

The vegetation is sparse in the way that stars are sparse — you can see individual plants, and they are separated by distances that make you aware of the space between them. They are low, dark things, not green. Chlorophyll is not the strategy here. The dominant ground cover is a kind of厚 textured crust — part mineral, part biological — that clings to the ridges and spreads slowly outward in lobes, dark maroon edged with a faintly luminous blue-grey where the growing margin is active. It uses the thermal gradient, drawing energy from the warmth beneath and the cooler air above, something between a thermophile and a lichen. In the basins where water collects, a different community takes hold: pale, translucent filaments, almost like fiberglass insulation, that float just beneath the water's surface and seem to shimmer as the faint air currents move across the pools. These are the only things here that catch light in any meaningful way. They are not bright. They are the dimmest possible version of visible.

The larger plants — and there are some, perhaps one every fifty meters, sometimes clustered in loose stands of four or five — are trunk-like structures, no taller than a person, black and rough, with a texture like burnt coral. They do not photosynthesize. They tap the conductive veins below, drawing electrical current and using it to fix carbon from the mineral substrate. Their "leaves" are flat, dark plates at the top, angled to catch condensation, and they drip almost continuously in the cooler hours, creating small wet zones around their bases where the filament-mats thrive. When the wind picks up, the plates vibrate at a frequency just below hearing, and you feel it in your sternum before you register it as sound.

---

The animals are the surprise.

The plateau should not support this much life. The vegetation is sparse, the light is almost absent, the environment is harsh and strange. But the thermal engine below provides a foundation that sunlight never could — a constant, reliable energy source that doesn't depend on day or night or season, because there is no day here, not really. The sky is a permanent low overcast, a deck of cloud so thick and so wide that it constitutes the plateau's weather in its entirety. Sometimes the clouds thin enough to suggest a brighter region above — not sun, but a general lightening, a grey that becomes a lighter grey. Sometimes they thicken until the darkness is absolute and you cannot see your own hands. There is no rain in the forecast, but there is always the possibility of it, and the air itself is humid enough that moisture condenses on any surface cooler than the ambient temperature, which means on you, if you were here, which you are not.

The animals have evolved for this darkness. Most are eyeless. Some have eyes, but they are reduced to light-detecting spots that register only the faint bioluminescence of the filament-mats and the dim shimmer of the mineral crust's growing edges. They navigate by sound, by vibration, by the thermal signatures of the ground and of each other, and by a chemical sense so refined that they can detect the difference between a puddle that formed two hours ago and one that formed six.

The most visible — if that word means anything here — are the *striders*, which is not their name, because they have no name, but it will serve. They are tall. A meter and a half at the shoulder, standing on four slender legs that end in splayed, pressure-sensitive pads. Their bodies are narrow and deep, built for covering ground, and they move across the plateau in loose groups of ten to thirty individuals, picking their way among the basins and ridges with a deliberation that looks almost ceremonial. Their skin is dark, matte, slightly textured like very fine sandpaper, and it is warm to the touch — they regulate their body temperature not against the environment but *with* it, running hot because the environment runs hot, and this saves them the metabolic cost that warm-bloodedness usually demands. They feed on the mineral crust, scraping it up with broad, flat mouthparts and digesting it in gut chambers that host thermophilic bacteria. They are, in a sense, eating the landscape. They are also, in a sense, farming it — their scraping encourages new growth at the margins, and the crust behind a strider group is often thicker and more vigorously growing than the crust ahead of it.

Smaller things live in the basins. Flat, many-legged organisms that move through the shallow water with an undulating motion, filtering the filament-mats and the mineral particulate they trap. They are the size of a hand, and there are thousands of them in any given pool, and when a pool dries up, they bury themselves in the mineral sediment at the bottom and wait. They can wait for years. When the water returns, they return with it, emerging in a timescale of hours.

Above the striders, above everything, are the *clickers* — small, fast, winged things that use echolocation in the dense air. Their clicks are the most consistent sound on the plateau: a rapid, dry ticking that comes from all directions at all times, like the sound of a thousand clocks that are almost but not quite synchronized. It is not loud. It is the quietest kind of abundance — a sound that you would not notice until it stopped, and then you would notice its absence the way you notice when a fan switches off in a room you've been sitting in for hours.

---

The plateau is active. Not violently. Not dramatically. But in the way a body is active — heartbeat, breath, peristalsis. The thermal engine pulses. The current in the conductive veins fluctuates in cycles that have nothing to do with day or night — they are tied to the mantle below, to the slow convection of deep rock, and they run on a rhythm that is somewhere between twelve and eighteen hours, irregular, organic. When the current is high, the crust glows brighter at its edges, the striders are more active, the filament-mats in the pools shimmer more intensely. When it drops, everything contracts. The striders stand still. The clickers go silent. The pools go dark. It is the closest thing to nightfall this place has.

Wind moves across the plateau in broad, slow currents, not gusts but migrations of air that take minutes to arrive and minutes to pass. They carry the smell of hot mineral and the faint ozone-tang of electrical discharge from the veins. They ripple the water in the basins. They make the trunk-plates vibrate, and the subsonic hum spreads across the landscape in waves that you feel more than hear, a pulse that moves through the ground and the air and the water all at once, and the animals feel it too, and they move with it or against it according to preferences you cannot determine.

The erosion is complete, which is another way of saying it is finished, which is another way of saying the landscape is in balance. The basins will not deepen. The ridges will not flatten. The thermal engine will not intensify or fade, not on any timescale that matters to the things living here. This is what the plateau looks like. This is what it has looked like for a very long time. This is what it will look like for a long time to come.

---

There are no structures. No marks of making. No artifacts, no tools, no remnants, no paths, no clearings, no arrangements that suggest intention. The plateau is not a ruin. It was never built. It was never used. It is not waiting for anything.

And yet — and this is the thing that stays with you, if you were here, which you are not — the plateau is *industrial*. The processes happening beneath the surface are the same processes that happen in a generator: electromagnetic induction, thermal gradients driving current, conductive materials in specific arrangements producing usable energy. The landscape is doing what a power plant does. The animals are doing what machines do — converting energy, performing work, cycling materials through systems of exchange and transformation. The trunk-plants are fixing carbon the way a factory fixes carbon. The striders are mining the surface the way a continuous mining machine mines it. The clickers are running sonar.

It is not that the plateau resembles industry. It is that industry resembles the plateau. This came first. This is the original version. The engines that humans build — or that any builders build, anywhere — are copies of this, diminished and specific, extracting one thread from a process that here runs whole and unbroken across a continental scale.

The darkness makes it hard to see all of this. The quiet makes it hard to hear. But if you stand still long enough — and you would need to stand still for a long time, because this place operates on timescales longer than human attention usually allows — you would begin to feel it. The hum. The pulse. The slow, vast, mechanical heartbeat of a landscape that is doing everything a civilization does, without being one, without needing to be one, without ever knowing or caring that it is.

The striders move across the dark ground. The clickers tick. The pools shimmer and dry and fill again. The crust grows. The current flows. The clouds do not break.

This is the machine that was never built. It is running. It has always been running.
