# The Rhizome Halls

You feel the sound before you hear it. A pressure in the sternum, a vibration that travels through the floor and up through your boots and into your teeth. It is not one sound but a layered architecture of noise — a low, tectonic hum from somewhere far below, a mid-range thrum of machinery still turning over at geological patience, and above it all a white roar of air being forced through narrow channels in the rock. The decibels pile on each other until silence becomes a thing you remember rather than experience. You shout and your voice dies a foot from your mouth, swallowed whole.

---

The entrance is a wound in a hillside. Not a cave mouth — nothing so romantic. It is a cut, industrial and deliberate, a rectangle of darkness bite-shaped into grey-green stone. The edges are still sharp. Frost clings to the raw faces where the rock was parted months or years ago, and the frost does not melt because the temperature down here sits at a level that makes exhaled breath visible as a brief white ghost before the air movement shreds it apart. Cold radiates from the walls themselves, from the stone, from everything. You are underground and you are cold and the cold is not seasonal. It is structural. It belongs to the place the way bedrock belongs to the place.

The tunnel descends at a shallow grade for perhaps two hundred meters before it opens, and when it opens, it *opens*.

---

You step into a chamber that you cannot immediately find the edges of. The ceiling — if there is a single ceiling — is lost in a permanent dimness that your headlamp cannot fully penetrate. The light catches moisture in the air, a fine suspended dampness that is not quite rain and not quite fog but something between, a condensation-event that exists because the chamber is vast and cold and the air moving through it carries water it cannot hold at this temperature. The moisture deposits itself on every surface in a thin, persistent film. You touch a wall and your glove comes away wet. The water does not drip so much as *bleed* from surfaces, gathering in slow, patient lines that find the lowest points and pool there in shallow, black, perfectly still mirrors that reflect nothing because there is almost nothing to reflect.

The scale is valley-sized. That is the only honest comparison. The chamber is not a cave but a geography, a hollowed region with its own floor plan, its own zones, its own weather. Pillars of unexcavated rock rise here and there in the dark like the stumps of enormous trees, and between them the space extends — left, right, forward — past the reach of your light, past the reach of your shouting, which is inaudible anyway.

---

And everything — the floor, the walls, the pillars, the ceiling you cannot see — is *covered* in plant matter.

Not decorated. Not colonized. *Constituted.* The vegetation here is not a thing that grows on the substrate; it has become the substrate. A pale, dense mat of root fiber and rhizome and cold-adapted moss covers the stone in layers thick enough to walk on without ever touching rock. It compresses underfoot like frozen peat, springy and damp, and where you tear it you see the cross-section: a weft of white rootlets, dark hyphae threading through like wire through insulation, compressed organic matter below, and beneath that, more root, more fiber, more living mass extending down to where it meets stone and grips. The walls wear the same coating. The ceiling, where your light reaches fragments of it, is hung with root systems that have grown downward in defiance of gravity, seeking moisture in the air, each one pale and finger-thick and beaded with condensation that slides along its length in a slow, continuous transit.

There are no trees. There are no flowering plants. Nothing here photosynthesizes in any way you would recognize, because the light is almost nonexistent and what light there is comes from two sources: the faint bioluminescence of the moss itself — a blue-grey cold glow, barely enough to see your own hand by, more sensed than seen — and, at distant intervals, the amber flicker of industrial lamps still drawing power from sources you cannot identify.

The moss is the dominant organism. It is the architecture. Where it has grown thick enough, it forms walls, partitions, gentle berms that divide the chamber into zones and passages without any evident design — just the logic of growth, the slow accumulation of living mass in the directions where moisture and mineral seepage are richest. In places the root matrix has grown so dense it has become structural, weight-bearing, forming arches and buttresses that hold up slabs of stone ceiling or create shelves of living fiber strong enough to stand on. The plant is the building. The building is the plant.

---

The industrial presence is scattered and strange. Not a factory in any organized sense — there was no unified operation down here — but fragments, artifacts, the mechanical equivalent of shed antlers. A diesel generator the size of a wardrobe sits against one wall, dark and silent, its exhaust pipe driven into the root-mat like a spear, and the root has grown around the pipe and into the pipe and the pipe is now a vascular element in a larger system, carrying not exhaust but moisture, condensed and channeled. A few hundred meters away — you walk for minutes through the deafening hum and the dim blue light and the cold — there is a pump station, three electric pumps bolted to a concrete pad that itself sits on the root-mat floor, and one of the pumps is still running. You can feel it more than hear it, which is saying something given the noise. It cycles once every forty seconds or so, a shudder that passes through the floor and up through your legs, and from its outlet a thin stream of water arcs into a channel the roots have grown to receive. The water is being moved from somewhere to somewhere else for a purpose that was designed by people who are no longer here, and the root system has incorporated the infrastructure into its own hydraulic network. It is unclear now whether the pump serves the plumbing or the plumbing serves the plant.

Further in — deeper, colder, the grade still descending — there are rails. Narrow-gauge, rusted, the ties half-swallowed by root-mat. A single ore cart sits overturned on its side, and the moss has grown over one wheel and through the spokes and into the cart's bed, and inside the cart a small garden of paler moss has taken hold, a different species, fed by whatever mineral residue remained in the metal. This is the built environment: not ruins in any dramatic sense but small, functional things left behind, being slowly digested.

---

The sound does not let up. You try to identify its components and you partly succeed: there is a deep, near-geological throb that must be water under pressure moving through fissures in the rock far beneath the floor, a sound more felt than heard. There is the mechanical drone of whatever still-running machinery persists in the dark — pumps, perhaps a ventilation fan somewhere in the distance, turning at a speed that produces a sustained chord in the low register. And there is the air itself, the chamber's breath, the slow exchange of atmosphere through channels too narrow for the volume, which produces a continuous white roar that fills every frequency you can perceive and several you cannot but which you register as a physical sensation, a ringing in the bones of your skull.

It does not fluctuate. That is the uncanny thing. The sound is deafening and it is *steady*, the way the cold is steady, the way the dimness is steady. This is a place of patient constants. The moss grows at a rate you cannot perceive. The water moves along its root-channels in a slow, continuous crawl. The pump cycles. The air moves. The temperature does not change. You have been here for an hour or three and nothing has visibly altered, and you understand that nothing will alter in the time you have, that the processes at work here operate on a scale of weeks and months and years — the root-mat thickening by millimeters per season, the condensation slowly mineralizing the surfaces it touches, the machinery failing one component at a time and being incorporated, fiber by fiber, into the organism that has already claimed everything else.

---

There are animals, barely. Once, in the distance, something pale and low moves across the floor of the chamber — a creature the size of your hand, many-legged, translucent, feeding on the moss or on something within the moss. You see it for two seconds and then it is gone into a crevice in the root-mat and you do not see another. In the pools of standing water there are dark shapes, still, that might be insect larvae or might be something else. They do not move when you approach. The ecosystem here is nearly monotonic — the plant, the water, the mineral substrate, and a thin scattering of consumers so sparse they barely register as life. You are the loudest thing that has been here in a long time, and even you are inaudible.

---

The chamber extends. You walk for what might be a kilometer and the character of the space shifts in small ways — the root-mat thickens and thins, the ceiling lowers and rises, the pools cluster in one region and vanish in another. In one section the industrial remnants concentrate: a row of electrical conduit runs along a wall for fifty meters, clipped to brackets that are themselves bolted to stone, and the root-mat has grown over and around the conduit until it is a ridge in the wall like a vein under skin. A junction box hangs open, its components green with oxidation and white with mycelial filaments. A work light, face down on the floor, half-buried, its cord trailing into the mat and gone. Someone worked here. Someone installed this infrastructure with purpose and effort and the plant ate it and the sound went on and the cold went on and now the purpose is indistinguishable from the root that swallowed it.

The light from your headlamp catches something distant. A wall. Perhaps the far wall of the chamber, hundreds of meters away, covered in the same blue-glowing moss, the same root-mat, the same slow beading of condensation. Or perhaps not a wall but another pillar, and beyond it more space, more chamber, more plant, more dark. The scale defeats orientation. You are in a region, not a room. You are inside something that is still growing.

---

You turn back. The walk takes longer than it should, or perhaps exactly as long as it should — time is difficult here, the dimness offering no cue, the sound offering no variation, the cold offering no relief. Your path is marked by your own boot-prints in the root-mat, shallow impressions that are already softening at the edges, the fibers slowly re-expanding, the evidence of your passage being gently erased. By the time you reach the entrance tunnel, the prints behind you are filling in. By the time you reach the surface, they will be memory. The cut in the hillside lets you out into whatever weather the surface has, and the sound drops from deafening to merely loud to merely present as you climb, and then you are outside and the silence of the open air is so sudden it feels like a wound.

Behind you, in the dark, the pump cycles. The root drinks. The chamber roars. It will be doing this when you have forgotten it.
