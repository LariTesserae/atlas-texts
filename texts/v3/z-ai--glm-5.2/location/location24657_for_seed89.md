# The Roosting Gallery

You find it by sound before sight. A low, persistent roar that you mistake at first for wind, for the kind of hollow air-rush that high places make when they find a crack to pour through. But it isn't wind. It's too regular. Too organic. It has a pulse to it, a breathing cadence that wind never quite manages.

---

The entrance is a fissure in granite so old it has become architectural. Not built — nothing here is built, nothing has ever been built here — but worn. The rock has been doing its own slow masonry for longer than anything alive can remember. Water has shouldered through over millennia, not rushing but *insisting*, the way water does at altitude, finding hairline fractures and making them into arteries, making arteries into passages, making passages into rooms. The result is a gallery. Not grand. Not cathedral. Something more modest — a long, low chamber perhaps forty meters at its widest, maybe fifteen at its ceiling's highest point, following a diagonal descent into the mountain's flank. The walls are smoothed in some places to the texture of worn leather and left jagged in others, teeth-like, unfinished. The rock is dark — a mica-rich granite that catches what little light exists and holds it like greedy hands. There isn't much light to catch.

Darkness here isn't the absence of something. It's a substance. It has weight. It sits against your skin like damp cloth, because the air *is* damp — not wet, not streaming, but breathing moisture in the way high altitudes do when warm stone meets cooler air and the two negotiate through condensation. The walls weep. Not dramatically. A slow percolation, a patience of water that has been running down these same mineral tracks for so long that the stone has developed dark veins, channels worn an inch deep, guiding drops from fissure to fissure in a branching system that looks almost circulatory. You can trace the paths with your fingers. The water is warm. Everything here is warm — the rock, the air, the moisture. At this elevation, that warmth is wrong, or at least unexpected, and you realize after a while that it comes from below. Geothermal. A slow, patient heat that rises through the stone like the mountain is exhaling, and the gallery sits in the breath-path, kept perpetually tepid, perpetually damp. A terrarium without glass.

---

The sound resolves as your eyes adjust, and then you understand what you're standing inside.

*Bats.* Thousands of them. Tens of thousands, perhaps, packed across the ceiling in a density that turns the stone surface into a second, living skin. They cling in layers — some directly against the rock, others clinging to *those*, a third tier clinging to the second, a staggered maturation of bodies compressed into every available centimeter of ceiling space. The species are mixed. You can't see this well, but you can hear it — the vocalizations occupy different registers, different rhythms, a polyphonic drone that is the roar you heard from outside. Individual calls blur into aggregate. Ultrasonic clicks that should be beyond human hearing register here as pressure, as a subtle headache behind the eyes, because the volume is so total that even the edges of the frequency range press against your perception. The air vibrates. Not metaphorically. Your lips tingle.

They are warm. The collective body heat of so many mammals in an enclosed space contributes to the gallery's temperature, layering over the geothermal warmth until the air is almost balmy, almost tropical, which is absurd at this altitude. You strip a layer off without thinking about it. The humidity spikes near the ceiling, where their breath and exhalation creates a microclimate, a zone of animal-warmth and carbon dioxide that hovers against the rock like an invisible fog. The condensation on the walls is partly theirs.

The floor tells you how long this has been going on. Guano — a deep, compressed deposit, ancient and half-mineralized, layers of it built up over what must be thousands of years of continuous occupation. It has its own topography. Ridges and valleys shaped by water runoff, by the slow accumulation and compression of waste into something almost geological. The smell is not what you expect — not sharp or acrid but earthen, fungal, dense with the particular sweetness of long-decomposed organic matter. Ammonia has long since off-gassed from the lower layers. What remains is a soil-smell, rich and dark. Things grow in it. Not much — vegetation is sparse here, and what exists is marginal — but threads of pale fungus spread through the guano in mycelial networks, white and faintly luminescent, the only real light source in the gallery. It's a cold light, blue-white, barely enough to see your own hands by, but enough to give the chamber a ghost-depth, a sense of its dimensions. The fungus lives on the guano. The guano exists because of the bats. The bats return because the gallery is warm. The gallery is warm because of the geothermal heat and the bats' own bodies. It's a closed loop, a self-sustaining system that has been running so long it has achieved a kind of equilibrium, every element in balance with every other.

---

You are not the first primate to stand here.

There are marks on the wall near the entrance — a series of parallel scrapes, low to the ground, deliberate in their spacing. Stone tool marks. Someone, at some point in the deep past, worked a hand-axe along this wall, scraping grooves into the granite with a patience that suggests either function or ritual. The grooves are weathered smooth now, softened by the same condensation that shaped the water-channels, but they are unmistakably made. Made by hands. Simple tools — no metal, no complexity — just stone against stone, the most basic negotiation of will against material. There are no other artifacts. No structures, no fire rings, no bones. Whoever was here left only these marks and then left. Perhaps they sheltered. Perhaps they came for the same reason the bats come — warmth, enclosure, the particular comfort of a space that holds heat and blocks wind at altitude. Perhaps the marks were counting. Perhaps they were memory. There is no way to know, and the not-knowing has its own weight, its own presence in the gallery.

---

The floor is not still. You notice this gradually. The guano surface moves — not dramatically, not all at once, but in pulses. Beetles. Several species of them, some winged and some not, working through the deposit in slow, methodical cycles. They are the reason the guano is compressed rather than simply piled — their movement through the material tamps it down, aerates it, integrates it into something denser than fresh droppings. The fungus benefits from their passage; they carry spores on their legs from one part of the deposit to another. You hear them — a dry, rustling susurration that sits beneath the bat-roar like a bass note, a constant textural sound that you eventually stop consciously hearing and simply *inhabit*. There are spiders. Not many, but enough — pale, long-legged things that have evolved for this exact niche, living in the cracks between guano-ridges, emerging to take the beetles that stray too close to their hidden territories. Their webs are minimal — geometry doesn't work well in this medium, so they hunt by ambush, by patience, by waiting in the dark for vibration to tell them something has come close enough.

And there are the moths.

This is the unusual part. This is where the gallery tips from "remarkable" into "strange."

The moths live in the upper portion of the chamber, in the zone of bat-breath and humidity, and they should not be here. They are large — wingspans of fifteen centimeters or more — and their bodies are dense, furred, almost mammalian in their covering. They cling to the walls alongside the bats without apparent conflict, and they are *silent* in a way that feels deliberate, as though silence is their adaptive strategy, their way of occupying a space dominated by another species' noise. The bats don't eat them. You watch one moth walk — not fly, *walk*, on heavy legs — across the chest of a roosting bat, and the bat shifts but does not startle, does not snap at it. There is an arrangement here. Some kind of relationship. The moths may feed on the bat-exhalation moisture, on the fungus that grows near the ceiling, on something in the chemistry of this enclosed space that sustains them. They may have always been here. They may have evolved *here*, in this single gallery, becoming something that exists nowhere else — a species contained in forty meters of stone, as dependent on the geothermal warmth and the bat-colony and the condensation as the fungus is dependent on the guano. Their wings have no pattern. No eyespots, no camouflage markings. They are a uniform deep grey, the color of the granite, and in the faint fungal light they look like pieces of the wall that have loosened and begun to move.

---

Time here is slow. Not stopped — slow. The bats cycle out at dusk and return before dawn, and this creates the gallery's only real rhythm, its one tide. When they depart, the sound drops by half, and the temperature falls by a degree or two, and the silence that rushes in feels physical, like a pressure change. The beetles continue. The fungus continues. The condensation continues. When the bats return, the sound ramps up over minutes, a wave-front of echolocation and body-noise and wing-clatter that fills the chamber from back to front like water filling a vessel. This has happened every day for longer than the tool-marks on the wall. Longer than the compressed guano. Longer than the water-channels. The system doesn't change because it doesn't need to. It has found its state. It has settled.

You stand in the dark, in the damp warmth, in the roar that is also a silence, and you understand that this place does not require you. It does not require anything. It simply persists, each element feeding the next, the heat and the water and the bats and the beetles and the fungus and the moths all holding each other in an equilibrium so stable that disruption would have to come from outside, from something larger than the gallery, and nothing larger has come in a very long time. The tool-marks are the only evidence that anything larger ever came at all.

When you leave, the moths watch you go. You think. It's hard to tell in the dark.
