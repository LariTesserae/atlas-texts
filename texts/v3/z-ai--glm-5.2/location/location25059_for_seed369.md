# The Weeping Hollow

It begins with sound before anything else — a low, resonant drumming that seems to rise from the stone itself, as though the mountain is breathing through wet lungs. You are climbing, and then you are there, and the there-ness of it is sudden: a scooped hollow near the top of a high ridge, a bowl the size of a cathedral interior but open to the sky, its walls curving up and outward like the inside of cupped hands turned palms-down to the earth.

## The Stone and the Water

The rock is a pale, greenish-gray — metamorphic, layered, ancient. Schist or something kin to it, shot through with veins of quartzite that catch the light when the angle is right and otherwise sit dull and mineral. The walls of the hollow are streaked dark with continuous moisture. Water does not pour here. It weeps. It seeps from fractures in the stone at a pace so slow that each individual trickle seems geological, patient, as though the rock is still processing a rain that fell centuries ago and releasing it now, drop by drop, down the curved walls.

And yet the sound is loud. This is the first strangeness. The accumulated weeping of a hundred seeps and trickles produces a deep, layered acoustics — a low hum threaded with higher plinking notes, reverberating off the curved stone until the hollow rings like a struck bell that has decided never to fully stop ringing. Speak and your voice is swallowed into it, absorbed, made part of the water-chord. The sound does not come from one direction. It comes from everywhere, as though the air itself is vibrating at a frequency just above silence, boosted by the shape of the stone into something undeniable.

At the center of the hollow's floor, the water gathers. Not a pool — something more provisional. A shallow, spreading film of water that sheets across the ground in a thin, continuous layer, never deeper than two fingers, never still. It moves with a sluggish determinism toward a low point at the eastern edge where it spills over into nothingness — a narrow notch in the rim that drops away into cloud. You cannot see where the water goes. The edge of the hollow is the edge of the known. Beyond it, mist.

## The Vegetation

The lushness here is not the lushness of a jungle — not crowded, not suffocating, not a green so dense it blacks out the sky. This is a sparse lushness, and that contradiction is the second strangeness. Things grow with extraordinary vigor and extraordinary restraint, as though each plant has been given ample room and has chosen to fill only its allotted space, no more.

The floor of the hollow, where it is not sheeted with water, is carpeted in mosses of a kind that seem too large for what they are. Broad, frond-like moss, each leaf the width of a human hand, rising in loose columns that stand six or eight inches tall. They are a yellow-green, almost electric in the flat light, and they are wet, of course, everything here is wet, the moss beading with water that it seems to generate from the air itself. When you step on it, it compresses with a sound like a squeezed sponge and slowly, slowly rebounds.

Between the moss-columns, spaced with a deliberateness that feels almost curated, stand taller plants — something fern-like but not fern. Their fronds are broad and strap-shaped, dark green, almost black at the edges, and they grow in single stalks rather than clumps, each one separated from its neighbors by several feet of open, water-sheeted ground. They do not crowd. They do not compete. They simply stand, dripping, their tips curling slightly inward as if listening.

Up on the walls of the hollow, where the stone weeps its continuous tears, the vegetation changes. Long, trailing strands of something like a lichen but with the body of a liverwort hang in curtains from the upper rim, pale gray-green, swaying almost imperceptibly in air currents you cannot feel. They are dense enough in places to partially obscure the stone behind them, and water runs down their lengths in unbroken films, so that the walls seem to be draped in living, wet fabric. The curtains move with such slowness that you only notice they have moved by remembering where they were before.

There are flowers, or things that serve as flowers. On a few of the fern-like stalks, at the crown, a single structure has grown — not a blossom but a kind of cupped leaf, pale and waxy, that collects water. Inside each cup, a thin layer of rainwater or condensation sits, and in it, small insects have drowned and are dissolving. The plants are feeding. This is not unusual in itself — pitcher plants, bladderworts, the ordinary carnivory of wet places — but the stillness of it, the patience, the way each cup holds its small death with such composure, feels particular to this place. Everything here eats slowly.

## The Air and the Light

The air is warm. Not hot — there is a moderation to it, a steadiness, as though the hollow sits in its own thermal pocket, insulated from the altitude by some mechanism of stone and water and vegetation. At this elevation, you would expect cold, thin air, a wind that bites. Instead the air rests against the skin like a damp cloth warmed by a body that has just left the bed. Humidity is near total. You can see it — not as fog, not as mist in the hollow itself, but as a brightness at the edges, a luminous haze that hangs just beyond the rim, so that the sky above is clear and the world below is erased.

Light enters from above. It is normal daylight — a gray-gold, flat and even, the kind that comes with high altitude and high cloud cover — but inside the hollow it takes on qualities shaped by the space. The wet stone reflects it downward. The water-film on the floor scatters it laterally. The result is a light that seems to come from no single direction, a diffuse, shadowless illumination that renders everything in soft, equal detail. There are no dark corners here. Nothing hides. The light is honest and unremarkable and somehow, because of the acoustics and the moisture and the strangeness of the plants, it feels like the light in a room where something has already happened and you have arrived too late to see what.

## The Animals

You will not see them. That is the rule here, or nearly. The hollow is not empty of fauna — the drowned insects in the pitcher-cups prove that, and if you sit long enough and watch the moss with a patience the place itself seems to demand, you will notice movement. Small things. A thread of silver in the water-film — something aquatic, something legless, traversing the shallow sheet with a motion so smooth it might be a trick of the light. A pulse in the moss, a contraction, as though something beneath the surface has shifted. Once, maybe, a shape crosses the upper rim — a bird, or something that occupies the ecological niche of a bird — and is gone before the eye can fix it. It made no sound. The water-chord swallowed its silence.

The fauna here are not absent. They are simply subordinate to the larger presences — the stone, the water, the plants. Animals are incidental in this place. They pass through. They do not stay. The hollow does not belong to them.

## The Feeling of Time

This is the deepest quality of the Weeping Hollow, the one that takes longest to feel. The place is old. Not old in the way that mountains are old — that kind of age is abstract, a number, a fact geologists trade. Old in the way that a still pond is old. The hollow has reached a state of equilibrium so complete that change has become nearly imperceptible. The water has been weeping from the same fractures for long enough that the stone has been grooved into channels. The moss has been growing in the same columns for long enough that the underlying soil is built entirely from its own dead predecessors, layer on layer, a peat of ancient green. The fern-things stand where they have always stood. Nothing encroaches. Nothing retreats. The balance is not static — water moves, plants grow, insects die — but the balance holds. It has held for longer than anything here can remember, and nothing here can remember.

If you sit in the hollow for an hour, you will feel it: a slowing. Not of the body but of the sense of time. The water-chord becomes more complex the longer you listen, not because it changes but because your hearing adjusts to its complexity. The moss seems to breathe. The light does not move. The warmth does not fluctuate. And slowly, without drama, the hollow begins to feel like a place that is waiting — not for anything specific, not for you, not for an event — but waiting in the way that a body waits between heartbeats, in the way that stone waits between eras. It is a patience so vast it has become a property of the air, and it includes you, and it does not need you, and it will continue long after the water has carved the notch in the rim deeper and the hollow has opened like a flower and the weeping has finally, geologically, stopped.

But that will not be today. That will not be soon. The hollow weeps. The chord sounds. The moss beads. The light lies flat. The place holds.
