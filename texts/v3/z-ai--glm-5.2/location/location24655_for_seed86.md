# The Roaring Dark

The first thing is the sound. Before the place has shape, before your eyes adjust, there is sound — a compressed, reverberating roar that seems to come from every direction at once and no direction at all. It is not one sound but thousands, layered and folded against stone until they become a single living hum. Chittering. Clicking. The wet scrape of bodies against rock. High, thin vocalizations that race along the ceiling like water finding a channel. And beneath all of it, lower than you can quite hear but feel in your sternum, a rhythmic thrumming — wingbeats, countless wingbeats, folded into the stone acoustics until the air itself seems to pulse.

You are underground. Not in a tunnel — nothing so confined. This is a chamber, or rather a system of chambers, so large that the far walls exist only as a suggestion, a darkening of dimness into absolute black. The ceiling rises somewhere above, lost in shadow except where faint light enters through a network of fissures — cracks in the rock overhead, some no wider than a finger, some broad enough to admit a pale grey wedge of sky. The light that does get in is scattered, diffused, stripped of warmth by the time it reaches the floor. It catches moisture in the air, glints off wet surfaces, and gives the whole space a submerged quality, as though you are looking at everything through shallow, still water.

---

The stone is young. That is the word that comes to mind, though it is a strange thing to say about rock. The formations are sharp. Stalactites descend in angular, almost crystalline geometries — not the soft, rounded columns of ancient caves worn smooth by millennia of slow water, but jagged, toothed things, their surfaces still ridged with the record of how they formed, layer by layer, each ridge crisp. The floor is uneven, broken into terraces and shallow basins by a network of cracks that run through the limestone like veins. Some of these cracks are dry. Others weep — a slow, patient seepage that collects in the basins and forms shallow pools, never more than a few inches deep, never clear, always faintly clouded with mineral sediment that settles into a pale film on their surfaces. The water is cold. You know this without touching it. The air around the pools carries a specific kind of coolness, a dampness that is different from the general cool of the chamber — sharper, more local, carrying the smell of wet calcium and something faintly organic, like the ghost of pond water.

The rock itself is a pale grey, almost white where moisture has cleaned it, darkening to a deep blue-grey in the dry recesses. In places the surface has a peculiar texture — not smooth, not rough, but fibrous, as though the stone were made of compressed threads. These are the places where the limestone has fractured along lines of included fossil material, ancient shells and coral fragments that have left their shapes pressed into the rock like fossils in a museum drawer. The young erosion has exposed them recently, geologically speaking. The surfaces are fresh. The edges are keen. You could cut yourself on some of the protrusions if you moved carelessly.

---

There is vegetation, but it does not command attention. It exists in the margins, in the places where moisture and the faint light from the fissures overlap — thin films of moss, a deep, almost black green, growing in the cracks where water seeps. Here and there, where a broader fracture admits a shaft of dim light, a sparse colony of ferns has taken hold, their fronds pale and unnaturally elongated, reaching for the light with a desperate, slow-motion urgency that makes each one look like a hand grasping upward. There are no flowers. There is no color in the vegetation that would register as color in this light — everything is a variant of grey-green or yellowish white. In the deepest recesses, far from the fissures, there are fungal growths — pale, rounded forms that cling to the wet rock like blisters, not luminous but somehow lighter than the surrounding stone, as though they generate a faint warmth that the eye reads as brightness. They do not move. Nothing here moves in the way that plants move with wind. There is no wind. The air is still, and has been still, it seems, for a very long time.

---

But if the vegetation is sparse, the life here is not. The fauna is overwhelming.

The ceiling is the first thing you notice once your eyes adjust — and then you wish you had not looked up, because the ceiling is covered. Covered. Not uniformly, not in a blanket, but in clusters and colonies, in dense roosting masses that cling to the rough stone in their hundreds, their thousands. Bats. But not one species — many. You can see the difference even in the dim light by their size, their posture, the way they cluster. Some are small, with bodies no larger than a thumb, and they roost in tight, pressed groups that look from below like a single dark, furred surface. Others are larger, with wingspans perhaps a foot across, and they hang with more space between them, each one distinct, each one twitching, cleaning, folding and unfolding one wing and then the other. And there are others — much larger, darker shapes in the higher recesses, visible only as silhouettes against the faint light from the fissures, hanging motionless except for the slow, rhythmic expansion and contraction of their ribcages. These might have wingspans of two feet or more. You cannot tell. They are too far up, too deep in the shadow.

The sound — the roar that fills the chamber — is partly theirs. The high-frequency chittering, the social vocalizations, the scrape of claws on stone. But there are other sounds too, and these belong to the other life.

The floor moves. Not dramatically, not like something is coming toward you, but in patches, in areas where the dim light catches movement. Insects — not the kind you would expect in a cave, not pale, eyeless troglobites, but dark, hard-shelled forms, beetles of some kind, that move across the wet stone in loose, purposeful formations. They are not uniformly distributed. They cluster around the pools, around the organic matter that has fallen from above — guano, bat droppings, which accumulates on the floor in layered deposits that are damp and compact and smell faintly of ammonia and earth. The beetles process this material. You can hear them, if you crouch close: a faint, wet clicking, like tiny fingers tapping on damp paper.

There are spiders too. Not webs in the corners — these are hunting spiders, long-legged, fast-moving, that run across the stone floors and walls in quick dashes. They are larger than you would like. Some have legspans of three inches or more. They navigate by vibration, by the sounds of the beetles and the other insects, and they strike with a precision that suggests they have done this many, many times, in this same place, in this same way, for a very long time.

And there are things in the pools. Not fish — nothing that large or that visible — but smaller organisms, crustaceans perhaps, pale and translucent, that move along the bottom of the shallow basins with a slow, deliberate motion. When disturbed — when a beetle approaches the edge to drink, when a pebble drops in — they retreat into the sediment and vanish. Then, after a stillness, they emerge again, and resume their slow patrol of the pool floor as though nothing happened.

The larger animals — the mammals — are not limited to the bats. In the broader passages, where the chamber connects to tunnels that lead away into darkness, there are signs of regular use. Scratches on the walls at consistent heights — claw marks, deep and fresh, grooved into the soft limestone. Scat, dried and dark, deposited in what appear to be territorial markers. And in one passage, a wide, low corridor that slopes gently downward, there is a broad, flat depression in the guano layer — a resting place, a bed, used repeatedly by something large enough to leave a body print five feet long. What made it, you cannot say. You hear something, deeper in the corridor, a slow, heavy exhalation. Then silence. Then the same exhalation again. Rhythmic. Sleeping. Or resting. Or simply being, in a place where it has been for a long time and will continue to be, because nothing here changes.

---

The place is in equilibrium. That is what strikes you most as you spend time here — not the strangeness of the life, not the scale of the chamber, but the absolute, almost oppressive stability of it. The light does not change. The temperature does not fluctuate. The moisture does not increase or decrease. The bats roost and wake and fly and return, but they do not migrate, do not shift their colonies. The beetles process the guano at the same rate it is deposited. The spiders hunt the beetles at the same rate the beetles reproduce. The pools fill at the same rate they drain. The water level does not rise after rain because the rain does not reach here — or if it does, it does so through the same channels, at the same rate, always, and the system has absorbed it into its balance long ago.

Even the erosion seems to have slowed to a crawl. The stone is young, fresh, sharp — but it is not getting sharper. The formations are not growing visibly, not in the timescale of a visit, not in the timescale of a lifetime. They are where they are. The dripping that formed them continues, but the deposition and the dissolution have reached a balance — each drop adds as much as it removes, and the stalactite maintains its shape, its edge, its precise geometry, indefinitely.

The sound is constant. The bats do not quiet. The insects do not rest. The chorus does not swell or diminish but holds at one volume, one texture, one unbroken wall of biological sound that fills the chamber from floor to ceiling. It is loud enough that you feel it in your teeth, in your sinuses, in the bones behind your ears. It is the sound of a living system that has found its frequency and holds it.

---

There are traces of people. Not structures — nothing built, nothing assembled, nothing that rises from the natural rock with the intentionality of construction. But marks. On one wall, near a pool that catches the most light from a broad fissure above, there are scratches that are not animal scratches — they are too regular, too intentional. Lines, crossed lines, a pattern that might be a map or might be a count or might be nothing, made by a hand holding something sharp. The marks are not deep. They have not cut far into the stone. But they are there, and they are deliberate.

Nearby, on the floor, half-buried in the guano and the beetle-processed sediment, there is a stone. It is not remarkable — a piece of chert, dark grey, about the size of a fist, broken from a larger piece. But one edge has been worked. Flakes have been struck from it, leaving a sharp, curved edge that fits a human hand. It is a tool, or the beginning of one. It is old. It has been here long enough to be partly absorbed into the floor deposit, to become part of the layer. But it was made. Someone sat here, in this dim light, near this pool, and struck a stone against another stone until it had an edge.

There are a few other traces — a blackened patch on the ceiling of a low alcove, the remnant of a small fire, long since cooled, its smoke stain preserved by the still, damp air. A pile of broken shells near one pool — snail shells, cracked open in a consistent way, the work of something that ate here repeatedly. Whether the something was human or animal is impossible to say.

Whoever came here did not stay. Did not build. Did not leave more than these fragments — the tool, the marks, the smoke stain, the shells. Perhaps they came to collect something. Perhaps they came to hunt — the bats, the larger animals in the passages. Perhaps they came because the chamber is here, and humans go into places that are here. But they left, and the chamber continued without them, and the beetles processed the guano, and the bats roosted, and the water seeped, and the sound held.

---

You stand in the chamber and the sound is everything. It is so loud that silence, when it comes — when you step outside, when the passage closes behind you — feels like a physical pressure, a popping in your ears, a sudden absence that is almost more overwhelming than the sound was. The chamber lives without you. It lived before the tool was dropped, before the marks were scratched, before the fire was lit. It will live after. The bats will roost. The beetles will process. The spiders will hunt. The pools will fill and drain. The stone will hold its shape. The light will fall through the fissures in the same dim, scattered pattern, and the air will carry the same cold damp, and the sound will continue — that vast, layered, unchanging roar of a system that found its balance long ago and sees no reason to change.
