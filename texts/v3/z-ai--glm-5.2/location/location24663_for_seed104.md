# The Drowned Plateau of Aur

There is no shore.

There hasn't been one for a length of time that no longer has a useful name. The ocean here — if you can call it an ocean; it is warmer than any ocean should be, and it does not move the way oceans move — sits on top of the world. Not at sea level. Not in a basin. *On top.* It occupies a continental plateau so vast and so elevated that its surface would, in another physics, be indistinguishable from a sky. The water is warm. Not tepid, not tropical — warm the way a living body is warm, the way blood is warm, carrying heat from somewhere internal and structural. The temperature does not come from the sun. The sun is a dim copper smudge, visible only as a brightening of the murk overhead, a rumor of light filtered through what might be kilometers of water or might be something else entirely that merely behaves like water, that has learned to.

You descend — or the equivalent of descending, since direction has begun to lose its conviction — through layers.

---

**The first layer** is the canopy.

Gigantic flora grow upward from the plateau floor, and *upward* is the wrong word because they also grow sideways, inward, and in directions that do not correspond to any axis you can name. They are not kelp. They are not trees. They are something that predates the distinction between plant and geological formation — between *growing* and *accreting.* Their trunks, if trunks they are, reach hundreds of meters. Ribbed. Wet with a substance thicker than water, a sap or lymph that glows faintly, a sick amber-green, the only reliable light source in this place. The glow is biological. It comes from colonies of microorganisms living in the outer tissue of the plants, and it pulses — not rhythmically, but *conversationally.* One trunk brightens and another, hundreds of meters away, dims. They are speaking, or doing the thing that in this place passes for speaking. The light is dim by any standard. You could read by it, barely. It makes everything the color of old bruises.

The canopy is dense. The trunks grow close enough that their upper structures interlace, forming a lattice, a living ceiling. Between the trunks, smaller growths fill the gaps: sheet-like organisms, translucent, hanging like curtains; clusters of bulbous pods that open and close slowly, filtering nutrients from the water; tendrils that drift in the faint current, each one as long as a river and as thin as thread. Nothing here is sparse. The saturation is total. Every cubic meter of water holds something living, or something that was living and is now becoming something else. You move through this layer the way a diver moves through a wreck — carefully, constantly adjusting, because the space between one living wall and the next is barely a body's width.

There are no fish here. No animals at all, not in the first layer, not in the second. The fauna parameter is vanishingly low. This is a world that evolved past the need for mobile life, or never arrived at it, or arrived at it and then set it aside the way you set aside a tool you no longer use. What moves here is moved by chemistry, by current, by the slow contraction and expansion of tissues. The ecosystem is complete without consumers. Everything is a producer. Everything feeds everything else through proximity, through contact, through the gradual dissolution of one surface against another.

---

**The second layer** is the midwater, below the canopy.

Here the trunks thin slightly, and the space between them opens into something like a cathedral interior — not vast emptiness, but *structured* space, a geometry of columns and crossbeams and hanging growths that creates the impression of architecture. The water is warmer here. The current is stronger. It moves in slow, massive rotations — not tides, exactly, but thermal cycles, the plateau breathing. Warm water rises from below, cools as it reaches the canopy, sinks again along the outer edges. This circulation has been running for so long that it has worn channels in the living rock — not stone, not coral, something between — channeling the flow into rivers within the water, invisible currents that you enter and exit without warning, each one carrying a different temperature, a different density, a different taste of mineral and compound.

The silence here is profound. Not the absence of sound — there are sounds, many of them — but their character. Everything is muffled. The density of the water, the density of the growth, the density of the *atmosphere* of this place — all of it dampens vibration. What you hear, when you listen, is a low, continuous murmur that seems to come from everywhere at once. It is the sound of biological process. The sound of tissues growing and dissolving. The sound of chemical reactions occurring at a rate too slow for human perception but too constant to be silent. If you press your ear to one of the trunks — and you can, because they are everywhere, you cannot avoid touching them, the space is that dense — you hear a deep, rhythmic thrum. Not a heartbeat. A *processing.* The trunk is converting one substance into another. It has been doing this for longer than most geological eras.

Here and there, in the second layer, you find the structures.

They are not buildings. They are barely *built.* Low walls, knee-high if you had knees, made of the same material as the plateau floor — that living rock, that half-coral half-stone substance — shaped and stacked by something that had hands or the equivalent. The shapes are simple. Rectangles. Circles. A few spirals that might be decorative or might be functional or might be the remains of a language written in three dimensions. They are old. Not as old as the plateau. Not as old as the trunks. But old enough that the vegetation has grown *through* them, incorporating them the way a tree incorporates a fence post left too long against its trunk. The walls are not ruins. They are *inclusions.* The forest has eaten them and kept them and made them part of itself.

There is no technology here. No tools. No metal. No shaped stone beyond these simple walls. Whatever made these structures either never developed beyond stacking, or developed past everything and returned to stacking as a final gesture, a last mark. The walls do not enclose anything. They do not protect anything. They simply *are,* and their being is enough to mark this as a place where something once stood and looked at the water and thought, briefly, about shapes.

---

**The third layer** is the floor.

You reach it not by descending — you have been descending, but the word loses meaning — but by passing through a transition zone where the trunks end. They end all at once, as if following a rule. Below a certain depth, they do not grow. The floor of the plateau is visible here, if *visible* is the word, in the amber-green glow that filters down from the canopy above. It is flat. Not gently sloping, not rolling — *flat,* the way something becomes flat after being worn down for an amount of time that makes the word *erosion* seem too active, too dramatic. This is not erosion. This is the state that erosion arrives at when it has finished its work and has nothing left to do. The floor is smooth. It is warm. It is alive, or once was, or is in the process of deciding.

The floor is covered with a mat of growth — not plants, not moss, not bacterial film, but something that is all of these and none of these. It is thin. A few centimeters thick. It covers everything. When you touch it — and you will touch it, because it is everywhere — it is warm and dry, despite the water, as if the water does not quite reach it, as if there is a boundary layer, a skin between the liquid and the solid that the liquid cannot cross. The mat is the oldest thing here. Possibly the oldest thing anywhere. It has been growing and dying and growing again on this floor since before the trunks reached the canopy, since before the canopy formed, since before the water found its way to the top of the world. It is the primordial surface. The first and final condition.

Here, the reality begins to fray.

It starts subtly. The light — already dim, already uncertain — begins to behave incorrectly. Shadows fall in directions that do not correspond to the glow of the trunks above. The floor extends in directions that should, by any geometry, curve back on themselves but do not. You walk — or drift, or whatever you do here — in a straight line and arrive at a point you have already passed, but the point has changed, or you have changed, or *change itself* has become a location rather than a process. The water, which has been consistently warm and consistently dense, begins to vary — not in temperature, but in *quality.* Some of it is more water than other parts of it. Some of it is closer to a memory of water, or an intention of water, and you can feel the difference as you pass through it, a thinness, a lag, as if reality is rendering one frame behind itself.

The vegetation, down here, grows in patterns that are not patterns. Spirals that do not close. Branches that join their own stems. Loops of tissue that form complete circles, feeding into themselves, and when you follow the circle with your hand you find that it does not have a beginning or an end, that it is not a growth but a *state,* a shape that matter takes when it has been in one place for long enough to forget what shape it is supposed to be.

---

There is weather here, despite the submersion.

Not storms — nothing so violent, nothing so *narrative.* But the plateau has its own climate, its own internal weather system, born of the thermal cycles and the biological output of the forest and the sheer scale of the place. It is continental in extent. The water above it moves as a body, as a single mass with its own inertia, and when that mass shifts — when the slow rotation changes speed, when the thermal gradient between floor and canopy alters by a fraction of a degree — the change propagates as something that is not quite a current and not quite a wave and not quite a sound but is felt by everything in the water at once. The trunks respond. The canopy pulses. The mat on the floor tightens or loosens. It is a breathing. The whole plateau breathes, and the breath takes days, and when it exhales, the water moves, and the light shifts, and the geometry of the space reconfigures itself into a new arrangement that will hold for another cycle before shifting again.

This has been happening for longer than the walls have been walls.

This will continue happening for longer than the walls will be anything at all.

---

And in the quiet — in the deep, saturated, *physical* quiet of a place where sound is dampened by the sheer density of existence — you become aware of one more thing. The plateau is not passive. It is not a setting. It is *doing* something. The biological processes, the chemical conversions, the growth and dissolution and regrowth — they are not aimless. They are not entropy. Something is being produced here. Something is being maintained. The mat on the floor is processing the water, the trunks are processing the mat, the canopy is processing the light, and the whole system, vast and ancient and almost silent, is engaged in an activity that, if you had to name it, you would call *staying.*

The plateau is staying.

It has been staying for so long that staying has become its nature, its purpose, its physics. It will not erode further because it has already reached the shape it wants to be. It will not change because change is the process by which things arrive at their final form, and this has arrived. The water will not drain. The forest will not die. The light will not brighten or darken. The walls will not be added to or taken away. Everything that is here is here because this is where it ends up when everything else has been tried.

This is the landscape at the end of process. Not dead. Not finished. *Settled.* The way sediment settles. The way a river finds its final course and runs it forever. The way a sound, after echoing long enough, becomes the room it was spoken in.

You are in the room. It is warm. It is dim. It is full. It is quiet in the way that only the most ancient things are quiet — not because nothing is happening, but because everything that is happening has been happening for so long that it has become indistinguishable from silence. The glow of the trunks pulses. The mat beneath you holds. The water carries you in its slow rotation, and you turn with it, and the turning is the weather, and the weather is the breathing, and the breathing is the staying, and the staying is the oldest law this place knows.
