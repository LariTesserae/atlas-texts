# The Humming Underneath

## First Impression

The sound finds you before the place does.

Not a sound — a pressure. A vibration that enters through the sternum and travels the ribs like a struck tuning fork. It is not one thing making this sound but ten thousand things, each adding its frequency to a total that stops being audible and becomes atmospheric. Your jaw aches. Your vision hums at the edges.

You are below. Not far below — the stone overhead is close enough that in places you could press both palms flat against it, and in others it lifts to a cathedral emptiness you cannot see the top of. But the ceiling is not stone everywhere. In many places it is root. In more places it is *something else.*

---

## The Substrate

There is no soil here. There is no water.

What there is — and this takes time to understand because it is everywhere, because it is the floor and the walls and the medium through which you move — is plant matter. Not dead, compressed, fossilized plant matter like coal. Living plant matter. Or something adjacent to living. The entire substrate beneath your feet is a woven mat of rhizome-like structures, pale as candle wax, branching and rebranching in fractal networks that extend in every direction including down, always down, into a darkness that suggests depth without confirming it. When you step, the surface gives slightly, like walking on a trampoline made of tendon. It is warm. Body-warm. Warmer than the air, which is itself warm — a wet warmth, paradoxically, given the near-total absence of liquid water.

Because there is no water. Not as you know it. What cycles through these plants — and something cycles through them, you can see it, faint pulses of movement beneath the translucent skin of the larger rhizomes — is something else. A fluid the color of weak tea, thinner than water, with a surface tension that seems almost nonexistent. It beads incorrectly. It climbs. You see it moving *up* the walls of root-mass in thin films, defying nothing because gravity seems to be a suggestion here, not a law. The plants drink it. The plants are made of it. It is not water and it is doing the work of water and this is the first wrong thing you must accept.

The substrate breathes. Not metaphorically. The mat beneath your feet rises and falls on a cycle of roughly eleven seconds — a slow inhalation, a slower release. When it rises, the pale fluid moves visibly through the rhizome channels, brightening them. When it falls, they dim. You are standing on a lung.

---

## The Vegetation

The word "vegetation" is insufficient but language was built in a world that is not this one.

The plant-life here is not ornament. It is architecture. It is geology. It is the medium of existence. Above the rhizome-mat, the space is filled — *filled*, in the way a sponge is filled with water, in the way a crowd fills a square — with structures that are undeniably botanical in origin but have departed so far from the template of leaf-and-stem that applying the word "plant" feels like a taxonomic shrug.

There are columns. They grow from the mat and rise to the ceiling, sometimes branching into structural arches that meet and fuse with other columns, forming load-bearing frames that hold the underground space open. They are not wood. They are not cellulose, exactly. They have the color and translucency of amber, and inside them you can see dark threads — vascular structures, perhaps — winding upward like smoke frozen mid-rise. They creak. Each column produces a low, groaning tone as it sways microscopically in the breathing cycle of the mat, and because there are hundreds of them, each with its own pitch, the cumulative sound is a chord. A chord that shifts. That never resolves.

Between the columns, filling the space at every level — knee-high, waist-high, overhead, everywhere — are *masses*. There is no better word. Irregular growths that might be fronds, might be organs, might be the equivalent of coral polyps expanded to the size of medicine balls. They are dark green at the edges, shading to near-black at their cores, and they pulse. Not with the breathing cycle. With something faster. Something that looks, disturbingly, like peristalsis. These masses are layered — three, four, five deep in places — so that moving through this space means pushing through curtains of living material that yield and close behind you, that stick briefly to your skin with a mild adhesion that feels curious rather than predatory, that *taste* the air around you through some chemical sense you cannot identify.

And threading through all of it — the columns, the masses, the mat, the ceiling roots — are filaments. Hair-thin, luminous, the color of milk. They are everywhere. They form a web so dense that at a distance they create the illusion of fog, of suspended particulate, of weather indoors. They catch light from sources you cannot initially locate and scatter it into a diffuse ambient glow that is neither bright nor dim. It is simply *present*, the way light is present on an overcast day. You can see. You cannot see far. The filaments tremble constantly, reacting to vibrations in the air — and since everything here vibrates, they are always in motion, a shimmering that makes the whole space feel like it is underwater, or like you are inside something's cornea.

Some of the filaments carry the pale fluid. You see it moving through them in rapid pulses, like signals in a fiber-optic cable. The network is communicating. Or distributing. Or both.

---

## The Fauna

And then there are the *things that move.*

The sound — the deafening, bone-conducting roar — is mostly them.

They are not insects. They are not mammals. They are not birds or reptiles or fish, though each of these words flickers through your mind as a comparison and is discarded. They are the fauna of a world where the plants are the geology and the water is not water, and they have evolved accordingly, which is to say they have evolved *strangely.*

The most numerous — the ones responsible for the bulk of the sound — are small. Thumb-sized. They move in groups of thousands, perhaps millions, swarming through the filament-web like fish through kelp, and they *click*. Each click is a tiny percussive snap, mechanical-sounding, like a knuckle cracking. A million knuckles cracking, continuously, in rolling waves that move through the swarm as density shifts. The swarm itself is dark — nearly black — but each individual has a stripe of the milk-luminous color along its back, so that in motion the swarm looks like a river of static, of television snow, flowing through the air with purpose and speed. They do not avoid you. They pass through you, or rather through the space you occupy, and their bodies are soft, warm, and faintly moist against your skin. The contact lasts a fraction of a second per individual but there are so many that it becomes a sustained sensation, like being sandblasted with warm rice.

They are eating the filaments. Or harvesting them. Or doing something to them that results in the filaments being broken and rapidly regrown, a process that happens so continuously that the web is in a state of permanent reconstruction, tearing and healing simultaneously.

Larger things move in the upper space, beyond the masses, where the columns arch toward the ceiling. You see them in fragments — a dark curve of something limb-like, a flash of translucent membrane, a joint that bends in too many places. They call to each other in sounds that are not clicks but *tones*, long and resonant and harmonic, and when two or more of these tones overlap they create beats — interference patterns that you feel in your teeth. These calls are loud enough to be individually distinguishable within the wall of sound, which means they are *very* loud, and they seem to serve a territorial or mating function, because the callers move toward each other when they call, and the space above shifts and shakes as they collide, grapple, or mate — you cannot tell which, and the distinction may not be meaningful here.

On the ground — on the breathing mat — things crawl. Things with too many legs and not enough symmetry. Things that are long and flat and move like tongues tasting a surface. Things that are round and roll. Things that sit in depressions in the mat and do not appear to move at all until you realize they are moving, just very slowly, eating the rhizome from the inside, creating the channels through which the pale fluid flows. They are the erosion. They are the weather. The mat is being consumed and regrown in a continuous cycle and these slow ones are the consumers, and the plants are growing faster than they can be eaten, and the excess growth is what creates the columns and the masses and the filaments, an economy of biomass that has nowhere to go but *up* and *out* and *into itself.*

There are things that eat the clickers. You see this happen. A growth on a column — one of the dark masses — suddenly dilates, opens like a pupil, reveals a wet interior that is the same amber color as the column but translucent, and the clicker-swarm flows past and a fraction of it — thousands of individuals — veers into the opening as if drawn, and the opening closes, and the mass pulses once, and the clickers are gone. The mass returns to its previous state. It looks identical. You count: it happens every forty to sixty seconds. The columns are feeding. The architecture is alive and hungry.

---

## The Structures

In the midst of this — not apart from it, not above it, but *within* it, the way a hermit crab lives within a shell — there are signs of habitation.

Not by the fauna. By something else. By something that used tools.

The structures are small. Rare. You find them in the spaces between columns where the masses thin slightly and the filament-web is less dense, creating pockets of relative — relative — quiet. They are made from the same materials as everything else. Rhizome-segments, cut and shaped, lashed together with filament-bundles. Small enclosures, barely large enough for a person to crouch in. Openings that face away from the dominant flow of the clicker-swarms. Inside, there are marks — scratches on the amber surface of the rhizome, regular and deliberate, that might be tallies, might be language, might be decoration. There are tools: a cutting implement made from a sharpened mass-fragment, still faintly pulsing. A container — a hollowed section of column, sealed at both ends with packed mat-material, holding a small reservoir of the pale fluid. A scraping tool. A hook.

The technology is Stone Age adapted to a world without stone. Everything is bioplastic, living or recently living, shaped by hand and friction and the application of that curious non-water fluid, which seems to soften the rhizome-material when applied in quantity, allowing it to be molded before it hardens again.

There are not many of these structures. A handful across the area you can explore. But they show repair, modification, reuse. Someone has been here for a long time. Someone has learned to live in the lung.

---

## The Atmosphere

The air is thick. Not with moisture — there is no water to moisturize it — but with *particulate.* Spores. Cells. Fragments of filament too small to see, floating free. The luminous dust that gives the space its diffuse light also fills the air, and you breathe it in, and it tastes like nothing you can name. Mineral and vegetal simultaneously. Warm. Faintly sweet in a way that sits wrong on the tongue, the way artificial sweeteners sit wrong, the body expecting something that isn't there.

The warmth is biological. The mat generates it. The columns generate it. The masses generate it. The combined metabolic output of this ungodly density of living material has heated the space to a temperature that is comfortable for the plants and the fauna and, presumably, for whoever built those small structures. It is the warmth of a compost heap, of a body, of a room full of people. It is the warmth of *process.*

The light — and you eventually trace its source — comes from the filaments themselves. Not all of them. A subset. They glow with a steady bioluminescence that brightens and dims on no cycle you can identify, creating a light that is adequate but never still, that shifts and flows so that shadows move without anything casting them, so that the space seems to *breathe* visually as well as physically.

---

## The Rules

This place has laws, but they are not your laws.

Gravity is negotiable. The pale fluid ignores it. Some of the fauna ignore it — the clickers move in three dimensions with equal facility, and the things in the upper space seem to rest on the filament-web as if it were solid, their weight supported by the tension of a million hair-thin threads.

Growth is the default. Everything here is growing. The mat is growing. The columns are growing — you can see it happen, the amber material extruding upward at a rate that should be invisible but is not, is almost perceptible, like watching a clock hand that you know is moving. The masses are growing. The filaments are growing. The space is being *filled,* actively, and the things that live here — the clickers, the crawlers, the slow eaters, the feeders — exist to check that growth, to consume the excess, to keep the system from sealing itself entirely. They are not parasites. They are *regulators.* Without them, the plants would close every space, fill every gap, and the underground would become a solid block of living material. The fauna are the erosion that the geology lacks.

Water is absent and irrelevant. The pale fluid — whatever it is — does everything water does and several things water cannot. It solvates. It transports. It heats. It cools. It adheres and climbs and penetrates. It is not water. It is *what water would be if water had evolved.*

And the sound. The sound is the sound of all of this happening at once. The clicking. The calling. The groaning of columns. The peristaltic pulse of masses. The tearing and regrowing of filaments. The slow crunch of the eaters in the mat. The breathing. The breathing. The breathing. It does not stop. It does not quiet. It is the sound of a place that is alive in every cubic centimeter, that is building itself and eating itself and building again, and it is the loudest thing you have ever heard, and after a time — an hour, perhaps, or a day, time being another negotiable law here — you stop hearing it as sound and start hearing it as *voice.* The place is speaking. The place is saying, continuously, with every breath and click and groan: *I am here. I am here. I am here. I am still here.*

## What Emerges

You begin to notice the patterns.

The clicker-swarms do not move randomly. Their flow follows channels in the filament-web, and the channels correspond to the vascular threads in the columns, and the vascular threads correspond to the rhizome-network in the mat. The entire system — floor to ceiling, plant to animal, structure to inhabitant — is a single continuous circuit. The fluid moves up through the columns, out through the masses, into the filaments, where the clickers harvest it, process it, and deposit it back into the mat as waste, where the rhizomes absorb it and send it up again. The fauna are not separate from the plants. They are *part of the plants.* Mobile organs. Detachable mouths. The system has externalized its digestion, its reproduction, its defense, and called them animals.

The small structures — the huts, the tools — exist at nodes in this circuit. Places where the flow slows. Places where the fluid pools in the rhizome and can be collected. Whoever built them understood the system. Lived inside it. Was, perhaps, another mobile organ — the part that *builds,* that creates internal structure the way bone creates structure inside a body. The tools are not separate from the ecosystem. The cutting implements are made from the same material as the columns. The containers hold the same fluid that flows through everything. The inhabitants were not *in* the system. They were *of* it.

And the system is still growing. Still finding new configurations. The columns in one area have begun growing at an angle, creating a spiral that was not there before. The masses in another area have changed color — darker, denser, their pulse-rate doubled. The clicker-swarms have altered their routes to accommodate these changes. The filaments are rewiring. This is not a stable ecosystem. It is an *embryonic* one. It is still figuring out what it wants to be.

The ceiling, in one place, has cracked. Not stone — the root-mass has split, and through the split you can see sky. A dim sky, amber-colored, but sky. The system is reaching upward. It is not content with the underground. It is *emerging.*

And in the newly opened space, at the boundary between below and above, the plants are already growing. New columns, thinner, more flexible, reaching toward the light with the blind urgency of something that has just discovered a direction it did not know existed. The clickers are already there, swarming around the new growth. The masses are already forming. The circuit is already extending.

The sound does not change. The sound does not need to change. The sound was always the sound of expansion, of a system becoming more of itself, and it will continue as long as there is space to fill and material to convert and the warm dark in which to do it.

You are standing inside something that is waking up. You are standing inside something that is becoming. And the small structures at the nodes, and the tools within them, and the marks on their walls — these are not the remnants of a civilization that lived here. They are the first stirrings of a civilization that is *being grown,* the way bone is grown, the way root is grown, the way the pale fluid climbs the wall because that is what it does.

The lung breathes. The sound continues. The place says its one word, over and over, in ten thousand voices:

*I am here. I am here. I am here. I am still here.*
