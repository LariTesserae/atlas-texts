# The Weeping Terrace

It begins with sound. Not sound — Sound. A single unbroken chord that fills the chest cavity before the ears can sort it, a pressure more than a noise, like standing inside a bell that was struck once and never stopped ringing. It comes from everywhere. It comes from the rock. It comes from the water. It comes from the green.

You are high. You know this because the sky is close — not metaphorically close but *close*, the way a ceiling is close, a pale luminous lid pressed down over the world with cloud-stain spreading across its inner surface like bruising. The air is thin and scalding. Each breath arrives hot and wet, saturated, almost drinkable. Your lungs feel full before you've finished inhaling. There is no breeze. There has never been a breeze. The air simply *is*, motionless and dense, a thermal bath held in place by some rule that does not include convection.

Below — and "below" is a word that strains here, because the geometry keeps slipping — a terrace-city unfolds across the flank of something immense. Not a mountain. Mountains have peaks. This has no peak. It rises behind you and above you and then continues rising in ways that the eye refuses to track, the slopes curving inward at angles that suggest the whole mass might be folding over on itself, slowly, over geological time, like a fist closing. But it is not changing. Nothing is changing. The fist was always here, mid-close, and will always be here, mid-close. The rock is dark, almost black, wetted permanent, streaked with mineral deposits in reds and yellows and pale chemical whites that have run down the stone faces and frozen in their tracks — not frozen by cold but frozen by *stasis*, by the absolute refusal of this place to proceed from one moment to the next. The streaks look like tears. The rock looks like it has been weeping for so long that the weeping became architecture.

And it *is* architecture. The terraces are built. Not constructed — not with tools, not with hands — but *built* in the way a coral reef is built, by accumulation, by the patient deposition of form upon form upon form. Walls rise from the rock face in courses of fitted stone that might be stone and might be compressed organic matter, petrified root-systems and calcified growth pressed into blocks with bevelled edges and courses so regular they ache with intention. There are doorways. There are windows. There are streets — narrow, stepped, switchbacking down the terraces in a grid that reveals itself only when you have climbed high enough to see the pattern, the way a tessellation reveals itself only from distance. The streets are paved with flat dark stones fitted together so tightly that the seams are nearly invisible, and water films every surface, a permanent slick that catches the diffuse overhead light and gives the ground the look of black glass.

Everything is wet. The water does not fall and does not flow and does not pool. It *manifests*. It appears on surfaces — on walls, on paving stones, on the underside of lintels, on the ceilings of the low tunnel-like passages that connect terrace to terrace — as a fine beading, a condensation so universal that there is no dry surface in the entire city. Water gathers into beads and the beads gather into threads and the threads gather into rivulets that run down the walls in paths that have been the same since before memory, tracing the same mineral channels, depositing the same oxides in the same patterns. In some places the water has carved shallow basins in the stone over what must be millennia, and these basins are full, perfectly full, each one a dark mirror holding a precise volume that neither overflows nor diminishes. The water table is the surface. The ground is a membrane stretched over water, and you can feel it — a tension underfoot, a hydraulic hum that joins the greater Sound and becomes part of it.

The vegetation is the city's other inhabitant.

It is everywhere. Not climbing over the architecture but *integral* to it, as though the walls were built to hold the roots and the roots were grown to hold the walls, a mutualism so complete that separating the two would destroy both. Broad thick leaves — dark green, almost black in the wet, waxy-surfaced, each one the size of a shield — emerge from cracks in the masonry in dense overlapping clusters that form canopies over the streets. The trunks are massive, gnarled, bowing under their own weight, but they do not move. Nothing sways. There is no wind to sway it. The branches hold their positions with the rigidity of beams, and the leaves hold theirs with the rigidity of shingles, and the whole canopy forms a continuous roof over the terrace-city, a second ceiling below the first, green and dripping and absolutely still.

Vines thicken around the doorways. They have grown into the shapes of the frames, filling the openings not quite completely, leaving gaps that might once have been passages and are now green-lipped thresholds too dense to cross. Ferns erupt from every horizontal surface — from windowsills, from stair-treads, from the flat tops of walls — in fronds that uncurl halfway and then stop, held in permanent half-birth, their fiddleheads tight and wet and gleaming. Moss carpets everything that lies flat. Fungi — bracket-shaped, shelf-shaped, some of them as wide as a person's arm-span — jut from the vertical faces in tiered arrays that resemble balconies, pale and fleshy and somehow architectural, as though the city is growing new rooms.

The roots have broken through the paving in places, heaving up slabs that now rest at angles, permanently tilted, and in the gaps between root and stone more water collects, more basins form, more mineral deposits crystallize in fractal arrays of red and yellow and white. The whole city is a slow-motion explosion of biological matter pressing through mineral substrate, and it has arrived at a point of equilibrium so perfect that the explosion appears frozen. The roots will not grow further. The stone will not crack further. The water will not move further. This is the final state. This has always been the final state.

But the Sound.

The Sound is not coming from the water and it is not coming from the rock and it is not coming from the vegetation. The Sound is coming from the *edges*. Where the terrace-city meets the void below — and there is a void below, a drop so immense that the bottom cannot be perceived, a cliff-edge beyond which the world simply *stops* — the Sound rises like heat. It rises in a column. You can feel its directionality, its verticality, a roaring that comes UP from below and passes through you and continues up into the compressed sky. At the cliff's edge the air is different. The diffuse light fails. The green canopy retreats. And the stone — the dark wet stone of the terrace walls — becomes *transparent*.

Not glassy. Not crystalline. Transparent in the way that deep water is transparent: you can see into it, and what you see is *more stone*, extending back and down into depths that should not exist, layers of masonry receding into a dark that is lit from within by a faint reddish glow, and in those depths there are *more terraces*, more streets, more doorways, more of the root-architecture, all of it submerged in stone as though the visible city is only the uppermost film of a structure that extends downward without limit. The Sound comes from down there. From the deep city. From the part of the structure that has been buried — or has buried itself — or was never above ground at all.

And here is where the place begins to come apart.

Because when you look at the cliff-edge, the geometry does not hold. The terraces below you curve in a direction that the terraces above you do not share. The streets in the transparent stone run at angles that intersect the streets on the surface in ways that produce impossible junctions — staircases that meet their own undersides, doorways that open into the *interiors* of walls, rooms whose far walls are the *exteriors* of other rooms that are oriented toward a sky that is below. And the Sound — that single unvarying chord — contains, when you listen closely (and you are always listening closely, because you cannot stop), *harmonics*. Structures within the noise. Rhythms that are not rhythms because they do not repeat, exactly, but *recur*, in the way that a spiral recurs, each pass similar but slightly — sickeningly — shifted.

You cannot walk to the cliff-edge. You can walk *toward* it, but the streets rearrange. Not dynamically — the stones do not move, the roots do not shift. But the *connections* between streets change. A passage that led downward now leads upward. A junction of five streets becomes a junction of three. A staircase that descended now ascends, and the treads you have already walked are behind you in the wrong order. Nothing has moved. Everything is where it has always been. But *where it has always been* is not a fixed coordinate. The city is in equilibrium with itself, but the equilibrium is *topological*, not spatial — a shape that is consistent with itself but inconsistent with the space it occupies.

The vegetation does not care. The roots grow through the impossible junctions as though geometry were a soil condition. The leaves canopy over streets that fold into themselves. The water beads on surfaces that are simultaneously walls and floors. The fungi shelf out from stone that is transparent from one angle and opaque from another. The plants have settled this paradox. They live in the topology, not the space. Their roots find purchase in the *connections* between things, in the relationships, in the structure of the structure rather than its position. They are the true inhabitants. They have been here longest. They will be here when everything else — the stone, the water, the light, the Sound — has finished not-changing and arrives at whatever lies beyond not-changing.

There are no animals. No insects. No birds. Nothing moves. The Sound is not a voice; it has no source that breathes. The water is not alive; it does not flow. The vegetation does not photosynthesize — there is no sun, only the flat pale light that presses down from the low sky and the faint reddish glow that seeps up from the transparent stone, and neither of these changes in intensity or color. The plants are not growing. They have *grown*. They are the completed fact of growing, the noun rather than the verb.

The heat is the final cruelty. It is not the heat of a sun. It is the heat of the place itself — metabolic, geological, *narrative*. The stone is warm. The water is warm. The air is warm. The vegetation is warm to the touch, as though the leaves are holding a temperature that was generated inside them and has nowhere to go. The heat is part of the equilibrium. It does not build. It does not dissipate. It simply exists, saturating everything, a thermal state that has no gradient and therefore no reason to change.

You stand on a terrace halfway between the sky-lid and the cliff-edge, surrounded by green so dense you can touch it on both sides, on stone so wet your reflection stares up at you from the ground, in air so heavy it tastes like drinking, in heat so total it has become a kind of silence inside the Sound. The city extends in every direction — the directions that exist and the directions that do not — and it is complete. It has always been complete. It will always be complete. The Sound fills your chest. The water films your skin. The roots hold the walls and the walls hold the roots and the geometry holds neither and neither holds the geometry.

This is the Weeping Terrace. It does not want you here. It does not mind you here. It does not know you are here. It is a place that has arrived at the end of its own becoming, and the end of its becoming is also its middle and its beginning, and it holds all three at once, the way a chord holds all its notes, and it will not stop.
