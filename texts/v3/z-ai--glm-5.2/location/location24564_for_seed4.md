# The Gutted Bole

You enter through a crack that was not here last season.

---

The opening is narrow — a split in the bark of a living tower that your people call *Motherroot*. The crack runs vertically for perhaps twelve body-lengths, fresh enough that the wood inside is still pale, still weeping. Sap beads along the split's edges, slow and cold, catching what little light filters down from the canopy three hundred feet above. The sap smells of iron and tannin and something underneath both — something mineral and ancient, like wet stone drawn from deep clay.

You turn sideways and step in.

---

The interior is a chamber. Not a cavern — a chamber, architectural in its intent, though no hand carved it. The heartwood of Motherroot rotted out in a single season — a wet rot, aggressive and fast, the kind that comes when the river floods and the water table rises and the roots sit drowned for too long. The rot ate the core and left the outer wood standing, a hollow column maybe forty feet across and God knows how tall. The walls are still alive. You can see it — the faint pulse of moisture moving through the sapwood, the pale threads of cambium layering themselves over the wound, trying to close it, trying to heal the breach. It will close. In twenty years, maybe fifty, the bark will have sealed over and the chamber will be gone, compressed back into the body of the tree. But for now it is here. Young. Open. Sharp-edged in the way that recent wounds are sharp.

The floor is wet. Not flooded — not a marsh, not a pool — but *wet*, the way a wound is wet. Water surfaces through the root mat that forms the ground, rising between the fibers in slow beads that pool into shallow channels no wider than your thumb. The air does most of the work. It is saturated. You feel it on your skin immediately — a cold, fine wetness that is not quite rain and not quite fog but something in between, a condensing atmosphere that deposits moisture on every surface, every wall, every exposed shoulder of wood. You breathe it in and it tastes of tannin and cellulose and the faintest edge of decomposition. Not unpleasant. Living. The smell of a living thing's interior.

It is cold. Not the cold of winter — there is no winter here, not in the sense of frost and snow — but the deep, steady cold of shade and moisture and mass. The temperature of the interior of a living body. It sits in the low fifties, maybe colder, and it does not change. Day or night. Season to season. The wood holds its temperature the way a stone well does, and the water rising through the roots is colder still, drawing up the chill of the deep soil. You feel it in your feet first, then your hands, then the small of your back where your shirt rides up. It is the kind of cold that does not bite but that settles, accumulates, works its way into the joints over hours. The people who live here do not stay still for long.

---

And there are people here.

You see the settlement as your eyes adjust — and they must adjust, because the light inside the bole is almost nothing. What little enters comes through the crack you came through, and through a few other fissures in the bark above, thin shafts of gray-green that fall like ropes across the chamber and are swallowed by the wet dark. Most of the interior is lit by the people themselves — by small clay lamps burning rendered insect fat, set in niches cut into the sapwood, flickering with a low orange light that moves across the walls like something breathing.

The settlement is small. Forty people, perhaps fifty, living in a space that could hold ten times that number. This is what strikes you first — the *sparseness* of it. The chamber is vast relative to the habitation. Structures are scattered across the floor of the bole with wide gaps between them, separated by stretches of wet root-fiber and shallow water, connected by pathways made of laid sticks and split bark. The emptiness is not neglect. It is design. The people here need space — space to see what comes in through the cracks, space to move, space to keep the living walls at a distance they can watch.

The structures themselves are grown, not built. This is the thing you must understand: the people here do not construct their dwellings. They *cultivate* them. Each dwelling is a living plant — a dense, bulbous fungus-wood hybrid that the people have trained, over years, into hollow chambers with walls of interleaved fiber and chitin. They look like enormous gourds, or like the cysts that grow on the roots of diseased trees, pale and smooth and slightly luminescent in the lamp-light, each one perhaps the size of a small room. Doorways are circular holes cut into the living wall and sealed with woven bark-fiber curtains that hang wet and heavy. Inside, the floors are dry — the fungus-wood draws moisture inward through its walls and sweats it out through its outer skin, a constant biological dehumidification that keeps the interiors habitable. The people sleep on beds of compressed moss. They store tools in alcoves grown into the walls by careful incision and patient feeding.

There are perhaps fifteen of these dwellings, scattered across the chamber floor. Between them, other structures: a drying rack made of bone and cordage, where strips of something dark and leathery hang in the saturated air. A workshop — an open space where a woman sits cross-legged on a root, chipping a flake of chert into a scraper with a small hammerstone, the *tick-tick-tick* of the work echoing softly in the tall space. A hearth — the only true hearth — a pit of packed clay where a low fire burns, fed by precious dry wood hauled down from the canopy above, smoke rising in a thin column that finds a fissure in the bark and escapes. The fire is not for warmth, not primarily. It is for light. For the curing of hides. For the rendering of fat. The cold here is not something fire can fix.

---

The tools are simple. Stone. Bone. Cordage twisted from inner bark. Needles carved from the chitin plates of large insects — and this is where you begin to notice the fauna.

They are everywhere.

Not threatening — not exactly — but *present*, overwhelmingly, pervasively present, in a way that makes your skin prickle and your breath go shallow. The floor moves, if you watch it. Not all of it, not dramatically, but in patches, in the shallow water that pools between the roots, there are things moving. Small things. Larvae, pale and legless, wriggling through the wet fiber in dense clusters, feeding on the rot. Beetles — flat, dark, the size of your thumbnail — navigating the root-maze in single file, following chemical trails you cannot sense. Centipedes moving with mechanical precision along the base of the walls, hunting. You see a spider the size of your palm crouching in a fissure above one of the dwellings, its web spanning the gap between two root-buttresses, strands thick and wet with condensation, and in the web — dozens of small flies, caught and wrapped, a larder.

And these are only the ones you can see.

The walls are alive with them. The bark, the sapwood, the cambium — every surface in the chamber is habitat, is *territory*, divided and subdivided among species you cannot count. You shine your lamp at the wall and the wall *shifts* — thousands of small bodies redistributing themselves away from the light, a wave of motion that passes across the wood like wind across water. Mites, springtails, tiny beetles, things you have no name for, living in the cracks and pores of the living wood, feeding on the sap, on the rot, on each other. The air has its own population — midges and fungus gnats drifting in slow clouds through the saturated space, attracted to the lamps, to the heat of bodies, to the carbon dioxide of breath. You breathe through your teeth. You learn this quickly.

The people here have made peace with this. More than peace — they have built their economy on it. The insects are their meat. Their hides. Their chitin for tools and armor. Their fat for lamp oil. The drying rack holds strips of beetle-larva, cured and smoked, dense with protein. The woman at the workshop is making a scraper to process larva-hide — thin, translucent, surprisingly tough. The fungus-wood dwellings are partly fed by the waste of the human inhabitants and partly by the remains of processed insects, a closed loop of biological input that keeps the living architecture healthy and growing.

But the peace is not absolute. You notice the nets — fine mesh woven from spider-silk and bark-fiber, hung across doorways and ventilation holes. You notice the repellent — a paste of rendered fat and crushed aromatic fungus smeared around the base of each dwelling, a chemical barrier that the crawling things will not cross. You notice the children do not sleep on the floor. Their beds are slung from cords, hanging a foot above the root-mat, and the cords are coated in the same repellent paste. You notice, near the hearth, a pile of empty carapaces — the remains of something large, something with jointed legs and mandibles wide enough to wrap around a human forearm. One of the older men sees you looking and holds up his left hand. He is missing two fingers. He does not speak of how.

The settlement is quiet, but the quiet is not silence. It is the sum of ten thousand small sounds — the whisper of legs on wood, the wet click of mandibles, the faint rustle of wings, the creak of living sapwood expanding in the moisture, the slow drip of condensation falling from the upper walls into the shallow water on the floor. Underneath all of it, so deep you feel it more than hear it, the pulse of Motherroot herself — the long, slow hydraulic rhythm of a living tree moving water through its body, a heartbeat measured in minutes, not seconds. The people have stopped hearing it. You will not. It will be in your chest when you sleep tonight, if you sleep.

---

The woman at the workshop finishes her scraper and holds it up to the light. The edge is fine, almost glassy. She tests it against her thumbnail and nods. Around her, on the root where she sits, a line of ants moves in perfect order, each carrying a fragment of something white — fungus, you think, or perhaps eggs. They pass within inches of her hand. She does not flinch. They do not deviate.

A child emerges from one of the dwellings, rubbing her eyes. She is perhaps four years old. She is naked except for a wrap of larva-hide around her middle, and her skin is damp, and she walks barefoot across the wet root-fiber to the hearth, where her mother is stirring a pot of rendered fat and wood-ash into a paste. The child sits. Her mother hands her a strip of dried larva. She eats without speaking. A beetle walks across her foot. She watches it. She does not disturb it.

This is the world. This is the whole of it — this chamber, this light, this wet, this cold, this crawling abundance. Above you, the canopy goes up three hundred feet into air that is warmer and drier and brighter, a world away. Below you, the roots go down thirty, forty, into soil that is colder still and utterly dark. The people here live in the margin between — in the wound, in the rot, in the brief architectural grace of a space that will not last. They know this. The dwelling-growers select for speed, not longevity, because the chamber is closing. The cambium advances. Each year, the walls creep inward by the width of a finger. In fifty years, the space between them will be too narrow for habitation. The dwellings will be crushed. The settlement will end.

They do not seem to mind. When you ask — carefully, through gestures, through the few words of shared language — the woman with the scraper looks at you as though you have asked whether the river minds reaching the sea. She gestures at the walls. At the crack you entered through. At the darkness beyond it, where other trees stand, other roots grow, other wounds will open in other seasons.

*There is always another split in the bark*, she says. *The water always rises. The rot always comes. We go where it goes.*

She turns back to her work. The scraper bites into the hide. The lamps flicker. The walls breathe. Somewhere in the dark above you, something with many legs moves across the living wood, and the wood creaks beneath it, and the sound is swallowed by the vast wet quiet of the interior of the world.
