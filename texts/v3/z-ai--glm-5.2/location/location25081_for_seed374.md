# The Constant City of Dripping Stone

It is never quiet here. That is the first thing.

The sound begins before you arrive — a thick, layered roar that you feel in your sternum before you hear it with your ears. It is not one sound but many, compressed into a single continuous wall of noise that never rises, never falls, never pauses. It has been this way as long as anyone can remember, which is as long as anything can remember, because nothing here changes. The sound is the place, and the place is the sound.

---

## What the Land Is

The ground sits low. Not below sea level, but close — a broad, shallow basin where the land barely pulls itself above the water table. You could dig anywhere with a stick and hit wet clay within a hand's-length. You don't need to dig, though. The water comes up on its own, seeping through hairline cracks in the stone, collecting in shallow grooves that someone — many someones, over time, or all at once, it's hard to tell — carved into the bare rock to channel it where they wanted it to go.

The rock itself is new. That sounds wrong when you say it, but it's the only word that fits. The stone has the look of something that recently solidified — smooth in places where it was liquid, pocked in others where bubbles rose and popped and left their small craters behind. It is warm to the touch. Not hot, not volcanic, but warm the way a sleeping body is warm, as though the earth here is in a state of low, continuous incubation. The bedrock has the color of wet sand, or pale clay, and where the water touches it, it darkens to a rich umber that never quite dries.

There are no hills to speak of. The land extends flatly in every direction, and the horizon — if you can call it that, in this dimness — is a faint pale line where the mist thickens past the point of seeing through it. The sky is low. Not cloudy exactly; the air itself is close, saturated, heavy with water that condenses on every surface. You breathe it in and it coats your throat. The light that reaches the ground has been filtered through so much moisture that it arrives diffuse, directionless, a flat grey-gold glow that casts no shadows.

It has always been this warm. It will always be this warm.

---

## The Water

Water is everywhere and nowhere dramatic. No rivers, no waterfalls, no lakes. Instead, water emerges from the ground in a thousand small ways — welling up through fissures, weeping from the faces of cut stone, beading on surfaces faster than it can evaporate. The air is so saturated that evaporation barely happens at all. The water has nowhere to go, so it sits. It collects. It covers everything in a thin, persistent film.

The channels carved into the ground are the settlement's primary infrastructure. They form a dense, branching network — not planned, not geometric, but organic, as though the people who made them were following the water's own preferences and simply helped it along. The channels are shallow, most no deeper than a finger, and narrow enough to step across. They connect every part of the settlement, threading between structures, pooling in collection basins carved at regular intervals, overflowing and feeding into the next channel downstream. The water moves slowly. It is not in a hurry. Nothing here is in a hurry.

Some of the channels have been deepened over time — or were deepened once, long ago, and have not been touched since. Their walls show the marks of stone tools: rough, irregular gouges, patient work. The stone is soft enough to work with harder stone, and the people here have no metal. Their tools are river cobbles, shaped by use, worn smooth on their working faces. They are adequate. They have always been adequate.

The collection basins are the closest thing the settlement has to public spaces. They are wider than the channels, roughly circular, and the water in them is clear — clearer than the film on every other surface, as though the basins let the water settle and become itself. If you lean over one, you see pale stone beneath, and the dim gold of the sky, and your own face, and the faces of whatever creatures have come to drink.

---

## What Grows Here

Nothing grows here. That needs clarification.

There is no green. No leaves, no roots, no stems, no photosynthesis. The ground is bare stone and wet clay. The air has no pollen. The surfaces have no moss, no lichen, no film of algae. If you came from a world with plants, this would strike you immediately — the absence of that particular texture, that particular color, that particular way that living things soften the angles of stone. Here, the stone stays sharp. The angles stay hard.

What there is instead — and there is a great deal of it — is fungal. Not fungal in the way of mushrooms in a forest. These are not forest things. They are pale, rubbery, structural things, and they grow only where the water collects and the stone stays warm. They coat the insides of the channels like a living lining, a felt-like mat of white and cream-colored threads that is slimy to the touch and smells faintly of yeast. In the collection basins, they form thicker colonies — lumpy, rounded masses like wet bread dough, sometimes fist-sized, sometimes as large as a head, clinging to the stone beneath the water's surface.

This is the primary produce of the place. Not farmed, exactly — tended. The people here have learned that if they keep the channels clear and the basins clean, the fungal colonies thrive. If they cut away the mature growth and leave the base, it regrows. It always regrows. It has always reggrown. The rate of growth is exactly equal to the rate of harvest. This is not coincidence; this is how the place works. Equilibrium. The people did not design it. They simply live inside it.

The fungal material is edible, after a fashion. It must be pressed to remove excess water, then dried — but drying is difficult in this air, so it is more often eaten wet, with the consistency of soft cheese and a flavor that is bland and faintly sweet. It sustains life. Not beautifully, not exuberantly, but completely. Nobody starves here. Nobody has ever starved here.

## The Animals

This is the part that surprises, if you have expectations: the place is full of animals.

They are not animals you would recognize immediately. They are pale — white, grey, translucent in places — the way creatures get when they have never seen direct sunlight. They are warm to the touch, which you might not expect, and they are everywhere.

The most common are small, six-legged things the size of a hand, with bodies like flat ovals and soft, hairless skin. They move slowly, deliberately, and they live in the channels, feeding on the fungal mats. They are the primary reason the fungal growth stays in check when people aren't harvesting — they graze continuously, methodically, working their way along the channels in lines. If you pick one up, it does not struggle. It sits in your hand, warm and soft, and waits to be put down. They have small mouths, circular, like the mouth of a lamprey, and dark eyes that don't seem to focus on anything.

There are larger things too. Wading creatures, knee-high, with long, thin legs and bodies like inverted teardrops, that stand in the collection basins and filter water through their wide, flat mouths. They make low, resonant sounds — not calls, exactly, more like the vibration of a drum — and these sounds are part of the wall of noise. There are many of them. There have always been many of them. Their population never grows. It never shrinks. Every basin has the same number it has always had.

And there are predators. Not large, but present — low, sprawling things with heavy heads and wide, grinding teeth, that move along the channels at night (though "night" here is barely distinguishable from day; the light only gets slightly dimmer) and eat the grazing animals they can catch. They are few. Their numbers are stable. They kill exactly as much as they need to, no more, and the grazer populations absorb the losses without fluctuation.

The people here do not hunt the predators. They do not hunt anything. They eat the fungal material. They watch the animals and the animals watch them, and there is a kind of truce that is not negotiated but simply inherent — a recognition, on both sides, that the system is complete, that every thing has its place, that nothing needs to be added or removed.

The animals are loud. This is important. The grazing animals make a continuous, clicking, grinding sound as they feed — their circular mouths working against the fungal mats. The wading creatures produce their drum-tones. The predators make a low, rhythmic exhalation that carries farther than it should. There are flying things too — not birds, but flat, kite-like organisms with no discernible head, that drift through the dim air on columns of warm, moisture-laden updraft, and they make a sound like fabric tearing, slowly, continuously. All of these sounds, layered and combined and unending, are the sound of the place. They do not stop.

---

## The Settlement

The people have built densely. There is a lot of open ground around the settlement, but within its boundaries, everything is close.

The structures are stone. They are made from the same soft, warm stone that the ground is made of — cut in blocks with stone tools, stacked, fitted without mortar. The stone is soft enough when freshly cut that it can be shaped by hand, and it hardens slowly on exposure to air — or rather, it would, if the air weren't so saturated. In this atmosphere, it stays slightly soft, slightly yielding, and the buildings have a organic, rounded quality, as though they were grown rather than built. Corners are not sharp. Walls are not straight. They curve and settle and conform to the ground they sit on, and the ground conforms to them.

The buildings are small — most no larger than a room — and they are packed close, separated by narrow passages just wide enough for one person to walk through. The passages are also channels; water runs through them, shallow, and the walking surfaces are raised slightly on either side, so that you walk with water between your feet. The buildings have no doors. They have openings — low, oval, requiring you to stoop — and inside, the floors are damp stone, and the walls are beaded with condensation, and the air is warm and close and smells of yeast and wet minerals.

There are many buildings. Hundreds. Enough for a population that numbers in the thousands, if you count carefully. They cluster around the largest collection basins, which serve as the settlement's hearts — not plazas, exactly, because they are full of water and wading creatures and the sound of drums, but the places where people gather, where the fungal material is harvested and distributed, where the channels converge.

The settlement has no edge that is clearly defined. The buildings thin out gradually, the channels narrowing to capillary threads, until you are walking on bare wet stone with no structures in sight, and the mist closes around you, and the sound — somehow — is still there, still the same, still everywhere.

It has been this size for as long as anyone knows. Buildings do not fall into disrepair, but they are not new either. The stone does not crumble — it is too soft to crumble, too wet to crack, and it sits in its shapes with the patience of something that was placed and has not yet been given a reason to move. Nothing is under construction. Nothing is being demolished. The settlement is complete. It has always been complete.

---

## The Light

The light is a flat, pale gold, and it comes from everywhere and nowhere. The sky — if it is a sky — is a low ceiling of luminous mist that does not move, does not shift, does not darken or brighten. There are no suns, no moons, no stars. The light simply is, the way the water simply is, the way the sound simply is. It is warm light, and it makes everything look like it is being seen through gauze. Colors are muted. The pale stone is pale. The pale animals are pale. The pale people are pale. Everything is the same few shades of cream and umber and grey, and nothing catches the light because there is no direct light to catch.

It is not dark. You can see. But you can see the way you see in a dream — everything present, everything clear enough, but nothing sharp, nothing brilliant, nothing vivid. The farthest buildings are slightly indistinct. The closest are slightly soft. The animals in the basins are shapes rather than details. The people around you are present but not distinct.

The light does not change with time because time does not change the light. There is no morning. There is no evening. There is now, and now, and now, and it all looks the same.

---

## The People

They are here. They have always been here.

They are short — not remarkably so, but noticeably, the way people are who have grown up in low-ceilinged spaces and eaten the same food for their entire lives. Their skin is pale, almost translucent, and it has the damp, soft quality of something that has never been dry. Their hair is fine, colorless, and lies flat against their heads, wet always. Their eyes are large and dark and do not react to changes in light, because there are no changes in light to react to.

They do not speak. This is not because they cannot — their throats and mouths are capable of sound. They do not speak because the sound is already full. The air is saturated with noise the way it is saturated with water, and to add a voice to it would be like adding a drop to the ocean. They communicate through touch — brief, precise gestures against each other's hands and arms, a kind of tactile shorthand that is as fast and nuanced as speech and considerably quieter. When you watch them, you see their hands moving constantly, touching each other in passing, in conversation, in the flow of their daily work, and the communication is dense and continuous and as layered as the sound.

They harvest the fungal material. They maintain the channels — clearing debris, reshaping the edges with their stone tools, deepening where the flow has slowed. They tend to the collection basins. They eat. They sleep in their small, damp rooms, on beds of stacked fungal material that has been pressed and dried as much as the air will allow, which is not very much. They wake. They work. They eat. They sleep.

They have no history, because nothing has changed. They have no religion, because there is nothing to explain. They have no government, because there is nothing to decide. The channels need maintaining. The basins need tending. The harvest needs gathering. These things are known. They have always been known. The knowledge is not written — they have no writing, though they have the tools for it, the soft stone and the sharp stones to mark it with — because knowledge that never changes does not need to be recorded. It is held in the hands, in the body, in the doing of it.

They are not unhappy. They are not happy. They are present. They are here, in the warm, wet, dim, deafening equilibrium of a world that finished becoming itself a long time ago and has not yet found a reason to begin again.

---

## The Sound

It is worth returning to the sound, because the sound is the thing you cannot escape.

It is not painful. It is not violent. It is simply total. The grinding of the grazers. The drumming of the waders. The tearing-drone of the flying kites. The low exhalation of the predators. The constant, musical dripping and trickling of water through channels. The faint, underlying hum of the warm stone itself, which vibrates at a frequency below hearing but above silence, felt in the feet, felt in the bones. All of it, all the time, at the same volume, in the same proportion, with the same rhythm — which is to say, no rhythm at all, because rhythm requires change, and this sound does not change.

It fills you. After a time — and time here is long, because it does not pass so much as accumulate — the sound stops being something you hear and becomes something you are. Your heartbeat aligns with it. Your breathing aligns with it. The touch-language of the people, their hands on each other's arms, takes on the cadence of the sound, and you realize — slowly, because everything here is slow — that the sound is not noise. The sound is the medium. It is what the place is made of, the way the stone is what the buildings are made of. The people live inside the sound the way they live inside the air and inside the water. It is not something they experience. It is something they inhabit.

You can stand at the edge of the settlement, where the buildings thin and the channels narrow and the mist thickens, and the sound is the same. You can stand at the center, where the largest basins hold the most wading creatures and their drumming is deepest, and the sound is the same. You can go inside a building and close the opening with your body and the sound is the same. It does not attenuate. It does not shift. It is everywhere, evenly, completely, the acoustic equivalent of the light — flat, directionless, without source, without end.

---

## What This Place Is

This is a place that has arrived at itself. It is not developing. It is not decaying. It is not waiting for something. It is the thing it is, and it has been the thing it is for longer than the people here can reckon, because they have no reckoning, because reckoning requires change and there is no change to measure.

The stone emerged from something — heat, pressure, the slow geological cooking of wet sediment — and it is done emerging. The water has found its level — everywhere, just below the surface, just above the surface, everywhere at once. The fungi have found their substrate. The animals have found their niches. The people have found their work. The sound has found its volume. The light has found its brightness. The temperature has found its degree.

Everything touches everything. The water touches the stone. The stone touches the fungi. The fungi touch the grazers. The grazers touch the predators. The predators die and fall and the fungi grow on their bodies and the stone absorbs what remains. The people touch the fungi and the water and the stone and each other. The sound touches everything. The light touches everything. It is a closed system, a complete system, a world that needs nothing from outside because outside is just more of the same — more mist, more flat stone, more shallow channels, more dim gold light, more of the endless, changeless, deafening now.

If you are here, you are part of it. Your warmth joins the warmth. Your dampness joins the dampness. Your hands learn the touch-language, or they do not, but either way you are here, in the sound, in the wet, in the dim, and the place holds you the way it holds everything — without effort, without intention, without change, without end.
