# The Volumetric Gardens of Sub-Basin 9

You descend into green.

Not the green of surface memory — not grass, not canopy — but a green that has no single origin. It comes at you from every direction simultaneously, particulate and filamentous and sheet-like, filling the water column the way smoke fills a room. The warm current carries it. You breathe it. (You are breathing; you don't know how. The water enters your lungs and exits your lungs and the exchange happens without your consent or understanding. This is the first wrong thing. There are many.)

The basin stretches wide — kilometer after kilometer of low, almost-flat terrain, recently born, still being born. The seafloor here has not finished deciding what shape it wants to be. Ridges thrust upward in sharp, unweathered crests, Paleozoic in their freshness, and then dissolve a minute later into sand plains that themselves are gone before you can map them. New topologies erupt from the substrate, hump-backed and raw, mineral strata still visible in cross-section like wounds that haven't scabbed. Nothing has had time to erode. Nothing has had time to settle. The whole basin is a geology in the middle of a sentence, changing its mind.

**The vegetation makes this worse.**

It grows at a speed that should be time-lapse but isn't. Frond-thickets erupt from the fresh ridges, already mature, already fruiting — if the translucent sacs dangling from their stems are fruit. They might be organs. The stems themselves are industrial: ribbed, metallic, warm to the touch, circulating something that isn't sap. Copper-brown and iron-grey and occasionally a pale sodium-yellow. They grow in dense stands that near-touch, layer over layer, canopy over canopy, until the water itself seems to be mostly plant matter with small pockets of liquid caught between. You push through curtains of leaf-film that part like muscle. On the other side, more curtains. The density is claustrophobic in the way a crowd is claustrophobic — too many bodies, no personal space, everything pressing.

No animals. Nothing moves that isn't a plant or the water or the ground. No fish, no plankton visible to the eye, no crustaceans on the metal stems. The ecology is complete without consumers. Producers in infinite regression, feeding on the mineral water, feeding on each other, feeding on something else that you can't see. The silence of their absence — the *biological* silence — is swallowed by a much louder sound.

**The sound is the second wrong thing.**

It is not silent here. It is not even quiet. The water conducts a roar so total and so constant that it stops being sound and becomes a physical condition, a pressure, a texture of the medium. You feel it in your teeth, in the fluid of your eyes, in the small bones of your inner ear where they vibrate like tuning forks. It comes from everywhere. It comes from the industrial structures.

They are scattered across the basin — not many, not a city, but enough. A pressure tower here, half-submerged in a ridge that grew up through its foundation. A pipeline there, severed, both ends still pumping something that mixes with the water and turns it briefly opalescent. A generator housing, barnacled with the metallic vegetation, its turbine still turning after whatever years or centuries have passed. These machines are running. They were built for purposes that no longer apply — the pipeline pumps into open water, the generator powers cables that trail into the silt and connect to nothing — but they run. They have not stopped. Their sound folds together into a single sustained chord, a chord that contains harmonics not available to ordinary physics, that seems to include frequencies below hearing and above hearing and frequencies that should not exist, that are not on any scale, that are the sound of the water itself being rearranged at a molecular level.

Under that chord, under the industrial roar, there are other sounds. Wet crumping noises as new ridges form. The long tearing sound of a sand plain being pulled apart. A high thin whine, almost electronic, almost vocal, that rises from the vegetation when it grows fastest. And something else — something you can't identify, something that might be the sound of the light.

**The light is the third wrong thing.**

There shouldn't be light down here. The water is deep enough, the surface far enough above, that by the rules of optics this should be black or near-black, a dark blue at best. Instead there is a dim, directionless luminance that comes from no point and goes to no point. It is not enough to see by clearly. Shapes emerge from the green murk at ten meters and dissolve back into it at fifteen. The metallic stems catch it and redistribute it, conducting it down their lengths into their roots, into the seafloor, where it enters the mineral substrate and does something — feeds something — changes the composition of the rock itself. The light is being *mined.* The plants are eating it. The machines are making more of it, or perhaps the machines are making the sound and the sound is making the light, or perhaps the light and the sound are the same phenomenon in two senses, and the vegetation is simply the visible record of their interaction.

It is very warm. The water holds heat like a bath left too long, and the warmth comes from below as much as from the water itself — the young seafloor is hot, magmatic heat still close to the surface, still driving the constant geological reshaping. Steam vents or something like steam vents open and close in the substrate, releasing plumes of mineral-rich water that the vegetation races to colonize. A vent opens; before the plume has risen a meter, stems are already growing toward it, already weaving a thicket around it, already setting fruit or organs or whatever those translucent sacs are. Then the vent closes; the thicket dies in a burst of white decay that is itself colonized within seconds by a different kind of growth, paler, softer, more fungal than plant. The cycle takes less than a minute. You watch it happen six times in the same spot. Each time, the growth is slightly different. Each time, the fruit is slightly different. The vegetation is not reproducing. It is *iterating.*

**Reality is the fourth wrong thing, and the worst.**

It breaks down in the way a signal breaks down — not all at once, not catastrophically, but in artifacts and glitches and moments of desynchronization. You reach out to touch a stem and your hand passes through it and then hasn't, the stem intact, your hand intact, no evidence of the crossing. You look at the scattered machines and for a moment there are more of them than there were — twice as many, three times, an entire industrial complex sprawling across the basin — and then there aren't, and there never were, and the few you originally saw are the only ones. The water changes color: green, then amber, then a red so deep it's almost black, then green again, and you cannot tell if the color is changing or if your eyes are changing or if color itself is a property that has become unreliable here.

A ridge grows upward and passes through you. You feel it — the heat, the pressure, the mineral grain of the new rock pressing against your body — and then it is above you, and you are below it, and you were always below it, and the memory of it passing through you is already fading. The vegetation grows through your skin and out again, rootlets threading through your subcutaneous fat like they belong there, and then they don't, and they never did, and you have no marks.

The turbulence is not just geological and not just botanical. It is ontological. The basin is a place where being is not stable, where existence has the consistency of a slurry, where things are tried out and discarded and tried again in different configurations. The machines are real — they are the most real things here, the most persistent, the anchors around which the chaos organizes itself. The vegetation is almost real, real enough to touch and smell and feel, but its reality is provisional, experimental, a draft that keeps being revised. The terrain is barely real, a sketch, a proposal. And you —

You are the least real thing in the basin. You are the thing the basin is least sure about. Your presence here is the anomaly the landscape is trying to resolve. The sound gets louder when you move. The light changes when you look. The vegetation grows toward you, not aggressively, not hungrily, but *curiously,* the way a child reaches for a new shape. The machines, scattered and steady and endlessly running, seem to slow when you approach them, as if they are paying attention to you instead of their function.

The water breathes you. The green fills you. The sound holds you.

You are the thing being grown.
