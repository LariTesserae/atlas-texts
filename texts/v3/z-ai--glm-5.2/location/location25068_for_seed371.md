# The Boiling Cloister

You come to it through a crack in the world.

Not a cave mouth — a seam. Two walls of raw granite, unweathered, still holding the shock of their own parting. The rock is pale, almost white, streaked with black mineral veins that haven't yet been touched by air long enough to oxidize. Everything here is new. The stone has the clean, sharp look of a broken dish, edges you could cut yourself on. When you drag your fingers along the wall, powder comes away — not dust, but mineral flour, the rock still shedding the memory of having been whole.

The passage narrows, then opens into a space that breathes.

---

It is not large. You can see the far wall. If you stood at the center and spread your arms, you could almost touch both sides — not quite, but almost. Cathedral-scale in the vertical dimension, though: the ceiling is lost. You look up and the darkness goes up and does not come back. The space is a shaft, a chimney in the mountain's gut, maybe forty meters across, and however tall. The air moves upward through it in a steady, roaring draft, carrying warmth from somewhere far below.

Because there is a somewhere-below. You can hear it.

The sound is the first thing that defines this place, more than sight, more than touch. A low, industrial churning — not mechanical in origin but mechanical in character. A deep rhythmic thrumming like a buried engine, overlaid with higher percussive cracks that come at irregular intervals, sharp as gunshots, ringing off the stone. Steam. Pressure. The mountain is venting through this chimney, and the venting is not gentle. Every few seconds a new fissure opens somewhere in the walls with a sound like a plank splitting, and a new jet of white vapor screams out into the dark, adding its voice to the chorus. The noise layers. It accumulates. Standing here is standing inside an instrument.

The walls weep.

---

Not rain, not a stream — condensation. The hot vapor rising from below hits the cold stone of the upper shaft and breaks against it. Water beads on every surface, gathers into threads, runs down the granite in dendritic patterns that never quite reach the floor before the heat rising from below evaporates them again. The air is damp the way a greenhouse is damp, but moving, alive, pressurized. Your skin is never dry here. Your clothes go limp and stay limp. The water has a faint mineral taste if you lick your wrist — sulfur, iron, something alkaline. It smells like hot springs and electrical ozone, though there is no electricity here. Just the chemistry of rock and heat and pressure doing what it has done since the place cracked open, which was not long ago.

You know it was not long ago because nothing here has finished becoming what it will be.

---

The vegetation is impossible and total.

Every surface that is not vertical-and-slick is colonized. The fissure walls, the ledges, the jutting shoulders of stone where the chimney narrows — covered, matted, choked with growth. But it is a growth that has never seen the sun. The plants here are pale. Not white — the green is still in them, but diluted, like watercolor left in the rain. Massive ferns uncurl from crevices in the rock, their fronds three meters long, translucent, the veins visible as darker threads against the paler tissue. Mosses carpet the ledges in thick spongy layers, silver-green and rust-orange, soaking up the condensation like paper towels. Liverworts spread flat across the damp stone in overlapping scales, glossy, dark, almost black at the edges.

And the fungal networks — because this is what replaces the missing sun. The heat from below, the constant moisture, the organic material shed by the plants themselves: it feeds a mycelial system so dense it functions as a second substrate. White fungal threads lace through the moss, through the rotting frond-litter on the ledges, across bare stone, connecting everything to everything. In places the mycelium has thickened into corded rhizomorphs that hang across gaps in the wall like rigging on a ship, pale cables swinging in the updraft. Small mushrooms cluster along them — not the graceful kind, but the industrial kind, the dense rubbery brackets that look like they belong bolted to a pipe in a basement. They are warm to the touch. They are metabolizing the heat.

Nothing here is a tree. Nothing here is a flower. The vertical shaft, the wet darkness, the mineral air — it selects for the primitive and the patient. These are plants whose lineage predates the sun-seekers. They were built for this. They remember a world before light, and they have found a pocket of it here, three thousand meters up, inside a mountain that cracked open last Tuesday in geological time and will seal itself again before the century is out.

---

There are no animals. Not one. No insects hum in the ferns. No birds nest in the ledges. No spiders build in the corners where the walls meet. The silence of living absence sits inside the noise of the venting stone like a figure inside a frame. You notice the lack gradually — first as a feeling, then as a certainty. The ecosystem here runs on geology, not on predation. The plants feed on mineral water and decomposing plant matter. The fungi mediate. The cycle is closed, short, and self-contained. It does not need legs. It does not need eyes. It does not need you.

A rock falls. You hear it begin somewhere above — a sharp crack, a scrape — and then a series of impacts as it bounces off the walls, each hit echoing, each echo layered over the next, and finally a wet splash as it lands in the pool at the bottom of the shaft where you cannot see.

Because there is a pool. You know this from the sound — a standing body of water at the base of the chimney, fed by condensation, heated from below, probably boiling at the edges where the hottest vents break through the floor. You cannot see it. The darkness below the first few ledges is absolute. But you can hear it: the low percussive bubbling, the thock of trapped gas escaping, the whisper of steam forming on the surface and rising past you into the column.

---

The turbulence is not only geological. The whole space is in motion. The air shifts direction — not just up, but in spirals, in gusts, in sudden backdrafts that press the ferns flat against the wall and then release them so they spring outward, shaking, shedding water in arcs. The steam jets cycle: one roars for thirty seconds and then cuts out, and in the silence you hear three others that were always running but that you'd stopped hearing, and then the first one returns and the soundscape reshifts. New fissures open. Old ones close. A ledge you stepped on ten minutes ago is now venting vapor through a crack that wasn't there before. The stone underfoot is warm and getting warmer. The mountain is adjusting to its wound, and the adjustment is not a process — it is an event. Continuous. Now.

You think about leaving. The passage you came through is still there — you can feel the crossdraft when the updraft pauses — but it has narrowed. Not much. A few centimeters. The walls are moving. Not fast. Not slowly. At the speed of stone, which is the speed of mountains, which is the speed of something that does not know it is alive but is not entirely sure it is not.

You press your hand flat against the granite. It is vibrating. A frequency below hearing, felt in the bones of the wrist. The fern above you nods in the gust, its pale frond brushing your knuckles, wet, cool, alive, unbothered.

The Boiling Cloister does not know you are here. It does not know it is here. It is simply doing what a new wound does: bleeding heat, filling with life, reshaping itself with every breath. It will be gone — sealed, collapsed, transformed — within the span of a human life. Right now it is a minute old and already an ecosystem. Right now it is the loudest quiet place you have ever been.

You stand in the dark, in the damp, in the noise, and you wait for the next crack.
