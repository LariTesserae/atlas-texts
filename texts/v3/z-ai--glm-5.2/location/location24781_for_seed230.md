# The Hollow of Yne

You come up the switchback path cut into grey schist, each step worn smooth by centuries of feet — or by the slow grinding of the path itself, as though the stone remembers passage and offers it back as polish. The air thins. Cool, not cold. The kind of cool that sits against the skin without insisting on itself. You notice it mostly when you stop moving.

The hollow opens like a cupped hand at the top of the ridge. It is not large — you can see the far wall from the near wall, the whole of it in one glance, a basin of weathered rock perhaps forty paces across and twice that long, rimmed by low cliffs that break the wind but not the light. Light comes in evenly, diffused by a sky that is neither overcast nor clear but something in between — a pale luminous grey that seems to have no source and casts no hard shadows. It is the same light at midday as at evening. Perhaps it is always this light here.

Within the hollow, structures. Not many — seven, perhaps eight, depending on where you draw the line between wall and ruin. They are built from the schist of the ridge itself, flat stones stacked without mortar, fitted with the patience of hands that had time and no metal harder than copper. The walls stand shoulder-height or a little above. Roofs where they survive are corbelled — stone slabs laid inward, course by course, until they nearly meet, capped by a single flat stone. The doorways are low. Not because the people were small, but because the wind is persistent and a low door keeps more warmth inside.

There is no one here. There has been no one here for a long time.

But the settlement is not ruined. That is the first strange thing, though it does not feel strange — it feels settled. The walls are weathered, yes, their edges softened by ages of thin rain and wind-borne grit, the stone surfaces developing a satin patina like river pebbles. But nothing is toppled. Nothing is crumbling. The structures have reached a state of equilibrium with the forces that act on them — the intermittent rain, the wind that comes over the rim in slow smooth sheets, the condensation that forms on east-facing walls in the early hours and dries by mid-morning. They are not decaying. They are not enduring. They simply *are*, in the same way the ridge itself is.

Between the structures, open ground. Stone dust and thin soil, the color of old bone. Patches of lichen — crustose, grey-green and rust-orange — cling to the lower courses of the walls and to exposed rock surfaces. They grow with an almost geological patience, and they have reached the edges of where they will reach. You can see the boundary where lichen stops and bare stone begins, a line so consistent it looks ruled. The lichen does not advance past it. It does not retreat. It is where it is.

A few plants. Not nothing, but nearly. Tufts of a sedge with black-tipped blades grow in the cracks where walls meet ground, drawing moisture from the condensation channel that forms there each morning. A single woody shrub, no taller than your knee, twisted by wind into a permanent eastward lean, grows in the shelter of the largest structure. Its leaves are small and grey and waxy to the touch. It has seeds but no flowers. It might be always in this state — leafed, seeded, unmoving. There is no dead growth beneath it, no leaf litter. What it drops, the wind takes or the stone absorbs.

Water comes lightly and without urgency. The cliffs around the hollow seep after the mist that passes through in the early hours — not rain, exactly, but air so wet it deposits itself on every surface. You can see it on the stones: a fine sheen that darkens the schist from grey to near-black, that gathers along edges and drips — slowly, irregularly — into shallow channels worn into the floor of the hollow. These channels are old, smoothly grooved, the work of water over a time impossible to measure. They lead to a low point in the western rim where a notch has been cut — or has formed — and the water drains out and down and away. The dripping is the loudest sound here. A slow, arrhythmic plinking, three or four seconds between drops, sometimes longer. It is the kind of sound that becomes the silence rather than breaking it.

The other sounds: wind, but not as you expect it. The hollow is shaped to catch wind and calm it simultaneously. What enters as a steady blow over the rim descends in slow eddies, stirring nothing, moving nothing, audible as a low sustained breath against the cliff faces. You hear it more as pressure against your ears than as a sound. Occasionally, a stone somewhere adjusts — a faint gritty click, a fraction of a millimeter of settlement — and then silence again. These adjustments happen rarely. They are the settlement still settling, still finding its exact rest, after all this time.

Inside the structures, the air is still. A different quality of cool — drier, closer. The corbelled roofs are soot-stained at their peaks, evidence of fires that were, once. The hearths are shallow depressions in the stone floors, lined with heat-fractured schist. Ash is not present; it has been taken by the wind or by time, leaving only the stain and the shape. In the largest structure — the one with the shrub — there is a stone shelf cut into the wall, and on it, three objects: a smooth river cobble too large to be accidental, a copper pin green with patina, and a piece of bone that might be a tool handle or might simply be a bone. The objects are dusty but undisturbed. They sit where they were placed. No one has moved them. No one will.

The technology of the place is in its stone. Everything that was built here was built with what the ridge provided — schist in slabs that split cleanly, a copper vein somewhere below that yielded enough metal for pins and edges, bone from animals that are no longer here. The fitting of the stones is the highest craft on display. There are no carvings. No decoration. The precision of the masonry is the aesthetic, and it is sufficient. Walls run true. Corners are plumb. The settlement is a piece of practical geometry laid in stone.

Beyond the rim of the hollow, the ridge continues — bare, grey, sloping down in all directions into haze. The visibility is limited not by fog but by the quality of the light, which seems to thin with distance until the land dissolves into the sky. You cannot see far. You can see enough. The world beyond is stone and thin soil and the occasional pale flash of a bird turning far below, riding whatever current exists at this altitude. The bird does not call. It passes and is gone, and it does not come back, and its absence is not noticed.

The hollow feels complete. Not empty — complete. There is space between the structures, space inside them, space between the lichen and the bare stone, between the shrub and the wall, between one drip of water and the next. The space is not absence. It is part of the composition. The settlement was built with an understanding that what is left open matters as much as what is built, and the result is a place where you can stand at the center and feel the weight of each structure, each presence, as discrete and particular. Nothing crowds. Nothing is lost. The proportions are the proportions of a place that was made once and then finished, and has needed nothing since.

You feel the damp on your face. You hear the next drop fall. The light does not change.

It will not change.
