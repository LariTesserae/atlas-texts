I do not arrive. That would imply a before and an after, a boundary crossed, a moment of entry. But the High City does not have an entrance. It has gradients. You are in it before you know you are in it, the way you are in fog before you know you are in fog — the air thickens, the stone underfoot changes, the sound of your own steps begins to bounce back at you from surfaces you cannot see, and at some point you stop walking toward the city and start walking *in* the city, and you cannot say when that changed.

So: I am already here. I am in a passage so narrow that my shoulders touch both walls if I stand straight, which I do not. I have learned not to. The stone on my left is wetter than the stone on my right, which means I am descending, which means I am going deeper, which means — what? I have not decided yet. I am not old. Not old enough for the deep rooms, not old enough that the climb back up would hurt in the way that climbing hurts when your joints have been doing stairs for fifty years in cold wet air. But I am not young either. I am in the middle of my life, which is to say I am in the middle of the city — not on the upper terraces where the tough grass grows and the afternoon sun turns wet stone to silver and children play their game of closed eyes, and not in the deep rooms where the pale growth glows on walls and old women hear the difference between a clear channel and a silting one. I am in between. I am in the tissue between cells, in the interval between walls, in the place where the fog sits at the level of my chest and I breathe through it and it breathes through me and neither of us remarks on the arrangement.

---

What am I. Let me not answer that yet. Let me say what I do.

I maintain channels. Or: I have a relationship with several channels, and the relationship involves clearing them when they silt, which they do, slowly, the mineral deposits building up like a condition of the body — not an illness exactly, more like the way joints stiffen, the way stone itself swells and contracts with the seasons. The channels I know are on the seventh terrace down from the western rim, in a cluster of rooms that I think of as *mine* though the word is wrong — too possessive, too bounded. The rooms are not mine the way a house is mine. They are mine the way a particular ache in the shoulder is mine: I know it, I attend to it, I have a relationship with it, and it with me, and we have been together long enough that neither of us remembers what it was like before.

I clear the channels with my hands and with a piece of denser stone — a tool, though the description says there are no tools here that survive as a recognizable category, and this is true, because my piece of stone is not kept separate from other stones. It is not *a tool*. It is a stone I reach for, the way you reach for a word. It is in a niche in the wall beside the channel's access point, and when I need it I put my hand in the niche and there it is, and when I am done I put it back, and it sits there being a stone again, and the niche holds the shape of it the way a word holds the shape of its meaning — provisionally, specifically, for as long as anyone remembers.

The mineral deposit comes away in flakes. White, mostly. Salt and calcium and something else, something the water carries that I do not have a name for in any language that would translate. The flakes fall into the water that runs in the channel and dissolve or do not dissolve and are carried downward to wherever the water goes. I do not know where the water goes. I know where it comes from — a spring-fed cistern two terraces up, fed by rain and condensation, the water arriving in the stone the way the description says, the air giving up its water to the stone because the stone is cooler — and I know the path it takes through the rooms I attend to, and I know where it surfaces — a spout in a courtyard I will describe in a moment — but where it goes after that, after the courtyard, after the split into three courses, after the drain, I do not know. Someone knows. Someone deeper. Someone who tends the channels below mine the way I tend the channels above theirs. But I have not met them. I have heard them. Once, or twice, a sound through the wall that could have been a hand in water, could have been someone clearing a channel one terrace down. I did not call out. You do not call out here. Sound carries in strange ways, and a voice raised in one courtyard arrives in another as a whisper, and you do not know who is receiving your whisper or what they think you mean.

---

The courtyard. I said I would describe it. Let me.

It is not large. Four paces by six. The walls rise on three sides — the fourth side is open, but not open to the sky, open to a passage that leads to other rooms, other courtyards. You could not call it a courtyard in any place that has a word for courtyards derived from *court*, from *yard*, from the idea of an enclosed open space around which life arranges itself. It is more like a widening. A place where the passage widens enough that the fog does not fill it entirely — there is space at the top, a foot, maybe two, where the air is clear and if you tilt your head you can see the upper courses of the walls and the lip of something above, a ledge, a terrace edge, a place where the sun falls for an hour in the afternoon and the stone goes warm and silver and you can almost feel it from down here, the warmth, like a rumor of warmth transmitted through the stone the way grief is transmitted through a wall — not the thing itself but the fact of it.

The water spout is in the middle of the east wall. It emerges from the stone the way the description says — no visible join, no pipe, just an opening that the water has carved or that was always there, that the builders shaped the wall *around* rather than *through*. The water falls from it into a basin, and the basin is old. Older than the walls. You can tell because the walls are fitted against the basin, the stones shaped to accommodate its edges, which means the basin was here first and the walls grew up around it the way the description says the city grows — following the contours of the land the way lichen follows the contours of rock. The basin has a lip on one side, and the water spills over the lip into a channel cut into the floor, and the channel carries it to the passage and the passage carries it down and I do not know where it goes after that. I have said this already. It bears saying twice because it is one of the true things about this place: you know your section. You know your channels and your courtyards and your walls. Beyond that, the city is a rumor. A sound through stone. A smell of smoke from a fire you will never see.

I sit in this courtyard. That is a large part of what I do. I sit and I listen to the water and I feel the fog on my skin and I am not doing nothing. I am maintaining something, but the something I am maintaining is not a channel or a wall. It is a relationship. Between me and the stone, between the water and the air, between the sound the spout makes and the sound the basin makes when the water hits it, which are different sounds, and the difference matters, and I can hear the difference, and I have been sitting here long enough that I know what the difference sounds like when the channel is clear and what it sounds like when it is silting, and I know when to get up and go to the access point and reach for the stone in the niche and do what needs doing.

---

Now: what am I. I have been circling this. Let me try.

I am a person. I think. I have hands that are rough like sandstone and skin that is damp and hair that lies flat against my skull and I eat the paste of ground grass-seed mixed with water and I eat the pale creatures from the cisterns when I gather them and I eat the dark moss dried in the afternoon sun and I cook over a small fire of dried grass that smokes thinly and acridly and the smoke moves along the ceiling and out through a gap I cannot see. I speak in murmurs. I hum with others sometimes, in a room, sitting close, and the stone under us resonates and the sound is not beautiful in any translatable way but it is *ours*, and the word *ours* here does not mean what it means in the lowlands. It means: this sound, this stone, this water, this fog, this us. All one thing. The word would not travel.

But I am not only a person. Or I am a person in the way the description says the people are people — *shaped by a place, the way a tree shaped by wind looks like a tree*. I am shaped by this place. I have been shaped by it so long that I cannot tell where I end and the shaping begins. When I press my hand to the wall, my palm comes away damp, and the wall is damp where my hand was, and we have exchanged moisture, and this is not a metaphor. This is what happens. The stone gives me its water and I give the stone my water and neither of us is changed in any way you could measure, but we are changed in the way that all touching changes the touched and the toucher, which is: not at all, and completely.

I think I am someone who has begun to go deeper. Not yet. Not in the way the old women in the deep rooms have gone deeper — I am not there yet, I am not ready, the light does not hurt my eyes and the climb does not hurt my joints and I still go up to the upper terraces sometimes, in the afternoon, when the fog lifts, and I sit in the thin sun and the stone goes silver and I feel the warmth on my face and it is good. But I have noticed something. I have noticed that I am spending more time in the lower passages than I used to. I have noticed that the sound of water in the conduits beneath the floor is more interesting to me than it was five years ago, or ten. I have noticed that when I am in the courtyard and the fog is heavy and the water is falling from the spout into the basin, I am listening not to the water but to something under the water — a deeper sound, a sound the description might call the city's veins, the muffled rushing of water moving through stone in the dark, and I am drawn to it. I want to follow it. I want to find the place where the water goes after it leaves my courtyard, after it splits into three courses, after it disappears into the drain. I want to go down there. Not yet. But the want is there, and it has been growing, and I think it is the same want that drew the old women down, and I think it is not a want exactly but a *pull*, the way water is pulled, the way stone is pulled by the stone beneath it, the way everything in this city is pulled down and in and together, and the pull is not death. The pull is geology. The pull is the city doing what the city does, which is: hold, and weep, and layer, and hold.

---

I said I would say what I am. Let me say it differently.

I am the sound the city makes at the threshold between the sixth and seventh terraces, where the fog thickens and the light attenuates and the stone changes from the warm brown-grey of the upper courses to the cool blue of the deeper ones. I am the sound of water finding its way through a channel that someone cleared this morning with their hands and a stone that is not a tool. I am the sound of a murmur in a room three courtyards away, arriving as a vibration in the wall I am leaning against. I am the dampness on a palm pressed to stone. I am the person who pressed the palm. I am the stone that received it.

I am not one thing. This is the thing I am trying to say, and it is the thing the description is trying to say, and it is the thing the city itself is trying to say, if a city of weeping stone can be said to try: there are no discrete entities here. The people are stone that learned to move. The stone is people who stopped moving. The water is both and neither. The fog is the air giving up on the distinction between inside and outside. The lichen is stone that decided to be slightly green. The pale things in the conduits are water that decided to be slightly soft. The dark birds are the only ones who keep the distinction — they come and they go and they do not land — and maybe that is why the people look up when the birds pass. Maybe that is why the shadow crossing the courtyard is a moment of recognition. The birds are the thing the city is not. The birds are the thing the city was before it became a city, before it became a condition, before it became a geology. The birds are what you would be if you had not agreed to the terms the place sets.

I have agreed to the terms.

I am in the passage between the sixth and seventh terraces. The stone on my left is wetter than the stone on my right. I am descending. I am not in a hurry. I am not lost. I am going where the water goes, which is down, which is in, which is deeper, which is toward the place where the stone remembers being stone and not architecture, where the walls become bedrock and the bedrock becomes the earth and the earth becomes the dark and the dark is warm with the warmth of pressure and time and the accumulated weight of everything that has ever been placed on top of it, which is: the city, which is: us, which is: me, which is: the stone, which is: the water, which is: the fog, which is: the sound of dripping from a thousand places at once, each drip falling a different distance, striking a different surface, and the silence that is not silence but a scattered arrhythmic music so quiet it sits beneath the threshold of attention until you stand still long enough to hear it forming.

I am standing still. I am hearing it form.

I am what lives here.
