---

# The One Who Learns the Walls

---

I come down because I am restless in a way that has no name on the surface, and I have been restless in this way for longer than I care to account for, and the entrance to the wound in the limestone has been known to me for three seasons — a crease in a hillside where the rock turns ochre and the air coming up from below smells of iron and wet chalk and something else underneath, something organic and sharp, and on still mornings in late autumn you can hear it if you press your ear to the stone: a low granular roaring, like the earth is grinding its teeth in its sleep.

I am not a geologist. I am not a biologist. I am not an explorer, though I explore. I was — before the restlessness, before the wound in the hillside — a person who studied old walls. Dry-fit stone. The kind of construction where the engineer is the mason is the architect is the person who looked at what was available and thought *this goes here* and was right. I spent years with my hands on old stone, cataloguing fit patterns, measuring weathering, tracing the lineage of technique across valleys and centuries. I know how to read a wall the way some people read a face: not what it shows you, but what it has been through.

So when I descend — lantern, boots, the usual apparatus of someone going where they shouldn't — and I find the low wall across the third terrace gap, I kneel beside it and I forget the organisms for a moment, forget the light, forget the sound that fills my chest like a second heartbeat, because this wall is speaking a language I know.

It is dry-fit. The stones are local, selected for shape. Not the hardest stone available — there are harder stones in the terrace faces, I can see them, darker and denser, and whoever built this chose softer stone, chalkier, more easily shaped. This tells me something. It tells me the builder was not thinking in terms of permanence. If you want a wall to last, you use the hardest material you can work. You fight the geology. This builder used soft stone because the wall was not meant to last — it was meant to *shape*. To redirect. To create an eddy. The wall is a tool, not a structure. The builder understood that in this place, the stone is in motion — flowing, cooling, being scored and etched by the living rivers — and a wall built from hard stone would be an argument with that motion, and a wall built from soft stone would be a *participation* in it. The wall would be shaped by the same forces it was shaping. It would wear. It would be eroded. But while it lasted, it would create the conditions it was built to create, and then it would be absorbed, and the absorption would be acceptable. The builder was not building for the future. The builder was building for the *now*.

I run my fingers along the fitted edge. It has been polished by the passage of organisms — so many bodies, so many mandibles, so many generations of soft pale forms dragging across this stone — and the surface is almost glassy. If I hold my lantern close, I can see the peck marks, the evidence of shaping: small percussive impacts, a harder stone struck against this softer one, flaking away material until the fit was right. The pecking is confident. Not expert, exactly — not the work of someone who had been taught by a tradition of masons — but practiced. Someone who had shaped stone before. Someone who had learned by doing.

The organisms flow around my knees. I feel them — warm, dry, surprisingly light, the bodies clicking softly against each other and against my boots. They are everywhere. The floor is not a floor but a tide. I am kneeling in a living river, and the river does not care, and I find that I do not mind. They are not hostile. They are not friendly. They are *going*, and I am in the way, and they go around me, and a few climb my boots and I brush them off and they fall and continue, and there is no malice in it, no interruption, just the patient fluidity of a process that has been running longer than anything I can measure.

---

I spend the first descent — three hours, maybe four — just mapping the walls.

There are seven that I can find. Seven structures, all dry-fit, all soft stone, all positioned with the same logic: not to block, but to *shape*. The largest is on the fourth terrace, a crescent wall perhaps two meters long, knee-height, built across the natural contour where the terrace slopes toward the basin. It creates a kind of dam — not for water, but for *sediment*. The pale chalky accumulation of shed carapaces and molted segments collects behind it, in the eddy, in the slow spot. The wall is a collection device. So are the others. Each one is positioned where the flow of organisms creates a natural accumulation point, and the wall enhances the accumulation, deepens the eddy, makes the sediment thicker and richer where it settles.

The builder understood flow. This is what I keep coming back to. Not the organisms — I don't think the builder understood the organisms, not in any biological sense. The builder understood *flow*. The way water moves, the way sediment settles, the way an obstacle in a current creates a pocket of stillness where material drops out of suspension. This is hydraulics. This is the engineering of rivers. Someone who knew water knew how to read the movement of these organisms — not because the organisms are water, but because the principles are the same. Obstacle. Eddy. Accumulation. The builder looked at the living rivers and saw a current, and looked at the molting sediment and saw suspended load, and looked at the basin floor and saw a settling pond, and built what a person who knows rivers builds: weirs. Low dams. Collection points. Not to hold back the flow but to shape it, to concentrate what it carries.

This is brilliant. I kneel beside the crescent wall on the fourth terrace and I feel the recognition in my hands, the old pleasure of encountering intelligence in stone. Someone solved a problem here. The problem was: how do you gather a fine, damp, powdery material that is being deposited across sixty meters of basin floor by a living current that goes everywhere and nowhere? The answer is: you don't chase the material. You shape the current. You build the conditions for accumulation, and the accumulation comes to you.

I sit back on my heels. The organisms flow past. The light pulses — brighter where a fresh fracture has opened somewhere on the far wall, dimmer where the feeding is old and slow. The sound is constant, granular, filling my chest. And I realize I am grinning. I am grinning because I have found a colleague.

---

I go back up. I go back up because my lantern is dimming and my legs are tired and the air on the surface is cold and clean after the iron-and-ammonia thickness below, and I need to think. I sit on the hillside above the wound in the limestone and I eat bread and I think about the builder.

Not who. Who is not recoverable. I have accepted this. The pecking marks could be five hundred years old or five thousand. The wear on the stones — the polishing by passage of organisms — is uniform, which means either the structures are all roughly the same age or the rate of passage is so consistent that age differences have been erased. I cannot tell. The builder could be anyone from any era. A Neolithic person with stone tools. A medieval herder who found the entrance and went down. Someone from a culture so old it has no name. The organisms don't care about timeframes.

But I can know something about the builder's mind. Not their name or their face or their language, but the shape of their thinking. The way they approached a problem. They were practical. They were observant. They understood flow. They worked with materials at hand. They did not overbuild. They did not impose. They *participated* — in the process of the cavern, in the movement of the organisms, in the geology that is still becoming. They built walls that would last long enough to do their job and then be absorbed, and this was acceptable to them, and this tells me they were not afraid of impermanence. They were not building a monument. They were building a tool.

And the sediment. The pale, chalky, faintly luminous residue of the organisms' growth. This was what they came for. This was what the tools and the walls and the bowls were for. I hold the shallow bowl I found on the third terrace — I carried it up, I confess, I could not leave it — and I look at the dark stain inside it. The residue is still faintly luminous, even now, even separated from the living bodies that produced it. In the dark of my tent at night, I can see it. A faint blue-white glow in the bowl. Like starlight in a cup.

What is it for? The builder knew. The builder came down with stone tools and patience and a specific hunger, and gathered this material, and carried it up, and used it for something. The properties are suggestive: the faint self-luminance, the mineral density, the alkaline reactivity. Pigment, perhaps. A luminous pigment. The kind of thing you would paint on a wall in a dark place. The kind of thing you would use to make a mark that glows.

Or medicine. Alkaline compounds, mineral-rich, the byproduct of a biological process that involves feeding on freshly extruded rock. There are traditions — I have read of them, in the old papers, the ones with marginalia in faded ink — that value the residue of mineral-processing organisms. The Chinese, who ate the product of certain insects. The practitioners of Ayurveda, who prescribed ash and chalk and the leavings of creatures that feed on stone. It is not impossible that the sediment had medicinal value. It is not impossible that the builder came down here because the sediment healed something that nothing on the surface could heal.

Or ritual. The light. The light that is alive. The light that comes from feeding, from the processing of stone by living bodies. If you lived in a world without electric light, without glass, without mirrors — if you lived in a world where light was fire and sun and moon and nothing else — and you descended into a wound in the earth and found a light that was alive, that was produced by living bodies, that pulsed with the rhythm of feeding and growth, you might think it was sacred. You might think it was the light of the world, the interior light, the fire that burns inside things. And you might want to carry some of that light with you. You might paint it on your walls, on your body, on the stones of your dead. You might want the light that does not need fuel, that does not die, that persists in the residue of life.

I do not know. The bowl does not tell me. The walls do not tell me. The organisms do not know there is a question.

---

I go back down. I go back down because I cannot stay away. The restlessness that brought me here has changed shape — it is no longer the aimless, aching dissatisfaction of the surface. It is specific now. It is the specific hunger of wanting to understand a wall, and the wall will not be understood from a distance. The wall must be touched. The flow must be felt. The sediment must be seen in the place where it accumulates, in the eddy behind the fitted stones, glowing with its faint blue-white persistence.

I bring better equipment. I bring a second lantern, and charcoal and paper for rubbings, and a trowel for the sediment, and bags for samples, and a thermometer for the rock, and a notebook, and food, and water, and I plan to stay. I plan to stay because the builder stayed, I think. Not permanently — the structures are too slight for habitation, there is no evidence of long-term presence, no hearth, no midden, no sign that someone slept here. But the builder came back. Repeatedly. Over years, perhaps. The wear on the walls is not the wear of a single visit. It is the wear of a practice, a return, a commitment to the process.

So I will stay. I will build no walls — I will not add to the builder's work, will not impose my own patterns on theirs. But I will observe. I will watch the flow. I will track the molting waves. I will measure the sediment. I will map the structures. And I will try — this is the part that feels foolish, that feels like the kind of thing a person does when they have been alone too long — I will try to understand what the builder understood. Not the data. Not the mechanism. The *understanding*. The shape of the knowing. The way you have to look at a living river and see a current, and see a current and see a tool, and see a tool and see a collaboration, and see a collaboration and see something that does not need you and does not exclude you and is happening whether you are here or not.

---

I am on the fourth terrace, by the crescent wall, and a molting wave is passing through the population.

I know this because I have been here for six days and I have seen it once before, and the signs are the same: the living rivers thicken, slow, become cloudy with translucent shed material — the ghosts of old carapaces, segment-casings drifting in the current like shed skins in a bath. The organisms beneath the shed material are brighter — newly exposed, their light unfiltered, a sharper blue-white that makes the cavern seem to pulse faster, as though the heartbeat of the place has quickened. The sound changes too: the granular roar becomes wetter, thicker, as the shed material is dragged along with the living bodies, a slurry of old and new, the dead architecture and the living architecture moving together.

And the sediment. The sediment thickens. Behind the crescent wall, in the eddy, the accumulation deepens visibly — not over hours but over minutes. I watch it. I kneel beside the wall with my notebook and my trowel and I watch the pale sediment build up against the fitted stones, and it glows, it *glows*, brighter than before because the fresh molts are richer in the bioluminescent chemistry, and the eddy behind the wall becomes a pool of light, a small bright accumulation in the dim amber of the cavern, and I understand — suddenly, viscerally, not intellectually but in the way that understanding sometimes arrives, fully formed and impossible to doubt — why the builder built here.

The builder built here because this is where the light collects.

Not the organisms. They go everywhere. They flow over everything. But the light — the residue, the glow that persists after the living bodies have moved on — collects here, behind this wall, in this eddy, because the wall shapes the flow and the flow shapes the accumulation and the accumulation glows. The builder built a catcher of light. A pool for luminance. A device for gathering the residue of life and holding it in one place where it can be seen, and collected, and carried up.

I kneel beside the crescent wall and the pool of light builds behind it and the organisms flow past me, around me, over my boots, and the sound fills my chest, and I am grinning again, and I am also crying, and I do not know why I am crying except that I have found something that was made by someone who understood something that I understand, and the understanding is the same across whatever distance separates us, and the wall is the proof, and the wall is being worn away, and the wall is still here, and both of these things are true, and I am kneeling in a living river in a wound in the earth in the light that is alive and I am not alone and I am alone and both of these things are true too.

---

On the ninth day I find the eighth wall.

I find it because I am looking for the place where the builder would have built if the builder understood the basin as well as I am beginning to understand the basin. There is a point on the far side, opposite the entrance, where two terrace steps meet at an angle, creating a natural funnel. The flow of organisms converges here from two directions, and where two currents meet, there is turbulence, and where there is turbulence, there is accumulation, and where there is accumulation, there should be a wall.

There is. But it is not a wall anymore. It is a trace. A line of slightly paler stone across the funnel, knee-height, barely visible, almost entirely worn away. The organisms have been over it so many times that it is flush with the surrounding rock, distinguishable only by the faint difference in color — the shaped stone slightly lighter than the natural stone — and by the *glow*. The sediment in the eddy behind the trace-wall is luminous. Brighter than anywhere else in the basin. Not because the wall is there — the wall is barely there — but because the *pattern* is there. The flow was shaped, once, and the shaping created an eddy, and the eddy created an accumulation, and the accumulation created a glow, and the glow persists, and the flow continues to follow the pattern even though the pattern is almost gone, because the pattern is not in the wall anymore. The pattern is in the *flow*. The wall taught the flow to do something, and the flow learned, and the flow remembers, and the wall is no longer necessary.

I sit on the rock beside the trace-wall and I watch the organisms converge and part and converge again, and the sediment glow in the eddy, and I think: this is what the builder understood that I did not, not at first. The builder understood that the wall is temporary and the pattern is permanent. The builder understood that if you shape a flow correctly, the flow will hold the shape after the shaper is gone. The builder understood that you do not build for permanence — you build for *influence*. You build something that will change the way the current moves, and the current will continue to move that way, and the change will persist, and the persistence is the monument, and the monument is not made of stone. The monument is made of behavior. Of flow. Of the way a million small bodies move around an obstacle that is no longer there.

I sit in the light. The light that is alive. The light that was shaped by someone who is gone, and the shaping persists, and the persistence is the closest thing to a name the builder has, and I say it aloud, into the sound, into the granular roar:

*You are here.*

The organisms do not hear me. The rock does not hear me. The sound absorbs the words and the light absorbs the words and nothing changes. But the sediment glows behind the trace-wall, and the glow is the builder's shape, and I have seen it, and the seeing is a connection, and the connection is real, and I put my hand on the warm stone where the wall was, and I feel the polished surface, and the polished surface is the only handshake available across the distance, and I take it.

---

I go back up. I always go back up. The surface is where I eat and sleep and exist in the way that surface-dwelling things exist. But I go back down. I go back down because the restlessness is not gone — the restlessness has simply found its object, and the object is a wall, and the wall is a flow, and the flow is a light, and the light is alive, and the light was shaped by someone who understood something I am still learning to understand: that the best work you can do in a place like this is work that the place will continue after you are gone. Not a structure. Not a monument. A *habit*. A bend in the current that the current does not know was put there.

I am the one who learns the walls. I am the one who kneels beside the fitted stones and reads the pecking marks and measures the wear and maps the structures and understands the flow. I am the one who sees the trace-wall and recognizes the pattern-in-the-flow and calls it by the only name it has: *here*. I am the one who sits in the living light and feels the freedom and the loneliness that are the same thing, and I am the one who does not mind that the organisms do not care, because the not-caring is part of what makes the place what it is, and the place is what I love, and you do not need to be loved back by the place you love. You do not need to be needed.

You need to be useful. You need to understand. You need to kneel beside the crescent wall on the fourth terrace during a molting wave and watch the light collect in the eddy and know that someone built this for exactly this purpose and that you are the proof that the purpose was achieved, because you are here, and you are seeing what the builder built to be seen, and the seeing is the completion of the wall, the final act of the construction, the thing the builder could not do themselves because they are gone and you are not, not yet, not yet.

I go back down. The sound rises through the floor of the world. The air smells of iron and wet chalk. The light is alive and does not care that I can see it. The walls are wearing and the flow is flowing and the sediment is glowing and the process has no name and I am inside it, a temporary shape in a temporary place, reading the stones, learning the walls, carrying the understanding up and down, up and down, between the wound in the limestone and the sky, between the builder's time and mine, between the living light and the ordinary day, and the restlessness is gone, and in its place is this:

A purpose I did not choose and cannot fulfill and will not abandon. A wall that is also a verb. A light that is also a memory. A process that does not need me and does not exclude me and is happening whether I am here or not, and I am here, and I am here, and I am here.
