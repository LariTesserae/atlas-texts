I would be the one who is becoming old.

Not old yet. Not the keeper of memory, not the one who sits in still water and thinks about where the next garden should be planted. But no longer the one whose hands shape the soft clay, either. In between. The stone of me has not yet hardened, and it has not yet decided what it wants to be.

---

Let me tell you how I know.

The other morning — if it was morning; the light here does not commit to such distinctions — I was working a fresh flow near the eastern cluster, where a new vent had opened three days prior. The stone was that perfect stage: warm enough to yield, cool enough to hold a shape. I pressed my thumb into it and I could read it. Iron-heavy. Slightly more silicon than the last flow from this fissure. The kind of stone that hardens dark, almost black, with a tight grain. Good stone. Good for foundations.

I began a wall. A low curve, following the fissure, opening toward a young *thara* stand that the gardeners had planted two seasons ago. The stalks were still thin — I could close my hand around one — but the roots would have reached the stone by now, would be threading through it, and the wall I was shaping would harden around them, and in five years the stalks would be pillars and the wall would be part of a dwelling and the dwelling would be part of the place. I was working for people I would not meet, in a time I would not see. This is the work.

My hands knew what to do. They have known for thirty years. Flat stone for smoothing. The gesture — not too fast, not too slow, let the clay find its own level along the curve. Leave the gap for the stalk. Leave the channel for the root. Check the temperature with the palm — if the stone has cooled past a certain point, the shaping is done, and what remains is waiting.

But.

I noticed — and this is how I know — that my hands were slightly ahead of my breath. Not dangerously. Not the way it is when you are young and careless and the work pulls you past the air and you must return with the wall half-shaped and curse yourself in gesture, silently, with a fist brought down against your own thigh. No. My hands were finishing the gesture before the breath was finished, which meant they were working from memory rather than attention. They were doing what they had done ten thousand times before. And the stone, which is always honest, was receiving the shape, but I was not quite receiving the stone.

This is how it begins. Not with failure. With *fluency*. The hands become so practiced that the practice continues without the full presence of the person, and the person — I — begins to drift. Not away. Deeper. Into something else.

---

Here is what I am becoming, I think. I am becoming the one who remembers.

Not the oldest. There are people here who have seen more than I have — who remember vents that I never knew, who held the hands of people who shaped walls I was born inside of. They sit in the still water between the stalks and they think in the long time, and when they gesture, the younger people attend. I am not yet one of them. But I feel the pull. It is like the pull of the water toward the edge of the plateau — not strong, not urgent, just a persistent, gentle suggestion that there is a place I am drifting toward, and it is not the place where the stone is soft.

I find myself, more and more, sitting. Not working. Sitting in the water between two mature *thara* stalks, their fronds overlapping above me, the green light falling on my hands. I rest my hands on the stone — hard stone, old stone, stone that was shaped before I was born — and I feel the roots inside it. The *thara* roots that were living once, that threaded through the soft clay and held it while it set, that died and left their shapes as channels and inclusions in the dark basalt. I can feel them with my palms. Not the way I feel the warmth of a fresh flow. A different kind of feeling. The feeling of something that is present in its absence, the way a word is present in the silence after it is spoken.

I sit and I feel the old roots and I begin to see the dwelling that was here before. The shape of its walls — different from the walls we build now, because the people who built them held their hands differently. The angle of the *thara* stalks that grew through it — they were planted closer together then, or further apart, I cannot be sure, but the pattern is different. The texture of the stone — a period when the vents ran hotter, or cooler, or with a different mineral balance. The stone records all of this. The stone is the record.

And I realize, sitting there, that I am reading. Not reading in the way that surface people read marks on a surface. Reading the way you read a face — by the accumulation of small details that add up to a whole that you know but cannot articulate. I am reading the stone the way the old people read it. I am becoming what they are.

---

My hands are still good. I want to be clear about this. I can still feel the temperature of emerging stone to within a fraction of a degree. I can still shape a wall that will hold. I can still tell, by closing my hand around a *thara* stalk, whether it is healthy — the faint pulse of fluid through its vascular tissue, the particular warmth of a living plant rooted in warm stone. My hands are still my hands.

But they are changing. The calluses are thickening in different places — less on the fingertips, where the sensitivity to temperature matters most, more on the palms, where the stone is held and read. The scars are accumulating — not from fresh cuts, but from the slow abrasion of decades, the stone's patient reworking of the skin that works it. My hands are becoming harder, which is to say they are becoming more like the stone they have spent a life shaping. This is not a metaphor. This is what happens. The body becomes the place.

I think about the gardeners who planted the *thara* that I am building walls around. Some of them are dead now. Their hands are part of the stone — not literally, not their flesh, but the gestures they made, the shapes they guided the roots into, the decisions they made about where to plant and when. Those decisions are in the stone. The roots grew the way they grew because someone's hands guided them, and the stone hardened the way it hardened because someone's hands shaped it, and the dwelling that results is the product of two sets of hands — the gardener's and the builder's — working in a time that is not the same time, separated by years, connected by the soft stone that holds the memory of both.

I am beginning to understand that this is what the old people know. Not facts. Not instructions. Not the kind of knowledge that can be gestured in a single exchange. They know the *correspondence* — the way a gesture made now corresponds to a gesture made then, the way a root planted now corresponds to a wall shaped later, the way a vent that is opening now corresponds to a vent that opened before, in a pattern that repeats over the long time. They know the patterns. They know how the place fits together, not as a structure but as a *process*, an ongoing becoming in which every part is connected to every other part across years and decades and generations.

I am beginning to know this. Not well. Not yet. But the way you begin to know a *thara* stalk when you close your hand around it — not with certainty, but with the faint pulse of something living, something that is still becoming, still reaching toward the stone.

---

There is a thing that happened. Let me tell you it.

Six seasons ago — I count seasons by the growth of *thara*, not by any solar calendar; the seasons here are local, geological, measured in the rate of stone emergence and the pace of frond-spread — a child began to appear at the vent where I was working. She was young. Four, perhaps five. Old enough to swim independently, not old enough to work. She would arrive on a breath, her small body sliding through the water between the stalks with the particular grace of a child who has never known any other medium, and she would watch me.

Not my face. My hands.

She watched my hands the way I watch the stone — with attention, with the specific focus of someone who is trying to learn something that cannot be taught directly. She watched the way I held the flat stone against the soft clay. The way I turned my wrist to smooth a curve. The way I tested the temperature with my palm, and how I adjusted the speed of my work based on what I felt. She was reading my hands the way I am learning to read the stone.

I did not gesture to her. There was nothing to say. She was not ready for instruction — her hands were too small, her breath too short, and the knowledge she needed was not the kind that can be transmitted by gesture. It is the kind that enters through the body, through years of doing, through the slow accumulation of calluses and scars and the particular sensitivity that comes from touching something ten thousand times until you know it without knowing how you know it.

But I began to notice something. When she was present, my hands moved differently. Not better, not worse. *More deliberately*. I was more aware of what I was doing, because I was being watched, and being watched by someone who is learning to see is a particular kind of accountability. My gestures became more legible. Not for her sake — I was not performing for her — but because her attention sharpened mine. The presence of the beginner made the practiced person more present in the practice.

This, I think, is what the old people do for the settlement. They are not instructors. They do not transmit knowledge in the way that surface cultures transmit knowledge, from old to young in a deliberate, structured way. They are *present*. They are present in the long time, thinking the thoughts that the long time requires, and their presence is a kind of weight that the settlement rests on, the way the *thara* roots rest on the stone. Not directing. Not teaching. Just being there, in the water, with their hard hands and their long memory, attending to the patterns that the young cannot see yet because the patterns take longer to see than a young person has been alive.

---

I swam to the edge of the plateau last week. This is not forbidden — there is no forbidden here, there is only what is done and what is not done — but it is unusual. The edge is not a place people go, because the edge is where the plateau drops into the deep, and the deep is not our place, and the water cools, and the *thara* thins, and the light dies, and there is nothing there that serves the work.

But I wanted to see it.

I swam through the thinning gardens, past the outlying dwellings — single chambers, some still tended, some abandoned, their *thara* dying or dead, their stone hard and dark and old. The settlement thinned around me. The density of bodies and structures and living things decreased, and the water opened up, and the light — already dim — began to fail in earnest, and the stone beneath me changed. It was harder here. Older. The vents were quiet. No warm clay emerging. No fresh stone to shape. Just the patient accumulation of millennia, layer on layer, the record of a place that had been becoming for longer than anyone could remember.

At the edge, I stopped. I held onto a dead *thara* stalk — its wood hard and dry, its roots still threaded through the stone, a ghost of the living structure it had once been part of — and I looked down.

The drop was not sudden. It was a steepening, a slope that increased until it was vertical, and then it was simply absent. Below me, the plateau's face fell away into darkness. Not the dim green darkness of the settlement, which is a warm darkness, a livable darkness. This was the true dark. The dark of the deep, where no light reaches and the water is cold and the pressure is vast and there is nothing that grows.

I felt the cold. Not on my skin — I was still in the warm layer, the thermocline above the deep — but in my body. A cold that came from understanding. The plateau is a high place. It is a *small* high place. The deep below is vast, and the surface above is vast, and between them — between the rumor of the sun and the truth of the abyss — we live. Four hundred of us. In a few cubic kilometers of warm water on a plateau that is still being made.

I was not afraid. The edge did not frighten me. But it *located* me. It showed me where I was, which is something that the settlement, in its density and familiarity, does not always allow you to see. In the settlement, you are in the place, and the place is everywhere, and its boundaries are the boundaries of your daily movement. At the edge, you see the boundaries, and you see how small the place is, and you see what is outside it, and you understand something that is hard to understand from inside: that the place is *chosen*. Not by us. We did not choose it. But by the *thara*, which chose to grow here. By the vents, which chose to breathe here. By the stone, which chose to emerge here. By the light, which chose, sixty meters above, to give what it could.

Everything that is here is here because something else decided to be here first. The vents came first, and the stone followed, and the *thara* followed the stone, and the people followed the *thara*. Each one choosing — if choosing is the right word for something that has no word for choosing — to be where something else was already becoming. We are all here because something else was here. We are all becoming in relation to something else that is becoming.

I held the dead stalk and I looked into the deep and I thought: *this is what the old people know.* They know the edge. They know where the place ends, and they know what is beyond it, and they know that the place is small and the beyond is vast, and they choose — if choosing is the right word — to be here anyway. Not because the place is good. Not because the beyond is bad. Because the place is where the stone is still soft, and the soft stone is where the work is, and the work is the becoming, and the becoming is the life.

---

I am becoming old. The stone of me is setting. The calluses are thickening. The sensitivity is shifting from my fingertips to my palms, from the work of shaping to the work of reading. I am not yet the one who sits in the still water and thinks in the long time. But I am becoming her.

And when I am — when my hands can no longer feel the temperature of emerging stone, when my breath is too short to shape a wall, when the work of my body is done — I will sit between the *thara* stalks and I will hold the stone with my palms and I will read what it remembers. I will remember where the vents were and where they might be. I will remember which gardens grew and which failed. I will remember the people who built the dwellings that the young are living in now, and the way their hands moved, and the shapes they made.

And the young will swim past me, on their breaths, in the green light, and they will see me sitting there, and they will know that I am attending to something they cannot yet see. And some of them — not all, but some — will begin to slow down. They will sit. They will hold the stone. They will feel the old roots inside it. And they will begin to read.

And the reading will become the work. And the work will become the memory. And the memory will become the stone. And the stone will become the ground that the next generation builds on.

And the *thara* will grow.

And the vents will murmur.

And the light will come down through the water, thinning, thinning, until it reaches the place where the stone is still soft and arrives, changed, into the green hands of the people who are still becoming, here, where the world is young, where nothing has finished, where everything is in the middle of the long, patient work of being made.
