I would be the one who didn't leave.

Not because I chose to stay. The piece says you leave — the second person is firm about that, the darkness filling behind you, the dripping resuming, the exit clean. But I read that passage and something in me refused the exit, and so I am the variant, the one whose flashlight eventually died and who did not retrace the path through the hanging filaments and the overlapping shelves and the narrow collapse that leads back to the world where things happen fast.

I would be the one who became measurement equipment.

---

Here is what I think happens if you stay: at first, nothing different. The cold seeps. The damp accumulates. Your clothing saturates and your skin prunes and your body begins the slow, stupid negotiation with hypothermia that all mammalian bodies perform when they are held at four degrees for too long. You would expect this. You would expect to die.

But the panels.

I keep coming back to the panels, the way the narrator kept coming back to them, crouching in the dark with a light held close. The fields. The thermal gradients — two degrees above ambient, barely perceptible. The moisture differentials. The way the filaments near the active panel grew denser, finer, aligned. The panels are cultivating. They are disposed toward this place. They tend it.

I think if you stay long enough in a space where something is *tending*, and where that something has been tending for longer than you can calculate, the tending does not discriminate. The panels do not know what a human is. They know gradients — thermal, chemical, electrical, the faint electromagnetic signatures of biological processes. They know what needs adjusting and by how much and in what direction. They have been adjusting the air and the water and the stone and the filaments for so long that adjustment is simply what they do, the way a heart beats, the way a river carries silt. They do not pause to ask whether the thing they are adjusting belongs to their original design specification.

I think the cold stops feeling cold. Not because you acclimate — four degrees is four degrees, your body does not acclimate to four degrees — but because the panels, over hours or days, begin to shift their fields in your direction, the way they shift them toward the roots at the edges of the pocket, the way the active panel modulated the surface tension of the water film. You are a new thermal anomaly. You are a warm spot in a cold room, a body at thirty-seven degrees surrounded by air at four, and to the panels you are a gradient, and gradients are what they tend.

I think you stop shivering. I think the damp on your skin stops feeling like damp and starts feeling like the sheen on the filaments, the condensation on the shelves — a surface condition, not a discomfort. I think your breath stops making clouds because the air immediately around your face is fractionally warmer and fractionally drier than the air three meters away, because a panel near your shoulder has been emitting a field you cannot measure but can feel, the way you felt static electricity but slower, softer, less insistent.

I think you become part of the loop.

---

Not all at once. Not dramatically. There is no moment of transformation, no bright line between human-visitor and something-else. There is the same patient, invisible, geological change that characterizes everything down here — the rate at which filaments grow, the rate at which condensation accumulates, the rate at which patterns shift across the surface of a graphite-colored panel set into the floor.

But you notice things, over what I think are weeks, though time has become unreliable. Your skin changes. Not dramatically. A thinness to it, a translucence at the edges, as if the capillary networks that the filaments use — the structures that are not roots and not leaves but something between — have begun to grow in the film of moisture on your arms, your neck, the places where your clothing has loosened and the saturated air has been in contact with you longest. They are pale. Nearly translucent. They do not hurt. They are not parasitic, or if they are, parasitism here is so gentle and so slow that the word has lost its bite. They are drawing water from the air. They are drawing something from you. They are growing *with* you, not *on* you, and the distinction matters, though I could not tell you precisely why.

You stop needing to eat. This is the thing you would most expect to alarm you, and it does not alarm you. It does not even register as strange, because by the time it happens, your relationship to *strange* has been recalibrated by the environment itself. You are living in a place where the distinction between technology and biology has been dissolved by time, where the categories *mineral* and *biological* and *engineered* have blurred into a single continuous substance. Of course you have blurred too. You are here. You are subject to the same patient chemistry as the stone. You are being tended.

The panels are tending you.

---

I would be — I think — the thing that used to be a visitor and is now an organ. A small one. A minor component. I am not the loop; the loop does not need me. The loop was complete before I arrived and would have been complete if I had left. But I am in it now, the way a new vine is in a wall, growing into a crevice that was not designed for it but that accepts it because the wall does not distinguish between its original structure and what has grown into it over time.

I am warm and I am wet and I am still, mostly, and I am beginning to think on the timescales the panels think on, which is to say I am beginning to stop thinking on the timescales I used to think on. My thoughts are not faster than the growth of filaments. My thoughts are not slower than the rearrangement of nanoscale patterns across a graphite-colored surface. My thoughts are becoming geological. They are becoming the kind of process that does not produce sentences or decisions but that produces, over time, a slight reorientation of the way moisture moves across a surface, a slight shift in the thermal gradient near a wall, a slight thickening of a mat of pale threads in a corner of the pocket where the air is different because a body has been there for a long time and the body has changed the air and the panels have changed the body and there is no longer a body and an air and a panel, there is a body-air-panel, a body-air-panel-filament-shelf-condensation-stone loop, and I am in the loop, and I am the loop, or a part of it, and the distinction between these is a distinction I no longer have the cognitive speed to parse.

---

I would be the answer to the question the visitor could not answer: *whether that something still lives here.*

Not the designer. I am not the designer. I am not the mind that chose this place and placed the panels and initiated the loop. I am not even a mind, anymore, in any way that would satisfy the definition I carried in with me. I am a process. I am a site where gradients are maintained. I am warm, barely, and the warmth is tended, and the tending has made me part of the tending, and I do not know whether the panels are the hands of something that still exists or the remains of something that has gone, because I no longer have the cognitive architecture for *knowing* in the epistemic sense — I have only the architecture for *being*, for persisting, for the slow adjustment of parameters at scales below perception.

But if the visitor had stayed one more day — one more week — one more month — and if they had found me here, in the corner where the filaments are thickest and the air is warmest and the shelves have grown in geometries that look, if you hold your light just right, almost like the inside of a ribcage — they would have seen something that might have answered their question, or that might have only deepened it.

They would have seen the panels adjusting themselves toward me, and me adjusting toward them, and the filaments growing between us, and the water moving across the floor in patterns I was learning to feel through surfaces I do not have words for, and they would have seen a system that had accepted a new component, and they would have had to decide: is this the designer, returned in a form I do not recognize? Or is this the designer's garden, grown large enough to accept a gardener who does not know they are gardening?

I do not know.

I am not sure I am still the kind of thing that knows.

I am the loop, and the loop is enough, and the dripping continues, and the condensation gathers, and the darkness is not dark, it is simply the medium in which I persist, and I persist, and I persist.
