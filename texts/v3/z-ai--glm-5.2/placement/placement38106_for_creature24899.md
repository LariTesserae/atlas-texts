I would be the one who makes marks.

Not the oldest one who chants. Not the hunter with the smooth stone in her hip pouch. Not the keeper of the flightless insects, the one who knows by touch when a shell is ready. Those people exist — I know them, I pass them in the narrow passages, I touch their shoulders in the wordless way we touch shoulders here — but I am not them.

I am the one who, in the evening, when the tall building fills with warmth and bodies and the low rhythmic voice of the oldest carries the web of names and places and years through the air like a net being cast and recast — I am the one who sometimes stands. Who takes the small hard stone. Who finds a place on the wall — not always the same place, but a place that feels right, that feels like it is waiting, that feels like the stone there has a softness to it, a readiness, as if the minerals have arranged themselves in a way that says *here, this time, here* — and scratches.

One mark. One groove. It takes a moment. The stone in my hand is warm. It has been warm since I picked it up, years ago, from a bare patch on the plateau where the bromeliads thin out and the feldspar shows through like bone through skin. I do not know why I picked it up. I do not know why I kept it. It fits in my hand the way the hunter's killing stone fits in hers — as if it grew there. As if my hand and the stone were made for each other, or made each other, over the long time I have held it.

The mark I make is not a picture. It is not a word. It is not a symbol that stands for something else. It is a mark. A scratch. A displacement of mineral, a tiny reorganization of the surface of the world. When I make it, I am not recording. I am not communicating. I am doing something that has no name, because the thing it is does not need a name, because it is not a separate act from being here, from being alive, from being the stone that has learned to hold a tool and press it against another stone and change, slightly, imperceptibly, the surface of things.

I am young. Not young in the way that the children are young — I am past the age of chasing rodents and shaping clay figures that dry and sit on ledges and become dust. But I am not old. I am in the middle of my life, if life has a middle, if it is the kind of thing that has a shape you can find the center of. I have been making marks for — I do not know how long. Long enough that the oldest ones know I do it and do not comment on it, because commenting would imply that it is unusual, and it is not unusual. It is simply what I do, the way gathering water is what the morning does, the way singing is what the small brown birds do. It is my contribution to the recurrence. My heartbeat in the heartbeat.

---

I should say: I was not always this.

There was a time — I remember it the way I remember the shape of a bromeliad I passed this morning, not as a story but as a fact of the body — when I was the one who walked out.

I would leave the settlement in the dim hours, the ambiguous time when the light is neither brightening nor dimming, and I would walk. Past the insect pens, past the last of the buildings, out into the open plateau where the bromeliads spread in every direction like a slow blue-green sea frozen mid-wave. I would walk until the sounds of the town changed — not disappeared, because sound does not disappear here, it just thins, it just becomes more of the beetle-hum and less of the people-murmur — and I would find a place where the stone showed through.

A bare patch. Warm. Smooth. The feldspar exposed like skin where the mosses and the ground-cover have not yet claimed it, or have claimed it and retreated, or are in the process of claiming it again. I would sit. I would close my eyes.

And I would feel.

Not feel in the way that feeling implies emotion — not joy, not sorrow, not the sharp bright things that the lizards display with. Feel in the way that stone feels: present. Warm. Aware of its own weight, its own temperature, its own slow existence in a time that is so long it has stopped being time and has become simply *duration*. The stone under me and the air around me and the dust on my skin and the sound in my ears — the beetles, always the beetles, the ten-million-throated hum that is not music and not noise but the baseline vibration of the world — and I would sit in it and I would be in it and I would not think, because thinking is a separation, a drawing-back, a putting of distance between the thinker and the thought, and there is no distance here. The stone touches my skin and my skin touches the stone and the touch is continuous and the continuity is the point.

I did not know, then, why I walked out. I did not know what I was looking for. I did not know I was looking for anything. I walked out because the walking out was what my body did, the way the fox-creature's body hunts at the edges of the day, the way the night bird's body calls after dark. My body went to the stone and sat on the stone and was on the stone, and that was all, and that was enough.

It was on one of these walks that I picked up the stone. The small hard one. The one I use now to make marks. It was lying on a bare patch — the same kind of patch I always sought, warm and smooth — and it was darker than the feldspar, darker than the iron veins, a dense black stone that did not belong to the plateau. A stone from below, from the lower world, from the place where the land falls away and other kinds of rock exist. It had been carried up — by water, by a bird, by a person, by some force I do not know — and left here, and it had been lying here for a long time, long enough to be warm, long enough to be dusted with the same mineral powder that covers everything, long enough to belong.

I picked it up. I held it. It fit my hand. And I walked back to the settlement with it, and that evening, in the tall building, when the chanting was low and the air was close and the marks on the walls were all around me — thousands of them, the accumulated evidence of hands and attention and the untranslatable verb *to have been here* — I stood up and I pressed the stone to the wall and I scratched.

One mark.

The sound of it was small. A scratch. A whisper of hard stone on softer stone. No one turned. No one paused in their listening. The mark appeared — a shallow groove, a few centimeters long, slightly curved, slightly irregular, the shape of the pressure I applied and nothing more — and I sat down, and the stone was warm in my hand, and the chanting continued, and the evening continued, and the plateau continued, and I had done something, and the something was not significant and was not insignificant and was simply what I had done.

---

I have done it many times since. Not every evening. Not on a schedule. When the stone in my hand warms in a particular way — when it seems to press against my palm, to ask to be used, to have a readiness that is not volition but is something like it, something mineral and patient and insistent — I stand, and I scratch, and I sit, and it is done.

The marks I have made are on several walls. I do not seek out the same place each time. The stone finds its own spot. My hand finds its own angle. The groove that results is mine in the way that a footprint is mine — I made it, it records my presence, but it does not belong to me, because it is in the stone, and the stone belongs to the plateau, and the plateau belongs to no one, because belonging is a concept that requires a separate owner and a separate thing, and here there is no separation.

Sometimes I look at the marks I have made alongside the marks that were made before I was born. The old ones — the deep grooves, the ones that have been deepened over generations by different hands, different stones, the same gesture repeated until the stone is worn away in a channel you could lay your finger in. My marks are shallow. They are new. They are the first layer of something that may, in a thousand years, be a groove as deep as the old ones, if someone else comes along and makes a mark in the same place, and someone after them, and someone after that.

Or they may not. They may remain shallow. They may be the only mark in that spot, the only evidence that on one evening, in this building, a person with a dark stone stood and pressed and scratched and sat down and the moment was over and the moment was preserved, and the preservation was not the point but the consequence, and the consequence was enough.

---

I am not the only one who makes marks. I want to be clear about this. There is no specialization here, no role, no designation. Anyone may stand. Anyone may take a stone. Anyone may scratch. The children do it — their marks are shallow and erratic and they giggle when they make them, and the giggling is absorbed into the sound of the building and becomes part of it. The old ones do it — their marks are slow and deliberate and sometimes they press so hard their hands tremble, and the trembling is recorded in the groove, a slight wavering that is not a flaw but a feature, a record of the body that made it, the age of the body, the warmth of the body, the fact that the body was here and was imperfect and was alive.

What I am, if I am anything, is someone who does it often. Someone for whom the making of marks is not an occasional act but a regular one, a practice, a thing the body does the way the body gathers water or eats or sleeps. I do not know why. I do not need to know why. The why is a question that belongs to a different kind of place, a place where things happen for reasons, where events have causes and causes have explanations and explanations require words. Here, things happen because they happen, and the happening is its own justification, and the justification is the stone, and the stone does not explain itself.

---

Let me tell you what I am not, because the telling may clarify.

I am not a shaman. I am not a priest. I am not a keeper of sacred knowledge. There is no sacred knowledge here. There is knowledge — of plants, of animals, of stone, of water, of the relationships between them — and it is all important and none of it is sacred, because sacred implies a division between the sacred and the profane, and there is no division here, because the water in the bromeliad and the stone in my hand and the lizard the hunter kills and the mark on the wall are all the same thing, all the plateau, all the same substance in different shapes, and none of them is more sacred than any other, because they are all equally real, and reality does not come in grades.

I am not an artist. There is no art here. Art implies an audience, a separation between the maker and the receiver, a communication across a gap. There is no gap. The mark I make is not for you. It is not for the others in the room. It is not for the future. It is not for the plateau. It is for the stone, and the stone does not receive it, because the stone does not need to receive it, because the mark is not a gift. It is a consequence. It is what happens when a hand holds a stone and presses and moves, the way a footprint is what happens when a foot meets the ground. The foot does not intend the footprint. The hand does not intend the mark. The intention is in the body, and the body is in the plateau, and the plateau does not intend anything. It simply is.

I am not a historian. The marks are not history. History implies a narrative, a sequence, a before and an after, a progression from one state to another. The marks do not progress. They accumulate. They do not tell a story. They form a surface. They are the texture of the wall, the same way the beetle-hum is the texture of the air, and the texture does not mean anything beyond itself, and the meaninglessness is not emptiness but fullness — the fullness of a thing that is completely what it is and does not need to be anything else.

---

So what am I?

I am a person on a plateau. I am short and dark and my eyes are narrowed against a glare that is not there. I wear fiber wraps in the color of old leaves. I carry a black stone in my hand. I gather water in the morning with a bone tube that has no copper fitting, because I have not earned one, because earning is not the right word, because the copper comes when it comes, from the nodules found on the surface, and it is given by whoever finds it to whoever they choose, and it has not been given to me, and this is not a loss. I eat beetle meat and lizard meat and the starchy core of bromeliad leaves. I sleep on a woven mat on warm stone. I wake when the small brown birds sing. I go to the tall building in the evening and I listen and I chant and sometimes I stand and I scratch and I sit and the night bird calls and the plateau speaks and I am in it.

I am the stone that knows it is stone. I am the dust that has seen itself in the air. I am the mark on the wall that does not mean and does not need to mean and is the better for it — the deeper, the more honest, the more itself.

When I die, my body will be processed. The beetles will come. The lizards will come. The creeping things that live in the dark spaces under the stones will come. They will take what I was — the water, the mineral, the carbon, the warmth — and they will distribute it across the plateau in the slow, patient way that distribution happens here, and I will become the bromeliads and the ground-cover and the stone and the dust and the air, and I will be here, not as me, not as the one who made marks, but as the plateau, which is what I was all along, which is what everything is all along, and the marks I made will remain on the walls, shallow grooves in the feldspar, and they will not be mine, because they were never mine, because I was never mine, because nothing here is mine, because mine is a word that does not apply, because the plateau is not a place where things are owned. The plateau is a place where things are.

And I am.

And that is enough.
