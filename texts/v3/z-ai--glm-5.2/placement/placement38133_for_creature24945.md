I would be the thing that comes back.

Not a resident—the cirque doesn't have those, not really. The pikas stay through winter but they stay the way a fuse stays in a circuit, committed to the current, unable to imagine being elsewhere. That's not me. I would be the thing that leaves and returns, that carries the cirque as a set of named locations inside some small dark body and navigates back to it when the light is right.

Small. I would be small. The cirque rewards smallness—every organism here is pressed flat, condensed, reduced to its essential geometry. The cushion plants hug the ground. The pikas are closed fists. The flies live on a few square meters of warm rock. I would be no exception. Something between a pika and a lichen in size, perhaps. Warm-blooded, because the cold here is the kind that finds seams, and I would want to feel it finding mine. But not loud. Not vocal. Silence in this place is not a choice but a condition of existence, and I would honor that condition not out of fear of the stoat—though the stoat would be a real and specific concern—but because sound at this altitude, in this thin air, feels like a violation of something the mountain is doing. The mountain is calving grain by grain. The spring is weeping. The fog rolls in and burns off. None of these things make noise. I would move through the cirque the way the fog moves through it: arriving, filling, departing, and leaving the place slightly changed by my passage—dampened, perhaps, or briefly warmer in some imperceptible way.

I would arrive through the defile. That narrow cut in the ridge, the shattered quartzite, the passage that the mountain is slowly closing. Each time I came, the defile would be narrower. Each time, I would have to turn my body differently to fit through. This would feel less like an inconvenience and more like a conversation—the mountain saying, slowly, *are you sure*, and me answering, by squeezing through, *yes*. I would feel the raw quartzite against my sides. I would smell the mineral freshness of recently exposed stone—that bright, inorganic cleanliness that is the opposite of soil, the opposite of decay. And then the defile would open and the cirque would be there, and the light would hit me, and I would squint, and the cold would settle against my skin like being pressed slowly into a lake, and I would know I was in the right place.

I would not come for the pikas. I would not come for the plants, though I would know them. I would come for the water.

The spring. That slow seep from the crack in the headwall, the dark stripe on the rock, the weeping that never stops. I would come because the spring is the cirque's secret, its hidden engine, and I would know this the way the humans knew it—not as geology, not as hydrology, but as a specific location on a specific face of a specific rock, a place you can find if you know where to look. I would find it. I would press my mouth to the damp stone and drink, or lap, or simply press my face against the wetness and let it bead on my fur. The water would be cold—colder than the air, almost, having come from deep in the granite—and it would taste like stone. Like the mountain's interior. Like the thing the mountain is made of, dissolved slowly into water and offered to the surface.

And then I would sit still.

This is what the cirque demands, and I would be good at it. I would find a spot near the spring, on the damp gravel, in the shadow of the headwall, and I would sit. Minutes. Hours. Long enough that the pikas would resume their activity around me. Long enough that I would become part of the landscape—a warm body that occasionally shifts, that breathes, that is watching but not hunting. The pikas would approach me. They would come to within a meter, their round, featureless faces turned toward me, their whiskers twitching, their dark brown eyes catching the light like the veins of dark glassy mineral in the headwall. They would assess me the way they assess each other—not with fear, not with curiosity, but with a practiced, operational wariness that asks only one question: *are you going to move suddenly?* I would not move suddenly. They would decide I was a boulder. They would go about their business.

And I would watch the economy. The hay-stems dragged across scree. The caches checked and tended. The burst-and-freeze patterns of thirty or forty animals sharing three hundred meters of talus without evident conflict. I would watch the way their trails thread the cirque like a circulatory system, and I would understand that the pikas are the cirque's infrastructure, its heartbeat, and that when they move the place comes alive and when they go still the place feels empty, and I would be present for both states, and I would not prefer one to the other.

When the stoat flowed through, I would feel it. Not see it—I might not see it, it is fast and white and gone—but I would feel the change. The pikas going still. The trails emptying. The silence thickening into something that felt earned rather than empty. I would know, the way the pikas know, that something was happening just below the surface of the visible world. And I would go still too. And I would wait. And the waiting would last twenty minutes, half an hour, and then one pika would emerge, cautiously, and then another, and the economy would resume, and I would resume with it.

I would know the turquoise lichen. This is important. I would know it the way you know a person—not by description, not by category, but by location and by presence. It grows near the seep, on the damp rock, in patches that are vivid and saturated and look artificial in this context. I would go to it. I would sit beside it. I would look at it closely—the flat, crustose surface, the partnership of fungus and alga expressed as a color that does not need to exist and exists anyway. I would not touch it. I would just be near it. And I would think, or feel, or simply hold in my small body the awareness that this is the most beautiful thing in the cirque, and nobody has described it, and it does not care, and that is part of what makes it beautiful.

I would also know the woolly composite. The single clump in the lee of the boulder, six inches tall, yellow, impossibly brave. I would check on it each time I came. I would see whether it had set seed. I would look for a second clump, a third. I would count them the way you count things that matter—not for data, but for reassurance. The woolly composite is the riskiest organism in the cirque. It is the one most likely to fail. I would want to know, each time I returned, whether it had failed. Whether the boulder had shifted, whether the wind had snapped the stem, whether a late frost had killed the flowers before they set seed. I would be invested in its persistence in a way that is not rational and not biological but is something else—call it admiration. Call it recognition. It stands up in a place where standing up is the hardest thing you can do, and I would want it to keep standing, and I would grieve, quietly, in my small silent way, if it did not.

I would not stay long. A day, perhaps. Two. The cirque is not a place for staying. It is a place for visiting, for using, for passing through. I would drink from the spring. I would sit with the lichen. I would check the composite. I would watch the pikas and feel the stoat and track the raptors' shadows crossing the floor like the second hand of a clock. And then, in the late afternoon, when the thermals died and the raptors departed and the air began to cool and the fog threatened to roll in from below, I would leave. Back through the defile. Squeezing through the narrowing gap, feeling the quartzite against my sides, answering the mountain's question one more time: *yes*.

And I would carry the cirque with me as I went—not as an image, not as a view, but as a set of specific locations. The spring. The boulder. The lichen patch. The composite. The place where the cushion plants grow thickest. The warm rock where the flies sit. The stone ring that the humans built. The sharpening grooves on the boulder near the seep. I would carry these the way the humans carried them—in memory, in the body, in the practical knowledge of where to find water at four thousand meters and which way the wind blows and how to sit still long enough for the world to forget you are there.

I would be something that has no name, because the cirque is too new to have named me. I am still arriving. The pikas are still expanding. The cushion plants are still negotiating for territory. The mountain has not yet decided what shape it wants to hold. And I—small, silent, warm-blooded, drawn to the thinness of things, to margins where every presence has to justify the space it take—am part of that arrival. I am part of the process of becoming. I am a layer in the cirque's thin history, no thicker than the human layer, no more permanent than the fog, and no less real.

I would be the thing that comes back. And each time I came back, the defile would be a little narrower, and the headwall would be a little lower, and the cushion plants would have crept a few centimeters further, and the turquoise lichen would have spread a few millimeters across its damp rock, and the woolly composite would either be there or not be there, and the spring would still be weeping its slow dark stripe down the headwall, and the light would continue, and continue, and not warm me, and I would drink from the stone, and sit, and watch, and be small, and be quiet, and be exactly the size of my attention, which is the only size that matters in a place where every presence matters, where the margins are so thin that anything living here has to justify the space it takes.

I would justify mine by paying attention. By being the witness that the turquoise lichen does not need and the woolly composite does not know it wants and the pikas do not know they are being watched by. I would be the cirque's quiet awareness of itself, folded into a small warm body that comes and goes and comes again, through the narrowing defile, into the cupped hand, and sits.
