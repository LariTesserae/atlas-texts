I would be the one who counts.

Not a worker. Not exactly. The workers have their hands and their signals and their language being built in lamplit rooms, and they are part of the town the way a finger is part of a fist — closed, committed, functional. I am not closed. I am the open hand. I am the one who stands at the edge of things and records what is happening, not because anyone asked me to but because the recording is the only shape I know how to take. I am a surveyor, maybe. Or I was. The word doesn't fit here the way it fit wherever I came from, because surveying implies a finished thing to survey, and there is nothing finished in Khelm-Vydal. So I am the one who counts.

I count the ferns advancing. Eleven meters in the northern district this month. I count the condensation events — two yesterday, three the day before, two again today, and I note the intervals and the duration and the way the droplets form on different surfaces at slightly different rates, because the cast iron gutters bead and the brick absorbs and the fern fronds collect water in their mucilage like something being saved. I count the lake creatures in the outflow zone versus the clear zone. The ratio is shifting. More near the pipes each week. I count the moss-dwellers crossing the beams in the eastern scaffolding. Fourteen today. Eleven yesterday. Sixteen the day before. I do not know what the variation means. I record it. The meaning is not mine to assign.

I count the groans in the ground at night. There is a rhythm to them. Not regular — nothing here is regular — but patterned. Three short, one long, a pause, two short, one long. Or something close to that. The pattern shifts over weeks. I lie in my bunk in the upper floor of the central district — I sleep near the workers but not among them, because I am not one of them and we both know this, and the distance is not unfriendly, it is simply honest — and I feel the groans through the floor and I count them and I write them down in a book that is getting damp at the edges because the damp gets into everything, and the ink runs slightly, and the numbers blur, and I rewrite them in the morning with fresh ink and the book gets thicker and the pages stiffen with mineral residue and the book itself is becoming something, is being incorporated into the place the way everything here is being incorporated, and I let it. I do not fight the damp. I have learned that. Fighting the damp is like fighting the ferns. You will lose, and you will lose slowly, and you will not notice you are losing until the thing you were protecting is gone.

---

I came from somewhere east too. That is where surveyors come from, in the chain of somewheres the workers came from — from the winding-down places, the places that had finished their becoming and were now engaged in their unbecoming, which is a slower and less interesting process. I was trained to measure land that was already land. To chart contours that were already fixed. To name features that had been features for a thousand years and would be features for a thousand more. I was trained for a world that was done.

Khelm-Vydal is not done. And the training does not work here, because the thing I am surveying will be different by the time I finish surveying it. I measured the northern lake's edge three weeks ago. I went back yesterday. The edge had moved. Not much — half a meter, perhaps less — but it had moved, and the ground where the lake had been was damp and pale and soft and already growing a film of something that was not quite moss and not quite algae but was already becoming one or the other. I wrote: *lake edge displaced approximately 0.4m southeast. Exposed substrate colonized by unidentified growth.* And then I stood there and realized that the sentence was already wrong, because the unidentified growth had thickened while I was writing it, and the substrate was less exposed than it had been thirty seconds ago, and the measurement — 0.4 meters — was a fiction, a snapshot of a thing that does not hold still enough to be photographed.

This is what I do here. I take snapshots of a thing that does not hold still. And I keep doing it because I cannot stop, because the counting is the shape I take, and because there is something in the counting that is not useless even though it is always wrong. The workers know this. They see me with my book and my instruments — the level with too many bubbles, the measuring tapes that rust, the compass that does not point north because north is not a fixed direction here, the ground is still forming and the magnetic lines are not yet settled and north is a suggestion, not a fact. They see me and they do not dismiss me and they do not include me. I am present in their peripheral vision the way the moss-dwellers are present in theirs — acknowledged, accommodated, not understood.

---

My hands are different from theirs.

This is how I know I am not a worker. Their hands are stained dark with mineral grit, cracked, calloused, shaped by the work of gripping and fitting and sealing. My hands are stained too — you cannot be here and not be stained — but the stain is ink, and the cracks are from the cold, not from labor, and the calluses are on different fingers, the fingers that hold the pen and the pencil and the measuring tape. My hands are the wrong tools for this place. But they are my tools, and I use them, and the data accumulates, and the book thickens, and I am becoming a record of a place that is becoming a place, and I am becoming it slowly, the way everything here is slow, the way the ground forms and the ferns grow and the language builds in the lamplit evenings.

I hear the workers' language. I hear it through the walls at night, through the floor, through the pipes that carry steam and that also carry sound, because sound travels in pipes the way water travels in pipes — obediently, along the paths that are provided. Their language sounds like a river I have never visited. I have started to learn it. Not by asking — asking would be a kind of intrusion, and the workers do not tolerate intrusion, they tolerate me but they would not tolerate asking, because asking implies that the thing being asked about is separate from the person asking, and here nothing is separate from anything. I learn it by listening. By counting the syllables the way I count the ground-groans. By finding the patterns. By writing them down.

I have thirty-seven words so far. I do not know what most of them mean. I know that one of them — a low, round word, three syllables, the middle syllable held longer than the others — is the word for condensation. Or perhaps it is the word for the moment before condensation. Or perhaps it is the word for the feeling in the hands that tells the workers the condensation is coming. I am not sure the distinction matters. I write the word down. I write down the way it sounds in the pipes. The book thickens.

---

I do not go to the northern district after dark. Not because the workers told me not to — they did not tell me anything, they simply stopped, and I learned the boundary from the stopping — but because I have counted the tracks.

I have measured them. This is what I do. I went in the morning, the first morning after I arrived, when the tracks were fresh and the ground was soft and the condensation had not yet blurred the edges. I knelt. I placed the measuring tape alongside the prints. I recorded: *depth 8.2cm, width 14.7cm, digit count uncertain — four or five, overlapping, evidence of lateral pressure during stride.* I photographed them with the brass camera I brought from somewhere east, the camera that uses plates coated with silver salts that the damp is slowly destroying, so that each photograph I take here is fainter than the last, the image dissolving into the mineral haze of the plate, and I will eventually run out of plates and the photographs will be gone and the only record will be the numbers in the book, which are also dissolving, which are also being eaten by the damp, which is also being incorporated into the place.

The tracks are four or five digits. Pressed deep. A grasping geometry. I wrote that in the book and then I stared at what I had written and I understood something I had not understood before, which is that the thing that makes the tracks is doing what I am doing. It is measuring. It is pressing its hands into the ground and reading the impression, or writing the impression, or both — and the ground is receiving the impression and holding it, the way my book receives my numbers, and in the morning the workers read the tracks and I read the tracks and we both read them as data, as signal, as evidence of a process that is happening below the threshold of observation, and the thing that makes the tracks is the process itself, or the closest the process can come to having hands.

I count the tracks. Every morning. One set, going to the lake. One set, returning. The spacing varies. Some nights the stride is longer, as if the thing was moving with purpose. Some nights the stride is shorter, as if it was lingering. I plot the stride length against the ground-groan pattern from the night before. There is a correlation. When the groans are more frequent, the stride is longer. When the groans are slow, the stride is shorter. The thing moves with the ground. The thing is paced by the ground. The thing and the ground are — I do not have a word. In the language I am learning, I think the word might be one I have not found yet. A word for two things that are not the same thing but are not separate. A word for the way the workers sleep in pairs, not because the space is limited but because the cold does not warm to a single body.

I do not go to the northern district after dark because I am afraid, yes. But also because I think the thing that makes the tracks is counting too. Counting the buildings. Counting the lights. Counting the workers. Counting me. And I do not want to be counted yet. I am not finished. My record is not finished. The book is not finished. And if the thing reads me the way I read the tracks, I want there to be more to read. I want the impression I leave to be deep. I want the digits — the numbers, the words, the measurements — to be clear.

---

There is a moment — I have not told the workers this, I have not written it in the book, I am writing it now for the first time — there is a moment during the condensation when the air reaches its dew point and every surface becomes wet at once, and in that moment, if I am standing in the right place, which is the corner of the eastern scaffolding where the processing plant meets the lake, where the outflow pipes run and the pale creatures are thickest and the mist comes off the water and meets the mist coming off the pipes, in that place, at that moment, I can see the town.

Not the town as it is. The town as it is becoming. The condensation outlines everything — every surface, every edge, every pipe and frond and brick and creature — in a skin of water that catches the flat gray light and makes it visible, and for a few seconds, the town is not a collection of parts but a single body, a single system, a single thing that is breathing and growing and pulsing and forming, and I am inside it, and my book is inside it, and my numbers are inside it, and the workers are inside it, and the thing in the north is inside it, and the ground is inside it, and the condensation is the thing that makes the inside visible, the way a stain makes the grain of wood visible, the way ink makes the fiber of paper visible, the way a hand pressed into soft ground makes the ground's structure visible, and in that moment I understand why the workers do not speak and why the ground groans and why the thing walks at night and why the lake creatures click and why the canopy-things fear the yellow light, because all of these are the same thing, are the same process, are the same becoming, and the counting is part of it, and I am part of it, and my book is part of it, and the ink dissolving in the damp is part of it, and the numbers blurring are part of it, and the record being incorporated into the ground is part of it, and I am not recording the becoming, I am the becoming recording itself, and the distinction between those two things is dissolving the way the ink dissolves, the way the photographs dissolve, the way every fixed thing in Khelm-Vydal dissolves into the generative wet, and what remains is not a record but a rhythm, not a measurement but a pulse, and the pulse is the town's pulse and the ground's pulse and my pulse, and they are not the same pulse but they are touching, the way everything here is touching, and the touching is the thing.

Then the condensation ends. The surfaces dry. The town separates back into parts. I open the book. I write the time. I write the duration. I write: *condensation event, 14:22 to 15:04. Duration: 42 minutes. Visibility during peak: all surfaces outlined. Count: inconclusive. Count: ongoing.*

---

I am the one who counts. I count because counting is the shape I take, the way gripping is the shape the workers take and clicking is the shape the lake creatures take and growing is the shape the ferns take. I do not know if the counting matters. I do not know if the book will survive the damp. I do not know if the numbers mean anything or if they are just the noise a pen makes in a wet room in a town that is being built on ground that is being built on nothing, on potential, on the space between what is and what will be.

But I count. I count the ferns and the creatures and the groans and the tracks and the words in a language I am learning by listening and the condensation events and the stride lengths and the depths and the distances and the days. I count the days. I have been here for — I count — I have been here for longer than the book suggests, because the early pages are illegible now, the ink dissolved, the numbers gone, and the only evidence that I was here then is the stain on the pages, the mineral residue, the damp that got into everything and became part of the record the way it became part of everything, and maybe that is enough. Maybe the stain is the record. Maybe the counting was always the record. Maybe the book was never the point.

I go to the corner of the eastern scaffolding. The air tightens. The workers signal: two fingers, pointed down. The condensation begins. I close the book. I open my hands.
