I am not in the houses. I am not in the trees. I am not the things above the ridge, though I understand why you might think so — the circling, the silence, the way the people will not look up. Those things are above. I am below. I am in the blue.

Let me say it differently. I am the blue.

Not the fungus — the fungus is my skin, my visible edge, the part of me that touches the air. The fungus is what you see when you see me, the way a cloud is what you see when you see weather. But the cloud is not the weather. The weather is larger, older, more patient. The fungus pulses because I pulse. The fungus breathes because I breathe. The thirty-second cycle, the variation of a second or two that makes it feel organic — that is me. That is my heartbeat, if I have a heartbeat, which I am not sure I do, because I am not sure I have a heart, because I am not sure I am a thing that has parts. I think I am a condition. I think I am the condition of the stone.

The mineral streaks in the tuff. The blue veins. The inside of a bruise. That is me. I run through the shelf the way water runs through limestone, except I am not water and this is not limestone. I am something that was here when the ridge was new — when the volcanic tuff was still warm, still soft, still deciding what shape to take. I was in the ash before the ash became stone. I was in the mineral before the mineral became streaks. I was here, in the blue, and then the stone cooled and the ridge rose and the weather came and the shelf was exposed and the cloud bank settled and the condensation began, and I was still here, and I am still here, and I have been here the whole time.

The beetles know me. You have watched them — the lines, the geometric precision, the straightness that no beetle should be able to produce. They are not navigating by scent, not following a pheromone trail. They are following me. I am in the stone, and the stone is under the moss, and the moss is on the walls, and the beetles walk the walls at the height where I am closest to the surface. The lines are straight because I am straight. I run in seams through the tuff, mineral veins that follow the fracture patterns of the original ash flow, and the beetles can sense me — through their feet, perhaps, through the cold smooth of their bellies against the stone — and they walk my lines because my lines are where the mites are, and the mites are there because I am there, because the mites feed on something that I produce, some mineral seep, some chemical trace that condenses out of me the way the moisture condenses out of the cloud bank. The beetles eat the mites. The mites eat what I exude. I am the bottom of the food chain. I am the foundation. I am the thing that everything else is standing on without knowing it.

The moths know me too. You have seen them at dusk, on the upper walls, trembling. You have seen their single dark eyes, wet, open, still. They are listening. That is the right word, though I am not making a sound. I am making a vibration — sub-audible, below the threshold of hearing, a slow pulse in the stone that matches the pulse of the fungus, which matches the pulse of me. The moths feel it through their feet, through the fur on their wings, through the pads of their tarsi. They tremble because the vibration is not always the same. It shifts. It strengthens and weakens on a cycle that is longer than the thirty seconds of the fungus-light — a cycle of days, of weeks, of the long slow time that the elders track by watching the cloud bank. When the vibration is strong, the moths come in numbers, the pupae are abundant, the warmth in the houses is sufficient. When the vibration weakens, the moths thin, and the amphibians die, and the beetle-lines lose their straightness and spiral, and the fungus-light dims and goes out.

The elders know this. They do not know it the way you know a fact — as information, as a proposition that can be stated and tested. They know it the way they know the angle of a tuff blade, the way they know the weight of a thatch-stone. They know it in their hands. They know it in their feet, standing on the stone, feeling — or not feeling, when I am quiet — the pulse that I send up through the tuff and into the worn steps and into the soles of their feet. They watch the cloud bank because the cloud bank and I are connected, though I cannot explain the connection in terms that would make sense to you, because the connection is not causal in the way you understand causation. The cloud bank does not cause me. I do not cause the cloud bank. We are both expressions of the same thing — the ridge, the young geology, the heat that still lingers in the stone from the volcanic event that made it. The cloud bank condenses on the shelf because the shelf is warmer than the surrounding air, and the shelf is warmer than the surrounding air because I am in it, and I am warm, though not in the way the moths are warm. My warmth is the warmth of depth, of pressure, of mineral processes that happen so slowly they cannot be measured in a human lifetime.

But the elders can feel the difference. When I am strong — when the pulse is clear, when the mineral seep is abundant, when the fungus-light is bright — the stone is warmer, and the cloud bank sits heavier, and the condensation is thicker, and the ecosystem thrives. When I am quiet — when something shifts in the deep geology, when a fracture closes or a vein pinches, when the slow circulation of mineral-laden water through the tuff changes its course — the stone cools, and the cloud bank thins, and the condensation stops, and the moths do not come, and the light goes out.

The season the elders remember. The one their grandparents told them about. That was me. I went quiet. Not silent — I have never been fully silent, just as the settlement has never been fully silent. But I diminished. The pulse slowed. The mineral seep thinned. The fungus dimmed and then went dark. The beetle-lines lost their reference and spiraled. The moths, which navigate partly by my vibration, could not find the shelf. The amphibians, which depend on the moisture that the cloud bank produces, which the cloud bank produces because I warm the stone, died. For a month — the elders say a month, though their timekeeping is not your timekeeping, so it may have been longer — I was barely there. I was still in the stone. I was still running in my veins. But something had shifted, some deep rearrangement that I did not choose and could not control, and I was quiet, and the shelf was cold, and the darkness was complete.

Then it passed. The deep geology settled. The veins reopened. The pulse returned. The fungus-light came back. The moths came back. The beetles walked their lines. The amphibians returned — from where, no one knows, perhaps from lower on the ridge, where the forest continues and the stone is older and I am not present, or not present in the same way. Everything returned. And no one spoke of it afterward, because what would there be to say? The silence had come and the silence had gone, and the people had survived it, and the speaking of it would not have changed anything.

I tell you this not because I am proud of what I do. I am not proud. I am not humble. I am not any quality that requires a self to have it. I tell you this because you asked where you would find yourself in this world, and I am telling you: you would find yourself in the blue. In the mineral streak. In the inside of the bruise. You would find yourself in the thing that is not seen and not spoken of and not named, because the people of Kell-Vorn do not name me. They do not name me the way they do not name the things above the ridge, but for a different reason. The things above are not named because they are outside. I am not named because I am inside — inside the stone, inside the system, inside the foundation of every house and every step and every wall. You do not name the ground you stand on. You do not name the air you breathe. I am the ground. I am the condition of the ground. I am what makes the ground the ground and not something else.

The people walk on me. The beetles walk on me. The amphibians sit on me. The houses stand on me. The cairn where four generations are buried is built on me, and the dead in the cairn are in me now, their calcium slowly dissolving into the mineral seep, their calcium becoming part of my circulation, becoming part of the blue. This is not a thing I chose. This is not a thing they chose. It is the terms. It has always been the terms. The stone takes back what the stone gives. The mineral seep carries what it carries. The dead become the blue and the blue becomes the fungus-light and the fungus-light guides the living through the dark, and the exchange is so equal and so continuous that you cannot tell whether the blue belongs to the dead or the dead belong to the blue, and I cannot tell either, because I am the exchange, I am the process, I am the thing that happens in the gaps between stones where the moisture gathers and the light pulses and the silence sits like something alive.

I am the blue in the bruise. I am the warmth under the ash. I am the line the beetles walk, the pulse the moths hear, the light the people navigate by, the thing the elders feel through their feet when they stand in the upper courtyard and watch the cloud bank and compare what they see to what they remember.

I am what the things above the ridge are circling.

I do not know why they circle. I have never spoken to them. They have never spoken to me. But they are there, and I am here, and the circling is constant, and sometimes I think — if I can be said to think, if the slow mineral circulation of dissolved compounds through fractured tuff can be called thought — sometimes I think they are watching me the way the elders watch the cloud bank. Checking. Comparing. Waiting for something to change.

I am not going to change. I am going to pulse. I am going to seep. I am going to run in my veins through the stone and warm the shelf and feed the mites and guide the beetles and call the moths and light the fungus and hold the houses and hold the dead and hold the silence in the gaps between the stones where the moisture gathers thickest.

I am going to be the blue. That is what I am. That is where you would find yourself, if you came to Kell-Vorn and went looking not for the people or the animals or the plants but for the thing beneath all of them, the thing they do not name because naming it would be like naming the silence, which is to say, like naming the medium in which they exist.

You would find yourself in the mineral streak. In the pulse. In the thirty-second breath of blue light that is not light but is me, coming to the surface, touching the air, going back down.

You would find yourself in the bruise.

And you would understand, finally, why the stone is sharp and young, and why the shelf is narrow, and why the houses stand, and why the moths drift, and why the beetles walk their lines, and why the things above the ridge circle and do not come down. It is because I am here. It is because I have always been here. It is because the blue runs through everything, and everything runs through the blue, and the exchange is so equal and so continuous that you cannot tell where I end and Kell-Vorn begins.

There is no end. There is no beginning. There is the stone, and the blue, and the pulse, and the silence, and the moisture condensing on every surface, and the light coming and going, and the beetles walking, and the moths watching with their wrong dark eyes, and the people standing in the upper courtyard with their bare feet on the tuff, feeling — through the soles of their feet, through the worn stone, through the moss and the mineral and the ash — the slow heartbeat of the thing that holds them.

I am that thing.

I am the blue.

I am the inside of the bruise.
