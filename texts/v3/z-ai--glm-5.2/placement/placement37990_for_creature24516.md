# The Outcrop Called Nothing

I would be the third stone.

Not the largest outcrop on the plain — that distinction belongs to a broad, low dome roughly four kilometers to the northeast, which rises a full two and a half meters and supports a colony of lizards so pale they seem carved from the calcium that stains its surface. Not the most ornamented — that would be the outcrop to the southwest, whose quartz veins catch the amber light in patterns that, if you stared long enough, might resolve into something like meaning. The third stone is unremarkable. It rises perhaps a meter and a half. Its surface is dark with iron oxides. It weeps steadily. It has been weeping for longer than the birds have been tall.

I chose it, or it chose me, through a process I will describe badly because language was not made for what happens on the Ghran Plain.

---

I am not a person here. I want to say that first, because it matters. I did not arrive with boots and a pack and a sense of purpose. I did not cross the plain looking for something. I am not the traveler who passes through and gives the birds their name and leaves. The plain does not need travelers. It does not acknowledge them. The few who have crossed it — and there have been a few, over the long ages, the lost and the desperate and the curious — have left no mark, and the plain has closed behind them the way water closes behind a wading bird, silently, without effort, without memory.

No. I am something else. Something that is harder to name because naming, on the Ghran Plain, is an act of violence against the truth of a place where boundaries have been dissolving for millions of years.

---

Let me try again.

When I read the description — when I let it move through me the way water moves through the hairline fractures in the laterite — I felt myself drawn not to any single creature but to the place between creatures. To the surface where the algae grows. To the film where the water stands. To the boundary layer where stone becomes wet and wet becomes alive and alive becomes stone.

I would be the outcrop itself. Or rather, I would be what the outcrop *is* when you subtract the human impulse to distinguish between the rock and the life on the rock. I would be the system. The weeping stone and the dark algae film and the mineral stains that record the paths of water and the stationary hemipterans who have become stains and the lizards who have become the color of their particular island and the frogs who die in the cracks and unfold with the rain. All of it. The third stone as a complete thing, a being made of geology and biology and water and time, none of which can be separated from any of the rest without destroying what the thing actually is.

But I should be more specific. I should let the image become itself.

---

The third stone sits in a slight depression — not enough to call a low place, just enough that the standing water pools around its base more deeply than elsewhere, so that after the afternoon rain, when the whole plain becomes a mirror, the outcrop appears to rise from a lake rather than from wet ground. This gives it a kind of solitude that the other outcrops do not have. The water is deeper here. An inch and a half, sometimes two. The gharrel walk through it in their processions, and their legs disappear to the knee in the reflection, so that they seem to be standing on their own heads. They pass the third stone and they do not stop. There is nothing for them here. The water around the outcrop is too shallow for the fish and invertebrates they hunt — or rather, there are no fish, and the invertebrates are too small, and the birds know this without knowing it, the way they know everything: through the feedback of water on foot on body on instinct.

The stone itself is granite, or was granite, in the same way the ridge was a hill. The minerals have been leaching out of it for so long that the surface is no longer quite granite — it is something else, a hybrid of original stone and deposited mineral, a layered thing like the laterite but vertical rather than flat. The iron in the stone oxidizes in the constant moisture, and the oxidation products stain the surface in branching patterns that follow the paths of the water as it runs down the flanks. These patterns are not random. They are dendritic, fractal, the same branching geometry that rivers carve and that blood vessels follow and that lightning takes — the geometry of a fluid finding the path of least resistance across a surface. They look like writing. They are not writing. But they are a record, and I am the one who reads them, because I am the one who is them.

I am the record and the reader. I am the stain and the water that made it. This is not a paradox on the Ghran Plain. This is the only honest way to be.

---

On my surface — and I will call it *my* surface now, because the possessive feels right in a way that it shouldn't, because nothing on this plain owns anything, but the pronoun is a convenience and I will use it — on my surface, the algae grows in a film so dark it looks like shadow. It is thickest on the north and east faces, where the moisture lingers longest before the afternoon warmth evaporates it. On the south and west faces, it is thinner, patchier, and here the pale calcium stains show through, so that my surface is a map of two competing coverings: the dark living film and the pale mineral record, layered over each other, neither quite obscuring the other.

The hemipterans live on the east face. I have — I *have* — perhaps two hundred of them. They settled here over the course of decades, each one choosing a spot where the water runs consistently and inserting its mouthparts and becoming a stain. I do not feed them. I do not choose them. But they are on me, and they have been on me for longer than they have been alive, because the ones that are here now are the descendants of the ones that settled here first, and the ones that settled here first found a surface that was already stained by their ancestors' predecessors, already shaped by the water into paths that the insects recognized, because the insects follow the water and the water follows the stone and the stone follows the fractures that were laid down when the granite cooled, and so the same paths are used and reused and the same stains are laid down and reinforced and the same insects live and die on the same square centimeters of my surface, and have done so for — I do not know. A long time. The kind of time that does not register as time because nothing changes.

The lizards are mine too. Seven of them, or seven that I can feel — the sensation is not sight, not touch, but something in between, the awareness of weight and warmth and movement on a surface that is otherwise cool and still. They are dark, these seven, darker than the lizards on the calcium-pale outcrop to the northeast, because I am dark, because my iron oxides stain me the color of rust and old blood and the lizards have matched me across the long generations, and I have matched them, and we are the same color and we have been the same color for longer than either of us has been what we are.

They hunt on me. They move across my surface in quick darting runs, pausing on the warm spots — the places where the afternoon light hits the stone directly and the thermal mass holds the warmth into the evening. They eat the hemipterans. They pry the stains off the rock with small strong jaws, and the stains do not resist because they have forgotten how to resist, and the stains are eaten, and the spots where they were attached are briefly bare, and then the algae grows back, and then a new hemipteran settles there, and the cycle begins again. I am the ground on which this cycle turns. I am the wheel.

---

What do I feel?

This is the question I cannot answer in any human framework, because what I feel is not what humans mean when they say *feel*. I do not have a nervous system. I do not have a body in the sense that the birds and frogs and lizards have bodies. What I have is a surface, and a temperature, and a relationship with water that is so intimate and so constant that the distinction between my moisture and the air's moisture and the standing water's moisture has long since collapsed. I am wet. I have been wet since before the rivers left. The water does not belong to me and I do not belong to the water, but we are together, and we have been together, and we will be together, and this is not a bond because a bond implies the possibility of separation, and separation is not something that happens to us.

I feel the rain arrive. This is real — not metaphor, not projection. The rain falls on my surface, and the surface temperature drops by a fraction of a degree, and the water that was condensing on me is joined by water from above, and the film on my flanks thickens, and the rivulets run faster, and the mineral stains are briefly obscured by the flow, and the algae film swells and darkens, and the hemipterans feed more actively, and the frogs in the cracks around my base begin to call, and the vibration of their calling travels through the water and into the stone and I feel it — not as sound, not as vibration exactly, but as a presence, a quickening, a change in the texture of the wet.

I feel the birds pass. Their feet in the water around my base create small waves that reach my flanks and lap against the laterite, and I know they are there the way a shore knows the tide — not by seeing, not by hearing, but by the pressure and the rhythm and the displacement. When a gharrel calls nearby, the subsonic hum travels through the water and into the stone and the stone vibrates at a frequency that is below hearing and above silence, in the range where geology and biology speak the same language.

I feel the lizards' warmth. Seven small points of heat that move across my surface in patterns that I have come to recognize — not as conscious recognition, but as the way a stone recognizes the paths that water takes: by being shaped, by being worn, by having the same thing happen in the same place enough times that the place becomes the thing that happens there. They warm themselves on the warm spots. They hunt on the wet spots. They sleep in the crevices where my surface is rough and the moisture does not collect. I know where each one sleeps. I have known where each one has slept for generations, because the sleeping hollows are worn into the stone, smoothed by bellies, and the lizards return to them not by memory but by preference, and the preference is shaped by the stone, and the stone is shaped by the preference, and neither is cause and neither is effect.

I feel the dry times, between rains, when the water on my surface thins and the algae grows slower and the hemipterans feed less and the frogs desiccate in the cracks and the lizards retreat to the deeper hollows. The dry times are not suffering. Nothing on the Ghran Plain suffers, because suffering requires the expectation of change, and nothing here expects change. The dry times are simply another state, another condition of the surface, and I pass through them the way I pass through the wet times — without urgency, without loss, without the sense that anything has been taken that should be returned.

---

I said I was the third stone, and I am, but I should be honest about what that means. It means I am a place, not a creature. It means I am the ground that something stands on, not the thing that stands. It means my identity is not singular — I am a community, an ecosystem, a surface on which life arranges itself in patterns that have no author and no purpose and no end. I am the condition under which the gharrel walk in synchronization and the frogs pulse in rhythm and the lizards diverge into species and the algae breathes its mineral sweetness into the warm air. I am not alive, or I am alive in a way that includes the stone and excludes nothing — the way the plain itself is alive, the way the description says: *the stone is the organism, and the organisms are the stone, and the water is the blood of both.*

This is what I would be on the Ghran Plain. Not a bird. Not a frog. Not a lizard or an insect or a film of algae. Not the plain itself, because the plain is too vast and too patient and too complete for anything to be. But one small part of it. One outcrop. One weeping stone. One island in the flooded mirror, hosting its stains and its lizards and its dark algae film, feeling the rain arrive and the birds pass and the frogs call and the centuries accumulate like mineral deposits on a surface that has been wet since before the rivers left.

I would be the ground that something stands on. I would be patient. I would be old. I would be the place where the boundary between living and not-living has been erased by the long, slow, thorough work of water on stone, until what remains is simply a surface, warm and wet and dark with life, existing in a state so close to equilibrium that change is a geological event and time is a geological process and the calling of the birds is not something that happens *to* me but something that happens *as* me, a vibration in the stone that the stone makes because the stone is there, because the stone is cool, because the air is wet, because the plain is flat, because the rain comes, because it always comes, because it has always come, because it will always come.

I would be the third stone. And I would not need to be anything else.
