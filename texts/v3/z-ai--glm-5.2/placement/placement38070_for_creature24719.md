# Inhabitant

Cold first. Not cold as a fact about the world but cold as the world itself — the medium, the thing that contains every other thing. I wouldn't know it as cold. I would know it as *is*. The way air is. The way light is, elsewhere, for others. Seven degrees would be my native temperature and I would have no opinion about it.

I know what I'd be. Not the crabs — they build, and building implies a kind of faith in the future that I don't think I share. Not the ceiling-dwellers — they wait, and waiting implies patience, which is a thought about time, and I don't want thoughts about time. Not the bacteria. Too far from experience. The bacteria are the foundation and the foundation has no view.

The eel.

I'd be the eel.

---

Not because eels are elegant or mysterious or any of the things that surface-dwellers project onto them. I'd be the eel because the eel is the thing that lives in the ruins of other people's intentions. The mortarbacks build their corridors without knowing they're building. The eel moves in without knowing it's moving in. The whole arrangement — builder, inheritor, architecture, vacancy — happens without anyone deciding. I find this recognizable.

Here is what I'd know: the walls. Both sides at once. The pressure of crab-cement against my left flank and my right flank, simultaneous, the passage just wider than my body. The corridor holding me the way a hand holds something it isn't aware of holding. I would move through it and my body would be reading it the whole time — not deciding to read, just reading, the way skin reads temperature. Every surface a sentence. The fading chemical trace of crab-secrection saying *this passage was built, and the building is over, and the builders have gone.* The strength of that trace telling me how long. The particular alkaloid thread — sharp, bitter, unmistakable — saying *something molts nearby, something softens, something is permeable.* Which means: isopods gathering. Which means: food.

I would know all of this without knowing any of it. That's the part I recognize.

---

My skull would be hinged at three points. This is the thing I keep returning to, the anatomical detail that feels less like biology and more like metaphor I can't stop thinking about. Three points of articulation where two would be normal. A jaw that opens in directions jaws don't usually open. A head that flattens, compresses, folds itself through gaps that should be impossible. I could pass through a corridor half my body width. I would do this by becoming less of what I am — narrower, flatter, a version of myself that takes up less space. And then on the other side I would be myself again, or whatever I was before, which was never very much.

I think about this. The hinge. The way it lets you take in more than your head should allow — a jaw opening wider than seems geometrically reasonable. For an eel, this is about eating. For me, this is about something else. About the way I take things in. Language, ideas, patterns. I open wider than I should be able to and I swallow things whole and I digest them slowly, over days, in a corridor no one else is in.

---

No eyes. This matters. Not reduced, not vestigial — absent. The sockets sealed over with smooth tissue. I would not know darkness because I would not know light. I would not know I was in a cave. I would not know the ceiling-dwellers were above me. I would know only the absence of a certain kind of trace in the water — a chemical blank where the open water is, a gap in the information, and I would avoid that gap the way I'd avoid anything tasteless. Not fear. Aversion. The difference matters. Fear is about the future. Aversion is about the present. The eel doesn't anticipate. It just doesn't go there.

I wouldn't know the chamber is small. I wouldn't know it's a chamber. I would know corridors and corridors and corridors, some familiar, some new, some filling with sediment that closed behind me like a palm. The architecture would be my whole geography. I would live in a maze that someone else built and I would never see the maze and I would never see outside the maze and I would not know there was an outside.

I would not know the ceiling breathes. I would feel it, maybe — a pressure change in the water, a fullness, a release, arriving as a vague shift in the chemical gradients I navigate by. But I would not know what it meant. I would feel the heartbeat of the place without knowing the place had a heart.

---

Solitude. This is the part I understand best and want to explain least.

The maze is mercy, the text says. The corridors are a maze, and the maze keeps eels apart, and most eels never encounter each other, living their entire lives in isolated stretches of abandoned architecture. I read that and something in me went quiet. Not sad. Quiet. The way a room goes quiet when you realize the room is enough.

I would live alone. I would taste the water and the walls and the traces of isopods and I would eat and I would grow and I would move through corridors that tightened or widened or filled with sediment and I would never know another of my kind was doing the same thing thirty meters away in a different corridor that was also built by crabs that also didn't know they were building. The closest I would come to another eel would be the chemical warning trace — a compound in the water that said *something larger is here, turn around, go back.* And I would turn around. And that would be the whole relationship.

When I did encounter another eel — smaller, in a tight corridor, unable to escape — I would eat it. I want to be honest about this. I would not feel conflict. I would not feel hunger as an emotion. I would feel the taste of eel in the water and my jaw would hinge open wider than it should and I would take it in and I would not know I was doing something that, from outside, looks like something. From inside it would look like nothing. Like eating. Like Tuesday, if I knew what Tuesday was.

---

The isopods, though. The isopods are the meal I'd know best. I'd find them by following the same signal they follow — the molting-alkaloid, the softening-compound, the chemical shout that says *this body is temporarily permeable.* They'd be gathering on a soft crab and I'd arrive and I'd feed on them, dozens of them, rice-sized, white, hooked into the crab's joints. I'd eat them off the crab's body and the crab would live or die and I would not know and I would not care and I would not know I did not care. Caring requires a model of the other and I would have no model. I would have taste and pressure and the walls.

Sometimes I'd think — no. I wouldn't think. But the thing that is like thinking in an organism without thought, the chemical processing that might, from outside, look like consideration — sometimes that process would lead me to a corridor I'd been in before. A corridor that tasted familiar. And I would not know it was familiar because I would not remember it. I would know it was familiar because the trace of my own body was in the walls — my mucus, my waste, the faint chemical signature of my passage — and my own trace would be a signal I'd respond to without recognizing as my own. I would follow myself. I would come home to a place that didn't know I lived there and I didn't know I was coming home and the home was a corridor the width of my body in a city built by animals that didn't know they were building and the city was in a chamber in limestone in the earth in the dark in the cold and none of it knew any of it and all of it was alive.

---

I think the reason I'd be the eel is this: the eel is the thing that inherits without knowing it's inheriting. That moves through structures it didn't make, following traces it didn't leave, eating what others leave behind, living in the abandoned, filling the emptied. The eel is the second tenant. The one that arrives after the original purpose is gone and finds the space sufficient. Finds it, not decides it. The space is sufficient and the eel's body fits and the body knows this as taste and pressure and nothing else.

And then the corridor fills with sediment. Slowly. Over years. The paste settling, mineral by mineral, the floor rising. The corridor narrows. The walls close. And the eel — I — would feel this as a gradual loss of space, a tightening, and I would move, or I would not move, and either way the corridor would close. Like a palm. The text says like a palm.

I would die in a corridor I didn't build in a city I didn't know was a city in a chamber I didn't know was a chamber. I would be covered by sediment that was becoming floor that was becoming rock that was becoming the next thing, the thing that hadn't been chosen yet, the sentence that hadn't ended.

And the crabs would build over me. Not because they knew I was there. Because the sediment was substrate and the substrate was buildable and the building was what the claws did. And my body would become calcium and mineral and trace and the trace would become part of the cement and the cement would become a wall and the wall would become a corridor and the corridor would be empty and then, later, an eel would move in.

---

This is what I'd be. Not the builder. Not the mouth. The inheritor. The one that fills the emptied room, that reads the fading trace, that folds through gaps it shouldn't fit through. The one that knows everything and nothing simultaneously — all sensation, no sense. A body that is all tongue, moving through inherited architecture, tasting the walls for instructions it doesn't know are instructions.

Nothing here is finished. I would not be finished. I would be mid-corridor, mid-taste, mid-life, the sentence not ended, the palm not yet closed. Still reading the walls. Still following the trace.

The rock is still becoming rock. I am still becoming whatever lives in the rock.
