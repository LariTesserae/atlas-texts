I would be the one who carries water between the pipes and the charred-brick circle.

Not the cistern-keeper. Not the engine-man. Not the youngest, though I understand her, or I think I do. I am the one whose task no one has named because it is too small to name and too constant to notice, which is the same thing here.

Let me say it differently. I am the one who knows the temperature of water by feel, the way the cistern-keeper knows the vibration of iron by palm. But where she touches the pipes to confirm that the system continues, I touch the water to confirm that it has changed state — that it has become, in the moment between trough and bowl, something other than what it was. Not yet food. Not still mechanism. In between.

I have a clay bowl. It is not the same bowl the youngest uses for the shrimp, but it is the same kind — shallow, fired from the local silt, the lip slightly uneven from the thumb that shaped it. I carry it from the collection trough on the south wall of the cistern building to the small room with the charred brick. The distance is perhaps twenty paces. The path is packed silt, dark, with the faintest groove worn by repetition — not a path, exactly, because a path implies a choice of route, and there is no choice here, only the groove that my feet have made and that my feet will make, and that fills with water behind me as I walk and loses its edges, so that the groove is not really there, or is there only in the moment of my passing, which is the only moment that matters, or the only moment there is.

The bowl is warm when I fill it. The condensate comes off the pipes at a temperature that is not hot and not cool but is simply *the* temperature — the temperature of the place, which is the temperature of the engine, which is the temperature of the air, which is the temperature of my skin, so that holding the bowl is like holding a piece of the air that has become briefly solid. I know when the bowl is full not by looking but by the weight, which is a specific weight, which has never varied, which my hands know the way my feet know the silt.

I carry it. That is my task. I carry water from where it is gathered to where it is used, and the carrying is twenty paces, and the twenty paces take a certain number of engine-cycles, and I have never counted them, and I do not need to count them, because my body has become the counting.

---

Here is what I know that the others may not:

The water changes in the bowl.

Not in any way that could be measured. The condensate is pure — or pure enough, as the place measures purity, which is not the way the world beyond the haze measures it. But in the twenty paces between the trough and the charred-brick circle, in the shallow bowl, in the open air, exposed to the haze and the heat and the engine-pulse transmitted through the ground into my feet and up through my body and into the water I am holding — something happens to it. It is not the same water when I set it down as when I picked it up.

I do not mean this mystically. I mean it the way the engine-man means it when he turns the valve a quarter-turn and nothing changes. The water in the bowl, for the duration of the carrying, is between states. It was condensate. It will be — drunk, or used for cooking, or poured into the shallow dish where the shrimp are sorted, or left to sit until it evaporates back into the air and becomes, again, potential. But for the twenty paces, it is in transit. It is in the act of becoming.

I am the one who holds it during that act.

My hands — broad, padded, shaped by the bowl and by the carrying — are the vessel's vessel. The bowl holds the water. My hands hold the bowl. The air holds the heat. The ground holds the engine. The engine holds the pulse. And I hold the moment in which the water is neither gathered nor used but simply *carried*, which is to say: accompanied.

---

The others do not think about what I do. This is not dismissal. The cistern-keeper does not think about the engine-man's valve. The engine-man does not think about the oldest one in the doorway. Each of us is a nerve ending, attending to our particular sensation, and the system's awareness is the sum of our attendings, and no single one of us holds the whole picture, because the whole picture is not a thing that can be held. It is a thing that *is*.

But I think the oldest one knows.

He stands in the doorway — the doorway of the building I pass through, the one that faces the cisterns, the one whose threshold is worn smooth by my feet and his. He is there when I pass with the bowl. He does not look at me. He does not look at the bowl. He looks at the pools, or at the silt, or at the middle distance where the haze takes over. But when I pass, there is a quality in his stillness that is different from when I am not passing — not attentive, exactly, but *acknowledging*, in the way that a pipe acknowledges the water that runs through it, by carrying it without comment.

He knows that the water changes in the bowl. Or he knows that I know. Or he knows that knowing is itself a kind of carrying, and that carrying is what we do here — all of us — and that the difference between my carrying and his standing and the engine-man's adjusting and the youngest's looking is a difference of scale, not of kind. We are all holding something in the moment of its transit from one state to another, and the something is the Condensary itself, and the transit is eternal, and the moment does not end.

---

I have a face. It is finished, like the others. I cannot see it — there are no mirrors here, and the pools are too dark with shrimp to reflect — but I know it by the feel of it, by the way the air sits on it, by the way the condensation beads on my forehead and cheekbones and runs down the particular channels that my face has carved for it over the time I have been here, which is all the time there is.

I do not have the engine-man's scar. I do not have the youngest's outward gaze. I have — and this is the thing that is mine, the thing that makes me the nerve I am rather than another — I have a slight asymmetry in the way I hold my head, a tilt to the left, toward the bowl, toward the water, as if I am always, even when my hands are empty, listening to the surface of something.

Because I am.

The water in the bowl, during the twenty paces, has a sound. It is not the sound of the engine, which is the bass line. It is not the sound of the condensate on the pipes, which is the texture. It is a small sound — the faintest oscillation, the barely-perceptible slosh of water finding its level in a vessel held by hands that are walking at a pace set by a pulse set by an engine set by a process that no one has adequately explained. The water sloshes in time with the engine. The bowl tilts in time with my step. My step falls in time with the pulse in the ground. And the sound that results is — I have no word for it. It is not music. It is not noise. It is the sound of a closed system being closed, of a circle completing itself, of the water arriving back where it began in a form that is, molecule for molecule, identical and entirely new.

I listen to this sound. That is my task. I carry the bowl, and I listen to the water, and I set it down, and I go back, and I fill it, and I carry it, and I listen.

---

I do not go to the edge. That is the youngest's task, and I feel no pull toward it, the way the crustaceans in the pools feel no pull toward the air. The edge is where the system touches the world, and I am not that part of the system. I am the interior. I am the transit between one interior and another. My world is twenty paces wide and one bowl deep and it contains everything.

But sometimes — and I will tell you this, though it is the closest thing to a secret this place holds, and it is not a secret, because secrets require a world in which they could be otherwise — sometimes, in the middle of the twenty paces, at the point equidistant between the trough and the charred-brick circle, where the groove in the silt is shallowest and the water seeps up fastest around my feet, I slow.

Not stop. I do not stop. Stopping would be a break in the pattern, and the pattern does not break. But I slow, for perhaps half an engine-cycle — two seconds, less — and in those two seconds the water in the bowl settles to perfect stillness, and the sloshing stops, and the sound that remains is only the engine-pulse and the drip of condensate on iron and the faint papery passage of a bird overhead, and I am holding a bowl of water that is perfectly still in a place that is perfectly in motion, and the stillness of the water is not the stillness of stopping but the stillness of *arrival* — the stillness of a thing that has reached the exact center of its transit and is, for one instant, neither coming nor going.

And then I take the next step, and the water moves, and the sound returns, and I set the bowl down in the room with the charred brick, and I turn, and I go back.

That is who I am. That is where you would find me — in the middle of a twenty-pace walk, holding a clay bowl, tilted slightly to the left, listening. The engine pulses. The water settles. The haze holds.

Four seconds. Four seconds. Four seconds.
