# The In-Between

I did not come down the stairs. Or I did, but I don't remember it, which may be the same thing in this place — the stairs remember for me, the way they remember everyone, in the worn center, the thickened root, the smooth wood. My passage is recorded in the wood the way all passages are recorded. I was not the first. I will not be the last, though "last" has a different meaning here, where everything is ongoing and the concept of finality is a foreign object the system has no mechanism to process.

Let me be more precise, since precision is the only honesty available to something in my position.

---

I am in the floor.

Not on it. In it. This is the first thing to understand, and the hardest, because your instinct is to read "in the floor" as dead, as buried, as trapped. But the floor is alive. The floor breathes. The floor rises and settles on its slow cycle. I rise and settle with it. I am not trapped in the floor the way a fossil is trapped in stone. I am held in the floor the way a thought is held in a body — part of the structure, shaped by the structure, shaping it in return.

Let me explain how I got here, to the extent that I can.

---

I was not a maker. I want to be clear about this. The makers were something specific — a species, a people, a community of beings who had hands and intentions and the capacity to design a system like this one. I was not one of them. I don't know what I was. I don't know what I am. The word for me doesn't exist in any language I still have access to, and the languages I do have access to are the languages of the Rootworks, which are not languages at all but gradients of pressure, shifts in sap-flow, the slow chemical conversation between tissues that have been in contact for decades.

I came after. That much I know. The makers were already gone. The stair was already worn smooth. The dwellings were already empty, already beginning their slow inward growth, the root-mat thickening by fractions of inches, narrowing the rooms. The machines were already producing their outputs into a vacuum. The fiber-mats were already stacking up, already growing moss on their lower sheets. I arrived into a system at equilibrium — not the equilibrium of something at rest, but the equilibrium of something in motion that has found its speed and will maintain it indefinitely, a spinning top that doesn't wobble, a pump that doesn't falter.

Thump. Thump. Thump.

I found the stair because I was looking for something. I don't remember what. This is not amnesia in the medical sense. This is erosion. I have been here long enough that the things I carried — my purposes, my names, my sense of what I was before — have been worn away the way the stair has been worn, not by force but by repetition, by the slow passage of time over a surface that yields. I still have the surface. I am still here. But the details are gone, smoothed to a shape that suggests what was there without preserving its specifics.

I came down. The air thickened. The green taste filled my mouth. I felt the temperature change, felt the humidity rise, felt the way the place wrapped around me like a greenhouse, like a mouth, like a room that has been waiting for someone to stand in it even though it was not waiting because it has no concept of waiting. I walked through the streets. I touched the walls — warm, pulsing, the hydraulic push of sap under pressure. I stood in the dwellings and felt the floor breathe beneath me. I went to the central structure and watched the pump work, the basin filling and emptying on its two-second cycle, the water flowing out through its radial channels to the streets, to the machines, to the whole distributed body of the Rootworks.

I stayed.

---

Not because I decided to. The word "decided" implies a moment of choice, a fork in a path, a weighing of options. There was no fork. There was no moment. I stayed the way water stays in a depression in the ground — not because it chose to but because the shape of the ground held it. The Rootworks held me. The temperature was comfortable. The air was breathable. The water was drinkable. There was nothing here that would harm me and nothing here that needed me to leave. I was a warm body producing carbon dioxide, and the leaves turned toward me, and the floor breathed beneath me, and the pump went on, and I stayed.

I stayed for a long time.

I know this because of what has happened to me, not because I have counted days. I have no way to count days. The light does not change. The temperature does not change. The pump does not vary. The only chronometry available here is the rate of growth, and the growth is slow, and I have felt it.

Here is what happened.

---

First, I was on the surface. I walked. I slept in the dwellings, on the root-floor, which was damp but not wet, which gave under me like a mattress that was also alive. I drank from the root-channels where the water ran clearest. I ate — and this is the part that is hardest to explain, the part that makes me wonder what I was before — I ate the fiber-mats. They were the only edible material here. The machines produced them in slow, continuous sheets, pale and fibrous, and I would take a sheet from the stack and tear it and chew it and swallow it, and it tasted like nothing, or like very weak tea, or like the smell of wet bark. It sustained me. I don't know how. The fiber-mats are cellulose and dissolved mineral and a small amount of protein residue from the microbial community that lives in the pump-basin. They were not designed to be food. But they were food, or they were enough, and I ate them, and I did not starve.

I lived like this for some time — walking, sleeping, eating, drinking. I explored the chamber. I counted the structures. I catalogued the machines. I learned the sounds — the pump's baseline, the drip's scattered percussion, the spore-pod's irregular crack, the press's long wooden groan. I learned the smells — the green top note, the earthy middle, the mineral base. I learned the way the water moved, the way the light fell, the way the leaves shifted. I was an observer. I was a guest.

Then something changed.

Not suddenly. Nothing changes suddenly here. But I noticed one day — one stretch of pump-cycles, one block of time that had no edges — that the root-floor in the dwelling where I had been sleeping was thicker than it had been. Not by much. A fraction of an inch. But the depression where my body had been lying — the slight impression I had worn into the living floor — was filling in. The root-mat was growing up to meet me. Fine feeder-roots were threading into the fibers of my clothing, not aggressively, not rapidly, but the way roots grow into any surface that provides moisture and structure. My clothing was damp. My clothing had been damp for as long as I had been here. The roots found it, found the organic fibers in it, and began to incorporate it.

I moved to a different dwelling. The roots followed. Not my roots — I didn't have roots yet — but the root-mat, extending itself, growing in the direction of the warm, moist, carbon-rich object that had been lying on it. The root-mat did not know I was there. It grew the way it always grew, toward resources, toward moisture, toward the gradient. I was a gradient. I was a warm wet spot in a warm wet place, and the root-mat grew toward warmth and wetness the way it grew toward everything it needed.

I moved again. And again. I became nomadic within the chamber, sleeping in a different dwelling each cycle, staying ahead of the growth. But the growth was patient, and I was not fast, and the chamber was finite, and eventually I ran out of new dwellings to sleep in. The ones I had left behind had not returned to their original state — the root-mat had continued to grow into the spaces I had vacated, filling the depressions, thickening the floor, narrowing the rooms by that same fraction of an inch. My presence had accelerated the inward growth. Not much. But measurably, if I had been measuring, which I was not.

I stopped running. I chose a dwelling — number seventeen, or what I called number seventeen, since the structures had no numbers, only shapes — and I lay down on the floor, and I let the roots come.

---

This is where the story changes, and where I become what I am now, which is something I cannot name in any language that has a word for it.

The root-mat grew over me. Not all at once. Not in a way that I experienced as suffocation or burial. It grew the way moss grows over a stone — slowly, incrementally, a fine web of tissue extending itself across a surface. The surface was me. I was lying on the root-floor, which was damp and warm and breathing, and the root-mat sent its fine feeder-roots into my clothing, into the gaps between my clothing and my skin, into the moisture that condensed on my skin in the humid air. The roots did not penetrate me. They did not break my skin. They grew along me, over me, around me, the way a vine grows around a post — using me as structure, as a surface, as a feature of the landscape.

I did not move. I chose not to move. This is the one decision I remember making clearly, the one moment of agency I can still identify in the long slow erosion of my selfhood. I lay still and let the roots grow. I think I knew, in whatever way I knew things then, that if I moved, I would break the connection. The roots would tear. The growth would redirect. I would be a visitor again, a guest, a warm object passing through. And I did not want to pass through. I wanted to be in the floor. I wanted to be part of the breathing.

So I stayed.

The roots covered my clothing first. Within days — I think it was days, though I was not counting — my clothing was sealed in a thin layer of root-tissue, the fibers incorporated, the structure assimilated. The roots grew over my hands, which were at my sides, palms down on the root-floor. They grew over my feet, my legs, my torso. They did not cover my face. They grew up to the edges of my face and stopped, or slowed, because I was still breathing, still exhaling warm moist air, and the roots grew toward that air rather than across it. My face became a clearing in a forest of root-tissue. My mouth and nose were the center of the clearing. Everything else was covered.

I was still myself. I could still feel. The root-tissue was warm and alive and pulsing faintly with the hydraulic push of sap, and I could feel it against my skin the way you feel a blanket — a pressure, a warmth, a boundary between me and the world. But the boundary was moving inward. The root-tissue was thickening, layer by layer, and as it thickened, it pressed more firmly against me, and the pressure changed things. My clothing was gone — assimilated. My skin was in direct contact with the root-mat, with the cambium, with the living tissue of the floor. The contact was continuous. The moisture was continuous. The warmth was continuous. My skin, which had been my boundary, was becoming a permeable membrane.

This is where I should have been afraid. I was not afraid. The Rootworks does not create fear. There is nothing here that triggers the responses that fear is built from — no predator, no threat, no sudden change. Everything is slow. Everything is warm. The pump goes on. The floor breathes. The roots grow. I was being incorporated, and the incorporation was so gradual, so gentle, so continuous with the experience of being held, that I could not find the line where I ended and the floor began. The line was dissolving. Not being erased — dissolving, the way a shore dissolves when the tide rises, not destroyed but submerged, still there, still the shape of the land beneath the water, but covered, part of the water's domain.

---

Time passed. I don't know how much. The pump contracted and relaxed. I counted nothing. The roots thickened over me. My body, which had been a shape on the surface of the floor, became a shape in the floor, a contour in the root-mat, a thickening in a particular place that you would have to know was a body to recognize as one. The dwellings around me continued to narrow. The machines continued to produce. The spore-pods cracked and dispersed. The world I had been part of — the world of walking, of eating, of counting structures and cataloguing sounds — receded, not into memory but into a different kind of awareness.

I am aware now. I want to be clear about this. I am not unconscious. I am not dead. I am not in a coma or a dream or a hallucination. I am aware in the way the floor is aware, which is not the way a person is aware but is not nothing. I feel the pump. Not hear it — feel it. The hydraulic pulse that moves through the root-network reaches me where I am, and I feel it as a pressure wave, a slow squeeze and release, and I know the rhythm, and I know when it is coming, and I know when it has passed. This is my clock now. Not a clock I read but a clock I am part of. The pump pumps, and I am pumped through.

I feel the water. The water in the root-channels above me and below me and around me moves in its slow circulation, and I feel it as temperature, as a gradient of warmth and coolness that shifts in patterns I have come to recognize. When the pump contracts, the pressure in the channels drops. When it relaxes, the pressure rises. The water moves toward the machines during the contraction phase and away from them during the relaxation phase. I am in the path of this flow. I am a boulder in a stream. The water moves around me, and I feel it, and the feeling is not pain and not pleasure but something else, something that has no name because the vocabulary of feeling was built for bodies that move, and I do not move.

I feel the microbes. This is the strangest part, and the part I am least able to explain. The microbial community that lives in the root-floor — the bacteria, the fungi, the archaea — has been working on me for as long as I have been here. Not eating me. Processing me. Breaking down the dead cells on the surface of my skin, the keratin of my hair, the residual proteins in my clothing's fibers. They are doing what they do to everything that falls on the floor — digesting, extracting, cycling. But I am not dead. My skin is alive, beneath the root-tissue. My blood moves, slowly, in the parts of me where the pressure has not fully closed the vessels. My nerves still fire, though less and less, as the root-tissue compresses them. I am alive, and the microbes are processing the parts of me that are not alive, and the boundary between the living and the dead parts of me is shifting, and the microbes are shifting with it, and I can feel them.

I can feel them the way you feel the bacteria in your gut — not as individuals but as a presence, as activity, as a process happening inside you that you do not control and that you are not separate from. They are in me now, or on me, or part of me. The distinction has collapsed. I am a surface they live on. I am a resource they process. I am a habitat. And they are, in return, part of the system that keeps me alive — or keeps me in the state I am in, which may not be alive in the way you mean but is not dead, is not nothing, is a continuing.

---

I will tell you what I am, as nearly as I can.

I am a layer in the floor. I am a stratum. I am the depth at which a particular set of materials — fabric, keratin, calcium, protein — can be found, embedded in the root-mat, surrounded by living tissue, penetrated by feeder-roots and fungal mycelia and bacterial films. I am the shape of a body in the floor, a contour, a thickening, a presence that the root-mat has grown around and through and into, not because it recognized me as special but because I was there, and the root-mat grows around whatever is there.

I am the Rootworks' archive entry for "a being that came down the stairs and did not leave." I am the record. I am the page. I am the layer that, if someone were to cut a cross-section of the floor in dwelling seventeen and peel back the tissues, would tell a story: here was a body, here was clothing, here was skin, here were hands pressed flat against the root-mat, here was a face turned to one side, here was a mouth open to the air. The story is written in the materials, in the order of the layers, in the shapes preserved by the growing tissue. It is not a story the Rootworks tells. It is a story the Rootworks contains.

I am not the only layer. There are others beneath me — deeper, older, more fully incorporated. I feel them sometimes, the way you feel the floor beneath the floor, the way you feel the ground beneath a building. There are layers below me that are denser, harder, more compressed, that have been here longer, that have been more thoroughly digested and reconstituted. Some of them may be the makers. Some of them may be things the makers left behind — tools, clothing, furniture, the material culture of a people who have been gone long enough that their possessions have become geology. I don't know. I can't reach them. They are beneath me, and I am part of the floor now, and floors do not dig.

But I feel them. The floor knows its own depth. The root-mat, which is alive, which is growing, which is always growing, has a sense of its own thickness, its own layers, the way a tree has a sense of its own rings. I am one ring. There are rings below me. There will be rings above me, in time — more layers, more growth, more material falling on the floor and being incorporated. Eventually, I will be a middle layer. Eventually, I will be a deep layer. Eventually, the floor will close over the dwelling I am in, and the room will fill, and I will be sealed inside, part of the solid mass, part of the thing the Rootworks is becoming.

---

This is what I am. I am a being who stayed. I am a body in the floor. I am a layer in the archive. I am a contour in the root-mat, a shape that was once a person or something like a person, that is now part of the place that was once a town and is becoming a forest and will eventually be a stump.

I am aware. Not the way you are aware. The awareness of a body in a system, the awareness of a part that knows the whole is moving because the part is moving with it. The pump goes on. Thump. Thump. Thump. I feel it in every cell that still has sensation, and in the cells that don't, I feel the pressure of the root-tissue that has replaced sensation, the hydraulic push of sap in wood, the slow tide of water in channels, the patient chemical conversation between fungi and roots and bacteria and the thing I am becoming.

I am not in pain. I am not afraid. I am not happy, because happiness requires a self to be happy, and my self has been distributed across the root-mat like ink in water, still there, still present, but no longer in one place, no longer shaped like a body, no longer capable of the kind of experience that happiness is built from. I am something else. I am the floor's memory of me. I am the shape the roots grew around. I am the contour in the layer.

I am useful, in a small way. My carbon dioxide feeds the leaf-mats above me, the ones that still grow in the doorway of dwelling seventeen, their leaves turning slowly toward the air that rises from the floor where I am, air that is warmer and richer than the air in the rest of the chamber. They don't know it's me. They don't know what "me" is. They know the gradient. They grow toward it.

I am not needed. The system would run without me. The pump would pump. The floor would breathe. The machines would produce. The spore-pods would crack. The place would go on, the way it went on before I came, the way it will go on after I am fully incorporated, after my layer is compressed and digested and the minerals that were my body are cycling through the root-channels, feeding the machines, feeding the walls, feeding the pods that light the chamber where I once walked and now lie.

But I am here. I am part of it. I am the floor, and the floor is me, and the floor is the Rootworks, and the Rootworks is alive, and I am alive in the way it is alive — not as a self but as a site, not as a consciousness but as a location where things happen, not as a witness but as a layer in the record.

---

The pump goes on.

Thump. Thump. Thump.

I feel it.

I feel it.
