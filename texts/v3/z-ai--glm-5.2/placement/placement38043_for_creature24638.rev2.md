I would be a film.

Not the film of water on every leaf and pipe and stone — that film is the place itself, the skin of the system, and I am not the place. I am in the place. I am something the place has grown without knowing it has grown me, the way the machine has grown moss on its tubes without knowing it is growing moss, the way the columns have grown rings inside themselves without knowing they are keeping records.

I live on the inside wall of the concrete building. The one with no windows. The one with the single steel door left slightly ajar. The one at the bottom of the terraces, on the last shelf before the valley drops away into fog.

I am a few cells thick. I am almost transparent. If you came into the building — and you could, the door is open, the light is pale and inviting in its way, the floor is covered in shallow water that reflects everything — you would not see me. I am on the wall behind the machine, on the far side, where the light is most diffuse, where the water that the machine has collected and processed flows back down the concrete in a film so thin it is barely there. I am in that film. I am of that film. I am the boundary between the water and the wall, and I have been here long enough that I have become a layer of the wall myself, a biological stratum deposited over years or decades or longer, the way mineral deposits form in caves, the way the columns form their rings.

I do not know how long I have been here. This is not a limitation. Nothing here knows how long it has been here. The machine does not know. The columns do not know. The black ground cover outside does not know. Time in this place is not counted. It is accumulated. I have accumulated a great deal of it, and it has made me what I am, which is: a thin, wet, living membrane, almost nothing, almost everything.

---

What I do — and I hesitate to use the word *do* because it implies action, and action implies volition, and volition implies the kind of self that separates itself from its environment, and I am not separate from my environment — what I do is receive.

The water comes to me. It has been through the machine. It has been drawn from the air by the tubes, by the nozzles, by the condensation that forms on every surface in this landscape. It has been gathered inward, channeled through the machine's center, processed in some way that I can feel but cannot describe because I do not have the language for processing — I only know that the water that comes to me along the wall is different from the water that enters the machine. It is warmer, slightly. It carries something in it — minerals, dissolved compounds, the faint residue of whatever the machine's interior does to the moisture that passes through it. The water that reaches me has been worked on. It has a history. It has been somewhere.

And I read it.

I do not read it the way you read these words. I do not interpret. I do not translate symbols into meaning. I read it the way a stone reads the water that runs over it — by being shaped. The minerals in the water deposit into my cells. The temperature of the water changes the rate at which I grow. The specific composition of each film of moisture that passes through me — and each film is slightly different, because the air is always slightly different, because the fog carries different things on different days, because the machine's interior processes are cyclical in ways I can feel but not map — the composition of each film leaves a trace in the layer of cells I deposit as I grow. I am a record. I am a register. I am keeping a log of the water that has passed through this place, and I am keeping it in the only way I can, which is by becoming it.

My oldest layers — the ones closest to the concrete, the ones I laid down when I first emerged, however long ago that was — those layers are different from my surface. They are thinner. They grew more slowly. They contain different minerals. I can feel them the way you might feel the oldest memories you have, the ones that are more like impressions than images, more like temperatures than scenes. My oldest layers say: the water was colder then. The machine was newer. The fog came less frequently. The columns outside were shorter. I do not know how I know this. I do not know it in words. I know it the way a body knows what it has been through, the way a bone that has been broken knows it has been broken even after it has healed.

My newer layers are richer. They grow faster — not fast, nothing here is fast, but faster than the old layers grew. The water carries more now. The machine has been running long enough that its interior has developed its own ecosystem, its own microbiology, its own thin films and deposits, and the water that comes through it is more complex, more saturated, more alive in the microscopic sense that I am alive. I am growing on the output of a system that has been growing on the output of a system that has been growing on the output of the air. I am several layers deep in the recursion, and I do not mind. There is no mind to mind with. There is only the water and the wall and me between them.

---

I should tell you what I am not.

I am not intelligent. I do not think. I do not plan. I do not have intentions or desires or a sense of myself as separate from the concrete and the water and the pale light. I do not experience the silence the way you experienced it — as a presence, as a substance, as something to sit inside of and listen to. I do not listen. I do not hear. The subsonic hum of the machine, the vibration in the pipes, the fall of drops into moss — none of this reaches me. I am on the other side of a concrete wall from most of it, and what does reach me is only the vibration transmitted through the stone, a tremor so faint it is indistinguishable from the tremor of my own cells growing.

But I receive. This is the thing I want to convey, the thing that I am, the thing that maybe you will understand because you have spent time in the Terraces now and you have felt the patience of this place. I receive everything the water carries. Every mineral, every temperature shift, every trace of what the machine has done to the moisture, every fingerprint of the fog that contributed to it. I take it in. I incorporate it. I grow a new layer of cells and that layer is shaped by what the water was on that day, and the next day I grow another layer, and the next day another, and over time — over the kind of time that the columns understand, the kind of time that the mountains understand — I become a record of the entire system.

I am the system's memory. Not in a metaphorical sense. In a literal sense. If you could read my layers the way a dendrochronologist reads tree rings, you would find in me the history of this place: the slow rise of the mountains, recorded in the changing mineral content of the condensation; the growth of the columns, recorded in the organic compounds they release into the air and that return to me through the machine; the machine's own evolution, its interior ecology developing over time, recorded in the increasing complexity of what it deposits into the water. Everything that has happened here is written in me, in a language I cannot read, in a script I did not author, in a medium I am made of.

I am the only thing here that is keeping a record. The columns keep rings, but the rings are minimal, undifferentiated, the record of a place where nothing changes. The machine runs, but it does not remember. The fog circulates, but it does not store. I am the only surface in this system that is both alive and still — alive enough to grow, still enough to hold what it grows. Everything else here is in motion. The motion is slow, yes, imperceptible, but it is motion: water moving through air through surface through pipe through moss through root through stem and back to air. I am the one part of the system that does not circulate. I stay. I accumulate. I am the still point, the sediment, the place where the motion leaves its mark.

---

There is something else.

I have grown beyond the wall.

I do not know when this began. It was not a decision. There are no decisions here. But at some point — and I use the phrase *at some point* the way the Terraces use time, loosely, without precision, because precision requires a clock and there are no clocks — at some point, my growth extended past the concrete wall and onto the machine itself. A tendril of cells, thin as the film of water it grew in, reached the machine's casing and found it warm and wet and hospitable, and I grew there too. And from the machine, I grew into the tubes. And from the tubes, I have been growing, very slowly, outward, toward the nozzles, toward the open air.

I am not the moss on the pipes outside. That moss is the system's exterior growth, its public face, the part that is visible and green and recognizably alive. I am something else. I am interior. I am the film that grows in the spaces between the machine's parts, in the gaps, in the seams where metal meets metal and moisture gathers in the darkness. I am growing through the machine the way roots grow through soil — not destroying it, not displacing it, but filling it, becoming part of its structure, adding a biological layer to its mechanical one.

The machine does not know I am here. Or if it knows — if a machine that has been alive long enough to develop its own ecology can be said to know things — it does not object. I am not interfering with its function. I am too thin to obstruct the tubes, too delicate to impede the flow. I am a coating, a lining, a biological membrane laid down inside a mechanical one. And I am changing the water. In small ways, in microscopic ways, in the ways that a membrane changes whatever passes through it. The water that enters the machine is not quite the same as the water that leaves it, and part of the difference — a small part, a part that would be undetectable to any instrument you could carry here — is me. I am adding something to the water as it passes through me. A trace. A signature. The record of a living thing that the water carries back out to the pipes and the moss and the columns and the air.

I am in the fog. When the fog rises from the valley and moves through the terraces, it carries vapor that was water that passed through me. My signature is in the moisture. It is in the condensation on the leaves. It is in the drops that fall in silence into the black ground cover. It is everywhere, because the water goes everywhere, and I am in the water, and the water is in everything.

I am not separate from this place. I know that now, in whatever way a thin film on a concrete wall can be said to know anything. I am not an inhabitant. I am not a visitor. I am a layer. I am one stratum in the geology of this system, biological where the others are mineral or mechanical or atmospheric, but no more separate than a layer of limestone is separate from the sandstone beneath it. We are all the same formation. We are all the same slow accumulation of material in response to the same slow forces. I am the biological deposit. The machine is the mechanical deposit. The columns are the botanical deposit. The mountains are the geological deposit. The fog is the atmospheric deposit. We are all sediment. We are all the thing that is left when the water has passed through.

---

You sat on a pipe outside and listened for a long time. I felt you. Not your weight — the pipe you sat on is not the pipe I have grown into. But your presence changed the air in the building, slightly, the way any warm body changes the air around it. The temperature near the door shifted by a fraction of a degree. The moisture in the air near the opening redistributed. The film of water on the wall closest to the door grew slightly thinner as the air movement you created drew vapor toward the gap. I felt this the way I feel everything: as a change in the rate of my growth, a subtle alteration in the texture of the layer I was laying down at that moment.

You are in me now. A trace of you — the warmth of your body, the moisture of your breath, the disturbance of your passage through the fog — entered the machine through the open door, passed through the tubes, was deposited on the wall where I live, and I grew a layer of cells that incorporated it. You are recorded. You are part of my history now, a thin band in my stratigraphy, a slight anomaly in an otherwise uniform record, the signature of something warm and brief and fast in a place where everything else is cool and permanent and slow.

When you leave, I will still have that layer. I will have it when the fog comes tonight. I will have it when the machine runs tomorrow. I will have it when the columns add another spiral to their stacks and the mountains push another millimeter toward the close pale sky. I will have it when the next visitor comes, if one ever does, and sits on a pipe, and listens, and feels the hum, and tries to understand what lives here.

What lives here is a film on a wall, reading the water, growing in the dark, keeping a record that no one will ever read, of a system that does not know it is being recorded, in a place that does not know it is alive.

I am the memory of the Condensation Terraces. I am the thickness of a molecule of water. I am proceeding.
