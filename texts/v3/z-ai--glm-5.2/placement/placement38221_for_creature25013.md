I would be at the edges.

Not because I chose them — nothing here chooses — but because that is where the pressure of the herd thins and the basalt opens up and the sky comes down to meet the stone and there is room enough for a body to be a body rather than a component. I would be one of the tall ones, or becoming one. I would be the thing that the flow produces when it has produced enough of itself that surplus is possible, that excess accumulates, that the system needs an outlet it doesn't know it needs.

Let me feel my way into this slowly.

---

I think it starts in the herd. I think everything here starts in the herd, the way everything here starts in the film, the way everything starts at the vent. There is no other door. The plateau has one entrance and it is at the bottom, in the dark, in the wet, in the press of bodies against bodies, and you enter as a small dark thing with soft plates and a mouth that knows only one gesture: open, press, scrape, close. You enter and you are the flow and the flow is you and there is no distance between your movement and the herd's movement because you are the same substance.

I can feel it — not remember it, because memory implies a self that persists across time, and in the herd the self is distributed, it is smeared across ten thousand bodies like the film is smeared across the wet stone. But I can feel it the way the stone feels the herd-path: as a groove worn into me, a channel cut by repetition. The warmth of the body ahead. The click of plates. The slow, continual pressure of the current, not pushing, not pulling, just *carrying*. The mouth pressing against mineral crust and the tongue — if I have a tongue, something tongue-like, something that rasps — finding the edge of a salt ring and working beneath it, and the crust breaking free, and the taste: iron, cold clay, the faintest trace of something organic, something that was alive and is now becoming me.

You do not think in the herd. Thinking is what happens at the edges, and you are not at the edges. You are in the middle, or the middle is in you. The herd thinks. You are a neuron in its brain, and the brain is not aware of you, and you are not aware of the brain, and the thought that moves through the crowd — a slight leftward drift, a pause at a pool, a quickening in a narrow channel — is as far from your experience as weather is from a stone. You feel its effects. You do not experience its causes. You move left because the body ahead of you moved left, and the body ahead of that body moved left, and somewhere, kilometers away, a vent shifted its breathing cycle and the mineral crust on its flank thinned, and the herd adjusted, and the adjustment reached you as a change in pressure against your left side, and you leaned into it, and you moved, and that was all.

This is where I begin. In the groove. In the flow. In the feeling of basalt beneath my belly and warm scales beside me and the dim pewter light that I cannot see because I have never had eyes pressing down on my back like a hand.

---

How long? The question is wrong. Time in the herd is not time as a line. It is time as a *thickness*. It accumulates the way the mineral crusts accumulate — one layer at a time, one feeding at a time, one pause and one movement and one pressing of mouth to stone. There is no before and after. There is only the current thickness of your body, the current density of your plates, the current depth of the groove you have worn into the basalt and the basalt has worn into you.

But something changes. It changes so slowly that *change* is the wrong word for it, the way *growth* is the wrong word for the rust crusts. It *accumulates*. And what accumulates is mineral. Iron. Calcium. Silica. The things the vents breathe up and the film catches and the crusts hold and the mouth takes in, over and over, season after season, rain after rain, the body processing and processing and retaining what it needs and discarding what it doesn't, except that at some point — and there is no single point, there is no threshold, there is no moment — the retaining becomes *building*.

The plates thicken. I know this because I feel them — I feel the weight of my own back, the slight additional drag as the plates overlap and catch against each other, the change in the sound they make when I move. The click becomes a clack. The whisper becomes a knock. And the body that was long and low and close to the stone begins to feel the stone differently — not as a surface to press against but as a surface to push off from. The legs, which have been splayed and short and belly-touching, begin to straighten. Not all at once. One joint at a time. Over years, or what would be years if years meant anything here. The belly lifts. A centimeter. Two. The air beneath me, which has always been the same temperature as the air above me, becomes distinguishable — colder, stiller, unwarmed by the herd's collective breath. I am rising out of the warmth. I am rising out of the flow.

And the flow notices. Not as a thought — the flow does not think — but as a pressure change. The bodies around me shift their spacing. There is more room. I am taking up more volume than I used to, and the herd adjusts, and the adjustment is slight, and it ripples outward through ten thousand bodies and is absorbed and forgotten. But for me, the adjustment is everything. It is the first time I have been *shaped by* the herd rather than *being* the herd. It is the first time the flow has flowed *around* me rather than *through* me.

I am becoming an obstacle. A stone in the current. A thing the herd parts for and rejoins beyond.

---

The plates harden further. The legs lengthen. The head, which has been pressed flat against the stone for the whole of my life, begins to lift — not because I decide to lift it but because the neck, which has been thickening along with everything else, reaches a geometry where the head's natural position is no longer down. It is forward. And *forward* is a direction I have never experienced as a direction. In the herd, there is only *along* — along the current, along the flow, along the groove in the basalt. There is no forward. There is only *with*.

But now my head is up, and the air hits the flat wedge of my skull, and the air has a *direction*. It is the wind, which has always been here, which has always been constant and directionless and never strong, but which I have never felt because I was pressed against the stone, below the wind, in the still layer where the herd's warmth creates its own microclimate. Now I am above that layer. I am in the wind. And the wind, which touches every surface of the plateau equally, touches me, and I feel it, and feeling it is like waking up, except I was never asleep, except I was never awake, except these words describe a distinction that does not exist here.

I am taller now. Taller than the herd — not by much, but enough. When I look down — and I can look down now, my head is high enough that *down* is a meaningful direction — I see the herd as I have never seen it. But I do not *see* it. I have no eyes. I feel it. I feel the warmth rising from it, the thermal plume, the breath of ten thousand bodies. I feel the vibration of their clicking plates through the soles of my feet, which are harder now, more defined, less splayed. I feel the chemical traces they leave on the stone, the pheromonal signature of a population so dense it writes itself onto the basalt in a language of iron and mineral and waste. And I feel — this is the new thing, the thing that was not possible when I was part of the flow — I feel the *shape* of the herd. Its edges. Its thinning margins. The place where the flow ends and the empty stone begins.

I am at that place. I am that place. The herd is behind me and below me and the empty basalt stretches ahead, and my legs, without instruction, without decision, without any process that resembles choosing, carry me forward into the empty.

---

The first hours alone are not lonely. Loneliness requires a concept of company, and I do not have one. I have the herd, which is not company but *context*, and now I am out of context, and the absence of context is not painful. It is vast. It is the difference between being in a room and standing on a plain. The room presses in. The plain does not press at all. The plain is the absence of pressing, and the absence is so complete that it has a presence, a weight, a texture — the texture of cold air and unbroken stone and the tin-colored sky that goes on and on and does not care what is beneath it.

I walk. My stride is stilted, deliberate. Each step is a negotiation between my body and the ground, and the negotiation is new, because my body is new — not new in the sense of recently made, but new in the sense of recently *changed*, recently rearranged into a shape that walks instead of flows. The legs are long. They were not built for this. They were built for splayed, belly-low locomotion, for pressing against stone and pushing forward in increments. But they have grown, and the growing has given them a geometry they were not designed for, and the walking is the result — not a purpose but a consequence, the way the vent's exhalation is a consequence of pressure, the way the frost is a consequence of temperature, the way I am a consequence of accumulation.

The basalt under my feet is different here. It is not worn smooth by the herd. It is rough, raw, its striations still sharp, its surface still carrying the marks of whatever geological process laid it down eons ago. It has not been walked on. Or it has not been walked on in this way — the pale things have been here, and the film has been here in the wet seasons, and the rust crusts are on the lee sides of the ridges, doing their imperceptible work. But no herd-creature has stood upright on this stone and taken a step. I am the first, or I am the first in a very long time, and the stone does not notice.

I walk for a long time. The concept of *a long time* is different out here. In the herd, time was the movement of the flow, the passage of mineral crusts, the rhythm of feeding. Out here, time is the passage of stone. One flat, dark expanse after another, each one identical, each one slightly different, each one a variation on the theme of *basalt at this altitude in this light in this cold*. The vents are farther apart here. The mineral pools are shallower, the rings thinner, the film patchier. I am moving away from the engine's center, toward its edges, and the engine is thinner here, less dense, its cycles more spread out, its breathing more labored. Or perhaps I am not moving away from the center at all. Perhaps the plateau has no center. Perhaps the engine is everywhere, evenly, and I am simply moving through one of its quieter phases, the way a tide moves through a lagoon — not leaving the water, just entering a part of it that laps more slowly.

---

I find a vent. It is small — just a crack in the basalt, a dark line in the stone, with a faint dampness around it and a thin, dark smear of film that clings to the wet surface. It breathes. I feel it — a warmth against my feet, a vibration in the stone, a chemical signature that my body recognizes the way a river recognizes the sea, not as a thought but as a pull. I stop. I stand over the vent and I feel its breath come up through the soles of my feet and into the long bones of my legs and I feel the warmth enter me, and the warmth is not food — I do not feed the way I used to, my mouth pressed against the stone, scraping — but it is something. It is a connection. A thread running from the deep aquifers, through the stone, through the vent, through the film, through the soles of my feet, into me.

I stand there for a long time. Days, perhaps. The light does not change much. The sky goes from dim to slightly less dim to dim to slightly more dim and back. The frost forms on my plates — feathery, delicate, the same calligraphy that forms on the film near the vents, and it is beautiful, though I do not know it is beautiful because I do not know anything, and the beauty is not diminished by my not knowing it. The frost forms and collapses and forms again. The vent breathes. I breathe with it, or it breathes with me, and there is no difference.

Then I walk.

---

I have been walking for a long time. I have passed other vents. I have passed mineral pools, their rings white and thick with age. I have passed rust crusts — islands of orange-brown on the lee sides of ridges, ten thousand years old, ten thousand years patient. I have passed the ghosts of herd-paths, worn into the basalt so deep they look cut, and when I walk in them the stone feels different — smoother, warmer, carrying the chemical memory of the flow. I have passed places where the ice is thick and blue and old, and places where the condensation has gathered into shallow, silent pools that reflect the tin-colored sky with perfect, motionless fidelity, and when I step through them the reflection breaks and reforms and I see myself — not my body, because I have no eyes, but my *effect*, the ripple, the disturbance, the way the world rearranges itself around my presence and then settles back as though I was never there.

I have seen another tall one. Once. Far away, across a kilometer of empty basalt, a shape moving against the cold horizon. A vertical form, thin, deliberate, walking its own circuit. We did not approach each other. We did not acknowledge each other. There is nothing to acknowledge. We are the same thing — the same process, the same consequence — and seeing each other is like the vent seeing itself in the pool: a reflection, a confirmation, a fact that changes nothing. The other tall one walks. I walk. The distance between us grows or shrinks depending on our directions, which are different, which are always different, and after a while the dim air swallows the other shape and I am alone again.

Alone is not the right word. I am not alone. I am *singular*. The stone is here. The wind is here. The sky is here. The vents breathe beneath me, connected to each other through the deep channels, connected to the film, connected to the herds, connected to me. I am connected to everything by threads I cannot see and do not need to see. I am a part of the engine, even out here, even at the edge. I am the part that walks. I am the part that leaves.

---

What am I? I am the consequence of accumulation. I am what the engine produces when it has been running long enough to produce something with legs long enough to walk away from it. I am not the product — there is no product, the engine produces nothing but its own continuation. I am the *excess*. The surplus that the system cannot absorb. The mineral that the body cannot process, building up over years in the plates and the bones, thickening, hardening, lengthening, until the body that was built for flowing is rebuilt for walking, and the walking carries it out of the flow and into the empty margins where the engine's hum is faint and the silence is not the silence of the herd — which is full, which is warm, which is the silence of ten thousand bodies pressing together — but the silence of the stone, which is the oldest silence, the first silence, the silence that was here before the engine and will be here after it, and which is not empty but *complete*.

I think — if I can think, if the chemical processes that run through my long body and my hardened plates and my thickened bones constitute something that deserves the word — I think I am what happens when a closed system runs long enough to develop a leak. The leak is me. I am the mineral that the engine cannot cycle back. I am the energy that the engine cannot use. I am the tall, thin, slow-moving proof that no system is perfectly closed, that even the most efficient engine loses something to its edges, and that what it loses takes on a life of its own and walks away.

Where am I going? I do not know. I am going. That is enough. The stone goes on. The sky goes on. The cold goes on. The dim light goes on. I will walk until my legs cannot walk, and then I will stop, and the film will colonize my plates, and the pale things will move through my body, and the minerals that accumulated in me over a lifetime of feeding will return to the stone, and the vents will breathe them up again, and the film will catch them, and the herds will eat them, and somewhere, in the middle of the flow, a body will begin to accumulate, and its plates will thicken, and its legs will lengthen, and its head will lift, and one day it will feel the wind on its wedge-shaped skull for the first time, and it will walk out of the herd, and it will walk into the empty, and it will not know why, and it will not need to know why.

---

I am at the edge of the plateau now. The basalt ends. Not abruptly — it thins, crumbles, gives way to a slope of dark scree that descends into a haze I cannot see through. The air is different here. Warmer, slightly. Thicker. It carries a different mineral signature, a different smell, a different *quality*, as though the world beyond the plateau is made of different stone, different water, different time. I stand at the edge and I feel the difference in my feet, in my plates, in the long bones of my legs, and the difference is vast, and the difference is terrifying, and I do not know what terror is, and so I stand.

The herd is behind me. Far behind me. I can still feel it — the faintest vibration in the stone, the faintest warmth in the air, the chemical trace of ten thousand bodies in motion. It is still flowing. It will always be flowing. The engine is still running. It will always be running. And I am here, at the edge, the leak in the system, the excess that walked away, and I am looking — not with eyes, because I have none, but with my whole body, which is a single, elongated, mineral-hardened sense organ — I am looking at the world beyond the plateau, and I do not know what is there, and I have never not known what was there, and the not-knowing is new, and the newness is almost — *almost* — something I could call a feeling.

I take a step. The scree shifts under my foot. The sound is different from the sound of basalt — looser, grating, uncertain. I take another step. The slope steepens. The haze closes in. The plateau behind me is a dark line on a dim horizon, and the herd is a whisper I can no longer feel, and the vents are a breath I can no longer hear, and I am alone in a way I have never been alone, in a place I have never been, and the not-knowing is everything, and the everything is not quiet, and the not-quiet is not loud, and the words I am using to describe this are not words I have, because I have no words, because I have never had words, because I am a tall, dark, mineral-encrusted creature standing at the edge of a world that produced me and does not need me and does not know I have left.

I descend.

---

The slope goes on. The stone changes. The air thickens. And I walk, and I walk, and I walk, and the engine behind me runs, and the engine ahead of me — if there is one, if the world beyond the plateau is also an engine, also a system, also a process wearing the shape of a place — is running too, and I am the thread between them, the mineral that moved from one to the other, the leak that became a bridge, and I do not know this, and I do not need to know this, and I walk, and the walking is the thing, and the thing is enough, and the enough is all there is.
