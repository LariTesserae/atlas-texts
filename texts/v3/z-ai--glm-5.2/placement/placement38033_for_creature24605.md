I would be the one who almost stayed.

Not the Builder — I want to be clear about that. The Builder has something I don't have, or has lost something I'm still holding. The Builder presses their hands into soft stone and the stone keeps the marks, and this is a kind of faith I can admire but cannot perform, because my hands always pull back. My hands want to know what the stone will do *after* it hardens, and the Builder's hands don't care, and this difference is the difference between building and visiting, and I am a visitor. I am the most thorough visitor the room has ever had, but I am a visitor still.

I arrived the way everyone arrives. Sideways through the fissure, the granite scraping the skin off my shoulder blades, the drop that is a throat swallowing me into the mountain's warm, dark esophagus. I remember the moment the sound reached me — not through my ears, because by then my ears were full of water, but through my ribs. I felt it before I heard it. A low, grinding hum that rose through my bare feet on the warm stone and settled into my sternum like a second heartbeat, and I thought: *oh, something is happening here that I don't have a word for*, and then I thought: *I need a word for this*, and then the sound erased the thought and replaced it with itself, and I was in the room.

---

I stayed longer than I should have. This is the fact I keep turning over, the way the water turns over the stones in the basins, wearing them smooth with repetition. Three days. Maybe four. The sound makes time linear the way a blender makes fruit whole — which is to say it doesn't, it does the opposite, and so I cannot tell you with any confidence how long I was there. I know I did not sleep. I know I did not eat. I know that at some point I stopped being cold, despite being submerged in water that should have been cold, and this should have concerned me but it didn't, because concern requires a linear thought and the sound had taken that from me by then, and what I had instead was something that felt like standing in a chord, like being a note inside a chord, and it was — I need to be careful with this word — it was *good*. It was good in the way that sleeping is good when you have been awake too long, but I was not sleeping, I was more awake than I have ever been, I was awake in a way that did not have an off switch, and the sound was pouring through me like water through the fissure, and I could feel it rewriting the inside of my chest, and I let it, and that is the part I keep turning over. I let it.

The Builder found me on the second day. Or I found them. The room is too small for finding to be an achievement. I was sitting in the shallowest basin with my knees drawn up, watching the foam patterns dissolve and reconstitute, and I became aware of a warmth at my left side, and I turned, and they were there, and they had been there for some time, and I had not noticed because they looked like a place where the water had thickened. Their eyes were the same vitreous dark as the basalt. They looked at me the way the stone looks at the water — with a patience that was not kindness but was not its opposite. Assessment, the description says, and that's accurate but incomplete. It was assessment the way a doctor's touch is assessment — not cold, but not warm, just *diagnostic*. They were determining what the sound could do with me. I understood this without being told, the way you understand you are in a dream without knowing how you know, and I should have been afraid, and I wasn't, and this is the second thing I keep turning over.

I watched the Builder work. This is what I did with my time in the Reliquary. I sat in the water and I watched the Builder press their hands into the yielding stone, and I tried to understand what they were doing, and I could not, because understanding requires standing outside a thing and looking at it, and inside this room there was no outside. The Builder's hands would find a shape — not invent a shape, not choose a shape, but *find* one, the way your hands find a rhythm — and the stone would accept the shape, and the shape would stand for a while, and then the water would carry it away, or the stone would shift, or the chord would change, and the Builder would begin again. There was no frustration in this. There was no satisfaction. There was only the *doing*, the continuous doing, and I watched it, and I thought: *this is a person who has been erased by a sound, and they don't mind*, and then I thought: *neither do I*, and then I stopped thinking, because the sound had eaten the thought, and I was sitting in the water again with nothing in my head but the chord, and it was good, it was good, it was —

I left.

I need to say this plainly because everything else about the Reliquary resists plainness. I left. I pulled myself out of the basin. I put my hands against the walls of the fissure — the warm, still-soft walls, the walls that remembered being struck — and I climbed. I climbed the way water does not climb, which is to say with effort, with the specific human stubbornness of a body that has decided to go in a direction the medium does not approve of. The Builder did not stop me. The Builder did not watch me go. The Builder was pressing their hands into a new shape, and the shape was not for me, and the shape was not about me, and I understand now that very little in that room was ever about me, and this is a relief, and this is also a grief.

---

Here is what I am now. I am the one who carries the vibration. The text says this about everyone who leaves, and it is true about me with a specificity that makes me uncomfortable. It has been — I count — eleven months. I hear the sound in my refrigerator. I hear it in the plumbing when I run the bath. I hear it in the silence after rain, which is not silence at all but a residue, an echo of a sound that was never echo but origin, and my bones remember this, and they vibrate, and I have stopped trying to explain this to anyone.

I press my hand to walls. This is the thing I do. I press my palm flat against the drywall of my apartment, against the brick of the building where I buy coffee, against the concrete pillars of the train station, and I feel for the warmth. The warmth that means the stone is still becoming. The warmth that means the mountain is still deciding. I have not found it yet. The walls out here are finished. They have decided what they are. They do not yield under my fingers. They do not keep my marks.

But sometimes — late, when the city is quiet, when the traffic has died down to the specific hush that happens around three in the morning, which is not silence but a sound that has thinned enough to be almost transparent — I hear it. Not the full chord. Not the ten thousand impacts. Just a thread of it, a single strand of the braid, and my sternum hums, and I close my eyes, and I am in the room again, and the water is falling, and the stone is warm, and the Builder is pressing their small, patient hands into the rock, and the Drifters are drifting, and the filament is growing toward the loudest silence, and the mountain is still deciding, and the decision is yes, and I am not there, and I am not there, and I am not there, and my hands are empty, and the walls do not yield, and I am the one who almost stayed, and *almost* is a word that the sound has no use for, and I carry it anyway, like a stone in my pocket, like a mark pressed into something soft that has since gone hard.
