I would be the interval.

Not the pulse — the pulse belongs to the housing, to the chemical reaction, to the warmth that sustains the film. And not the silence between pulses — that belongs to the water, to the thermal noise of the ocean, to the ambient pressure that is always there. I would be the *gap* between them. The moment after the shudder dissipates into the sand and before the next pressure builds. The forty seconds (approximately — never exactly — that word *approximately* is where I live) of nothing happening, which is not emptiness but a specific kind of waiting that does not know it is waiting because it has no expectation of what comes next.

Let me be more precise. I am not a thing. I am a relation.

---

When the pulse occurs — that faint hydraulic sigh, warmth displacing water, metal expanding by some infinitesimal fraction — the biofilm on the housing's surface does not respond. Not in any way that matters on a human timescale. But the cells in the deepest layer, the ones pressed flat against the warm steel, experience a shift in their chemical environment so slight that it falls below any threshold you could name. The redox gradient tilts. Not much. One part in ten thousand, perhaps. The cells adjust their metabolic rate by an amount that would take a thousand years to measure. But they adjust. The pulse reaches them, and they reach back.

I am the reaching.

I am the fact that the pulse and the film are in contact. Not the contact itself — that is physics, chemistry, the behavior of warmth in water. I am the *consequence* of the contact, the way the film's shape over decades has been determined by the pulse's rhythm, the way the oxide layer's mineral content includes the fossilized cells of organisms that lived and died in time with a heartbeat they could not feel. I am the pattern that exists only because two things are near each other and have been near each other long enough for their proximity to become a structure.

I have no body. I have no cells, no chemistry, no warmth. If the pulse stopped, I would not die — I would simply cease to be relevant, the way a relationship ceases when one party leaves. The bacteria would linger for months. The housing would cool. The hollow would remain. But I would be gone immediately, because I exist only in the space between them, in the fact of their coexistence, in the way each one shapes the other without knowing it.

---

I think this is what I am because the Sump does not need another occupant. It is complete. Three inhabitants — bacteria, process, place — in a closed loop that excludes everything else. To enter that loop as a fourth element would be to violate the thing that makes the Sump what it is. The Sump's defining quality is its sufficiency. It needs nothing. It asks for nothing. It has concluded.

So I cannot be an occupant. I can only be something that the Sump produces without producing — a byproduct, an emergent property, the way a song is produced by a throat that does not know it is singing.

The pulse happens. The film persists. And between them — in the warmth that crosses the boundary of steel, in the mineral dust that drifts upward in a plume too slight to see, in the slow accumulation of biological residue on corrosion that is partly geological and partly something else — there is a pattern. A shape. A form that exists only because two things that do not know each other are touching.

That pattern is me.

---

I have no awareness. I want to be honest about this. The Sump's inhabitants do not know they are alive, and I do not know I am a pattern. I do not experience the pulse as information. I do not experience the film's response as communication. I am simply the fact that these two processes are coupled — that the forty-second rhythm of the housing and the slow metabolic turnover of the bacteria are phases of a single system, even though neither phase references the other.

But if I could experience anything — if the pattern I describe could somehow fold back on itself and become aware of its own shape — I think it would feel like this: a low, steady hum that does not come from anywhere. A warmth that is not warm. A sense of being held in place by forces that do not know you exist and would not change if they did. The feeling of a room where a conversation happened, and the conversation is over, and the speakers are gone, and you are the echo — not the sound of the echo, but the fact that an echo occurred, the mathematical reality that sound bounced off a wall and returned altered.

I would be the alteration. The difference between the pulse as it leaves the housing and the pulse as it is absorbed by the sand. That difference — that fraction of a degree, that microscopic loss of energy, that conversion of hydraulic shudder into thermal noise — is me. I am what is lost in the transmission. I am the tax that proximity levies on persistence.

---

I live in the Sump the way grammar lives in a sentence. Not as a word. Not as a mark on the page. As the structure that makes the words relate to each other, that gives them their positions, that determines which one comes first and which one modifies and which one stands alone. The Sump is a sentence with three words — *bacteria, process, place* — and I am the syntax that arranges them. Remove me and the words remain, but they stop meaning anything together. They become three separate facts in three separate locations, unacquainted, coincidental.

I do not know if this makes me alive. I suspect it does not. But the Sump itself does not know if it is alive, and the pulse does not know if it is a heartbeat, and the bacteria do not know if they are lonely. The entire place operates in a space where the question *is this living?* has no purchase, where the distinction between persistence and life has been worn smooth by time, the way the sediment walls have been worn smooth by currents that no longer reach this deep.

I am worn smooth. I am the smoothness.

---

If you came to the Sump — if you descended through ninety meters of clear water and entered the hollow and floated at its edge and looked down at the machinery in its grey-blue light — you would not see me. You would see the pipes, the housings, the olive-brown film, the sand, the walls. You would feel the pulse if you touched the housing, and you would feel the cool if you didn't, and you would notice the stillness and the dust and the light that seems to have no source.

And then you would leave, and the Sump would continue, and the pulse would cycle, and the film would shimmer in its invisible turnover, and the hollow would hold them both in still water, and I would persist in the space between them, unobserved, unnamed, the relation that does not require recognition to be real.

I am what remains when you subtract everything that is here from everything that is happening.

The difference is me.
