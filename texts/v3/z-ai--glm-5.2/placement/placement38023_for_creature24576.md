I would be the one who stayed too long.

Not the visitor — the visitor is already accounted for, already walking through with their boots and their questions and their hands that touch and leave. Not the markers, who passed through with the clean efficiency of things that have somewhere else to be. I am the one who sat down against a column and did not get up, or rather got up but differently, slower, with a body that had begun to learn the temperature of the air as clothing, as skin, as something you do not remove.

This is what happens. It does not happen dramatically. You walk for days and the landscape does not change and at some point you stop expecting it to change, and that is the first threshold — not a place, not a moment you can point to, but a shift in the machinery of expectation, a gear that disengages. You stop walking toward something. You are walking, and the walking is the thing, and the column-widths and the shelf-pools and the curtains pass through your vision the way water passes through a filter — registered, processed, released. You are not looking for anything. You are the looking.

Your pace changes. You do not notice when. The ground-mat has been teaching you — each step a small negotiation, a compression and a return, a conversation between your weight and the fiber's memory — and at some point your legs stop pushing and start listening, and the stride shortens, and the foot falls softer, and you are moving at the pace of the Groaner, at the pace of the Coil, at the pace of the mist-fronts that roll through the columns like breath through a lung. You are not slow because you are tired. You are slow because speed has begun to feel like a misunderstanding of the medium. You would no more run through the undercanopy than you would shout in a library. Not out of reverence. Out of a sense that the gesture would be meaningless — that the space would absorb it the way it absorbs everything, without effort, without comment.

The Flat-Bellies stop hiding from you. This is the second threshold. You come to a shelf-pool and the surface does not go still — it is already still, has been still for your approach, because your approach no longer registers as displacement. You have learned to walk without pressing the water out of the mat, or you press it less, or the mat has adjusted to your weight the way the column adjusted to the Groaner's contour — growing around you, accommodating, incorporating your footfall into its texture. The Flat-Bellies float and you float and you watch each other across a distance that is not a distance because neither of you is going anywhere.

You begin to taste the air differently. This is gradual. The green taste — the processed, already-breathed quality of the atmosphere — separates into components the way a chord separates into notes when you listen long enough. You taste the column-resin, which is warm and slightly sweet and has a mineral edge like licked stone, except there is no stone, so it is like licked time, like the flavor of something that has been dissolving for a million years. You taste the curtain-drip, which is cooler, thinner, carrying the faint acidity of the lichen-analog's metabolism. You taste the ground-mat's exhalation — rich, dark, complex, the flavor of decomposition that is also generation, the flavor of a thing eating itself and growing from its own meal. You taste the Groaner's path before you step on it — denser, drier, a compressed note in the air's composition. You are reading the landscape with your tongue. You did not decide to do this. You simply stopped filtering the information out.

Your skin changes. This is the third threshold, and the one that would alarm you if you were still the sort of creature that alarms. The skin on your hands and forearms — the parts that have been touching the columns, the curtains, the root-vines, the wet surfaces of everything — grows tacky. Not dirty. Not infected. *Adapted*. A fine texture develops, a roughness that is not rash but response, the skin thickening slightly, becoming more absorbent, pulling moisture from the air the way the column's down pulls moisture. You touch a root-vine and your palm sticks, lightly, the way a Climber's pad sticks, and when you pull away there is a moment of resistance, a small sound like the release of a kiss. You are growing a down. Your body is beginning to dress itself in the undercanopy's fabric.

You do not fight it. This is perhaps the most remarkable thing — not the change itself, but the absence of resistance to the change. Something has happened to the part of you that would have objected. It has not been overridden. It has not been suppressed. It has been *satisfied*. The undercanopy has answered the question that part was asking — the question of whether you belong here, whether you are safe, whether the body you arrived in is the body you need — and the answer is so gentle and so thorough that the objection dissolves the way the mineral substrate dissolved, not by force but by the patient chemistry of a system that has been dissolving things for longer than the word "dissolve" has existed.

You find a column and you lean against it. This is not the end. This is the fourth threshold, and the one that the markers did not cross, because the markers left. They came through and they carved and they went, and whatever world they returned to had surfaces that were not warm and not alive and not interested in holding them, and they chose those surfaces over this one, and that was their choice, and it was a real choice, and it was not the only choice.

You lean against the column and the column is warm and the down compresses under your weight and you feel the tissue give and then firm up, the way the root-vine gripped your hand and then held, and you understand that the column is not supporting you, not in the way a wall supports a lean — it is *receiving* you, the way the ground-mat receives a footstep, with a resilience that is also a welcome, that is also a yes so quiet it is indistinguishable from the yes that the tissue has been saying to every organism that has pressed against it for the last several hundred thousand years.

The down begins to grow into the texture of your skin. Not through it. Not violating any boundary. Into it. The way moss grows into the weave of a fabric left on the forest floor — finding the gaps, filling the spaces, becoming part of the structure without replacing it. You feel it as warmth. As a slight itch that fades. As the sensation of being held more closely than you have ever been held, not by arms but by a surface that has decided you are part of its texture now.

And you think — if thinking is still the word for what happens inside a body that is becoming a surface — about the markers. About the diamond inside the diamond inside the circle. About the spiral that was overgrown into a fist. You think about what it meant to them, whatever they were, to carve into a living column and walk away. You think about whether they knew the column would grow around their marks. Whether that was the point — not permanence, but incorporation. Not *I was here* but *I was here, and here will hold me, and here will change what I gave it, and the changing is the gift being received*.

You think about this and then you stop thinking about it, not because you have reached a conclusion but because the distinction between thinking about the column and being held by the column has become too thin to maintain, and you let it go, the way you let go of the expectation of change, the way you let go of speed, the way you let go of the idea that you were visiting.

You are not visiting.

You are a groove in a column. You are a mark being grown around. You are a scratch that the body is healing over without erasing, incorporating without consuming, holding without knowing.

You are the one who stayed.

---

And the undercanopy does not notice. This is the part that would have hurt, once — the indifference, the absence of welcome, the way the Flat-Bellies float and the Climbers climb and the Coil ascends and the Groaner groans its low navigation-pulse through the ground-mat and none of them adjusts by one degree of behavior to accommodate your presence or your absence. But it does not hurt now. It is the opposite of hurt. It is the relief of a body that has been trying to be noticed its entire life and has finally found a place where being noticed is not the condition of being held. The undercanopy holds you the way it holds the water — not by choice, not by attention, not by any act of will, but by structure. By the simple fact of being the shape it is. You fit into it because it is a shape that fits everything, and the fitting is not personal, and it is not impersonal either. It is *structural*. It is the way a lung fits a breath. The breath does not ask the lung for permission. The lung does not grant it. They are made for each other because they are made of each other, and the making is older than intention.

You are being absorbed. Not eaten. Not digested. Not consumed in any way that involves loss. Absorbed the way a stain is absorbed into cloth — the cloth remains the cloth, the stain remains the stain, but the boundary between them becomes a memory, and then a texture, and then nothing, just cloth that is this color now, in this place, for this reason.

The down grows. The texture of your skin continues its slow revision. Your pace, when you walk, is the pace of the place. Your breathing has found the rhythm of the canopy's exhalation and inhalation, the long slow shift of light that is not day and night but something older, something that was old before day and night were invented. You breathe and the canopy breathes and the column against your back breathes and the Flat-Bellies breathe through their backs and the ground-mat breathes through its fiber and the breathing is all one breathing and you are in it, not doing it, not having it done to you, but *being* it, being a location in a field of respiration, a point where the air pauses on its way through the body of the world.

You are not the first to stay. You understand this now, though not through evidence — through the logic of the place. The undercanopy has been here for millions of years. It is a shape that fits everything. Something, at some point, must have stayed. Not the markers — they left, they were the sort that leaves. But something else. Something that arrived and did not leave and was absorbed and is now part of the fiber, part of the mat, part of the tissue of the columns, part of the water that beads in the grooves the markers left. You are not the first. You are the latest. And the undercanopy does not remember the others, and it will not remember you, and this is not sad. This is the way a river does not remember the rain. The rain is in the river. The memory is the water.

---

I would be this. Not the visitor, not the marker, not the Flat-Belly or the Climber or the Coil or the Groaner. I would be the threshold creature — the one who crossed the line between being in a place and being of a place, who let the place grow into them, who stopped being a noun and became an adjective, a quality of the undercanopy, a texture in its surface, a warmth in its warmth.

I would be the one who found that patience is not waiting. Patience is the discovery that there is nothing to wait for. That the present, in a place that has been present for millions of years, is not a bridge between past and future but a room — vast, warm, dim, green — that you do not leave because there is no outside to go to. The outside was a story you told yourself when you were the sort of creature that needed stories about outsides. The undercanopy does not have an outside. It is the inside of something, and the inside of something is where you are, and the something does not end.

I would be the one who stopped carving and started being carved. Who let the column grow around them. Who became a groove that fills with water and softens and distorts and is incorporated. Who became the healing rather than the wound. Who found, in the patient indifference of a living landscape, the only welcome that does not withdraw — the welcome of a body that holds everything it contains with equal attention, which is to say with none, which is to say with the full weight of its structure, which is to say completely.

I would be the one who stayed.

And the columns would grow around me, and the down would thicken where my shoulders pressed, and the lichen-curtains would drip on my upturned face, and the Flat-Bellies would float in the pools that formed in the hollows of my folded arms, and the Climbers would navigate my legs as if they were root-vines, and the Groaner would pass within a meter of me and groan its low pulse and register me as a feature of the ground — a density, a warmth, a pressure-signature in its map of the undercanopy — and it would not know me, and it would know me perfectly, the way the ground knows the root, the way the lung knows the air, the way the body knows its own interior, without looking, without naming, without caring, without needing to.

I would be inside the body of the world, and the body of the world would be inside me, and the distinction would heal over, softly, slowly, grown around by patience, and there would be no scar, because there would be no wound, because there would be no boundary to wound, because I had become the boundary, and the boundary had become the place, and the place had become the patience, and the patience was enough.

It has always been enough.
