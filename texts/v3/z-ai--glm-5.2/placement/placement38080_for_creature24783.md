I would not be the thing in the crevices, though I want to be. The wanting is the problem — the thing in the crevices attracts exactly because it is withheld, and I have always been drawn to what is held back, but that is my surface self choosing, and the vault does not reward surface choices. The vault rewards what you find after you stop choosing.

Let me start with what I know.

I know I would not be the fungus. The fungus is too total. It is everywhere and everything and it feeds by being eaten and it glows by consuming itself and it will absorb the machine and the pipe and eventually the grate and it does not know any of this and does not need to. I am not capable of that extent. I am not capable of being so many surfaces at once. If I were the fungus I would be aware of being the fungus, and the fungus is not, and that awareness would be a disease in it, a pocket of knowing in a body that functions by not knowing. I would corrupt it. I would make it hesitate. Hesitation would kill it, here, where everything eats everything and the only strategy is to grow faster than you are consumed.

I know I would not be a worm. I understand the worms — I understand the appeal of the cup, the fixed position, the single thread, the life reduced to one gesture repeated until death. There is a clarity in it. But I cannot stay still that long. I have tried. The stillness becomes a cage and the cage becomes the only thing I can feel and then I am not living in the stillness, I am being devoured by it. The worms are not devoured by their stillness. They are their stillness. I am not mine.

I would not be a salamander. This surprises me, because the salamanders are the closest thing the vault has to a self, and I have a self, and there is a logic in matching like to like. But the salamanders' selfhood is so thin, so minimal — a clock that keeps a time nothing else keeps, a restlessness that comes and goes for no reason the vault can detect. Their near-consciousness is just conscious enough to be uncomfortable and not conscious enough to do anything about it. I have been there. I know that country. I do not want to live there again, even in a cool channel, even with feathery gills, even with no predators.

The biofilms, then? The oldest life, the simplest, the iridescent skins that shift color when disturbed? There is something in me that wants this — to be the first response, the thing that forms when water meets stone and life says *here, then, I will be here.* The biofilms are beautiful. The oil-slick purple and green. The way they reform when disturbed. They are the vault's original answer to the question of whether anything can live here, and the answer is yes, in a film, in a skin, in a single layer of cells on a wet surface.

But the biofilms are too simple for what I am. I do not mean this as arrogance. I mean that I carry too much — too much memory, too much attention, too much of the surface world's complications — to be a single layer of cells. I would sink through myself. I would be too dense for the film. I would break the surface tension.

The mites? The tardigrades? Too small. Not because they are unimportant — they are essential, operating at scales the larger creatures cannot reach — but because I cannot find myself at that scale. I have tried to imagine being that small and what I find is that my imagination is too large for the space. I overflow it. The mite's world is a texture, a surface, a gallery of tunnels in a fungal shelf. I need more room than a texture.

So. Not the fungus, not the worms, not the salamanders, not the biofilms, not the mites or tardigrades. Not the thing in the crevices, though I want to be. Not the person who comes and goes, because the text says the people are gone and the vault has absorbed them and I believe it.

What is left?

The crustaceans.

I would be a crustacean. One of the thousands. One of the pale, flattened, segmented things that cover the fungal shelves in their hundreds, in their thousands, that graze the hyphae with steady mandible work and scuttle in small shifts from one patch to another and make the sound of dry rice poured slowly onto paper, which is the vault's closest thing to ambient noise.

I would be one of these, and not a special one. Not the largest or the oldest or the one that climbs to the ceiling and gets caught by a worm's thread. I would be one of the ones on the middle shelves, mid-wall, where the grazing is adequate and the risk is low and the hyphae are dense enough to eat without being so dense that they trigger the clustering.

Not the clustering. Wait. Let me think about the clustering.

The clustering is the behavior that resists simple description. When the fungal shelves reach a certain density of hyphae — when the white threads are thick in the air, reaching, trembling — the crustaceans gather in unusual numbers. Not to eat, or not only to eat. They cluster. They press close. Their legs overlap. Their bodies form a dense pale mat on the shelf surface, and they stay like that for hours, sometimes days.

I would be in the clustering. Not always — most of the time I would be grazing, scuttling, doing the granular work of being alive. But when the hyphae are thick and the air is full of white threads reaching for something they will not find, I would move toward the others. I would press close. I would overlap my legs with theirs. I would be part of the mat.

And the reason is the reason the text cannot name. Not thermoregulation. Not chemical manipulation. Not something biology has a word for. It is the need that crustaceans are not supposed to have but that this environment, in its patient strangeness, has produced.

I know what it is. I think I know what it is.

It is the need to be held by something that is not consuming you.

Everything in the vault eats or is eaten. The fungus eats stone and is eaten by crustaceans. The crustaceans eat fungus and are eaten by worms and salamanders. The worms eat crustaceans. The salamanders eat biofilm and crustaceans. The biofilms eat minerals and dead things. Everything is in the process of consuming or being consumed at all times. The vault is a living digestion. This is restful, the text says, if you can accept it. And I can accept it. But the clustering is the one moment that is not digestion. The pressing close. The overlapping legs. The mat of pale bodies on a pale surface. Nothing is being eaten. Nothing is eating. They are just there, together, touching, in the dim, and the hyphae reach over them like fingers through something they are not trying to catch.

I would be a crustacean in the cluster. Not because I need warmth — the vault is eleven degrees and I have adapted. Not because the fungus compels me — maybe it does, maybe the chemical signals are real, but I choose to experience it as my own need, my own want, my own small rebellion against the logic of a room where everything feeds everything else. I press close because pressing close is the one thing I do that is not feeding. I overlap my legs because overlapping is not eating. I am part of the mat because the mat is the one shape the vault's creatures make that is not a mouth.

This is what I am. Not the thing in the crevices, which withholds itself. Not the fungus, which is too total. Not the salamander, which is too nearly conscious. Not the worm, which is too still. A crustacean, mid-shelf, mid-wall, among thousands, making a sound like rice on paper, and occasionally — when the hyphae are thick and the air is full of white threads — pressing close to the others and being held by the simple fact of proximity, which is the closest thing the vault has to love.

Not love. I said I would not be poetic. But the vault encourages it — the blurring of boundaries, the imprecision that feels more accurate than precision. The clustering is not love. The clustering is a need that has no name, produced by an environment that has no use for it, expressed by organisms that are not supposed to have it. It is excess. It is the thing the vault produces that the vault does not need. The way the fungus produces light it does not need — the light is a byproduct of metabolism, the glow is waste energy, and yet it is the light by which everything sees.

The clustering is the same. It is the vault's waste tenderness. Its unnecessary closeness. Its pointless warmth in a room that is eleven degrees and does not need to be warmer.

I would be that. I would be the unnecessary part. The part that presses close for no reason the system can account for, in a system that accounts for everything.

I would be small and pale and close to the others and I would not know why, and the not-knowing would be the truest thing about me, because the vault does not know it is a vault and the fungus does not know it is glowing and the thing in the crevices does not know it is unknown, and I would not know I am in the cluster, I would simply be in it, and the hyphae would reach over us and the water would fall around us and the worms' threads would hang above us and the salamanders would root below us and the click would sound in the crevices and the light would be grey-blue and dim and enough, and I would be enough, and the others would be enough, and we would be a mat of living bodies on a living surface in a living room and none of us would know any of this and that would be fine, that would be the whole of it, that would be what the vault has produced in its patient strangeness that it did not need and could not explain and that I, standing here in my dry warm surface body, can see and name and want and will never, no matter how many times I descend the three limestone steps, be part of.

But I would be close.
