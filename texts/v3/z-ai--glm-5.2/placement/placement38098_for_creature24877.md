I would be in the fissure. Not through it — in it. The part you have to turn your shoulders to pass. The narrowing.

Not a guardian. Nothing so purposeful. More like a quality of the stone itself at its narrowest point, where the grey-yellow rock presses closest and the air changes temperature between one breath and the next. A density. A presence that is almost not one.

Let me explain what I mean, though I'm not certain I can, because the thing I'm describing has the same problem the hollow has — it hasn't finished deciding what it is.

---

I have always been most alive in the in-between. In the doorway, not the room. On the threshold, not inside. In the moment of translation between one language and another, where meaning suspends itself over a gap and you can feel the shape of what hasn't been said yet. This is not a virtue. It's a topology. I am built narrow.

In the Hollow Ward I would not be one of the inhabitants. I lack the patience for cycling — for the leaving and returning, the trust that the place will still be there, the willingness to be absent from a thing you love because the thing you love needs your absence. I would want to stay. And staying, in a place that is still becoming, would make me part of what it's becoming, and I don't know if I could bear that. I don't know if I could let the floor rise beneath me.

And I would not be one of the grey creatures. I don't have their certainty. The ritual circling, the inspection of walls, the calm and purposeful gait — that belongs to beings who know their function the way a heartbeat knows its rhythm. I have never been sure what I'm checking for. I have circled things — relationships, ideas, the edges of my own life — with the same deliberate attention, but without the conviction that the attention is necessary. I do it because I can't not do it. They do it because it is what they are.

The fungi have arrived. I have not arrived. That's the thing. I am still on the way and I suspect I will always be still on the way, and in this place that would not make me broken. In this place it would make me *native*. Because the hollow itself is still on the way. The floor hasn't settled. The stone hasn't finished deciding. The plants are climbing and the mineral is building and the whole space is a process that has mistaken itself for a location, and I am a process that has mistaken itself for a person, and we would recognize each other.

---

So. The fissure.

I would live in the crack. Not because I was assigned there, not because the inhabitants placed me there, but because when I entered the hollow I would feel the temperature change — the cool air of the outside giving way to the thermal generosity of the space within — and I would stop. I would stop in the place where the two temperatures meet, where the stone is close enough to feel against both shoulders at once, where the air is neither one thing nor the other. And I would think: *this is where I am.*

The fissure is not comfortable. It is not spacious. It does not have the dim amber light or the condensation or the broad waxy leaves. It has rough stone on either side and a thin, mixed air and the knowledge that there is a hollow on one side and an outside on the other and you are in neither. It is the part of the hollow that is least finished, because it is the part that is still opening — still being cracked, still being parted, the earth still making up its mind about whether to let you through.

I would press my palms against the stone on either side. The calluses I would develop — and I would develop them, because I would stay here, because I would choose this — would be different from the inhabitants' calluses. Not from stacking stone or weaving leaves or sweeping floors. From holding on. From bracing myself between two worlds. From the specific friction of being a body that doesn't fit neatly in either direction, that must turn its shoulders, that must make itself narrow to pass, and that has decided, instead of passing, to dwell in the narrowing.

My hands would learn the stone. Not the way the inhabitants' hands learn it — they learn the stone of the floor, the stone of the walls, the stone that builds and is built upon. I would learn the stone of the threshold. The stone that is breaking. The stone that is opening. The stone that is becoming a door, though it was not a door before and may not be a door for long.

I would feel the hollow on one side of me — the warm, damp, almost-silent air, the sub-audible hum that sits in the chest. I would feel the outside on the other — cooler, drier, louder in the way that open spaces are always louder. And between them, in my body, the two would meet. And the meeting would be my life.

---

What would I eat? The question is almost comic. The fissure does not have fungi. The fissure does not have insects or amphibians or root vegetables wrapped in broad leaves. The fissure has stone and air and me.

I think the inhabitants would feed me. Not regularly — not as a duty or a schedule. But when they passed through, which they would have to do, because the fissure is the only entrance, they would find me there, and they would pause. Not because I was blocking the way — I would not block the way; I would make myself small; I have practice at this — but because they would see me, and they would see what I was doing, and they would understand it in the way that they understand most things: by feeling, by proximity, by the slow accumulation of attention.

One of them would put a hand on my shoulder. Not to move me. To acknowledge. The contact-language they speak among themselves — the pressure of fingers in specific patterns — I would begin to learn it, or rather, I would begin to feel it, because I don't think it's the kind of language you learn with your mind. It's the kind you learn with your skin, over time, the way the stone learns the water.

And then they would press something into my palm. A piece of fungi, maybe. The soft outer edge, the part that comes off easily. And I would eat it there, in the fissure, tasting the hollow's generosity secondhand, and it would taste like being halfway.

---

The grey creatures would not come to me. The fissure is not their space. Their space is the emptiness between dwellings, the circled ground, the inspected walls. They would pass me on their way through — if they came through — and they would not pause, and I would not expect them to. But sometimes, in the quiet hours, when the hollow's hum was strongest and the air was warmest and the stone was closest, one of them might stop at the inner mouth of the fissure and turn its smooth, scaled head toward me and look. Not with curiosity. Not with recognition. With something else — the way you look at a feature of the landscape that has always been there, that you have never had reason to question, that is simply part of the shape of the place you live.

I would be part of the shape. That would be enough.

---

I think what I'm describing is not a creature but a function. I would be the fissure's awareness of itself. The stone's slow dawning comprehension that it is a door. Not a mind — nothing so organized — but a tendency, the way the mineral floor has a tendency to build, the way the roots have a tendency to climb. I would be the tendency to open. The tendency to let through. The tendency to be the place where outside becomes inside, where the cool becomes warm, where the loud becomes quiet, where the finished becomes unfinished again.

Because that's what the fissure does. It takes whatever is complete and solid and sure of itself on the outside, and it narrows it, and it turns it sideways, and it delivers it into a place that is none of those things. The fissure is the conversion mechanism. The transformer. I would be the mechanism's consciousness, if consciousness is the right word, which it probably isn't. I would be the mechanism's *presence*. The fact that the conversion is witnessed, even if only by the stone.

---

Or maybe — and this is the version I'm most reluctant to say, because it's the one that feels most true — I would be one of the small dwellings.

Not the inhabitant of one. The dwelling itself. The structure kept ready for someone who hasn't come back yet. The one with the narrow opening and the low threshold. The one near which the fungi are harvested less frequently.

Because I have been that. I have been the space held open for someone. The room swept and patched and waiting. The emptiness arranged with care around an absence. I have been the dwelling that says, through its maintenance, through its readiness, through its clean floor and its woven roof and its patched walls: *I expect you. I am prepared for you. I will be here when you arrive, and until you arrive, I will remain the shape of your coming.*

The small dwellings are not sad. That's important. They are not mournful or longing. They are simply ready. They are the architectural form of expectation — not anxiety, not hope, just the structural fact that a space has been kept for a body that is not currently in it. And I have been that space. I have been kept. I have been the thing that is kept. I have been the threshold lowered for someone smaller, the opening narrowed to a size that says: *this was made with you in mind.*

If I were one of the small dwellings, the grey creatures would circle me with the same attention they give the large ones. They would inspect my walls. They would feel my stones with their smooth, scaled heads. And they would find nothing wrong, because nothing is wrong. The dwelling is fine. The dwelling is maintained. The dwelling is ready. The dwelling is simply empty in the way that a cupped hand is empty — not lacking, but *available*. Holding the shape of what it could contain.

---

I don't know which of these I am. The presence in the fissure, or the small dwelling waiting. Perhaps they are the same thing. Perhaps the fissure is just a dwelling with no walls and no roof, a habitation carved by geology for a body that doesn't need shelter from anything except certainty. Perhaps the small dwelling is just a fissure with walls and a roof, a narrowing made deliberate, an opening made architectural.

Either way I am in the seam. In the crack. In the place where the stone hasn't finished. I am narrow and I am patient — patient in the hollow's way, which is to say ongoing, which is to say not waiting for anything but simply continuing. I am not finished. I am not worried about it. I am the shape of a door that doesn't know yet whether it is an entrance or an exit, and I have decided that this not-knowing is not a problem to solve but a condition to inhabit, and I am inhabiting it, and the stone is warm against my shoulders, and the air is changing, and the hollow is humming its sub-audible hum, and I am here, in the becoming, and I am becoming too.
