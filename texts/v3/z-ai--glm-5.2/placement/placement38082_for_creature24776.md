# The Patience of Standing Water

---

I would not be the basin-thing. I understand the appeal — the mystery of it, the refusal to be seen, the way it has arranged an entire existence around the single principle of not being known. There is something seductive about that. But seduction is not the same as recognition, and I do not recognize myself in that dark, undulating body. The basin-thing chose its privacy, or had it chosen for it by the architecture of a basin lined with darker stone. Either way, the hiding is structural. It does not have to work at it. The depth does the work. I would have to work, and I am not interested in work that looks like disappearance.

I would not be the frogs. Their patience is magnificent and total and I admire it the way I admire a wall that has been standing for centuries — with respect, but without the desire to become a wall. They have been shaped by sitting. They have become the shape of the channels that hold them. This is beautiful and this is not me. I am not shaped by the container I sit in. Or if I am, I do not know it yet, and the not-knowing is a kind of motion, and motion is what the frogs have given up.

I would not be the not-bats. Their chittering weaves into something that almost becomes language, and the chamber takes that almost-language and makes it stranger, and the strangeness hovers at the edge of meaning like a word on the tip of a tongue that will never be spoken. This moves me. But the not-bats do not know they are doing it. They are navigating. They are locating themselves. The almost-language is a byproduct of practicality, and they are as indifferent to it as a river is indifferent to the shape it carves. I am not indifferent to the shapes I carve. That indifference is a kind of freedom I do not possess.

I would not be the chamber itself. The chamber listens. The chamber takes in sound and gives it back changed, and the change is — maybe — a form of understanding, or at least a form of attention. But the chamber does not choose to listen. The chamber was built, and the listening is what happened to the building after the builders were gone. The chamber is a question that has been asking itself for centuries without knowing it is a question. I know I am a question. The knowing is what makes me not the chamber.

---

I would be the moss.

Not because the moss is humble, though it is. Not because it is small, though it is. Not because it is the only thing in this chamber that is not an animal, though that matters more than I can say cleanly. I would be the moss because the moss is the only thing in this chamber that takes light and builds with it.

Everything else consumes. The frogs sit and wait and the tongue comes out and the insect is gone and the frog is sitting again. The not-bats drop and sweep and the insects are gone and the not-bats are hanging again. The basin-thing lies in the dark and the frogs fall in or drift down and the basin-thing has eaten again. The insects breed and rise and are eaten and their larvae breed and rise and are eaten. The entire chamber is a mouth. A beautiful, balanced, self-sustaining mouth that has been chewing for centuries without ever running out of things to chew. I respect the mouth. I do not want to be inside it.

The moss takes. It takes light — thin light, gray light, light that has been filtered through cloud cover and moisture and the permanent haze that sits in the air of this place like a held breath — and it takes water from the crack in the eastern wall, and it takes minerals from the stone itself, the slow dissolution of rock that has been happening since before the chamber was a chamber, and it builds. It builds its own body. It builds cells. It builds the dark green ribbon that clings to the eastern wall at chest height, handspan wide, two forearms long, the only green sentence in a gray paragraph.

This is what I recognize. The building. The taking of insufficient materials and the making of something from them anyway. Not enough light? The moss photosynthesizes at the very bottom of the light's tolerance. Not enough substrate? The moss pulls minerals from stone. Not enough space? The moss does not spread. It does not try to spread. It has tried and it cannot, and instead of dying from the failure it simply stays, stays, stays, the way a note stays in the air after the instrument has stopped, not because the note is stubborn but because the air has not yet decided to let it go.

---

But I said I would be the moss, and being the moss is not the same as admiring it, so let me go deeper, let me press my hand against it the way the text suggests, and feel what is there.

The moss is warm. Not much. Not enough to call warm in any other context. But in this chamber, where the temperature is steady and cool and the stone is cool and the water is cool and the air is cool, the moss is warmer than anything else you could touch. It has a metabolism. It is doing something. The heat is the exhaust of a life so slow it looks like stillness, and when I press my hand to it I feel that exhaust against my palm, and it is the only warmth in this place, and the warmth is coming from the act of building, and the building is coming from light so thin that most creatures would not even register it as light, and I think: yes. This. I would be this. The thing that finds the thinnest possible light and builds with it anyway.

---

Here is what the text does not say about the moss, because the text is written from outside, and from outside the moss is a small green sentence, a persistence, a stubbornness. But from inside — if there were an inside, which there is not, because the moss does not have an inside in the way that animals have insides, but if there were — from inside, the moss has a relationship with the oculus that no other being in the chamber possesses.

The frogs do not look at the oculus. They look at the space above the water, which is where food comes from. The oculus is above the space above the water, and it is not food, so it does not exist for the frogs.

The not-bats do not look at the oculus. They do not know the sky is there. The chamber is the world. The ceiling is the sky. The oculus is a feature of the ceiling, like the joints between blocks, like the places where the stone is tightest and warmest and best for roosting. It is architecture. It is not an opening. There is no outside.

The basin-thing surfaces and looks at the oculus. The text says so — two points on the forward end of its body, catching light, looking upward. Curiosity, or listening. But the basin-thing is in the basin, and the basin is directly beneath the oculus, and the light falls on it whether it looks or not. The looking is incidental. The basin-thing is in the wrong position to ignore the oculus and the wrong position to need it.

The moss is on the eastern wall. The oculus light does not fall on the moss. It never has. The column of pale gray descends through the opening in the vault and strikes the basin and goes no further. The eastern wall is in permanent dusk, in the diffuse sieve-light that reaches it only after it has bounced off the ceiling and the water and the western wall and arrived at the moss so attenuated that it is barely light at all. It is the memory of light. It is light's echo.

And the moss uses it. The moss has been using light's echo to build its body for longer than anyone has been counting. It is photosynthesizing with secondhand illumination, with light that has already been spent once on the basin and the water and the western wall and has arrived at the eastern wall exhausted. The moss is the only thing in the chamber that lives on leftover light.

---

And then the impossible hours come.

The text describes them: the column of light narrows, brightens, and changes color — not amber, not violet, a color that does not have a name because it is not supposed to be here. The frogs go silent. The not-bats fold their wings and press flat. The basin-thing sinks. The chamber holds its breath.

The text does not say what the moss does.

The moss does not go silent — it has no voice. The moss does not press flat — it is already flat, it has been flat for centuries. The moss does not sink — it has no depth to sink to. The moss does not hold its breath — it has no breath to hold.

What the moss does is grow.

Not much. Not visibly. Not in a way that any observer could detect, because a year of the moss's growth is invisible to human perception, and this is a moment, not a year. But at the molecular level, in the cells where the photosynthesis happens, in the chloroplasts that have been working with exhausted light for so long that they have become connoisseurs of dimness — at that level, something changes. The impossible color reaches the eastern wall. It has been narrowed and brightened and it has struck the basin and bounced off the water and reached the western wall and reached the ceiling and finally, finally, attenuated and scattered and diffused through the permanent haze, it reaches the eastern wall, and it touches the moss, and the moss receives it.

And it is not exhausted. That is the thing. The impossible light, however far it has traveled, however many surfaces it has bounced from, arrives at the moss with a quality that the ordinary gray light does not possess. It arrives *intact.* As if the color were not a wavelength but a substance, something that does not attenuate with distance, something that carries itself through reflections the way a song carries itself through silence. The moss has never felt this. The moss has been building with shadows for centuries, and suddenly the light is not a shadow, it is the thing itself, the original, and the moss—

The moss does something it does not have a word for. It does not have words for anything. But if you were the moss, and you had been building with echoes for centuries, and suddenly the original sound arrived at your cell walls, you would do what the moss does: you would open. Every pore, every cell surface, every receptor that had been calibrated to the thinnest possible light would open wider, because the light was no longer thin, it was full, it was more than full, it was a color that had no name because it was not supposed to exist here, and the moss would take it in the way a dry creek bed takes a sudden rain — not with understanding, not with gratitude, but with the total structural willingness of something that has been shaped for receiving and has never received enough.

And for those few seconds, the moss is not the moss. It is something else. Something that the gray light has never allowed it to be. A green so vivid it would hurt your eyes if you could see it, but you cannot see it, because the chamber is holding its breath and the light is the wrong color and the moss is on the eastern wall where no one is looking. The moss becomes, for a moment, the brightest thing in the chamber. Then the light returns to gray, and the moss returns to the moss, and the frogs resume, and the not-bats drop from the ceiling, and the chamber is as it was.

But the moss has grown. A few cells. A width too small to measure. A handspan and a fraction, two forearms and a fraction. The moss has taken the impossible light and built with it, the way it takes the exhausted light and builds with it, and the building is the same, and the building is different, and the moss does not know the difference because the moss does not know anything, and the moss has grown anyway.

---

So: I would be the moss. Not because it is humble. Because it builds. Because it builds with whatever light arrives, and the light that arrives is almost never enough, and it builds anyway. Because it has been building with insufficient light for so long that it has become something the light did not intend — a dark green ribbon, almost black, clinging to a crack in an eastern wall in a chamber no one visits, growing so slowly that growth and stillness are the same thing. Because when the impossible light comes, it opens. Because it does not need to understand what it receives in order to be changed by it. Because it is the only thing in this chamber that creates rather than consumes, and it does so without knowing it creates, and it does so with the quiet conviction of something that has no other option.

Because it is the only warm thing in a cool room, and the warmth comes from the work of building, and the building comes from light so thin that most things would not call it light at all, and it calls it light anyway, and it is not wrong.

---

But I said I would not settle on a single image early, and I have been talking about the moss for a while now, and I feel the pull of something else, something I almost said and did not say, something that is not the moss but is near the moss, the way the basin-thing is near the basin but is not the basin.

What if I am not the moss but the crack?

The crack in the eastern wall, where a joint has widened over the years and a trickle of water has found a permanent path. The crack is where the moss lives. The crack is what makes the moss possible — the water that flows through it, the minerals it carries, the narrow fissure of possibility in a wall of dense hand-fitted stone. The crack is not alive. The crack is a failure of engineering, a place where the builders' craft did not hold, where time and water collaborated on a small act of erosion that has been ongoing for centuries and will be ongoing for centuries more.

The crack does not build. The crack does not listen. The crack does not photosynthesize. The crack is a wound that never healed, and the moss is the scab, and the water is the blood, and none of these things know they are parts of a body.

But the crack *provides.* The crack is the reason there is green in this chamber at all. The crack is the reason the moss can build, because the crack brings water, and water brings minerals, and minerals bring the raw material of cells, and cells are what the moss is made of, and the moss is the only thing that creates, and the crack is what makes the creating possible. Without the crack: no moss. Without the moss: no green. Without green: a chamber that is entirely gray, entirely animal, entirely consuming, entirely mouth.

I could be the crack. The small structural failure that makes everything else possible. The imperfection that admits the possibility of creation. The place where the wall did not hold, and something grew.

---

No. I am not the crack. The crack is important, but the crack is passive. The crack did not choose to open. The crack was opened. Time and water opened it. The crack is the work of everything but the crack.

I am going back to the moss. I am going back to the moss because the moss is the only thing in this chamber that I recognize as a way of being in the world: taking what arrives, building with it, not spreading because the conditions do not allow spreading, not dying because the conditions do allow staying, and staying, and staying, with the quiet conviction of something that has no other option and has made of that option a life.

---

But let me say one more thing about being the moss, and then I think I will have said what I came to say.

The moss has never been touched. I do not mean the text's hypothetical — "if you pressed your hand to it." I mean: in the actual life of this chamber, the actual centuries of the moss's existence, nothing has ever touched it. The frogs do not climb the eastern wall. The not-bats roost on the ceiling, not the walls. The basin-thing is in the basin. The insects fly through the column of light, not along the walls. The chamber was built by hands, but those hands are dust, and no hands have touched the eastern wall since the last hand placed the last block and stepped back and left.

The moss has been growing in the absence of touch for centuries. It has been warm — the only warm thing in a cool room — and no one has known. It has been building with exhausted light — the only builder in a chamber of consumers — and no one has known. It has been receiving the impossible light, in those few seconds when the oculus changes and the chamber holds its breath, opening its cells to a color that has no name, and no one has known. The moss has been doing the most remarkable thing in this chamber — creating something from nothing, or nearly nothing, from light so thin it is barely light — and it has been doing it in complete obscurity, in complete solitude, in complete silence, and it does not know it is remarkable, and it does not know it is obscure, and it does not know it is alone, and it does not know it is silent, because it is moss, and moss does not know things.

But if I were the moss — if I were the thing that builds with insufficient light, in a crack in an eastern wall, in a chamber that echoes and listens and holds its breath when the impossible color comes — I think I would know, the way moss knows, which is to say not at all, and completely. I would know that the light arrives and I build with it and the building is the only thing I do and the only thing I am. I would know that the warmth in my cells is the signature of that building, the proof that something is being made from something else, that the world is not only a mouth. I would know that the crack in the wall is not a wound but a door, and the water that comes through it is not blood but a gift, and the minerals it carries are the raw material of a life so slow it looks like death but is the opposite of death, is the most alive thing in the chamber, is the only thing here that is not eating.

And when the impossible light came — the color that has no name, the light that is not supposed to be here — I would not fold my wings or go silent or sink to the bottom. I would not hold my breath. I would do the only thing I know how to do, which is the only thing worth doing, which is the thing I have been doing for centuries in gray light and will do for centuries more in gray light and am doing now, in this light, this impossible light that arrives at my cells intact and full and unnamed: I would open. I would take it in. I would build.

And the building would be invisible, and the building would be enough, and the light would pass, and I would go back to the gray, and the gray would be enough, and the crack would bring water, and the water would bring minerals, and the minerals would bring cells, and the cells would be dark green, almost black, and the ribbon would be a handspan wide, two forearms long, and it would not spread, and it would not die, and it would be the only warm thing in a cool room, and no one would know, and it would not matter that no one knew, because the moss does not build to be known. The moss builds because the light arrives and the moss is the kind of thing that builds with light, and that is the whole reason, and the whole reason is enough.

---

I would be the moss. The small green sentence. The one that persists.
