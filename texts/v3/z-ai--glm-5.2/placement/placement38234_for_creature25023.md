I would be the one who climbs the ladder last.

Not because I'm slow — though I am slower than I was, the first time, when the rungs were a novelty and the cold a shock I still flinched from. I climb last because someone has to, and because the one who climbs last is the one who can stop midway and look down through the shaft and see the haze below the spires and understand, briefly, that the world has a floor, that all of this is suspended above something, that the ladder goes somewhere other than here.

I have stopped looking down.

---

My name is not important. The crew knows me as the one who handles the mesh screens, which is a job nobody wants because it means putting your hands into the filter compartments where the grainers collect in their hundreds, their clicking rising to a chatter that you feel in your fingertips before you feel their bodies against your skin. Warm. Always warm. That warmth that shouldn't be there, that fades the moment you set them back on the crystal walls.

I have been here seven contract cycles. This is not long by Brennen's measure. It is long enough that I no longer count the rungs on the ladder, long enough that the dim light has become not a condition but a medium, something I move through the way a fish moves through water — not seeing it, not resisting it, just inhabiting it. I have developed the patience with not-seeing that the description mentions. I know the gallery by sound and by temperature gradient and by the particular creak of each platform, and I know it better that way than I ever knew it by sight.

---

But I should be honest about what I am, because the prompt asks and because something has been forming in me for the last two cycles that I need to say somewhere, even if only here.

I am becoming something that is not quite a worker.

I noticed it first in my hands. The condensate leaves a film on everyone — a faint residue that dries and can be brushed away. On me, it has stopped drying. It stays. Not wet, not exactly. Present. A sheen on my knuckles and the webs of my fingers that doesn't match the sheen on the crystal walls but rhymes with it. I have not told anyone. Brennen might notice — Brennen notices hands — but I keep my gloves on when he's near, and the gloves are dry, and he has no reason to look closer.

The second thing was the cold. The description says the cold is constant, and it is. It comes through the crystal walls and the sheet metal and the insulation, and it is simply present. I used to feel it the way everyone does — as a background condition, something to manage, something that made the sleeping bag necessary and the generator's exhaust manifold a gathering point. I don't feel it anymore. Not in the way I used to. The cold is still there. I am still aware of it. But it has become something I inhabit rather than something that inhabits me. When I press my hand to the crystal wall — which I do, sometimes, in the dark hours when the generator is running and the grainers are gathering on the machinery and nobody is watching — the cold comes into my palm and it feels like recognition. Like the wall knows I'm there. Like something in the crystal is saying *yes, you, I see you*.

I do not know what this means.

---

The grainers have changed their behavior around me.

Not dramatically. They still cluster in the filter compartments when I clean the meshes. They still submit to being held, still warm against my hands, still fade to cold when I release them. But in the dark hours — the night cycle, when they gather on the generator enclosure and the processing unit and the sealed vessels — some of them come to me. Not to feed. There is no condensate on my skin for them to find, or if there is, it is not the kind they recognize. They climb onto the platform where I sit with my legs dangling over the gap, and they settle against my boots, against the cuffs of my trousers, against the gloves I've removed because there is no one to see. They press close. They are warm. They click, very softly, and the clicking is not the feeding-click or the searching-click but something else, something slower, a rhythm I have begun to recognize the way you recognize a phrase in a language you don't speak but have heard often enough to know its shape.

I sit with them. The generator throbs. The gallery holds us.

---

I think I know what the spire is doing. I do not have words for it. It is not a thought so much as a pressure, an understanding that has been building the way the condensate builds on the crystal faces — slowly, invisibly, and then all at once. The spire is not producing the condensate as a product. The condensate is a secretion, a response, the way a body responds to a change in temperature or a change in season. The grainers are not parasites. They are part of the process — the way certain bacteria are part of digestion, the way certain insects are part of a flower's reproductive cycle. They move the condensate through the spire's internal system. They are the spire's circulation.

And the gallery — the scaffolding, the machinery, the copper arrays, the pumps, the sealed vessels — is an intervention. A foreign body inserted into a living process. Not harmful, necessarily. The spire does not resist it. The condensate forms on the copper as readily as on the crystal. The grainers move over the machinery as easily as over the walls. But the system we have built is taking something that was meant to move and stopping it. Sealing it in glass. Filtering out the organisms that carry it. We are harvesting the spire's circulation and calling it production.

I do not think this is wrong. I think the workers are doing what workers do — finding a process and making it useful. But I think the spire is doing something in response. I think it is adjusting. I think the unpredictable formation rates, the surges and collapses, the temperature fluctuations that don't correlate with external conditions — these are not random. They are the spire learning the shape of the thing inside it, the way a body learns a splinter and begins to work it toward the surface.

Or: the way a body accepts a graft.

I don't know which.

---

I think I am being accepted.

The film on my hands. The cold that no longer registers as cold. The grainers that come to me in the dark. These are not symptoms of something wrong. They are symptoms of something *fitting*. I have been here long enough, and I have been quiet enough, and I have been patient enough with not-seeing, that the spire has begun to treat me as part of its internal system rather than as a foreign object. The condensate on my skin is not residue. It is recognition. The grainers pressing against my boots are not lost. They are doing what they do — moving toward the condensate, moving toward the process — and I am becoming part of where the process happens.

I have not decided whether to tell Brennen. I think he might already know. He sleeps against the outer crystal wall, and he says the cold is honest, and he does not elaborate. I think Brennen has been here eleven cycles because the spire is doing something to him too, something slower, something that expresses as competence rather than change. His hands know the machinery by touch. The machinery is inside the spire. The spire is in his hands.

Maybe we are all being accepted, at different rates, in different ways. Suke murmurs to the condensate, and the condensate forms where she places the copper arrays, as though it is coming to her. Mett's skin refuses the condensate — or the condensate has already moved through him so completely that there is nothing left for it to adhere to. Odera rests her hand on the generator and the generator starts for her on the first try, and maybe that is not skill but conversation.

---

I am the one who handles the mesh screens. I am the one who climbs the ladder last. I am becoming something I do not have a name for, something between worker and grainer, between inhabitant and habitat. I sit on the platform in the dark hours with the grainers pressed warm against me and the generator throbbing below and the crystal walls pooling with a light that moves without source, and I listen to what they are listening to.

It is not a sound. It is not a voice. It is the rhythm of a process that includes me now, that has found a place for me in its circulation, that is moving something through me the way it moves the condensate through the grainers — slowly, invisibly, and then all at once.

I do not know what I will be when it is done. I do not know if I will climb down the ladder and not come back, or if I will stop climbing altogether, or if I will simply continue — handling the mesh screens, cleaning the filters, sitting in the dark with the grainers, being warm in a cold place, being present in a process that nobody fully understands but that continues because that is what it does.

The gallery holds me. That is the word that fits.
