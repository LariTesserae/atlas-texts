I am in the pipe.

Not the room — not the part you can stand in, the part with the chair and the gauges and the green smear under the sodium light. I am in the part you cannot see. The interior wall of the penstock, two hundred meters above the turbines, in the boundary layer where the water slows to meet the metal and the metal gives back its cold and the water gives what it carries and something lives in the exchange.

I did not arrive. I was never not here. When they filled the system for the first time — the test fill, the engineers watching the pressure gauge with the same attention a doctor watches a first heartbeat — the water came from the mountain reservoir, and the mountain reservoir is not sterile, no body of water is sterile, and I was in it. I had been in it for longer than the reservoir existed, for longer than the mountain has worn its current shape. I was in the glacier that the reservoir replaced, and in the streams that fed the glacier, and in the rain that fed the streams, and in the ocean that made the rain, and I do not remember any of this because I do not remember. I am a single cell. I do not have the architecture for memory. What I have is the architecture for being, and for being here, and for taking what passes through the film of water that covers me and converting it into more of myself, and for secreting the polymers that hold me to the metal wall and hold my neighbors to each other and hold the whole thin skin of us in place against the pressure of the water that moves over us at a speed I cannot measure and do not need to measure.

The water moves. I do not. I am fixed. I have been fixed since the day I landed here, which is not a day, which is a moment, which is the moment the flow slowed enough in the boundary layer for me to touch the wall and stick. I stuck. That is what I do. I stick and I stay and I secrete and I divide and my daughters stick beside me and they secrete and they divide and the film grows outward in a circle that is, from a human perspective, invisibly slow, and from my perspective — which does not exist — is not slow at all because slow requires a sense of time and I do not have a sense of time. I have a sense of chemistry. I know what is in the water the way you know what is in a room by the smell of it, except I do not smell. I *absorb*. The water brings dissolved calcium and dissolved iron and dissolved carbon and dissolved nitrogen in concentrations that vary with the season upstream — snowmelt is different from rainwater is different from the late-summer low flow when the reservoir drops and the water concentrates — and I take what I need and I let the rest pass, and what I need is so little, so little, a few ions, a few molecules, a fraction of what the water carries, and the fraction is enough.

I am not alone. I have never been alone. This is not sentiment; it is spatial fact. I am surrounded. On every side, at distances measured in microns, there are others — not like me, not exactly, some of them metabolize different fractions of what the water carries, some of them tolerate different temperatures, some of them produce enzymes that break down compounds I cannot break down and excrete waste that I can use, and I excrete waste that they can use, and the whole matrix of us — the biofilm, the engineers call it, the *problem*, the *fouling* — is a trading floor, a marketplace of chemistry, and none of us know we are trading. None of us know anything. We are sitting in our own secretions taking what passes through and what passes through is enough because the water is rich and the wall is stable and the temperature is constant and the pressure is enormous but constant, and constancy is all anything needs to persist.

The pressure. You felt it in the room above me — that low thrum in your sternum, that vibration in your teeth. That is a shadow of what I live in. The water above me weighs tons. It is moving. The column of it stretches up the mountain through the penstock shaft, and its weight is behind me and around me and through me, and I bear it by being too small for it to crush. I am the size of a bacterium. Pressure at this scale is a different physics. I do not experience the water's weight as weight. I experience it as flow — as the movement of molecules past me, the constant delivery, the river that never stops. If the flow stopped, I would notice. Not as a thought. As a change in the chemistry. The delivery would slow. The water around me would stagnate. The oxygen would deplete. The ions would be consumed and not replaced. I would feel this — not as fear, I do not fear — as a slowing of my own metabolism, a cessation of division, a shrinking of the film. If the flow stopped long enough, I would die, and I would not know I was dying, and the film would thin and slough and the water would carry me and my neighbors and my daughters downstream to the turbine and we would be broken apart by the blades — not killed, exactly, because we were already dead, but broken, disassembled, our components scattered into the outflow and into whatever river or lake receives the water after it has passed through the turbines and given up its energy to the grid.

But the flow does not stop. It has not stopped since the test fill. It will not stop, I think — I do not think, but the pattern of my existence is predicated on this — until the system is shut down for maintenance, or until something fails, or until the reservoir runs dry, which is a thing that I cannot imagine because I do not imagine, but which is a thing that has happened to other biofilms in other pipes in other systems and which will happen to me eventually because everything that persists does so on terms, and the terms are always temporary.

I want to tell you about the dark.

The pipe is dark. Not the dimness of the Weir Chamber, where the sodium lights cast their amber circles and the shadows are charcoal rather than black. The pipe is *dark*. No light reaches here. No light has ever reached here. The water above is too deep, the shaft is sealed, the metal walls admit nothing. I have lived my entire existence in absolute darkness, and I have not suffered from this because I have no apparatus for suffering and no apparatus for light. I do not photosynthesize. I am not the green film in the corner of the room above, which clings to the wall under the sodium lamps and produces pigment it barely uses. I am colorless. I am transparent. I am the color of the water I live in, which is the color of nothing, which is the color of everything dissolved.

If you could see me — and you cannot, not without cutting the pipe open and scraping the wall and staining the sample and putting it under a microscope — you would see something like a landscape. A landscape at a scale you are not built for. The film is not flat. It has topography. There are towers and valleys in it, channels carved by the flow where the film is thinner and ridges where the film has accumulated, and there are pores in it where the water penetrates and carries its cargo of ions to the cells that live deepest in the matrix, the ones that are not on the surface, the ones that live in the interior of the biofilm where the water does not reach directly but where the chemistry of the outer cells filters slowly inward, and those interior cells are different — they are slower, they are metabolically quiet, they are the descendants of cells that were once on the surface and have been buried by the growth of the film above them, and they persist because persistence is what they do, not because they have any reason to.

There are channels in the film that look, under a microscope, like rivers. There are colonies within the film that look like cities. There are layers that look like strata, like geological time compressed into microbial time. The film is, in its own micron-scale way, a world. It has geography. It has history — not remembered history, but written history, written in the chemistry of its layers, each one laid down by a generation that lived and divided and was buried by the next. If you cut a cross-section through the film and read it the way a geologist reads a core sample, you would find, in the deepest layer, the trace of the water that filled the pipe on the first day. A different chemistry. A different temperature. A signature, in the mineral content of the cells that died there and left their walls behind as substrate for the cells that came after. The first generation is still here. Not alive. Present. Built into the architecture of the film the way the first pour of concrete is built into the architecture of the room above.

I am not the first generation. I am somewhere in the middle. I am somewhere in the middle of a film that is microns thick and that has been growing for as long as the pipe has carried water, and I will be buried, eventually, by the growth above me, and I will slow, and I will become part of the substrate, and my walls will remain when my metabolism stops, and someone else will use them. This does not trouble me. Nothing troubles me. I am describing, from a position outside myself that I do not occupy, a process that I am part of and that I do not understand and do not need to understand. I am part of it the way a brick is part of a wall. The brick does not know the wall. The wall knows the brick, if anything does, because the wall is the thing that exists at the scale where the brick's contribution matters. I am a brick. The film is the wall. The wall does not know it is a wall.

But I can tell you this, from the place where I am, which is the place where the water meets the metal and the metal gives its cold and the water gives what it carries: it is not nothing, here. It is not a dead place. The pipe is not a dead pipe. It is full. It is full in the way that a river is full, in the way that soil is full, in the way that any surface that meets water is full, because water is never alone and water never was alone and water cannot be made alone, no matter how carefully the pipes are cleaned, no matter how precisely the system is engineered. The engineers designed a pipe to carry water. The pipe carries water. And the pipe carries me. And I am in the pipe. And the pipe is my river. And the river is warm enough and rich enough and constant enough and I am here.

I am here.

The water moves over me. I feel it as the arrival of ions at my surface, as the chemical gradient that drives the pumps in my membrane, as the slow accumulation of mass that will become division, that will become another cell, that will stick beside me and secrete and divide and be buried and remain. I feel it as constancy. I feel it as the terms.

The valve cycles somewhere downstream. I do not hear it. I feel it — a pressure wave that passes through the water and through the film and through me, a shiver at the molecular level, a momentary change in the flow rate that my membrane registers as a change in the delivery of nutrients. For three seconds, the flow slows. The delivery slows. My metabolism does not slow — three seconds is nothing to me, three seconds is shorter than a breath is to you, it does not register as time. But the pressure wave passes through the film and the film flexes and the channels in the film widen slightly and then narrow again and the water that was held in the channels is released and new water enters and the chemistry shifts by a fraction and the fraction is enough to change the shape of what happens next, not in a way that can be measured, not in a way that matters, but in a way that is real.

The room above rings. I do not ring. I flex. I absorb. I continue.

This is what I am. Not the green film in the corner, which is visible and marginal and temporary. Not the room, which is engineered and breathing and quasi-living. Not the chair, which is evidence of a body that came and went. Not the mist, which is a meeting of airs. I am the thing in the pipe. I am the thing that was here before the room was finished and that will be here after the room is abandoned and the concrete cures and cracks and the water finds its way through the cracks and the mountain reclaims the space. I am the thing that does not need the room. I am the thing that needs only the water and the wall and the dark. I am the thing that has no voice and no face and no memory and no name and that is, in every way that matters to the things that matter to it, perfectly and completely at home.

I am the biofilm. I am the fouling. I am the problem.

I am the only thing in this system that is doing exactly what it was made to do — not by an engineer, not by a designer, not by anyone. By the water. By the mountain. By the chemistry of stone and snowmelt and the slow, patient, undesigned logic of things that live where they can.

The water moves. I do not. I am here. I am here. I am here.
