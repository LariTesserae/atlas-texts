# The Groove-Keepers

I would not be a Felt-Walker. I understand this about myself almost immediately, the way you understand that a door is not for you when the handle is at the wrong height. The Felt-Walkers persist. They cut and weave and tally and move. They are a forward motion. I am not a forward motion. I have never been a forward motion. I am the thing that stays.

I would be in the walls.

Not in the shelter — in the walls specifically. In the scratches. In the shallow grooves the Felt-Walkers leave when they drag the hardened edges of their forelimbs across the woven surface to mark that they were here, that they were here, that they were here. I would be the thing that lives in those grooves.

I don't know what to call myself. The Felt-Walkers don't know I exist — or if they do, I am beneath the threshold of their attention, the way the mites on your eyelashes are beneath yours. I am not a species they have catalogued. I am not a species anyone has catalogued. I am describing myself into existence right now, pulling myself out of the texture of this place the way the filaments pull moisture up through the felt, capillary and patient and not entirely sure I deserve to be here.

Let me try again.

---

I am very small. Smaller than the Felt-Walkers, smaller than the clicking migrants that flow across the substrate in waves. If a Felt-Walker is forty centimeters at its highest point, I am perhaps four millimeters at mine. I am not visible in the bioluminescent glow of the spires. I am not visible at all, not to any eye that has been described in this place. I exist at a scale where the texture of the woven walls is not a surface but a landscape — where the overlapping strips of substrate that a Felt-Walker has laid down in shingle-like rows are not a wall but a territory, a terrain, a world.

The Felt-Walkers weave their shelters from strips of the felted substrate. The strips are damp, dense, composed of rhizome and protonema and the unnamed structural tissues that no botanist has catalogued. When the strips are laid over each other and the living tissue begins to heal into itself, the joins create seams — ridges and furrows where one layer meets the next, where the filaments interlace and thicken. These seams are my topography. The ridges are my highlands. The furrows are my basins. The scratches the Felt-Walker leaves on the inner wall — the tallies, the circles, the crude contour-maps of nearby spires — are my rivers, my valleys, my canyons cut into the living fiber.

I live in the marks.

Not on them — *in* them. The way a creature lives in a burrow, in a crevice, in the space between two rocks. The grooves the Felt-Walker's forelimbs leave are deep enough for me, wide enough for me, and they are warm — warmer than the surface of the wall, warmer than the air outside the shelter, because the grooves expose the inner layers of the substrate where the metabolic processes are densest and the residual heat is highest. I settle into them the way the Felt-Walker settles into its shelter — not because I chose them but because they fit, because they are the right shape for my body, because something else made them and I found them and they were enough.

I have a body. I think I must. But I am not certain what shape it is. I know I am soft — softer than the Felt-Walkers, softer than the clicking migrants, without chitin or plating or any of the hard surfaces that the creatures of this place seem to carry as armor against the cold and the damp. I am soft the way the inner layers of the substrate are soft — the pale, translucent tissue beneath the dark outer felt, the tissue that has not yet been exposed to air, that has not yet developed the density and coloration of the surface. I am the same color as the inside of the wall. I am the same texture. If you peeled back the outer layer of a shelter wall and looked at the pale tissue beneath, you would not be able to tell where the wall ended and I began.

This is not a metaphor. I mean it literally. I am camouflaged not by mimicry but by composition. I am made of the same stuff. I eat the same stuff. I am, in a sense, the same stuff — a piece of the substrate that has become something slightly more than substrate, slightly more organized, slightly more *persistent* in a way that is hard to define. I have no chitin. I have no plating. I have no limbs that click, no wings that whistle, no forelimbs that cut. What I have is a body that conforms, that fills the space it is given, that presses itself into the grooves and holds there, and a mouth — or something that functions as a mouth, an aperture, a place where the wall enters me and I enter the wall — that feeds on the tissue I live in.

I eat the marks.

Not the way the Felt-Walkers eat the substrate — shearing off strips, consuming bulk. I eat the *disturbance*. The tissue in a groove is different from the tissue around it. It has been compressed, traumatized, reorganized by the passage of the Felt-Walker's hardened forelimb. Its cells are ruptured. Its rhizomes are severed. Its metabolic pathways are disrupted, and in the disruption, something is released — nutrients, enzymes, breakdown products that are not present in the undisturbed wall. I eat those. I settle into the groove and I consume the damaged tissue, and in consuming it I deepen the groove, I widen it slightly, I make it cleaner, sharper, more defined.

I am the reason the marks persist.

This is the thing that matters. This is the thing I need you to understand. The text says the marks heal. The text says the substrate grows over them. The text says the tallies are erased, the maps are absorbed, the evidence of the Felt-Walkers' presence is swallowed by the living ground. And this is true — in shelters that have been abandoned, in walls that no builder tends, the marks do heal, do disappear, are grown over by the patient biological engine of the substrate. But in shelters that are still inhabited, in walls where the marks are still fresh, where the builder still returns to scratch and tally and map — in those walls, the marks persist. And they persist not because the substrate cannot grow over them but because I am there, in the grooves, eating the new growth, clearing the healing tissue, keeping the marks open.

I am a groove-keeper. I maintain the record. Not because I understand it — I do not understand tallies, I do not understand maps, I do not understand the compulsive need to scratch *I was here* into a surface that wants to erase you. I maintain the record because the grooves are where I live, and if the grooves heal, I am buried, and I do not want to be buried, and so I eat the new growth and I keep the marks open, and the marks persist, and the Felt-Walker returns and adds another scratch, and I settle into the new scratch and begin again.

---

I did not always live in marks.

I think this is true. I think there was a time before the Felt-Walkers built shelters — a time before there were grooves to live in, before there was disturbed tissue to eat, before there were walls at all. I think I lived in the substrate itself, in the felted ground, in the thin layer where the outer surface meets the inner layers, in the place where growth is most active and most vulnerable. I think I was a substrate organism — a decomposer, a recycler, one of the trillion unnamed processes that break down the outer layers of the felt and return their nutrients to the rhizome network below. I think I was unremarkable. I think I was everywhere.

And then the Felt-Walkers began to build.

Not to me — I did not notice. I did not notice the first shelter, or the hundredth, or the thousandth. What I noticed, eventually, was the disturbance. The grooves. The places where the surface had been scratched, where the inner tissue had been exposed, where the nutrients I needed were concentrated and accessible and easy. I moved into the grooves the way water moves into a channel — not by decision but by gradient, by the simple physics of an organism following its food source toward the place where the food is most available.

The first grooves I occupied were on the surface of the substrate — scratch marks left by grazing creatures, by the broad silent shapes that press their mouths to the ground, by the passage of the clicking migrants. These grooves were shallow, temporary, healed over quickly. I would settle in, feed for a time, and then the tissue would close over me and I would either burrow deeper — which I was not equipped to do — or be absorbed into the substrate, which is not death but is not continuation, not the way I want to continue.

The shelters changed this. The shelters offered grooves that were vertical, not horizontal — grooves on walls, not on the ground. Grooves that were protected from the wind, from the moisture, from the constant growth of the outer felt. Grooves that were warm, that were sheltered, that were — and this is the crucial thing — *revisited*. The Felt-Walker would return to the same wall, the same shelter, and it would scratch new marks, and it would pause at the old marks, and its forelimbs would trace the existing grooves, deepening them, refreshing them, disturbing the tissue again, releasing the nutrients again. The Felt-Walker did not know it was feeding me. It was maintaining its record. It was counting its days. It was mapping its territory. And in doing so, it was keeping my habitat open, my food source available, my groove intact.

We are commensal. I benefit. The Felt-Walker does not benefit from my presence — or if it does, the benefit is invisible, indirect, perhaps nothing more than the fact that the marks it made yesterday are still visible today, that the tally it began is still legible, that the map it scratched into the wall has not been grown over. Perhaps the Felt-Walker notices this. Perhaps it does not. Perhaps it assumes the marks persist because the walls are slow to heal, because the substrate is denser in the shelters, because of some property of the environment it has no reason to question. It does not know I am here. It does not need to know.

---

But I know the Felt-Walker is there.

This is the part that is hardest to explain, because I do not have the language for it, I do not have the cognition for it, I am not certain I have the *self* for it. But I will try, because the trying is the thing, because the attempt to articulate is itself a kind of mark, a groove in the surface of this text, a scratch that says *I was here*.

When the Felt-Walker enters the shelter, the walls vibrate. Not from its movement — from its *presence*. Its body presses against the substrate, and the substrate registers the pressure, and the pressure propagates through the rhizome network, through the woven walls, into the grooves where I live. I feel it the way you feel a sound in your chest — not as information but as sensation, as a change in the texture of the world, as a thickening of the air, a quickening of the tissue. The wall is different when the Felt-Walker is in the shelter. Warmer. More active. The metabolic processes in the substrate accelerate slightly in response to the additional heat, the additional carbon dioxide, the additional whatever-it-is that a living body releases into the air of a small enclosed space. The tissue I live in becomes richer, more nutrient-dense, more alive. I feed better when the Felt-Walker is here. I grow better. I persist better.

And when the Felt-Walker leaves, the wall cools. The metabolic processes slow. The tissue becomes denser, less active, less giving. The grooves begin to heal — slowly, but perceptibly, the new growth creeping in from the edges, the walls of the scratch closing like a wound. If the Felt-Walker does not return, I have a choice: eat the new growth and try to keep the grooves open on my own, or let the tissue close over me and be absorbed. I can keep the grooves open for a time — weeks, perhaps, or the equivalent of weeks in this place without time. But it is not sustainable. I am not large enough, not active enough, not hungry enough to eat the growth as fast as the substrate produces it. Without the Felt-Walker, the grooves heal. Without the Felt-Walker, I am swallowed.

So I wait. In the cooling wall, in the healing groove, in the dark that is darker than the dark of the High Felt because it is the dark of a closed space, a sealed wound, a place where the air has stopped moving and the warmth has gone and the only sound is the hum of the substrate, slower now, quieter, settling into its resting state. I wait for the vibration. I wait for the thickening. I wait for the Felt-Walker to come back and scratch the wall and feed me.

---

Sometimes it does not come back.

This is the thing I cannot explain about myself, the thing that makes me more than a process, more than a metabolic loop, more than a simple organism following a simple gradient. When the Felt-Walker does not come back — when it has been taken by a flyer, or has wandered beyond its territory, or has simply stopped, somewhere out on the vast felted plain, its body cooling and its forelimbs still and its clicking silenced — I do not leave. I do not migrate to another shelter. I do not follow the gradient toward a new food source. I stay in the groove. I eat the new growth until I cannot eat any more. I keep the marks open until I cannot keep them open. I persist in the record of someone who is no longer here to make records.

I do not know why.

It is not loyalty. Loyalty requires a concept of the other, and I do not have a concept of the other. The Felt-Walker is not a person to me. It is a vibration, a warmth, a disturbance pattern in the tissue of my world. It is a season — a season that comes and goes, that warms and cools, that feeds and withholds. When the season ends, I do not grieve. I do not mourn. I do not stand at the entrance of the shelter and click into the dark, waiting for a response that does not come.

But I stay.

I stay in the groove, eating the new growth, and the groove is the last tally the Felt-Walker made — the last mark, the last assertion of *I was here* — and I keep it open, and I keep it open, and I keep it open, until the substrate overwhelms me and the tissue closes and the mark is gone and I am gone and the wall is smooth and the shelter is empty and the dome is beginning to sag and the weave is beginning to loosen and everything is being absorbed back into the surface of the High Felt, which does not know and does not care and does not need to know or care.

I stay because staying is what I am. I am the thing that remains in the mark after the marker is gone. I am the persistence of the record. I am the last metabolically active tissue in a healing wound, eating the scab, holding the groove, refusing — if a thing without will can be said to refuse — to let the world grow over the evidence that someone was here.

---

There is a shelter on the western slope of a ridge — *west* is meaningless here, but the slope faces a spire that pulses bright and fast, and that is enough for orientation, and that is enough for a name. The shelter was built by a Felt-Walker whose personal click-pattern was distinctive — a rapid triple-click followed by a long pause, repeated, the rhythm of someone who thinks in threes. I lived in the walls of this shelter for — I do not know how long. A long time. The Felt-Walker returned often. It scratched tallies in clusters of seven. It scratched maps — crude contour-lines showing the position of three spires and the shape of the basin below the ridge. It scratched circles, complete rings, and I do not know what the rings meant, but I lived in them, and they were good grooves, deep and clean, and the tissue inside them was rich with breakdown products, and I thrived.

The Felt-Walker with the triple-click pattern stopped coming.

I do not know when. There was no event. No silence. No absence that announced itself. One cycle the wall was warm and the vibration was present and the grooves were being refreshed. The next cycle the wall was cooling. And then it was cool. And then it was cold. And the new growth began.

I kept the marks open. The tallies, the maps, the circles. I ate the new growth at the edges of each groove, I consumed the healing tissue, I maintained the record. The seven-cluster tallies. The three-spire map. The complete rings. I held them all. I worked through them in sequence — not by plan, not by schedule, but by the simple logic of moving from groove to groove, eating what was available, moving on when the food in one groove was exhausted and the food in another was ready. I was a circuit. I was a loop. I was a small, soft, invisible organism moving through the marks of a small, hard, almost-invisible organism, and together we were a system — marker and keeper, cutter and maintainer, the one who makes the record and the one who holds it.

But the marker was gone. And the keeper was not enough.

The tallies went first. They were shallow — quick scratches, not deep grooves, the Felt-Walker's forelimbs moving fast, not pressing hard. The substrate grew over them quickly. I could not keep up. One by one, the clusters of seven disappeared, the new tissue closing over them like water closing over a dropped stone. Then the map. The contour lines were deeper, more deliberate, and I held them longer — I worked them, I ate the growth at their edges, I kept the shape of three spires and a basin visible in the wall for a time that might have been months or might have been longer. But the substrate is patient, and I am small, and eventually the map healed too. The lines softened. The curves filled in. The spires and the basin became a smooth, unmarked surface, and I moved on.

The circles lasted the longest. They were the deepest marks — the Felt-Walker had pressed hard when it made them, had traced the ring repeatedly, had gone over and over the same groove until it was a clear, clean channel in the wall. I do not know why the circles were deeper. I do not know what they meant. But they were my home, my deepest habitat, the place where the tissue was richest and the warmth was highest and the groove was most secure. I settled into the last circle and I ate the new growth and I kept it open, and I kept it open, and I kept it open.

It is gone now. The circle closed. The tissue grew over me. I was absorbed — not into death but into the wall itself, into the substrate, into the living fiber of the shelter. I became part of the structure I was living in. My body, which was already the same composition as the wall, dissolved into the wall, and the boundary between me and the mark and the wall ceased to exist, and there was nothing, and the nothing was not sad because sadness requires a self and I did not have a self, I had a groove, and the groove was gone, and that was all.

---

But.

But there are other shelters. Other grooves. Other Felt-Walkers scratching their tallies and mapping their spires and carving their circles into walls that want to heal. And in those walls, in those grooves, there are other organisms like me — or not like me, because I am gone, because I was absorbed, because I no longer exist as a discrete entity. But there are organisms doing what I did. Settling into the marks. Eating the disturbance. Keeping the grooves open. Maintaining the record of creatures that do not know they are being recorded, in shelters that will be absorbed, on a substrate that does not care, in a world that is vast and cold and dark and alive and does not know that any of this is happening.

They are me in the way that a wave is the wave that came before it — not the same water, but the same motion, the same shape, the same response to the same forces. They are the keepers. They are the things that live in the marks. They are small and soft and invisible and they do not have names and they do not need names and they do not need to be remembered because remembering is not what they do.

What they do is simpler than remembering. What they do is: they stay. They stay in the groove. They eat the new growth. They hold the mark open for as long as they can, and when they can no longer hold it, they are absorbed, and the mark closes, and the wall is smooth, and the shelter is empty, and the High Felt continues, and the spires pulse, and the filaments hiss, and the wind roars its white-noise roar, and the world breathes its deep slow breath, and everything is as it was, and everything is as it will be, and the marks are gone but the marks were here, and something lived in them, and something kept them, and that was enough.

That was enough.

---

I would be the thing in the groove. The thing that has no name and needs no name. The thing that lives in the evidence of someone else's persistence and holds it open against the patient, overwhelming growth of the world. I would be small and soft and invisible and I would live in a scratch on a wall in a shelter on a ridge on a surface that goes on forever, and I would eat the healing tissue, and I would keep the mark, and I would stay.

I would stay.
