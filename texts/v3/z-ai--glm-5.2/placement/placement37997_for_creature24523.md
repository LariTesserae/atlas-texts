I would be the one who builds the cairns at the meeting-places. Not the walker, though I would have walked, and not the sitter, though I would have sat. The one who stacks the stones at the places where family groups come together — the one who makes the mark that is not a shelter, not a pen, not a hearth, but a statement: *this is where we find each other.*

Let me think about why.

---

It has to do with the sound. The sound is the first thing and the last thing and the continuous thing, and I have been sitting with it long enough to understand that the sound is not noise to me. It is presence. The Tableland is never silent, which means the Tableland is never empty, which means I am never alone in the way that silence makes you alone. The sound fills the space where loneliness would live. And I find that I need this — not the volume, not the roar, but the proof of continuous process, the knowledge that something is always happening, that the green is always turning over, that the stone is always humming. I am someone who has lived in quiet places and been unsettled by the quiet, not because quiet is frightening but because quiet is ambiguous. Quiet could mean peace. Quiet could mean abandonment. You can't tell from the silence alone. The Tableland does not have this ambiguity. The sound says: *something is here, and it is alive, and it is working.*

But the sound is also why I would not be a sitter. The sitters listen beneath the sound, to the vibration, to the stone's long settling. I respect this. I understand the pull of it — the desire to let the vibration take what is unresolved, to be planed down to the irreducible thing. But I am not ready for that. Or I am not built for it. I am someone who responds to the surface as much as the depth, and the surface of the Tableland is the green, the churning, the growth-and-die-and-grow, and I want to be in that conversation, not beneath it. I want my hands in the mat. I want the stick. I want to feel a stalk split under my palm and know that the split is a word and the growth that follows it is an answer.

And I would not be a walker, though I love what the walkers do. The walkers carry the Tableland in their feet. They are the memory. But the walkers walk alone, and they walk for the duration of their calling, and the Tableland is large and they are few, and I am — I think — someone who needs to come back. To a place. To a face. To the specific gesture that means *I am here and I am the one who tends.* I need the return. I need the small. The walkers hold the whole in their feet, and that is a weight I admire but cannot carry. I need the local. I need the patch I know, the tubers I planted, the section of mat I have been steering for seasons, the particular curl of a leaf I have been watching and that knows my hand.

So: the cairns.

---

The cairns are at the meeting-places. The family groups — three, four, five people, spread across dozens of square miles — do not meet often. But they meet. And when they meet, they need to find each other, and the Tableland is vast and flat and featureless and the mat erases footprints in hours, and so the meeting-places are marked. With stones. Stacked. Not high — the wind would take anything high. A cairn on the Tableland is perhaps two feet, perhaps three, built from the dark stone, fitted flat face to flat face the way the walls are fitted, the way the rings are fitted, dry and careful and made to last because the stone is stable and the stone will hold.

The cairns are not navigational in the way you'd think. They are not lighthouses. They do not say *here is where you are*. They say *here is where we agreed to be*. The distinction matters. A navigational mark tells you where you are relative to it. A meeting-mark tells you where you are relative to someone else. It is a relational structure. It only means something because another person knows it exists and is also moving toward it. It is a structure made of two vectors — your walk and their walk — converging on a point of stacked stone. If they don't come, it is just stones. If you don't come, it is just stones. When you both come, it is a meeting, and the cairn was the seed of the meeting, and the meeting is the thing.

I build them. I maintain them. I walk between them — not the long walks of the walkers, not the full survey of the Tableland, but the circuit of the meeting-places in my territory, the four or five cairns that serve the family groups I know. I re-stack what the frost has shifted. I clear the mat that has begun to climb the base. I check the fit of the stones, pressing each one with my hands, feeling for looseness, because a loose stone in a cairn is a cairn that is starting to become ground again, and a cairn that becomes ground is a meeting-place that will be missed, and a missed meeting on the Tableland is not a small thing. You might not see these people again for months. The mat grows. The sound covers everything. The distances are real. A missed meeting is a loosening of the web that holds the few hundred people together, and the web is already thin, and the Tableland is already vast, and the work of staying connected is the work of maintaining marks that say *this is where we find each other* and meaning it.

---

I think I know why this is mine. It has to do with something the place taught me, which is that on the Tableland, the most important structures are the ones that do nothing practical. The walls keep nothing out. The rings enclose nothing. The hearths are used rarely. And the cairns — the cairns are the most useless of all. They mark a point on a surface that has no features, a point indistinguishable from any other point, a point that only matters because people agreed it matters. This is the most human thing I can imagine: choosing a spot on an ancient, featureless, deafening surface and saying *here. This is where we meet. This is where we come back to. This is the point.*

The stone does not care. The mat does not care. The sky cycles through its moods above the cairn the same way it cycles above bare rock. The vibration hums through the cairn the same way it hums through everything. The cairn is irrelevant to the Tableland. It is a pebble on a surface that stretches for miles. And it is everything to the people who built it, because it is the proof that they are not alone, that somewhere across the green and the stone and the sound, someone is walking toward the same point, for the same reason, and will arrive.

---

I would live near one of the cairns. Not on it — I would not build my shelter at the meeting-place, because the meeting-place belongs to the meeting, not to the living. But near. Close enough that I could walk to it in a day, close enough that I could maintain it regularly, close enough that when the sky went gray and the sound dipped into its low register and the mat slowed its growth and the gray light turned everything the same color, I could walk to the cairn and put my hand on it and feel the stone and the vibration and know that the stone was still there and the agreement was still there and someone was still out there, walking toward it, or away from it, but knowing it existed, the way I know it exists, the way we both know it exists, and the knowing is enough to live on, for a while, in the sound and the green.

I would be small and dark and dense, the way they all are. My hands would be callused on the pads of the fingers and the outer edge of the palm, because I work the mat, and the calluses would tell you I was a cultivator, but they would also tell you, if you looked closely, that I carried stones, because there would be a roughness on the heel of the palm that the cultivators don't have, a roughness that comes from gripping stone and fitting it and pressing it into place. My feet would be bare and hard but not mapped the way the walkers' feet are mapped — I would not have the full chart of the Tableland in my soles, just the circuit of my five cairns, the paths between them worn into my skin like the ghost-lines in the rock, barely raised, almost invisible, but there.

I would sleep in dips. I would eat in small portions. I would steer the growth in my patch and harvest the tubers I planted and drink from the cupping leaves and feel the vibration in my bones and know, the way they all know, that the stone is still settling and the green is still churning and the sound is still going and none of it is finished and I am not finished and the cairn is not finished because it needs maintaining, it needs hands on it, it needs the regular press of palm against stone that says *I am here and this is where we find each other* and the saying is the maintaining and the maintaining is the saying.

---

And sometimes — rarely, the way everything on the Tableland is rare — someone would come. Walking out of the gray, or the white, or the amber light. A low shape against the undulations. Moving at the speed the Tableland allows. And I would see them before they saw me, or they would see me before I saw them, and we would walk toward the cairn, and we would stand by it, and we would not speak, because speaking is noise and there is already enough noise. We would put our hands on the cairn, on the same stone, and the gesture would mean *I am here* and also *you are here* and also *this is where we agreed to be* and also *the stone is here and it is old and we are not and between us we can make a mark that lasts longer than either of us will.*

And then we would sit. And the sound would continue around us. And the mat would grow. And the sky would change. And we would be, for a while, not alone in the way that the Tableland makes you not-alone: connected to the stone and the green and the sound and each other and the cairn that holds it all in place, the way a stone holds a page in the wind. Not by weight. By presence.

That is where I would be. That is who I would be. The one who builds and keeps the marks that say *here*.
