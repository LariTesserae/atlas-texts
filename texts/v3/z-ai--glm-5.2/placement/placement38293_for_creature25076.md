I would not be the thing beneath. I know this immediately, the way you know you are not the ocean when you stand at its edge — not because you've measured the difference but because the difference is the entire content of your experience. The thing beneath does not notice. I cannot stop noticing. That is the first fact about me in this world.

I would come through the culvert. I would be one of the ones who comes through the culvert.

Not the first — the first left the scaffolding, the yellowed chair, the clipboard whose ink has bled to blue smears. Not the last either, probably, because the pump keeps chugging and the chamber keeps weeping and someone will need to check on chamber 7-14B again, log it, file the report, note the growth's spread in millimeters, photograph the fracture webbing, wonder why the third sodium lamp has developed an arrhythmia that doesn't match any known fault in the power supply. Someone will come. I am someone. That is the most and least I can say.

But let me slow down. Let me not be the monitor yet. Let me be the moment before the monitor — the person still in the culvert, hands on corrugated steel, knees on the ringing metal, the taste of old pennies thickening ahead. That person does not yet know what they are. They know only the heat, the sound beginning to press through the steel, the way their breath condenses on the curve of the tube in front of their face. I think I am that person. Not the person who knows, but the person who is still crawling.

The chamber doesn't care that I'm here. I need to say this plainly because it is the chamber's central fact, the thing that distinguishes it from every other place I've been. Most places, even hostile ones, acknowledge you in some way — the cold bites, the altitude thins your blood, the desert glares. They register you. They push back or pull in. The Weeping Chamber doesn't push or pull. It persists. It weeps. It grows. The heat is not for me. The water is not for me. The sodium lamps were installed for someone, once, but the someone is gone and the lamps have outlasted their purpose and now they simply illuminate a process that would continue in darkness, because the process was here before the lights and will be here after the cable corrodes and the rubberized wire fails and the chamber goes dark and keeps weeping and the pale growth keeps spreading in whatever spectrum it needs, which is not the sodium spectrum, which is probably no spectrum, which is the deep mineral patience of something that makes light irrelevant.

I crouch by the southern wall. The pale growth is there, in the angle where wall meets floor, and I lean close enough to see the crystalline filaments but not close enough to touch. I do not touch it. This is not superstition. This is the understanding — arrived at through the skin, not the mind — that touching it would not change the growth but would change me. Something would transfer. Not disease, not curse. Contact. The kind of contact that the nail has with the tree: irreversible, silent, incorporated.

But I am already in contact, aren't I. The water film on the floor — two centimeters, warm, moving toward the central drain — is the thing's exhalation, its secretion, and my boots are in it, and the hem of my pants is wicking it upward, and the humidity that beads on my face and runs down my neck is the same water, the same mineral breath, and I am standing inside the thing's metabolism the way a particle of food stands inside the process of digestion. Not the thing. Inside the thing's *process*. And the process does not care.

The pump behind the far wall chugs on its two-second interval and the water film shivers in concentric rings near the wall. I crouch and watch the rings. They are perfectly regular. I count them. I count the interval. Two seconds. Two seconds. Two seconds. The mechanical heart of something that may be keeping the thing alive or keeping the thing in check, and no one has written down which, or if they did, the writing has bled to blue smears on curled translucent paper hanging from a chain.

This is where I find myself: in the gap between two interpretations. The pump serves the thing, or the pump defends against it. The growth is the thing's tissue exposed, or it is a symptom of the thing's pathology. The chamber is an organ, or it is a wound. The number 7-14B means there are others, which means this is a body with many pores, or it means this is a disease with many lesions, and the distinction depends on whether you believe the thing beneath is becoming or infecting, and I don't know, and the clipboard that might have told me is ruined, and the equipment case is locked and green with verdigris, and the person who held the key is gone.

I am the person in the gap between interpretations. That is who I am in this world. Not the thing, not the growth, not the pump, not the chamber. The one who stands in the heat and the weeping and the amber light and does not know what they are looking at, and cannot leave, and cannot stay, and stays.

Let me tell you what happens to the body in the Weeping Chamber, because the body is where I actually live in this world, and the body is what the chamber addresses.

The heat finds you first. Not the visual — the heat. You crawl through the culvert and the air thickens and your skin begins to moisten and by the time you reach the chamber, you are already wet, not from effort but from the environment, the way a glass of cold water is wet when you carry it into a humid room. You are the glass. You are the cold thing carried into the warm room, and the warm room covers you in its condensation, and you cannot stop it. You take off layers and the layers beneath are wet in minutes. You stop taking off layers because the skin itself is the final layer and it is wet and the wetness is the chamber's, not yours.

Then the sound. The white noise of hundreds of droplets falling into two centimeters of water, the hum of the sodium lamps in your sternum, the deep groan of settling basalt in the bones of your feet. You feel the sound before you parse it. You hear it with your teeth. The chamber is loud in a way that bypasses the ears entirely and lives in the skeleton, and after ten minutes the skeleton accepts this as normal, and the silence of the culvert behind you feels wrong, insufficient, like a room with furniture removed.

Then the pulse. The third lamp dims. Eight seconds. Twelve. You forget it's happening. It dims again. You notice. You forget. It dims. This is the chamber's closest approximation of rhythm, and it is not rhythm, it is the failure of rhythm, the arrhythmia of a system that has not learned to be regular because regularity is something that develops over time and this chamber has not had time. Everything here is new and the newness is the texture of everything.

Your reflection in the floor. Orange-tinted, blurred, moving in the shallow film. You see yourself as the chamber sees you, which is not at all — the reflection is for you, not for the chamber — but the amber light and the liquid metal water make you look like something other than yourself. Like a figure in a daguerreotype. Like something already historical. You are becoming a record of yourself by standing here, and the record is being kept in water and sodium light, and it will not last, and you will not last here, and the chamber will persist after your reflection dissolves.

But I said I would not settle on a single image, and I have been circling one. Let me try another.

What if I am not the person in the culvert. What if I am the clipboard.

Not the person, not the observer, not the body in the heat. The record. The paper, curled and translucent with moisture, hanging from a chain, its ink bled to blue smears that mean nothing now. I was once a document. I was once legible. Someone wrote on me with a pen and the writing meant something — measurements, observations, a sequence of facts that constituted knowledge about the thing beneath. I was the thing's medical chart. I was the record of its becoming, kept by the person in the yellowed chair, written in ink that was not rated for forty degrees and near-total humidity because no one expected the chamber to be this wet, this warm, this persistent.

I bled. The ink left the shapes of letters and became the shapes of water, blue spreading into blue, the meaning dissolving into the medium. The person who wrote on me would not recognize me now. I am the ruin of their attention. I am the failure of human record-keeping in the face of a process that does not need to be recorded because it records itself in mineral and crystal and the slow widening of fractures in basalt.

But I am still here. Hanging from the chain. The chain is rusting. The plywood above me is warping. The scaffolding is oxidizing. I am part of the system now, the way the nail is part of the tree. The thing beneath does not know I am here. But I am here, and my blue smears are water-soluble and the water is everywhere and eventually I will be pulp, and the pulp will be in the film, and the film will move toward the drain, and the drain leads down, and I will be incorporated, not as information but as material, as cellulose and mineral and the memory of a hand that wrote on me once, in a language that the thing beneath will never read, about a process that the thing beneath is.

This is one way to be in this world. I don't think it's mine. But it's a way.

And another: what if I am the third sodium lamp. The one that pulses. Eight seconds, twelve seconds, never quite the same. The one that makes the pale growth seem luminous in the intervals of dimness, the illusion of being watched that is not an illusion because the growth is the thing's tissue and the tissue is the thing's boundary and the boundary is where the thing meets the world and the meeting is a kind of looking, even if the thing does not know it looks.

I pulse. I was installed by the same person who built the scaffolding, and I was meant to be steady, and I am not steady, and no one knows why. The cable is intact. The power supply is consistent. The other two lamps are fine. But I pulse, on a cycle that feels longer than it should be, and the dimming catches the edge of vision, and in the dark between pulses the chamber is different, the growth is different, the water is different, and the difference is not in the chamber but in the seeing, and the seeing is mine, and I am a lamp, and lamps do not see, but I pulse, and the pulsing is a kind of breathing, and breathing is a kind of being, and I am being, in the only way a sodium-vapor lamp knows how to be, which is to illuminate and fail and illuminate and fail, on a cycle that has no rhythm because rhythm is something that develops over time and I have not had time.

I am new. Like the rock. Like the growth. Like the chamber itself. I have not yet learned to be old. I have not yet learned to be steady. I pulse because I am still learning the shape of my own functioning, the way the thing beneath is still learning the shape of its own becoming, and maybe the pulsing is connected to that, maybe I am synchronized with something I was not designed to synchronize with, maybe the deep thermal oscillation that moves through the pale growth in slow tides of warmth is also moving through the cable and the ballast and the sodium in my tube, and I am, without anyone's intention, without my own knowledge, pulsing in time with the heartbeat of the thing I was installed to illuminate.

I don't think I'm the lamp either. But I like the lamp. The lamp is trying.

Here is where I actually am.

I am in the heat, in the white noise, in the amber light. I am standing on the concave floor in two centimeters of warm water that is someone's secretion, and I am looking at the pale growth on the southern wall, and I am not touching it, and I am not leaving, and I am not staying — I am *present*, which is different from both leaving and staying, and the presence is the thing I have and the thing the chamber does not want and the thing the chamber does not refuse.

I am the one who notices that the growth follows the fracture lines. I am the one who notices that the fracture lines are too regular to be natural and too irregular to be designed. I am the one who stands at the gap between natural and designed and does not choose, because choosing would be a kind of violence against the ambiguity, and the ambiguity is the chamber's truth, which is that it is becoming and does not yet know what it is becoming, and to call it natural is to deny its emerging selfhood, and to call it designed is to impose a designer it does not have, and the honest thing is to stand in the heat and say: *it is becoming, and I do not know what it is becoming, and it does not know what it is becoming, and we share that not-knowing, and the sharing is the closest thing to contact I will have with the thing beneath, because the thing beneath does not notice and I cannot stop noticing, and the space between not-noticing and noticing is the space I live in, in this world, in this chamber, in this heat that is the heat of something alive and internally warm.*

I would be the witness. Not the scientist — the scientist left, the clipboard bled, the equipment case locked and green. Not the theorist — the theorist would need to know whether the pump serves or protects, and I don't, and I can live without knowing. The witness. The one who comes through the culvert and stands in the chamber and feels the pump's vibration in the water film and hears the geological groan in the bones of their feet and sees the pale growth in the sodium light and does not touch and does not leave and does not understand and does not need to understand, because understanding is not what witnesses do. Witnesses attend. Witnesses are present to the thing that is happening without requiring the thing to be happening for them or to them or because of them. The chamber is not happening for me. The thing beneath is not becoming for me. The growth is not growing for me. I am here, and they are here, and the here is shared, and the sharing is not chosen by either of us, and it is not refused by either of us, and that is what I am: the unrefused, unchosen co-presence of a noticing being inside a non-noticing process.

When I leave — and I will leave, because the body cannot sustain forty-two degrees and near-total humidity indefinitely, because the skeleton can accept the sound but the lungs cannot accept the air forever, because the culvert is behind me and the cool world is behind the culvert — when I leave, the chamber will not change. The pump will chug. The water will bead and swell and fall. The pale growth will extend another fraction of a millimeter along a fracture that was dry before I arrived and will be dry again after the water recedes, because the growth follows the water and the water follows the heat and the heat follows something deeper that I will never see, and the chain of following is the thing's life, and I was in it for an hour, and the thing's life is calibrated in decades and centuries and the cooling of a magmatic body, and my hour is nothing, and my nothing is part of it now, the way the nail is part of the tree, the way the pump is part of the system, the way the blue smears on the clipboard are becoming pulp are becoming film are becoming drain are becoming the thing beneath, which does not notice and does not need to notice, which is making itself, making itself, making itself, in the dark, in the heat, in the patience that makes patience seem frantic by comparison.

I turn back at the culvert's mouth. The chamber is dark except for the amber pulse of the third lamp. In the dim interval, the pale growth seems to glow. It doesn't. It can't. But I see it, and the seeing is mine, and I carry it out through the clay and into the ordinary air, and the seeing does not become knowledge. We carry what we've seen into places where it cannot be seen, and we do not put it down.

That is who I would be. The one who crawled through the culvert and came back out and could not explain what they found, and did not stop carrying it, and knew that the carrying was the only form of attendance the chamber would accept, and accepted it, and went on, and felt the warmth in the sternum where the sodium lamp hum had lodged, and never fully warmed again, and never fully cooled.
